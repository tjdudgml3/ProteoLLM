1
Introduction
Neurodegenerative diseases are characterized by progressive deterioration of the nervous system, with gradual and selective loss of particularly vulnerable populations of neurons. These processes eventually manifest as motor deficits and cognitive decline (
Schmidt et al., 2021
). The degenerative mechanisms often develop decades before the onset of clinical features (
Dugger and Dickson, 2017
). However, the current lack of sensitive and specific biomarkers continues to make early and accurate diagnosis an immense challenge.
The accumulation of misfolded and aggregated proteins is a neuropathological hallmark of most neurodegenerative diseases, implying an underlying severe impairment of cellular proteostasis and protein degradation (
Ciechanover and Kwon, 2015
;
Ross and Poirier, 2004
). Neurons are predisposed to the harmful effects of these protein deposits as well as damaged organelles, due to their longevity and inability to undergo cell division. In eukaryotes, the two major pathways of protein degradation are the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) and autophagy-lysosomal pathway (ALP). Progressive breakdown and perturbation of these systems, crucial for neuronal survival and functioning, results in accumulation of harmful protein aggregates and is a hallmark of aging as well as the onset of neurodegenerative disease (
Alves-Rodrigues et al., 1998
;
López-Otín et al., 2013
;
Schmidt et al., 2021
;
Watanabe et al., 2020
).
1.1
Ubiquitin and protein degradation in neurodegenerative diseases
The highly conserved 76 amino acid protein, ubiquitin, exists in abundance in all eukaryotic cells and plays a key role in both of the main cellular mechanisms for protein turnover, the UPS and the ALP. Ubiquitin is conjugated to substrate proteins through C-terminal glycine residue linkages. Ubiquitination occurs via the sequential actions of the E1 ubiquitin-activating enzyme, E2 ubiquitin-conjugating enzymes and E3 ubiquitin ligases. The fate of each modified protein is determined by the nature of the mono- or polyubiquitin linkage, with the majority being directed to proteasomal degradation and elimination through the UPS. Degradation-dependent and degradation-independent ubiquitin-signaling has been shown to play a major role in neuronal survival through both the UPS and ALP, as well as to be involved in membrane trafficking, DNA repair, transcription regulation, cell signaling, neuroinflammation and cell death control (
Bard et al., 2018
;
McClellan et al., 2019
;
Schmidt et al., 2021
;
Watanabe et al., 2020
). The functions rely on the availability of free ubiquitin and ubiquitin conjugates in intracellular pools (
Park and Ryu, 2014
). These reservoirs are maintained by the actions of deubiquitinating enzymes (DUBs) and
de novo
synthesis of monomeric ubiquitin. In neurodegenerative diseases, polyubiquitination of aggregating neurotoxic proteins have been shown to sequester ubiquitin and UPS components and deplete the pool of free ubiquitin, with detrimental effects on the ubiquitin-mediated processes (
Boland et al., 2018
;
Dantuma and Bott, 2014
). As this mechanism appears to be a shared disease feature, interventions aimed at reinforcing the proteolytic systems by increasing the recognition or handling of neurotoxic proteins offer wide-ranging therapeutic potential (
Boland et al., 2018
;
Dantuma and Bott, 2014
).
Whether misfolding and aggregation of specific proteins or degeneration of the proteolytic systems should be considered the principal neurodegenerative driving mechanism remains undetermined (
Deger et al., 2015
). However, the involvement of both mechanisms is supported by mutations in genes coding for either aggregating proteins or UPS components leading to the development of neurodegenerative disease (
Schmidt et al., 2021
). Moreover, a positive feedback loop has been shown to exist, where increased aggregation of toxic proteins further aggravates the impairment of proteasomal activity (
Bence et al., 2001
).
1.2
Ubiquitination of proteopathic aggregates
Modification of neurotoxic aggregates by ubiquitin and associated enzymes has been demonstrated in several neurodegenerative diseases (
Table 1
): In Parkinson's disease (PD) and Lewy body dementia (DLB), accumulations of α-synuclein constitute the major component of pathological deposits (synucleinopathies), either as cytoplasmic spherical filamentous inclusions in the midbrain or cortex (Lewy bodies) or thread-like dystrophic processes in presynaptic terminals of neurons (Lewy neurites). Immunoreactivity for ubiquitin can often be found as a halo around the α-synuclein core of the aggregates, as they are targeted for degradation by the UPS (
Boland et al., 2018
;
Spillantini et al., 1998
). Interestingly, some studies have found that unmodified α-synuclein is degraded by the proteasome in a ubiquitin-independent manner, suggesting that ubiquitination of the accumulating protein is an aberrant process reflecting loss of proteolytic control by a comprised UPS (
Tofaris et al., 2003
).

Diseases characterized by neuronal and glial accumulations of misfolded and insoluble tau protein include Alzheimer's disease (AD) and the FTLD-tau subtype of frontotemporal dementia (FTD). In these tauopathies, tau oligomers undergo ubiquitination to enhance proteasomal degradation as well as capture by autophagy receptors such as p62 (
Boland et al., 2018
;
Li et al., 2021
). The degradation signaling has previously been attributed to K48 and K63 polyubiquitin linkages, respectively, but emerging evidence also implicates K6, K11, K27, K29 and mono-ubiquitination in the processes, while K33 remains the least studied linkage type (
Li et al., 2021
;
Tracz and Bialek, 2021
). Recent evidence has suggested that tau ubiquitination may contribute to neuropathology by promoting its aggregation and mislocalization to dendrites. However, as ubiquitination promotes the clearance of soluble tau oligomers, which exhibit high seeding activity and toxicity compared to late-stage neurofibrillary tangles, the ubiquitination is most likely non-toxic in itself (
Li et al., 2021
).
TAR DNA-binding protein 43 (TDP-43) aggregates are a pathological hallmark of sporadic and genetic, tau-negative amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) and FTD (FTLD-TDP), and are frequently associated with ubiquitin and ubiquitin-associated proteins. Soluble TDP-43 is primarily degraded by the UPS, while aggregated conformations of TDP-43 are eliminated via autophagy (
Scotter et al., 2014
). The pathophysiological role of TDP-43 ubiquitination has been a long-standing question: Interactions between TDP-43 and specific E2 and E3 enzymes can either counteract toxic protein aggregation or appear to aggravate it, particularly in combination with other post-translational modifications such as phosphorylation and truncation (
Tran and Lee, 2022
). Aberrant effects of ubiquitination may also depend on the pathological TDP-43 species that are available as substrates, including misfolded, oligomeric, truncated and aggregated protein, or insoluble inclusions. Moreover, unchecked polyubiquitination of TDP-43 has been shown to sequester essential UPS components and deplete the intracellular pool of free ubiquitin (
Farrawell et al., 2020
). The same authors have presented similar findings in cell models of ALS with pathological aggregations of FUS or SOD1. In fact, this is a shared feature in neurodegenerative proteinopathies, which contributes to disruption of protein homeostasis and impedes the additional functions of ubiquitin.
Beta-amyloid pathology in AD is increasingly recognized as an intracellular as well as extracellular phenomenon. The toxicity of intraneuronal, soluble β-amyloid 1–42 (Aβ42) has previously been overshadowed by the considerably more abundant extracellular plaques, but are now recognized as a mediator of synaptic deterioration and prognosticator of cognitive decline (
Gouras et al., 2010
;
Takahashi et al., 2017
). The well-documented presence of ubiquitin in extracellular plaques in AD patients suggests that harmful Aβ42 are released from neurons, either intended for clearance as an active protective mechanism, or passively due to neurodegeneration. Conversely, neuronal uptake of Aβ42 forms is likely implicated in the propagation of pathology, akin to prion transmission (
Jucker and Walker, 2013
). The significance of such extracellular ubiquitination is not established, but it has been shown to potentially delay the breakdown of Aβ42 by the protease insulin-degrading enzyme (IDE), as well as modulate immune responses (
Bellia et al., 2019
;
Majetschak et al., 2011
;
Takahashi et al., 2017
).
1.3
CSF-ubiquitin as a biomarker in neurodegenerative diseases
Ubiquitin is thought to be released into the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) either passively due to neurodegeneration or by means of active secretion into the extracellular space. Although an invasive method of analysis, CSF sampling provides a specific and sensitive analytic tool, reflecting central nervous system conditions. However, the studies of CSF biomarkers in general, and CSF ubiquitin in particular are very heterogeneous and an overall assessment of the literature including gaps in knowledge could guide future studies and clinical decisions regarding the relevance of using CSF ubiquitin.
A novel biomarker in CSF may provide an additional tool for early diagnosis of neurodegenerative disease and differential diagnosis among them, or for assessment of disease progression and monitoring of response to medical intervention. Thus, the purpose of this systematic review was to review available literature on the potential clinical application of CSF ubiquitin in the diseases: AD, PD, DLB, ALS, FTD, and Huntington's disease (HD). The diseases being chosen based on representing a relatively homogenous patient group regarding average age of onset as well as an insidious disease progression. The diseases share common features of pathology involving harmful protein aggregates, as well as representing a universal challenge concerning accurate and early diagnosis (
Table 1
). Furthermore, biomarkers that reflect the effects of emerging disease-modifying drugs in clinical trials, irrespective of the specific aggregation pathology, are necessary to elucidate treatment response or even guide the initiation of treatment.
2
Material and methods
2.1
Eligibility criteria
Studies selected for the systematic review were original, full-text articles published in English, investigating the CSF levels of ubiquitin in AD, ALS, FTD, HD, DLB or PD. Studies had to include CSF samples from at least one of the mentioned diseases as well as a control group. Controls should either be healthy individuals or patients with other neurodegenerative or non-neurodegenerative, neurological diseases. Each study had to include at a total of least 10 participants.
2.2
Search strategy and literature selection
Six separate systematic literature searches were performed in English using the PubMed MeSH database to identify all relevant articles. Searches were performed on April 1, 2023. The search strings are listed below. After completing the literature searches, both title and abstracts were screened for relevance according to the eligibility criteria. Searches were performed with the filters “humans” and “English” applied. The following MeSH searches were carried out.
-
Ubiquitin AND (Alzheimer disease OR AD) AND (Cerebrospinal fluid OR CSF)
-
Ubiquitin AND (Parkinson disease OR PD) AND (Cerebrospinal fluid OR CSF)
-
Ubiquitin AND (Frontotemporal dementia OR FTD) AND (Cerebrospinal fluid OR CSF)
-
Ubiquitin AND (Lewy body disease OR DLB OR Lewy body dementia) AND (Cerebrospinal fluid OR CSF)
-
Ubiquitin AND (Huntington's disease OR HD OR Huntington Chorea) AND (Cerebrospinal fluid OR CSF)
-
Ubiquitin AND (Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis OR ALS) AND (Cerebrospinal fluid OR CSF)
Screening of title and abstracts for inclusion of studies was done by two authors.
2.3
Data extraction and study quality
Complete data extraction of the selected studies was performed by two independent authors using a standard data extraction form that included: Year of publication and first author; investigated diseases and types of control groups; study design and purpose; diagnostic criteria; inclusion and exclusion criteria; sex and age distribution for both disease and control groups; methods including CSF handling and analysis; blinding of analysts; and results of statistical analyses.

3.2
Preanalytical variables
Sampling of CSF and processing of samples varied among the included studies. Only two studies reported time of CSF sample collection during the day – in the morning hours before noon (
Chalbot et al., 2011
;
Constantinescu et al., 2010
), with fourteen not including any information on timing, and one study performing the CSF sampling post-mortem (
Kudo et al., 1994
). Fourteen studies stated that CSF samples were kept at temperatures of minus 70–80 °C, two studies reported storage temperatures of minus 20–30 °C (
Heywood et al., 2015
;
Manaka et al., 1992
), and one did not inform about storage (
Blennow et al., 1994
). In seven studies the CSF samples were stored in polypropylene tubes, whereas one study used silicone coated tubes (
Blennow et al., 1994
) and the remaining nine did not report anything on the matter.
3.3
Analytical variables
Nine studies analyzed CSF ubiquitin levels with different mass spectrometry techniques and the remaining eight used either enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) (
Blennow et al., 1994
;
Chalbot et al., 2011
;
Kandimalla et al., 2011
,
2014
), competitive ELISA (cELISA) (
Iqbal et al., 2005
) competitive enzyme-linked immunoflow assay (CELIFA) (
Kudo et al., 1994
) radioimmunoassay (RIA) (
Manaka et al., 1992
) or Western Blot (
Mendonça et al., 2011
). For mass spectrometry approaches, three studies used liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) (
Abu-Rumeileh et al., 2020
;
Heywood et al., 2015
;
Oeckl et al., 2014
), two used surface-enhanced laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (SELDI-TOF-MS) (
Constantinescu et al., 2010
;
Vinther-Jensen et al., 2015
), one used matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF-MS) (
Davidsson et al., 2002
) and three used parallel reaction monitoring-mass spectrometry (PRM-MS) (
Sjödin et al., 2017
,
2019
;
Toft et al., 2023
). Two studies employed a PRM-MS technique that quantified the CSF levels of two proteotypic ubiquitin peptides (
Sjödin et al., 2019
;
Toft et al., 2023
), whilst the other studies measured the intact ubiquitin protein, in either its free or conjugated form.
3.4
Patient data
The clinical workup of patients and controls differed between studies. All studies provided information on which diagnostic criteria had been used to diagnose the participating patients. A total of six studies reported having performed diagnostic imaging – either CT, MRI, or undefined brain imaging – on all included patients, except patients belonging to a pilot study cohort in one study (
Sjödin et al., 2019
). Thirteen studies included patients with a diagnosis of AD, of which six measured core CSF biomarkers for AD, namely t-tau, p-tau, or Aβ42, as a part of the clinical workup (
Abu-Rumeileh et al., 2020
;
Heywood et al., 2015
;
Kandimalla et al., 2011
,
2014
;
Sjödin et al., 2017
,
2019
). The 17 studies involved a total of 2366 participants, encompassing 841 AD patients, 30 with ALS, 63 with FTD, 94 with HD, 27 with DLB and 124 with PD. The control groups amounted to 1187 participants, comprising 449 healthy controls, 316 non-neurodegenerative or neurological controls, 191 patients with a different type of dementia or cognitive impairment than the one of interest in the study and 231 patients with other neurodegenerative or neuroinflammatory diseases than the ones included in our study. Of the 17 studies included, two stated having matched patient and control groups for age and sex (
Abu-Rumeileh et al., 2020
;
Constantinescu et al., 2010
), and two additional studies had matched only for age (
Heywood et al., 2015
;
Kandimalla et al., 2014
).

Parkinson's disease
CSF ubiquitin concentrations in patients diagnosed with PD were analyzed in five studies (

In the study by Sjödin et al. from 2019, the use of a PRM-MS technique revealed significantly decreased levels of ubiquitin in the PD group when compared to HC.
3.5.3
Lewy body dementia
One study examined levels of CSF ubiquitin in DLB patients compared with healthy controls, finding significantly increased levels, employing a LC-MS/MS technique (
Heywood et al., 2015
).
3.5.4
Frontotemporal dementia
Three studies evaluated the concentration of CSF ubiquitin in FTD. Two studies using LC-MS/MS found equal levels in patients and HC (
Abu-Rumeileh et al., 2020
;
Oeckl et al., 2014

CHMP2B
mutation.
3.5.5
Huntington's disease
CSF ubiquitin in patients with Huntington's disease was quantified in one study using a SELDI-TOF-MS technique. The study demonstrated increased levels of ubiquitin in both the manifest and pre-manifest HD groups when compared to gene-expansion negative family controls, but with only the HD-manifest patients reaching statistical significance (
Vinther-Jensen et al., 2015
).
3.5.6
Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis
Two studies measured CSF ubiquitin in ALS patients. Using western blot, ubiquitin was not detected in CSF from either the ALS or healthy control group
Mendonça et al., 2011
). Oeckl et al. did not detect any statistically significant differences in ubiquitin levels between ALS patients and controls using LC-MS/MS (
Oeckl et al., 2014
).
3.6
Comparative diagnostic value of CSF ubiquitin in different neurodegenerative diseases
To discriminate between the different diseases, three studies included analyses assessing the comparative diagnostic value of CSF ubiquitin. Discrimination between AD and FTD was investigated in two studies, both discovering a significantly higher level of ubiquitin in AD patients: Oeckl et al. performed ROC analysis for the classification, reporting an AUC of 0.776, a sensitivity of 53 % and a specificity of 100 %, using a cut-off level of 32.1 ng/mL ubiquitin (
Oeckl et al., 2014
). Abu-Rumeileh et al. reported an AUC of 0.880 with 87 % sensitivity and 80 % specificity at a cut-off value of 45.7 ng/mL (
Abu-Rumeileh et al., 2020
). Sjödin et al. showed a significant decrease of CSF ubiquitin in PD patients compared with AD and prodromal AD patients (
Sjödin et al., 2019
), whilst a previous study did not observe any difference between AD and PD (
Oeckl et al., 2014
). CSF ubiquitin levels were markedly higher in Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (CJD) than AD in two out of two studies (
Abu-Rumeileh et al., 2020
;
Manaka et al., 1992
), with one calculating an AUC of 0.848 with 77 % sensitivity and 90 % specificity, at a 68.3 ng/mL cut-off value (
Abu-Rumeileh et al., 2020
). No considerable differences in CSF ubiquitin were found between AD patients and patients with either ALS (
Oeckl et al., 2014
), DLB (
Iqbal et al., 2005
), vascular dementia (
Blennow et al., 1994
;
Manaka et al., 1992
), or MCI (
Sjödin et al., 2019
). Constantinescu et al. observed elevated CSF ubiquitin in PSP patients compared to PD, but this could not be reproduced in a subsequent study (
Sjödin et al., 2017
).

3.8
CSF ubiquitin as a disease severity marker
Two studies observed correlations between the levels of CSF ubiquitin and different disease severity markers. When patients in the AD group were categorized by intervals based on their MMSE score, one study showed a significant difference in the mean levels of ubiquitin, with higher levels of ubiquitin correlating with worsening of MMSE-score (
Kandimalla et al., 2014
). Another study, including patients who were carriers of the HD gene premanifest and manifest of disease, found correlations with CAG-Age Product (CAP) score as well as Unified HD Rating Scale - Total Functional Capacity (UHDRS-TFC) scores reaching statistical significance. Levels of ubiquitin in the manifest gene expansion carriers were found to increase with increasing CAP scores and decreasing UHDRS-TFC scores (
Vinther-Jensen et al., 2015
).
4
Discussion
Ubiquitin plays a key role in maintaining neuronal functioning and survival, as well as being a major but often overlooked component of pathological protein aggregates associated with neurodegenerative diseases (
Watanabe et al., 2020
). The purpose of this study was to review the available literature assessing CSF ubiquitin as a potential biomarker for diseases sharing common neuropathology involving, among other features, abnormal protein aggregation.
Patients with AD were the most abundantly represented patient group in the different studies included. When compared to control cohorts a significant increase in CSF levels was found in nine out of the 13 studies involving AD patients. Higher CSF ubiquitin levels could distinguish AD from FTD in two studies (
Abu-Rumeileh et al., 2020
;
Oeckl et al., 2014
), and from PD in one study (
Sjödin et al., 2019
). Despite limitations of the included studies, the results for the AD patient groups suggest a justifiable association between increased CSF ubiquitin and AD pathology. For the additional disease entities included in this review – ALS, FTD, HD, DLB and PD – similar conclusions cannot be drawn as available research is too sparse. Regarding ALS, the studies did not show any differences in CSF ubiquitin levels (
Mendonça et al., 2011
;
Oeckl et al., 2014
). For both HD and DLB, one study was available and showed a significantly increased ubiquitin concentration in comparison to controls (
Heywood et al., 2015
;
Vinther-Jensen et al., 2015
). In FTD and PD, respectively, one of three and one of five studies found significantly altered CSF levels in patients compared with healthy controls, with patients having lower ubiquitin levels than controls (
Sjödin et al., 2019
;
Toft et al., 2023
).
A definition of diagnostic criteria applied to each disease group was included in all studies. However, the studies were often limited by the lack of definite inclusion and exclusion criteria for both patients and controls, as well as limited documentation of selection and definition of control groups. Common to all studies was relatively small sample sizes, with nine of the studies having below 20 participants in the disease groups. Only four studies had matched patient and control cohorts for age (
Abu-Rumeileh et al., 2020
;
Constantinescu et al., 2010
;
Heywood et al., 2015
;
Kandimalla et al., 2014
). The activities of the two major proteolytic systems in the cell, the ALP and UPS, decline with age, suggesting a collapse of cell proteostasis being one of the principal hallmarks of aging as well as of neurodegenerative diseases, with a possible effect on baseline levels of CSF ubiquitin (
López-Otín et al., 2013
;
Rubinsztein et al., 2011
;
Schmidt et al., 2021
;
Tomaru et al., 2012
). Of the reviewed studies, nine included a correlation between CSF ubiquitin concentrations and age: Three demonstrated a positive correlation (
Iqbal et al., 2005
;
Oeckl et al., 2014
;
Vinther-Jensen et al., 2015
) and the remainder presented insignificant results. Based on available literature, it is indeterminable whether CSF ubiquitin levels remain unchanged or tend to slightly increase with age. Future studies should include measurements of CSF ubiquitin over time, as the temporal profile of CSF ubiquitin is likely to be associated with disease stage, but cannot be established with the cross-sectional data presented in the studies included here. Beyond establishing whether age-associated changes exist, longitudinal assessments are required to ascertain the extent of fluctuation; evaluate the potential as an early indicator of developing pathology; and explore the likelihood of an initial “sequestration-phase” followed by a subsequent “release-phase” with advancing neurodegeneration.
The different studies reviewed here showed great heterogeneity in study protocol and execution including analytical and pre-analytical variables. Methods of analysis were mainly divided between immunoassays and mass spectrometry techniques. Immunoassays traditionally show high sensitivity through immuno-selectivity offered by the specific antibody-antigen complexes, but limitations include selectivity only being as good as that offered by the antibody in use. Anti-ubiquitin antibodies exhibit differences in affinity for conjugated versus non-conjugated ubiquitin. Mass spectrometry techniques show high sensitivity as well as specificity but produce complex results requiring trained staff for accurate interpretations (
Cross and Hornshaw, 2016
). When conducting research on biomarkers, one of the many challenges is lack of reproducibility across studies and laboratories. Preanalytical errors still account for 60–70 % of all problems in relation to laboratory diagnostics (
Lippi et al., 2011
). Chalbot et al. and Constantinescu et al. were the only two studies that reported the timing of CSF sampling, and generally information on CSF sample collection, handling, transport, and storage was lacking or varied between the included studies (
Chalbot et al., 2011
;
Constantinescu et al., 2010
). Confounders in relation to CSF sampling of ubiquitin include the potential influence of diurnal variation and volume of sample fluid aspirated from each subject (
Janssens et al., 2019
). Several studies have suggested the presence of a physiological circadian rhythm of CSF protein levels, implying this could also apply to other biomarkers such as ubiquitin (
Bateman et al., 2007
;
Bjerke et al., 2010
). Further studies should aim for a standardized approach to study design and execution, including pre-analytical as well as analytical variables, producing results suitable for comparison.
In this review, the included neurodegenerative diseases exhibited different patterns of change in CSF ubiquitin: Levels were found to be elevated in AD patients as opposed to reduced amounts in PD and FTD populations. Furthermore, although only one study involved patients with DLB, the increased ubiquitin concentrations in this population stand contrast with the results for the PD group, as the diseases share the same pathological hallmark of misfolded alpha-synuclein inclusions. Regarding the increased levels in AD, a possible explanation may lie in the pathological protein aggregates comprising extracellular plaques of β-amyloid as well as hyperphosphorylated tau inclusions, termed neurofibrillary tangles. When these tangles mature, the neuron eventually dies and leaves behind “ghost” tangles in the interstitial space (
DeTure and Dickson, 2019
;
Dugger and Dickson, 2017
). Hypothetically, these types of ubiquitinated aggregates outside of the cell could constitute a source of CSF-ubiquitin, following ubiquitin-release via e.g., extracellular DUBs, interactions with glia or other mechanisms. The synucleinopathies are characterized by the presence of Lewy bodies and Lewy neurites within neurons and have not been associated with the same burden of extracellular pathology. This, however, mainly pertains to PD, given that the majority of DLB cases present with some degree of Alzheimer-type neuropathology (
Koga et al., 2021
;
Walker et al., 2019
), which might contribute to the elevated levels observed in DLB patients. While inclusions are primarily localized in the brainstem and limbic regions in PD, Lewy body pathology extends to involve the neocortex in DLB (
Koga et al., 2021
;
Walker et al., 2019
). Greater propagation of pathology could potentially result in an increased exposure of ubiquitinated proteins to the extracellular space, and ultimately the CSF, in DLB compared with PD. Furthermore, recent studies have reported heightened levels of NfL in DLB in comparison to PD (
Ashton et al., 2021
;
Gaetani et al., 2019
). The more extensive axonal degeneration in DLB may lead to an increased release of ubiquitin. In addition to DLB, CSF ubiquitin was found to be reduced in a cohort of patients diagnosed with FTD caused by
CHMP2B
mutation. A hallmark protein aggregation has not yet been identified in this genetic subtype of FTD, only accumulations of ubiquitin- and p62-positive intraneuronal inclusions (FTLD-UPS) (
Holm et al., 2009
). The mutation leads to buildup of deformed multivesicular bodies containing ubiquitinated proteins (
Ugbode and West RJH, 2021
), possibly causing additional intracellular entrapment of ubiquitin. Another potential cause of reduced CSF ubiquitin could involve impaired release of extracellular vesicles harboring ubiquitinated proteins, a process theoretically impacted by
CHMP2B
mutation.
As previously mentioned, the sequestration of ubiquitin and UPS constituents by polyubiquitinated protein aggregates, followed by depletion of cellular ubiquitin reservoirs, appears to be common features of neurodegenerative diseases, potentially impairing both UPS and ALP activity. Whether there is a compensatory upregulation of free ubiquitin varies between studies and may depend on the specific disease model investigated (
Farrawell et al., 2020
). A related missing link is the extent to which intracellular ubiquitin levels reflect the quantities that ultimately translocate to the interstitial space. These processes require further elucidation in future studies.
Under normal circumstances the concentration of ubiquitin in blood is three-to fivefold higher than CSF levels (
Majetschak et al., 2011
). For ubiquitin to be used as a CSF biomarker, it is relevant to examine whether elevated levels simply reflect emanation from serum. To this end, four studies included here reported a well-established measure of blood-brain barrier integrity, namely the CSF/serum albumin ratio (
Blennow et al., 1994
;
Chalbot et al., 2011
;
Oeckl et al., 2014
;
Toft et al., 2023
). None of the studies found the ubiquitin levels in patients to correlate with the albumin quotient, indicating that CSF ubiquitin is intrathecally derived, and probably diffuses from the brain and ISF to the CSF through the ependymal cells lining the ventricles.
Increased expression levels of CSF ubiquitin corresponded with disease progression and severity scales in two studies (
Kandimalla et al., 2014
;
Vinther-Jensen et al., 2015
), indicating that CSF ubiquitin might reflect the extent of ongoing neurodegeneration. In patients with prion disease, a class of infectious and neurodegenerative diseases caused by the propagation of pathological scrapie isoform of the prion protein (PRPsc), the disease course is typically characterized by rapidly progressing neurodegeneration and dementia. Several studies have observed substantially increased levels of CSF ubiquitin in CJD patients, discriminating the subjects from both healthy controls as well as patients with AD and other comparable diseases (
Abu-Rumeileh et al., 2020
;
Oeckl et al., 2014
;
Steinacker et al., 2010
). The association with neurodegeneration is supported by correlations of
CSF
ubiquitin with neurofilament light (NfL) and t-tau (
Abu-Rumeileh et al., 2020
;
Oeckl et al., 2014
;
Sjödin et al., 2017
,
2019
), as well as increased
CSF
ubiquitin following traumatic brain injury or brain ischemia (
Kurimura et al., 1997
;
Majetschak et al., 2005
).
Two studies evaluated the performance of CSF ubiquitin in discriminating between AD and FTD patients (
Abu-Rumeileh et al., 2020
;
Oeckl et al., 2014
). The studies reported sensitivities of 53–87 % and specificities of 80–100 %. While the values are not unlike what was recently presented for Aβ42 in a systematic review (
Kokkinou et al., 2021
), the authors reported a lower diagnostic performance of CSF ubiquitin compared with the neurodegenerative markers NfL and t-tau (
Abu-Rumeileh et al., 2020
). Some studies argue that CSF ubiquitin can be considered a specific biomarker for AD, due to correlations with Aβ42, tau, and the APOEε4 allele (
Kandimalla et al., 2014
). However, correlation with AD markers does not point to specificity, but only for association with AD pathology – just as ubiquitin has been shown associated with the protein aggregates in the other diseases included in this review. Nevertheless, other findings do suggest some degree of connection with disease pathomechanisms or affected brain region: Studies that include a variety of neurodegenerative diseases do not observe generally elevated levels of CSF ubiquitin, but only in certain diseases, i.e. AD and CJD (
Abu-Rumeileh et al., 2020
;
Oeckl et al., 2014
); in CJD, the CSF concentration is reported to differ significantly between molecular subtypes of the disease (
Abu-Rumeileh et al., 2020
); and in other diseases with increased NfL or t-tau, such as ALS, FTD and PD, CSF ubiquitin levels have been found unaltered or even significantly decreased, as described.
Collectively, whether CSF ubiquitin levels exclusively reflect neurodegeneration or if association with certain types of neurodegenerative pathomechanisms exists, remains to be fully elucidated. This is relevant in terms of implementation in the clinical setting, where other biomarkers of neurodegeneration such as NfL are already being used, both from serum and CSF samples, to assess the presence or absence of neurodegeneration (
Willemse et al., 2021
). NfL is a sensitive and unspecific marker of neuroaxonal damage and is exclusively present in neurons. Ubiquitin, however, participates in various cellular processes in all eukaryotic cells, and elevated levels may not necessarily relate to neurodegenerative diseases. The concentrations of NfL in plasma closely align with those in CSF (
Blennow et al., 1994
;
Khalil et al., 2018
). In contrast, a recent study assessed the plasma levels of ubiquitin for correlation with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and mild dementia in a cohort of 230 elderly but did not observe any significant differences related to cognitive functioning as measured by MMSE (
McFarlane et al., 2023
). Whereas Single Molecular Array is considered the gold standard for measuring NfL, accurately quantifying ubiquitin is challenging due to the high homology between ubiquitin species, such as free ubiquitin, conjugated monomeric and conjugated polymeric ubiquitin (
Kandimalla et al., 2014
;
Oeckl et al., 2014
;
Steinacker et al., 2010
;
Wang et al., 1991
). This is reflected in the diversity of analytical methods used in the included studies, as well as the observation that the reference range for CSF ubiquitin in healthy controls varies by 10-fold between studies (
Blennow et al., 1994
;
Kandimalla et al., 2011
).
Alternative strategies to exploit the biomarker potential of ubiquitin and UPS function might be required: While using serum ubiquitin as a measure of neurodegenerative disease burden might be challenging (
McFarlane et al., 2023
) – perhaps due to other sources than CNS-derived ubiquitin –markers such as the CSF/serum ubiquitin ratio and the DUB UCHL1 have shown promising results, and may outperform CSF ubiquitin as biomarkers (
Blennow et al., 1994
;
Oeckl et al., 2020
;
Öhrfelt et al., 2016
). Given the ongoing preclinical research directed at therapeutically modulating UPS activity, the ubiquitination state of specific substrates or broader ubiquitination profiles – rather than soluble amounts ubiquitin or UPS components – could potentially serve as future predictive biomarkers, or contribute prognostic information about the disease stage based on the levels of (dys)ubiquitination.
The present review exhibits a number of limitations. Most important is the constraint imposed by the number of exploitable studies that met the pre-defined criteria, resulting in an increased emphasis on CSF ubiquitin in AD. This skewness could be explained by prevalence of AD and the clinical practice of performing lumbar punctures in the diagnostic evaluation of AD in specialized centers. In turn, this practice is encouraged by the availability of CSF biomarkers of amyloid and tau pathology, whereas lumbar puncture for the diagnosis other neurodegenerative diseases is used more sparingly. Hence, CSF available for research investigations would primarily be derived from patients with AD. Furthermore, it is essential to acknowledge the potential influence of publication bias on the results of this review. Studies reporting negative findings are less likely to be published and would therefore not appear in our searches. The heterogeneous analytical and sampling methodologies described in the studies, alongside potential confounding factors such as disease stage, medication usage or co-morbidity, may impede the generalizability of the results as well as the attribution of CSF ubiquitin changes to the disease alone. The studies included in this review are all cross-sectional in design and thus, the correlations between CSF ubiquitin and other clinical or biochemical measures such as CSF Aβ42, p-tau or t-tau may yield valuable insights into the disease mechanisms, but cannot establish causal relationships. Lastly, in order for correlations between biomarkers to be comparable between studies, it is necessary to make calculations on individual disease groups, and not combine several groups or include all participants, which was often the case in the reviewed studies.
To facilitate the clinical utilization of CSF ubiquitin, or any other CSF biomarker, several practical aspects must be considered: CSF collection is an invasive procedure typically performed in specialized clinics, and the sampling and storage of CSF must be carefully standardized (
Campo et al., 2012
;
Simonsen et al., 2013
). Additionally, it is essential to harmonize the interpretation of results obtained from different analytical modalities to ensure their clinical applicability. Neurodegenerative diseases characterized by “late onset” are particularly complex and multifactorial. Patients with such conditions often present with a multitude of comorbidities and concurrently prescribed medications. These patient-specific variables warrant in-depth investigation to ascertain whether alterations in CSF ubiquitin levels genuinely reflect the underlying neurodegenerative disease.
Research into CSF-ubiquitin aligns with the broader objective of comprehending the biological underpinnings of neurodegenerative diseases, particularly with trends focusing on shared driving mechanisms, rather than disease-specific protein aggregation – the importance of which is emphasized by the high prevalence of patients with multiple concurrent proteinopathies (
Karanth et al., 2020
). Despite the extensive research on ubiquitin over decades, the available evidence supporting the utility of
CSF
ubiquitin as a biomarker in neurodegenerative diseases is sparse. Given its direct involvement in protein degradation and its dysregulation, the marker may offer mechanistic insights, perhaps in combination with other biomarkers. This requires that the differing levels in PD, FTD and ALS can be verified. If CSF ubiquitin levels solely represent a surrogate measure of neurodegeneration, it is difficult to imagine a diagnostic value of the biomarker and an unmet need for it in the clinical practice. Other already well-established biomarkers are more easily measured and show greater fold changes between patients and controls.
5
Conclusion
Ubiquitination is common in neurotoxic protein aggregates, and CSF ubiquitin levels has been evaluated as a biomarker in several neurodegenerative diseases. Studies vary in methodology due to the lack of standard measurement. In AD studies, elevated CSF ubiquitin is near-consistent. So is correlation with tau, while Aβ42 results lacked reproducibility. In the few studies available, HD and DLB patients showed increased CSF ubiquitin, while levels in ALS, FTD and PD have been similar to or below those of the controls. If the latter is validated, CSF ubiquitin may contribute more disease-specific information than all-cause neurodegeneration, which would increase the potential for reaching the clinical setting. This is otherwise unlikely, as more readily applicable neurodegenerative biomarkers have been implemented.
