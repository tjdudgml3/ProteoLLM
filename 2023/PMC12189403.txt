1.
Introduction
Single cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data is a powerful tool to map cellular diversity across developmental time and space. Among the types of cellular diversity that can be revealed by this approach are those which reflect the expression of genes that control cell function or “activity”. These transcriptional signatures of cell function have been referred to as “Activity Programs” (APs), and are distinct from “identity” programs of cells, which are often defined by expression of transcription factors that govern cell fate (
Kotliar et al., 2019
). As such, a group of cells defined by an AP can be a superset of cells across cell identities, or functionally differentiated subpopulations within a single identity. Defining APs has potential to shed light on the cell diversity of the embryo, and can reveal functions that are carried out in a cooperative manner by cells of seemingly different identities.
Membrane proteins account for a quarter of eukaryotic genomes and are central regulators of cell activity. Among them are small molecule transporters (SMTs;
Almén et al., 2009
) which regulate the movement of diverse bioactive small molecules across membranes (
Alexander et al., 2019
;
Almén et al., 2009
;
Dean et al., 2001
;
Hediger et al., 2013
). SMTs are comprised of the ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters and solute carrier (SLC) transporters, which encode numerous genes well studied for their roles in human disease and drug disposition (
Gottesman and Ambudkar, 2001
;
Lin et al., 2015
;
Nigam, 2015
). The ABC superfamily consists of eight families which make up between 50 and 80 proteins in deuterostomes (
Almén et al., 2009
;
Vasiliou et al., 2009
). SLCs consist of around 60 families with 350 subfamily members (
Alexander et al., 2019
;
Almén et al., 2009
;
Lin et al., 2015
). These collectively control the movement of diverse ions, metabolites, signaling molecules and xenobiotics across cell surfaces.
The transport of bioactive small molecules and metabolites is essential for numerous facets of growth and development (
Firestone and Chen, 2010
;
Onjiko et al., 2015
,
2016
;
Shipp et al., 2015
;
Turing, 1990
). In
Drosophila melanogaster
, the
ABCB
transporter
MDR49
regulates the movement of cholesterol for the Hedgehog signaling pathway, which is required for proper migration of primordial germ cells (
Deshpande et al., 2016
). In mice,
SLC13A5
regulates the transport of citrate in osteoblasts, odontoblasts, and ameloblasts and is required for the development of the skeleton with normal bone density (
Irizarry et al., 2017
) and the proper formation of the enamel matrix (
Dirckx et al., 2022
;
Irizarry et al., 2017
). In
Strongylocentrotus purpuratus
(purple sea urchin),
ABCC5
(
MRP5
) is required for proper pigment cell function and hindgut invagination, presumably through control of cAMP movement (
Shipp et al., 2015
). In
Lytechinus variegatus
(green sea urchin), the ectodermally-expressed sulfate transporter
SLC26A2/7
causes the ventral accumulation of sulfated proteoglycans, which skeletogenic primary mesenchyme cells use as a spatial cue for ventral positioning (
Piacentino et al., 2016
). Similarly, plants utilize transporters to control phytohormones critical to development (
Cho and Cho, 2013
). In
Arabidopsis thaliana
,
ABCB1
(P-glycoprotein,
MDR1
) takes part in creating a directional gradient of auxin at root and shoot apices to control direction of growth (
Geisler et al., 2005
) while
ABCB4
regulates the transport of auxin for the control of root hair elongation (
Cho et al., 2007
).
ABCB21
regulates auxin distribution in cotyledons for the control of phototropic bending upon emergence from the seed (
Jenness et al., 2019
).
A systems level understanding of SMT distribution holds promise for revealing functional diversity of cells, established by unique membrane selectivity profiles. Here, we use scRNA-seq data and
in situ
hybridization chain reaction (HCR) to map SMT APs across cell types in the sea urchin embryo. We describe SMTs associated with diverse biological functions including nutrient uptake, skeletogenesis, cellular protection and neurotransmission. The results add to growing evidence indicating that there are additional layers of diversity within the primary and secondary mesenchyme cell lineages.
2.
Results
2.1.
Clustering of S. purpuratus late gastrula (48 hpf) scRNA-seq data
In order to map SMTs within the embryo we first sought to define some of the major cell types present, based on expression of previously characterized “markers”. To accomplish this goal, we used cluster analysis of the scRNA data of
S. purpuratus
embryos at the gastrula stage. This analysis produced 23 clusters (
Fig. S1A
), and cell types comprising these clusters (
Fig. S1B
) were called according to the expression of known markers (
Foster et al., 2020
). These markers are tabulated in the
supplement
(
Table S2
). In addition to some of the prior PMC markers, additional markers of skeletogenic biology (i.e. genes encoding for proteins found in skeleton, or known to be associated with key processes in skeletogenesis, such as PMC fusion) were used in this study including
MMP18
,
matrilysin
,
cyclophilin-1 (cyp1)
,
COLP2alpha
,
COLP3alpha
, and
Kirrel2L_4
(
Amore and Davidson, 2006
;
Livingston et al., 2006
;
Lethias et al., 1997
, 2006;
Mann et al., 2010
;
Ettensohn and Dey, 2017
;
Wessel et al., 1991
).
2.2.
Characterization SMT expression patterns across cell identities in late gastrulae
To characterize the expression of SMTs, we focused our analysis on genes classified under “Transporters” (ID: 02000) in the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), which contained a total of 869 transcripts (
Kanehisa, 2019
;
Kanehisa et al., 2023
;
Kanehisa and Goto, 2000
). Within “Transporters”, we filtered for transcripts in “ABC Transporters, eukaryotic type” and “Solute carrier family (SLC)”. The combined list of transcripts from these two categories was further manually curated, to the best of our ability, to transcripts for genes involved in transport. This curation resulted in a list of 552 SMT genes, upon which all subsequent analysis of the expression patterns of SMTs are based. Subsequent visualization and discussion of these results focuses on “highly expressed” SMT genes, which we determined using the Seurat default parameters to select genes expressed by greater than 20% of cells within a cluster and having average log
2
FC values greater than 0.5.
The analysis revealed the expression patterns of 55 SMT transcripts (
Fig. 1A
). These SMTs included transporters of diverse small molecules including amino acids, anions, carboxylates, ions, lipids, metals, nucleosides, nucleotides, organic cations, signaling molecules, sugars, vitamins, and xenobiotics (
Fig. 1B
). Many of the highly expressed SMTs in urchins are homologs of those with roles in human genetic disease or functions in human drug metabolism (
Table S3
). These included several genes which were further investigated in this study including,
SLC18A1/2
(schizophrenia),
SLC6A5
(hyperekplexia), and
SLC35F3
and
SLC52A3
(vitamin deficiency).
Of the 55, nine were ABC transporters and 46 were SLCs. Some of the ABC transcripts included homologs of genes well studied in human disease, including lipid transporter
ABCA1
which is implicated in the progression of Alzheimer’s, and the xenobiotic transporters
ABCB1
and
ABCG2
which are major players in drug disposition. Notable drug metabolizing SLCs included the glycine/amino acid/neurotransmitter transporter
SLC6A5
, organic cation transporter
SLC22A4
, and organic anion transporter
SLCO4A1
. Those known for their functions in human neural function included glutamate transporter
SLC1A2
, taurine transporter
SLC6A6
(also annotated as “
Glyt6
”), and vesicular monoamine transporter
SLC18A1/2
.

To validate the

,

2.3.
Characterization of a pigment cell cluster associated SMT expression pattern
From these initial clustering and

Figs. 1A
and

SLC7A11

SLC35A5

SLC5A9

2.4.
Identification of SMT “activity pattern” clusters across cell types
Clustering in single cell sequencing experiments typically relies on the grouping of cells based on their transcriptional similarity (
Jovic et al., 2022
). However, it has been suggested that for genes which control cell function, multiple patterns of expression termed “Activity Patterns” (AP), can exist within or across the traditional cell transcriptional identities (
Kotliar et al., 2019
). These patterns can be obscured in traditional cluster approaches by binning of cells of divergent transport types within a single cell morphological identity or splitting of cells of common transport function across cell types.
In order to clarify and refine the picture of how SMTs are distributed, we performed a cluster analysis focusing solely on SMT gene expression. This produced 19 clusters (
Fig. 3A
) from 74 SMTs (
Fig. 3B
). As expected from cell type clustering, pigment cell SMT transcripts were enriched in AP cluster 8 (
Fig. 3B
). The list of SMT transcripts enriched in SMT AP cluster 8 included all the SMTs enriched in pigment cell type clusters found previously, except for
SLC7A11
and gaining an additional sugar transporter
SLC45A3
.
From these data we focused on clusters 15 and 16. The major transporters in these clusters included
SLC5A11
,
SLC22A4
, and
ABCB1
in cluster 15, and
SLC9A3
,
SLC13A2/3/5
, and
SLC39A13
in cluster 16. We observed that these corresponded to the patterns of transporters associated with primary and secondary mesenchyme cells previously observed in
Fig. 1A
. To validate this association, we mapped the barcodes of their cells to the barcodes of cells in cell identity type clusters. We used SMT AP cluster 8 as a control for this analysis, since it was predicted to be comprised mostly of pigment cells based on transporter expression, and indeed 92.3% of the cells in cluster 8 (
Fig. 4A
) represented cells from pigment identity cell clusters (394/427 cells). Clusters 15 and 16 comprised 82.7% (153/185 cells) of all cells from the two primary/secondary mesenchyme cell type clusters (
Figs. 1A
and
4B
). Cells from SMT AP cluster 15 composed 72.3% of the cells in primary/secondary mesenchyme 1 (96/132 cells), while those from cluster 16 composed all the cells in primary/secondary mesenchyme 2, with three additional cells.
This clustering of APs with a cell identity stood in contrast to both aboral/dorsal and oral/ventral ectoderm, which were composed of cells belonging to a diverse range of SMT APs (
Fig. 4C
and
D
). For example, SMT AP clusters whose cells composed at least 15% of the cells of the aboral ectoderm/neural cell type primarily included SMT AP clusters 0, 1, and 4 (
Fig. 4C
) while for the oral ectoderm cell type, these primarily included SMT AP clusters 2, 5, and 6 (
Fig. 4D
). These results suggested that the SMT APs observed in pigment cells and primary secondary mesenchyme might contribute to some specialized transport function of these cells.
2.5.
Characterization of SMT APs in primary and secondary mesenchyme cells
Our transporter-based clustering revealed three unique activity patterns in mesodermal cells. The mesodermal lineage in sea urchins is comprised of two populations, the primary mesenchyme cells (PMCs), which produce the larval skeleton, and the secondary mesenchyme cells (SMCs) which give rise to several tissues including pigment cells, muscle, coelomic sacs, and blastocoelar cells (BCs), all of which are presumed non-skeletogenic (
Ettensohn and Ruffins, 1993
;
Tamboline and Burke, 1992
). Given these fates, we examined their differential expression patterns of SMTs, PMC markers, SMC markers, and other highly expressed transcripts (
Fig. 5
). SMT AP cluster 15 expressed genes which are definitive markers of the PMCs, including
SM50, msp130, Alx1,
and
GataC,
and thus was called a “putative PMC” cluster (
Fig. 5
, green x-axis label).
In contrast, SMT AP cluster 16 exhibited more unusual patterns of marker gene expression. On the one hand, these cells express
Prox1
(
Figs. 5
and
6
), which is expressed in secondary mesenchyme cells (
Materna et al., 2013
;
Poustka et al., 2007
;
Slota et al., 2019
) but not PMCs. On the other hand, these cells also expressed several genes that are associated with PMCs. These included
cyclophilin-1
, a gene regulated by the
ETS-dri
gene battery during skeletogenesis (
Amore and Davidson, 2006
;
Livingston et al., 2006
;
Materna and Davidson, 2012
), and which was expressed by 96.49% of the cells. Another example was
Kirrel2L_4
, a member of the Kirrel family of adhesion proteins required for the fusion of PMC filopodia (
Ettensohn and Dey, 2017
). Additional skeletogenic genes included
hex
(
Howard-Ashby et al., 2006
) and fibrillar collagen
COLP2alpha
(
Lethias et al., 1997
). These cells also expressed matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs)
MMP18
and
matrilysin
, which have been identified in proteomics of purified spicules (
Mann et al., 2010
). The clusters were supported by HCR which revealed that both genes colocalize with
Prox1
in 24 and 48 hpf embryos (
Fig. 6
), but not with a PMC marker
SM50
. Based on these observations, cluster 16 was subsequently called a “skeletogenic SMC” cluster.
To define the skeletogenic SMCs, we performed HCR for
SM50
,
gcm
, and the fibrillar collagens
col1a(V)chain
with
COLP2alpha,
both of which are enriched in SMCs/cluster 16 (
Fig. 7A
). We also localized
Kirrel2L_4,
which was enriched in this SMC cluster and is closely related to a PMC fusion genes KirrelL and Kirrel2L (
Ettensohn and Dey, 2017
;
Fig. S2A
).
Col1a(V)chain
and
COLP2alpha
co-localized in SMCs and were not co-expressed with either
SM50
or
gcm
(
Fig. 7A
). Similarly,
col1a(V) chain
and
Kirrel2L_4
co-localized in SMCs and were not co-expressed with either
SM50
or
gcm
(
Fig. S2A
). The results indicated that the
col1a(V)chain
and
COLP2alpha
expressing cells are likely from the oral NSM lineage and are distinct from
SM50
positive PMCs and pigment cells. Given that the cell population is also
Prox1
positive and thus SMCs, we hypothesized the cells in cluster 16 most likely include blastocoelar cells.
Next, we sought to determine the localization of these PMCs and skeletogenic SMCs/BCs in relation to one another. The results suggested that cells belonging to SMT AP 15 and 16 were comprised of two separate populations of cells (
Fig. 7
). Consistent with the AP clustering, at both 48 and 72 hpf
msp130
and
angiopoietin-1
localized to PMCs and were spatially segregated from cells expressing markers of SMCs such as
thrombospondin
and
MMP18
. By 72 hpf most of the
MMP18
+
cells were arranged along the location of the spicule rods, alternating with
msp130
+
/
angiopoietin-1
+
cells (
Fig. 7B
).
2.6.
PMCs and BCs expressing SMT AP 15 and 16 are intimately associated with one another
We examined the pattern of expression of non-fibrillar and fibrillar collagens, previously shown to be involved in skeletogenic and differentially expressed among these two populations (
Lethias et al., 1997
;
Wessel et al., 1991
). We probed for
COLP3alpha
,
col1a(V)chain
, and
calumenin
(
Rafiq et al., 2012
), a marker of both PMCs and BCs (
Fig. 8
). At 48 hpf, the spatial localization of
COLP3alpha
was primarily enriched in PMCs,
col1a(V)chain
was expressed in what initially appears to be BCs, and
calumenin
colocalizes with proportions of both cell populations.
COLP3alpha
was also present in low levels in SMCs, specifically in the coelomic pouches. At 72 hpf,
COLP3alpha
was exclusively expressed in PMCs,
col1a(V)chain
was expressed in elongated cells along the expected locations of the spicule rods (
Fig. 8
).
2.7.
Localization of SLCs in PMCs and BCs
The basis for the finding of these segregated skeletogenic populations was their differentiated expression of two profiles of SMTs and additional validation by SMT AP clustering. The putative PMC cluster is enriched for the expression of
SLC5A11
,
SLC22A4
, and a half transporter
SLC26A5
. The blastocoelar cell cluster is enriched for the expression of
SLC9A3
,
SLC13A2/3/5
, and
SLC39A13
. The PMC-specific expression of
SLC5A11
and
SLC26A5
has been previously characterized (
Khor et al., 2019
;
Rafiq et al., 2014
). Here, we characterized the spatial expression pattern of citrate transporter
SLC13A2/3/5
, using
SLC26A5
and
calumenin
as positive controls (
Fig. 9
). In 48 hpf embryos,
SLC13A2/3/5
is expressed in cells adjacent to PMCs expressing
SLC26A5
and colocalizes with
calumenin
. In 72 hpf embryos,
SLC13A2/3/5
expression is sparse but is present in a few cells contacting PMCs at arm buds and at the tip of the growing spicule.
3.
Discussion
Though relatively little is known about the systems level workings of transporters in early development of any animal, a picture of their diversity and importance is emerging from studies in the sea urchin. Some SMTs already described in sea urchin embryos include bicarbonate transporter
SLC4A10
(
Hu et al., 2018
), myo-inositol transporter
SLC5A11
(
Rafiq et al., 2014
), amino acid transporter
SLC6A5
(
Meyer and Manahan, 2009
), the sodium hydrogen exchanger
SLC9C1
(
Windler et al., 2018
), the sulfate transporter
SLC26A7
(
Piacentino et al., 2016
), the xenobiotic transporters
ABCB1
(
Fleming et al., 2021
;
Vyas et al., 2022
) and
ABCC1
(
Gökirmak et al., 2016
), putative cyclic nucleotide transporters
ABCC4
(
Schrankel and Hamdoun, 2021
) and
ABCC5
(
Shipp et al., 2015
), and the lipid transporter
ABCG11
(
Perillo et al., 2020
). However, as this study highlights, these represent only the tip of the iceberg, and this study significantly extends that knowledge.
3.1.
Expression patterns of neurotransmitter and nutrient uptake SLCs
One notable expression pattern in this study was that of
SLC18A1/2
, which is a monoamine uptake transporter typically expressed in neuroendocrine cells (
Eiden et al., 2004
). We found a ring of cells expressing
SLC18A1/2,
suggestive of the pattern of cells in the sensory apical organ of sea urchin late gastrula (
Fig. 2A
). Consistent with this localization,
SLC18A1/2
in other organisms is typically responsible for the vesicular transport of serotonin between the cytosol and synaptic vesicles but it also transports monoamines such as norepinephrine, epinephrine, and dopamine (
Eiden et al., 2004
). In humans,
SLC18A1/
2 has been studied as a proton-serotonin exchanger (
Erickson et al., 1996
;
Essand et al., 2005
;
Lohoff et al., 2014
). Variants of human
SLC18A1/2
have shown to be responsible for different transport rates of monoamines
in vitro
(
Lohoff et al., 2014
). The expression pattern of
SLC18A1/2
we observed is strikingly similar to expression patterns of tryptophan 5-hydroxlyase (
TPH
), an enzyme required for the synthesis of serotonin, in the serotonergic neurons of late gastrulae of
Hemicentrotus pulcherrimus
(
Yaguchi and Katow, 2003
) and several genes encoding neuropeptides (
Wood et al., 2018
). This is intriguing because although sea urchins are thought to lack synapses (Burke, 2006), the expression of this gene would suggest that they retain a serotonin uptake mechanism.
Another interesting pattern of transporter expression was observed for
SLC5A9, SLC7A11, SLC35F3
, and
SLC52A3
, which are involved in absorption of essential nutrients in mammals (
Alexander et al., 2019
). In the sea urchin embryo, the expression of these transporters became enriched in the gut of the feeding larvae (
Fig. 2B
).
SLC35F3
and
SLC52A3
were enriched in the midgut while the expression of
SLC5A9
was in the foregut, hindgut, and pigment cells (
Fig. 2B
). In addition, our
in silico
and HCR results indicated several of these gut-associated SMTs were also in pigment cells (
Fig. 2A
), particularly at earlier stages. These included
SLC5A9
,
SLC35F3
, and
SLC52A3.
Prior studies have demonstrated intimate interactions between pigment cells and the gut (
Buckley et al., 2017
;
Fleming et al., 2021
;
Ho et al., 2017
). Others have shown that clusters representing compartments of the gut co-expressed both endoderm and pigment cell markers (
Paganos et al., 2021
;
Perillo et al., 2020
). It is conceivable that these transporters simply are expressed in the pigment cells early on and later shift to being expressed in the endoderm. However, an intriguing alternative possibility is that some pigment cells become embedded in the gut in later stages and play a role in transport functions. Indeed, recent scRNA-seq analysis of
S. purpuratus
datasets for later developmental stages have identified a subpopulation of cells co-expressing both hindgut and pigment cell markers (
Paganos et al., 2022
;
Perillo et al., 2020
). Furthermore, pigment cells have been found as two distinct subpopulations where one subpopulation is enriched in mitotic markers, suggesting a multipotent stem state (
Perillo et al., 2020
). It is possible that the cells from at least one of these lineages are enriched in a specific set of SMTs and are embedded in specific gut compartments.
3.2.
Activity pattern clustering suggests skeletogenic functions of secondary mesenchyme cells
A unique facet of this study was the clustering of cells based on their functional states, rather than on the basis of transcriptional regulator genes which might reflect their developmental trajectory. We hypothesized that because the molecular selectivity of the cell surface is so crucial for specialization of cell function, that clustering on the basis of SMTs might reveal previously “cryptic” phenotypes. The primary example of this was in the populations of mesodermal cells that were distinguished by expression of unique APs. One of these was the PMC cell lineage which has been well-studied in the sea urchin and is already established as a definitive skeletogenic subtype. However, the other population was from the secondary mesenchyme cell lineage, most likely the BCs, based on their expression of
Prox1
(
Fig. 6
).
The transporters in the PMCs include
SLC5A11
,
SLC22A4
, and a half transporter
SLC26A5
. The SMC/BC cluster is enriched for the expression of
SLC9A3
,
SLC13A2/3/5
, and
SLC39A13
. These transporters can take up blastocoelar molecules that could contribute to skeletogenesis. For example,
SLC13
is known to play an important role in citrate transport and bone morphogenesis in mammals, where 70% of whole body citrate content is in bone tissue (
Dickens, 1941
). Inhibition and knockout of
SLC13A5
results in significantly reduced bone mass and defective enamel and dentin formation (
Dirckx et al., 2022
). This would add to prior evidence that indicates that transporters expressed are involved in patterned movement of metabolites and signal molecules necessary for skeletogenesis. For example,
SLC26A2/7
is an ectodermally expressed transporter that controls the accumulation of sulfated proteoglycans necessary for ventral positioning of PMCs. Knockdown of this transporter resulted in significant spicule malformation (
Piacentino et al., 2016
).
When we examined other genes expressed at high levels in our AP clusters, we observed that a defining feature of both the PMC and SMC populations were the expression of skeletogenic genes. This was expected in the PMCs, which are definitive skeletogenic cells. However, these results were somewhat unexpected for the SMCs, which are not thought to form skeleton under normal conditions. This said, it has been previously demonstrated that some SMCs, specifically the BCs, are capable of “transfating” into skeletogenic cells if the PMCs are removed (
Ettensohn, 1990
;
Ettensohn and Ruffins, 1993
;
Tamboline and Burke, 1992
) and it is conceivable they play some skeletogenic role even under normal conditions.
Our results suggest that these “skeletogenic BCs”, which may be a subset of all BCs based on their observed numbers, express skeletogenic genes at relatively robust levels even under normal conditions. These include
Kirrel2L_4
, a member of the
Kirrel
family of adhesion proteins required for the fusion of PMCs (
Ettensohn and Dey, 2017
), as well as
MMP18
and
matrilysin
, which are the two most abundant MMPs found in purified spicules (
Mann et al., 2010
; in 85.96% and 91.22% of these cells, respectively;
Fig. 5
). Other potentially skeletogenic genes enriched in this cluster included
hex
(
Howard-Ashby et al., 2006
) and fibrillar collagen
COLP2alpha
(
Lethias et al., 1997
). Additionally, our
in situ
HCR results with
MMP18
and
angiopoietin-1
(
Fig. 7B
) revealed that this subset of BCs was positioned along the length of the PMC chain making up the skeletal rods.
An alternative possibility that we considered was that our AP cluster of skeletogenic SMCs actually comprised some mix of SMCs and a “cryptic” PMC population. However, several lines of evidence argue against this idea. One was the strong colocalization of
Prox1
(an SMC marker) with cells expressing
MMP18
and
matrilysin
(
Fig. 6
), both of which were highly enriched in the AP cluster 16. Consistent with this, neither of these genes colocalized with
SM50
or
msp130
cells, nor did
col1a(V)chain
, a fibrillar collagen marker unique to the AP 16 SMC cluster (
Fig. 7A
). We also observed exclusive segregation between cells containing
COLP3alpha
, a non-fibrillar collagen associated with the PMC cluster, and the fibrillar collagen
col1a(V)chain
(
Fig. 8
). The same segregation of cells in clusters 15 (PMC) and 16 (SMC) were observed in HCRs of
zinc metalloprotease 13
(
zinc MMP-nas 13
) and
SM50
(
Fig. S2B
). This could align with observations from previous studies indicating that SMCs, particularly BCs, are distributed along the skeletal rods (
Tamboline and Burke, 1992
). More definitive future evidence could come from fate mapping in lines of urchins where these cells have been marked, using the genetically-enabled sea urchin,
Lytechinus pictus
(
Vyas et al., 2022
).
Whatever the definitive identity of these cells, the two skeletogenic populations discussed in this study appear to be differentiated by their abilities to produce materials whose separated production and layering could produce the concentric pattern of mineral layers with embedded matrix fibers (
Mann et al., 2010
). PMCs are enriched for matrix proteins such as
SM50
and basement membrane collagens
COLP3alpha
and
COLP4alpha
(
Fig. 5
). Skeletogenic BCs are enriched for several types of fibrillar collagens such as
COLP1alpha
,
COLP2alpha
, and
col1a(V)chain
(
Fig. 5
). An alternating layering pattern of matrix substrate materials by PMCs and collagen fibrils by skeletogenic BCs would allow for the formation of discrete mineralized cylindrical structures containing matrix fibers (
Mann et al., 2010
). These matrix fibers could be the collagen fibrils produced by skeletogenic BCs.
Collagen fibrils are generally water insoluble under physiological conditions (
de Wild et al., 2013
;
Kadler et al., 2008
) and are critical for mineralization (
Costello et al., 2014
;
Li and Aparicio, 2013
;
Oosterlaken et al., 2021
). Aside from the mineralized structures themselves, these fibers visually appear to be the next most abundant water insoluble material that remains after purifying spicules (
Mann et al., 2010
). Furthermore, the skeletogenic BCs which exclusively express the genes for these fibrillar collagens also exclusively express the citrate transporter
SLC13A2
. Greater than 80% of the total mass of citrate present in the human body is contained within bone tissue (
Dickens, 1941
). It is known that citrate contributes to different aspects of biomineralization including initiation of inter- and intra-fibrillar collagen mineralization (
Jiang et al., 2020
;
Oosterlaken et al., 2021
;
Shao et al., 2018
) and stabilization of the hydroxyapatite nanocrystals which comprise mineralized collagen (
Costello et al., 2014
;
Delgado-López et al., 2017
).
3.3.
Conclusion
Much of our knowledge of sea urchin development comes from our understanding of the genes and proteins that build the embryo from the genome outward. A frontier of knowledge addressed in this study is the cell membrane, where cell-selective expression of a diverse array of proteins is necessary for specifying the cell’s capacity for uptake of nutrients, reception of signals, and protection from foreign molecules. Transporters represent approximately one-quarter of the membrane proteome of most eukaryotes and, owing to their relevance to human disease and drug disposition, provide a well-studied platform for understanding how certain cell types in the embryo are functionally differentiated from one another at the cell surface. In turn, this sheds light on how cells within the embryo may interact, through sharing of bioactive small molecules, to drive animal development.

Computing SMT AP clusters
To find SMT AP clusters, cells were clustered based on the expression data for 552 SMT transcripts. Functions used to find SMT AP clusters were identical to those used for calling cell types (
Table S1
). Subsequently, cell barcodes from each SMT AP cluster were matched to those from cell type clusters. These matching cell barcodes were counted, and counts were subsequently plotted (

).
4.4.
Confocal microscopy and in situ hybridization chain reaction (HCR)
HCR was performed using the protocol from (
Choi et al., 2018
). Probes were designed with insitu_probe_generator-v0.3.2 (
Null, 2021
) using only the CDS for each target. Probe sets were designed as pools containing probes for three to five transcripts. Probes were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT; Coralville, Iowa, USA), and amplification hairpins were purchased from Molecular Instruments (El Monte, California, USA). Target sequences, probe-hairpin sequence pools, and all metadata used throughout the probe generation process are available in the
supplement
. All files related to each of the aforementioned pieces of information are organized by the NCBI accession for each target. All samples were processed and imaged in a Cellvis glass bottom 384-well plate (Mountain View, California, USA).
Sample fixation and HCR processing steps are outlined in the Molecular Instruments RNA-FISH protocol for sea urchin embryos. Embryos used in this study were obtained by fertilizing eggs from a mixed batch of 5
S. purpuratus
females. After fixation and storage, these embryos were plated in a Cellvis glass bottom 384-well plate, where each well contained between 500 and 1000 embryos. Probes were prepared in 384-well plates, where each well contains a pool of five probe sets, where each probe set is specific to one of five hairpin amplifier sequences B1-B5 (
Choi et al., 2018
). This plate of probes was mapped and transferred to the plate of embryos for hybridization. Subsequent steps, including washing and amplification, were performed similarly.
Confocal microscopy was performed on a Molecular Devices ImageXpress HT.ai (San Jose, California, USA) using a 20X Nikon Plan Apochromatic objective. Laser channels used in this study include 750, 647, 550, 488, and 405 nm, along with the appropriate emission filters. Image post processing, which included adjusting brightness and contrast, scaling, and cropping of single embryos, was performed using ImageJ and custom macros. All images are 2D-projections of the appropriate ranges of stacks.
A total of three technical replicate “batches” were performed per developmental stage and timepoint. Each replicate consisted of a mixed batch of embryos from 5 females and 5 males. The fixed embryos from each batch were imaged on a single imaging plate, with 5 wells for each probe set. Negative controls for non-specific signal deriving from reagent chemistry include embryos which have been processed without probes (
Fig. S4
).
