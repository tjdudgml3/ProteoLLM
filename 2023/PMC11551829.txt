INTRODUCTION
Clinical presentation of systemic amyloidosis differs among subtypes, and an accurate subtype classification is important for the selection of type‐specific therapeutics.
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
In the 1990s, amyloid A (AA) was the most common subtype of systemic amyloidosis among the immunohistochemically classified autopsy cases.
5
,
6
In the 2010s, a systematic investigation of biopsied cases revealed that amyloid immunoglobulin light chain (AL) was the most common subtype of systemic amyloidosis.
7
,
8
In 2018, the Group for Surveys and Research of Amyloidosis in Japan (GSRA‐J) started a nation‐wide pathology consultation of amyloidosis,
1
and amyloid transthyretin (ATTR) was found to be the most common subtype of systemic amyloidosis in Japan.
9
Tateishi et al.
10
reviewed autopsy cases in a single Japanese institution (Kyushu University) from 1990 to 2020 and found that ATTR is the most common subtype of cardiac amyloidosis, followed by AA.
The Annual of the Pathological Autopsy Cases in Japan (APAC‐J) edited by the Japanese Society of Pathology and published since 1960 (
https://pathology.or.jp/kankoubutu/autopsy-index.html
) lists all cases autopsied during the year at the hospitals registered in the Japanese Society of Pathology. APAC‐J lists autopsy cases of systemic amyloidosis in Japan during each 1‐year period. GSRA‐J selected 249 autopsy cases of systemic amyloidosis listed in APAC‐J, Volume 55 (published in 2012) and preliminarily analyzed the subtype frequency by a questionnaire survey.
11
The results showed that AL was the most common subtype (33.3%), followed by AA (16.5%), and ATTR (8.8%). Surprisingly, the subtype could not be determined in 38.2% of the cases. This survey revealed that in Japan, the subtypes are not correctly diagnosed in many cases of systemic amyloidosis, mainly because some laboratories are unable to diagnose the subtypes of systemic amyloidosis immunohistochemically. This result urged us to establish a nation‐wide pathology consultation system of amyloidosis.
1
In this study, we analyzed the systemic amyloidosis cases autopsied from January 1, 2017, to December 31, 2018, and listed in APAC‐J, Volumes 60 and 61. When the subtype was not clear, we performed a questionnaire survey, immunohistochemistry, and proteomic analysis to classify the subtype. We also determined the subtype frequency of systemic amyloidosis cases, in which systemic amyloidosis was the main cause of death. The dramatic change in the subtype frequency among autopsy cases during the last three decades is discussed.
METHODS
Study flow
In APAC‐J, the number and name of each registered institution is described at the top of each page, followed by a table of each case, which describes the case number, age/sex, clinical diagnosis, main clinical department, pathological diagnosis, and treatment of each patient (Table
1
). The main cause of death was identified by autopsy and indicated in bold letters (Table
1
). These data are available in print and as PDF files attached to the Annuals. The APAC‐J, Volumes 60 and 61 (published in 2019 and 2020, respectively) listed 21 412 autopsy cases from January 1, 2017 to December 31, 2018. First, we converted the PDF files of APAC‐J into Excel files and selected systemic amyloidosis cases by a keyword search (Figure
1
, Table
1
). Next, we classified the systemic amyloidosis cases based on the criteria for the subtype classification (Table
2
). When we needed additional minor information (e.g., κ or λ in AL cases; organ distribution in suspected ATTRwt cases), we sent a questionnaire survey to the corresponding institutions. When we could not classify the subtype based on the criteria or after the questionnaire survey, we asked the corresponding institutions to send us ten pieces of 4‐μm‐thick sections from formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded tissue of representative organs for Congo‐red staining and immunohistochemical analysis. When we could not classify the subtype after immunohistochemical analysis, we asked the corresponding institutions to send us block specimens for proteomic analysis. After subtype classification, we took photographs of each case and returned the results and specimens to each institution.

Proteome analysis
We performed protein extraction and proteome analysis using liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (LC‐MS/MS) as previously described.
14
,
15
Briefly, laser capture microdissection (LMD7000; Leica Microsystems) was performed using the bright‐field setting to confirm the presence of amyloid deposits, which were placed into microcentrifuge tubes containing 10 mM Tris, 1 mM EDTA, and 0.002% Zwittergent 3–16. Collected tissue samples were heated at 98°C for 60 min. Samples were sonicated for 90 min and digested with 1.5 μL of 1 mg/mL trypsin (Promega) overnight at 37°C. Digested peptide mixtures were reduced with dithiothreitol at 95°C for 5 min and dried. Redissolved peptides in 40 μl of solution containing 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid and 2% acetonitrile, were analyzed using nano‐flow reversed‐phase LC‐MS/MS (LTQ Velos Pro; Thermo Fisher Scientific). We obtained the relative abundance of the identified molecules using the normalized spectral abundance factor.
16

DISCUSSION
In this study, the subtypes of 399 cases of systemic amyloidosis listed in APAC‐J were classified using a questionnaire survey, immunohistochemistry, and proteomic analysis. The subtype frequency of systemic amyloidosis cases, in which systemic amyloidosis was the main cause of death was also determined. ATTR was the most predominant subtype among the current autopsy cases in Japan (Figure
3
). As compared to the data in 1990's, the frequency of ATTR amyloidosis increased drastically (from 4.1%,
5
24.4%
6
to 44.4% in Figure
3
). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of a multicenter study on the nation‐wide subtype frequency of systemic amyloidosis cases autopsied in the 21st century.
In the past decade, various new drugs were developed to treat each subtype of systemic amyloidosis,
1
,
3
and accurate subtype classification became essential for the selection of type‐specific therapeutics.
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
The nation‐wide pathology consultation of amyloidosis that was started in 2018 by GSRA‐J
1
revealed that (1) ATTR is the most common subtype of systemic amyloidosis in Japan; and (2) the approval of tafamidis and technetium‐scintigraphy raised the awareness of ATTR cardiomyopathy, leading to an upsurge in ATTR‐positive cardiac biopsy cases in Japan.
9
These data are compatible with our findings (Figure
3
) and indicate that the subtype frequency of autopsy cases may precisely reflect the current situation of subtype distribution of systemic amyloidosis in Japan.
The dominancy of ATTR in autopsy cases can be explained as follows. First, aging of population with increased longevity in Japan may contribute to the increase in ATTRwt amyloidosis patients. As compared to the average age of subjects examined in 1990's
5
(64.9 years old), the average age of subjects examined in this study was 77.6 years old, indicating the aging of systemic amyloidosis patients in Japan. Although the average age of subjects examined in Strege et al.
6
was similar to this study (75.7 years old), the average age of ATTR patients was 88 years old, indicating the prevalence of ATTR amyloidosis in aged population.
Second, increased awareness to this disease
9
may also contribute to the increase in autopsied ATTRwt cases. As compared to the data obtained in the 1990's, the frequency of AA amyloidosis decreased precipitously (from 56.2%,
5
48.8%
6
to 9.3% in Figure
3
), possibly due to the improvement in therapeutics of rheumatoid arthritis and other inflammatory diseases,
3
,
17
which further contributed to the relative increase in the frequency of ATTRwt amyloidosis.
According to the Cancer Statistics in Japan (
https://ganjoho.jp/reg_stat/statistics/data/dl/en.html
), the numbers of patients with multiple myeloma, an important background disease for AL amyloidosis, were 2896 in 1990, 4120 in 2000, 6356 in 2010, and 7765 in 2018 (2.7‐fold higher than in 1990). From these data, we may estimate the increase in the number of AL amyloidosis patients in Japan, but the incidence of AL was 38.8% (Figure
3
) and similar to 32.2% in 1990's.
5
Because active myeloma is present only in approximately 10% of AL amyloidosis patients at the time of diagnosis,
3
we might not be able to detect the significant increase in the number of AL amyloidosis patients in Japan.
Major limitations of this study were as follows. First, the subjects analyzed in this study were autopsied individuals reported in APAC‐J, in which detailed information of each case was not available. Second, autopsy rate is now very low in Japan. According to the 2017 and 2018 annuals of the educational hospitals certified by the Japanese Society of Pathology (
https://e-learning.pathology.or.jp/course/view.php?id=54
), a total of 668 172 patients died at these hospitals and 20 371 cases (3.0%) were autopsied. Finally, we applied the phenotypical criteria for subtype classification based on the pathological descriptions of APAC‐J (Table
2
), because it was unrealistic to perform immunohistochemical analysis for all cases. The general pathology laboratories will need access to reliable commercial antibodies for ALκ, ALλ, and ATTR to accurately classify the subtypes of systemic amyloidosis. A robust consultation system to perform both immunohistochemical and proteomic analysis will also be necessary.
CONCLUSION
To reveal the latest subtype frequency of systemic amyloidosis among autopsy cases in Japan, we analyzed the systemic amyloidosis cases autopsied in 2017 and 2018 that were listed in APAC‐J. After conducting a questionnaire survey, immunohistochemistry, and proteomic analysis, we classified 399 systemic amyloidosis cases. ATTR was the most common subtype (44.4%), followed by AL (38.8%). In 168 cases (42.1%), systemic amyloidosis was the main cause of death. Of these cases, AL was the most common subtype (47.6%), followed by ATTR (41.1%).
