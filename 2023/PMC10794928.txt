SUMOylation is a reversible posttranslational modification that modulates the interactions, localization, stability, and activity of the protein substrates by attaching the small ubiquitin-related modifier (SUMO) (
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
). SUMO conjugation to target proteins occurs
via
a cascade of enzymatic reactions involving the heterodimeric SUMO-activating E1 enzyme (SAE1/SAE2), the SUMO-conjugating E2 enzyme UBC9, and a SUMO E3 ligase that increases the efficiency of SUMO conjugation (
5
,
6
). The SUMO pathway is highly conserved in eukaryotes and is generally required for cell proliferation and appropriate chromosome segregation in diverse organisms (
5
,
7
,
8
). In higher eukaryotic cells, a study with Ubc9 KO mice revealed that loss of SUMOylation leads to early embryonic lethality and apoptotic death of early embryonic cells with defects in nuclear organization and mitotic chromosome segregation (
9
). While there is only one SUMO protein in yeast,
Caenorhabditis elegans
, and
Drosophila
, the mammalian genomes encode three conjugation competent SUMO proteins, namely SUMO-1, and the highly similar SUMO-2 and SUMO-3 (
10
).
While proteins in the nucleus, cytosol, and the plasma membrane can all be targets of SUMOylation, the majority of identified SUMOylated proteins are transcriptional regulators, including transcription factors, cofactors, and chromatin-modifying enzymes (
6
,
7
,
11
). Indeed, SUMOylation mainly occurs in the nucleus, and numerous studies have shown that SUMOylation plays an important role in chromatin-related biological processes including transcription, DNA replication, DNA damage repair, and chromosome segregation (
1
,
4
,
8
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
). Notably, growing evidence indicates that the SUMOylation enzymes are resided primarily in the nuclear matrix, the nonchromatin, ribonucleoproteinaceous framework (
16
,
17
). For instance, the SUMO E3 ligase PIASy was reported to be predominantly enriched in the nuclear matrix (
18
). Similarly, protein inhibitor of activated STAT 1, another SUMO E3 ligase, was also enriched in the nuclear matrix and colocalization with nuclear mitotic apparatus protein (NuMA) though its SAF-A/B, Acinus, and PIAS domain (
19
). Recently, we reported that like PIASy and protein inhibitor of activated STAT 1, the SUMO E1 subunits SAE1/2 and additional PIAS family E3 ligases are also highly enriched in the nuclear matrix (
20
). The above observations raise the question whether SUMOylated proteins are enriched in the nuclear matrix and whether SUMOylation facilitates the nuclear matrix architecture by functioning as “molecular glue” mediating protein-protein interactions (
21
,
22
,
23
). In this regard, several nuclear matrix proteins, including NuMA, scaffold attachment factor B, and special AT-rich sequence binding protein 1, have been reported to be SUMO substrates (
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
).
In this study, we present evidence that SUMOylated proteins are enriched in the nuclear matrix. We investigate the potential role of SUMOylation acting as "molecular glue" important for the formation of nuclear matrix. Our results argue against SUMOylation as a molecular glue essential for nuclear matrix but suggests a role of nuclear matrix in mediating SUMOylation and its regulated biological processes.

Biochemical fractionation revealed that SUMOylated proteins are primarily associated with nuclear matrix
It is well-recognized that SUMOylation is enriched in the nucleus and has critical roles in all chromatin-based processes (
1
,
4
,
8
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
). Given the findings that the various components of SUMOylation machinery are enriched in the nuclear matrix (
18
,
20
), we wished to investigate whether SUMOylated proteins are enriched in the nuclear matrix or chromatin. To this end, we first employed a standard protocol for biochemical fractionation of cellular components into cytosol, nuclear extracts, and chromatin plus nuclear matrix fractions (
28
) (
Fig. 1
A
). Subsequent Western blotting (WB) analysis revealed that the protocol well separated cytosol marker GAPDH, nuclear marker lamin A/C, and chromatin marker histone H3 into the corresponding fractions (
Fig. 1
B
). WB analysis of the same fractions showed that, although both SUMO-1 and SUMO-2/3-modified proteins could be detected in the cytosol and nuclear extract fractions, they were mainly detected in the insoluble chromatin plus nuclear matrix fraction (
Fig. 1
B
). To determine whether SUMOylated proteins were enriched in chromatin and/or nuclear matrix, we used a modified protocol that first prepared nuclei from HeLa cells and then treated the nuclei with DNase I, which digested and released soluble chromatin into the nuclear extract fraction (
20
,
29
). After centrifugation to remove the nuclear extracts plus soluble chromatin fragments, the pellets were extracted with 2 M NaCl to remove insoluble chromatin and other extractable proteins, and the remaining pellet fraction was designated as the nuclear matrix (
Fig. 1
C
). As shown in
Figure 1
D
, the majority of SUMO-1 and SUMO-2/3-modified proteins remained in the nuclear matrix fraction which was essentially devoid of chromatin based on the level of histone H3.

To validate this observation, we employed a modified protocol that the nuclei pellets were lysed, and chromatin was digested with 5 units of Benzonase for 30 min (
Fig. 1
E
). The samples were then extracted with buffer containing 420 mM NaCl and centrifuged to generate nuclear extract and chromatin fraction and pellet fraction that contained the nuclear matrix. Subsequent WB analyses revealed that Benzonase treatment effectively mobilized histone H3 from the pellet fraction in the mock to the nuclear extract and chromatin fraction (
Fig. 1
F
). Notably, both SUMO-1 and SUMO-2/3-modified proteins were mainly detected in the pellet fractions regardless of Benzonase treatment or not (
Fig. 1
F
), further demonstrating that the majority of SUMOylated proteins are associated with nuclear matrix but not with chromatin.
To assess the relative enrichment of SUMOylated proteins in each fraction, we quantified the overall SUMOylated signal vertically across each lane and divided it by their relative levels of proteins based on Ponceau-S staining. From three independent biological repeats (
Fig. S1
), we quantified the relative enrichment of SUMOylated proteins as shown in

G

Figure 1

The selective association with nuclear matrix is likely a general feature of SUMOylated proteins, as the same observation was made in HCT116 cells and murine primary hepatocytes (
Fig. S2
). Thus, consistent with our previous report that the SUMOylation machinery is highly enriched in the nuclear matrix (
20
), SUMOylated proteins are also highly enriched in the nuclear matrix.
Immunofluorescent imaging confirmed that SUMOylated proteins are mainly localized in the nuclear matrix
Having observed by biochemical fractionation that SUMOylated proteins are primarily associated with nuclear matrix rather than chromatin, we wished to validate the finding by immunofluorescent (IF) staining assay. We treated HeLa cells with 0.5% Triton X-100 to permeabilize cells and then with Benzonase for 4 h to digest chromatin, followed by washes with CSK buffer (see
Experimental procedures
) to remove chromatin. The cells were then processed for IF staining for SUMO-1 or SUMO-2/3 together with lamin A/C or histone markers. Consistent with lamin A/C being the major component of nuclear lamina, IF staining of lamin A/C displayed a typical perinuclear ring pattern, although in some cells nuclear interior lamin A/C dots were also observed (
Fig. 2
A
). IF staining of histone markers acetylated histone H3 (H3ac) and lysine 4 (K4) trimethylated H3 (H3K4me3) confirmed that Benzonase treatment followed by washing effectively depleted chromatin from the cell (
Fig. 2
,
A
and
B
). 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) staining also confirmed the depletion of chromatin after Benzonase treatment (
Fig. 2
,
A
and
B
). However, Benzonase treatment did not obviously reduce the intensities of both SUMO-1 and SUMO-2/3 (
Fig. 2
,
A
and
B
). Thus, depletion of chromatin did not significantly alter the distribution or the intensity of SUMOylated proteins, further demonstrating that SUMOylated proteins are primarily associated with nuclear matrix rather than chromatin.

Stable association of SUMOylated proteins with nuclear matrix
To assess to what extent SUMOylated proteins are enriched in the nuclear matrix, we prepared nuclei from HeLa cells and then extracted nuclei with buffer containing an increasing concentration of salt. We then analyzed the levels of SUMOylated proteins and total proteins in each fraction by WB analysis and Coomassie blue staining, respectively. As shown in
Figure 3
A
for HeLa cells and
Figure 3
C
for HCT116 cells, increasing salt concentration extracted more and more proteins from nuclei as revealed by Coomassie blue staining, as expected. In fact, both cytoplasm and 400 mM salt fractions contained more total proteins than any of the nuclear matrix plus chromatin fraction. However, as revealed by WB analysis using SUMO-1 and SUMO-2/3-specific antibodies, both SUMO-1 and SUMO-2/3-modified proteins were mainly detected in the nuclear matrix plus chromatin fraction rather than extracted nuclear extracts (
Fig. 3
,
A
and
C
). Quantification of SUMOylated protein signals
versus

Fig. 3
,
B

). Because aforementioned results have demonstrated that SUMOylated proteins were associated with nuclear matrix rather than chromatin, these results provided further evidence that SUMOylated proteins are highly enriched in the nuclear matrix in comparison to cytoplasm and soluble nuclear proteins.

Proteomic analysis of HeLa cell nuclear matrix confirms the enrichment of SUMOylated proteins
The nuclear matrix is believed to provide the structural framework that organizes and maintains nuclear architecture, within which various nuclear processes take place (
16
,
17
,
30
). The proteomic analysis of nuclear matrix has recently been reported for
Drosophila
S2 cells and embryonic cells (
29
,
30
), but similar proteomic study has yet to be reported for any mammalian cells. On the other hand, multiple studies have reported the proteomic analysis of SUMOylated proteins from various mammalian cells (
31
,
32
,
33
,
34
). A most comprehensive analysis of SUMOylated proteome has identified 1409 SUMOylated proteins from HeLa cells cultured under regular condition (
33
). To further assess whether SUMOylated proteins are selectively enriched in the nuclear matrix, we prepared nuclear matrix from HeLa cells based on the DNase I digestion plus 2 M NaCl extraction protocol. The resulting insoluble nuclear matrix fraction was then subjected to label-free identification and quantification by liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry (LC/MS-MS). From three independent experiments, 2154, 2165, and 2101 proteins were identified with at least one unique peptide. A total of 1735 proteins were commonly identified among all three experiments (
Fig. 4
A
), and the correlation analysis indicated an excellent reproducibility (
Fig. S4
A
). We took the stringent criteria and considered only the 1735 proteins commonly identified in all three biological replicates as the nuclear matrix proteins. Because this was the first large proteomic analysis of mammalian nuclear matrix, to our knowledge, we compared it with that of
Drosophila
S2 cells and embryonic core nuclear matrix. The reported
Drosophila
S2 cell and embryonic core nuclear matrix proteins were filtered for ones with human homologs and being nuclear proteins. This yielded 657 and 502 proteins, respectively. As shown in
Figure 4
B
, 71.7%
Drosophila
S2 cell nuclear matrix proteins (471 out of the 657) and 80.5% embryonic core nuclear matrix proteins have counterparts in our 1735 nuclear matrix proteins, suggesting a nature of evolution conservation of nuclear matrix.

We next performed Gene Ontology (GO) analysis for the nuclear matrix proteins. This analysis revealed that the nuclear matrix was highly enriched for proteins involved in the following biological processes: ribosomal function and assembly, mRNA splicing, chromosome segregation, protein stabilization, mitotic spindle assembly checkpoint, and histone deacetylation (

Interestingly, molecular function analysis revealed the enrichment of helicases and transcriptional corepressor activity, whereas cellular component analysis showed significant enrichment for nuclear speck, chromosome, nuclear matrix, and so on. These results are in further support of nuclear matrix being the framework orchestrating various nuclear processes including ribosome assembly, RNA processing, chromosome regulation, protein stability, and transcriptional regulation, especially repression
via

,

To assess whether HeLa nuclear matrix is enriched of SUMOylated proteins, we compared the 1735 nuclear matrix proteins we identified with the aforementioned 1409 SUMOylated proteins identified by Hendriks

This result is consistent with our biochemical fractionation analysis and is in further support that SUMOylated proteins are selectively enriched in the nuclear matrix. We also carried out GO analysis for 498 nuclear matrix-associate SUMOylated proteins. Notably, besides ribosomal function and RNA processing, the nuclear matrix-associate SUMOylated proteins were particularly enriched for negative regulation of transcription by RNA pol II, DNA helicase activity, and histone deacetylation (
Fig. 4

,
B
–
D

Inhibition of SUMOylation by ML-792 did not significantly alter nuclear matrix composition
Having demonstrated that SUMOylated proteins are selectively enriched in the nuclear matrix, we next asked whether SUMOylation is important for nuclear matrix formation. This question is relevant because SUMO is believed to mediate protein-protein interactions and may serve as "molecular glue". We made use of ML-792, a potent specific inhibitor for SUMO E1 activating enzyme (
39
). Pilot experiments established that treatment with ML-792 for 12 h inhibited global SUMO-1 and SUMO-2/3-mediated SUMOylation events in a dose-dependent manner without affecting protein ubiquitination (
Fig. S5
A
). To quantitatively determine how inhibition of SUMOylation affects nuclear matrix, we carried out stable isotopic labeling of amino acids in cell culture (SILAC) experiments (
40
) by culturing HeLa cells without or with L-arginine
13
C
6
hydrochloride (84 μg/ml) and L-lysine-4,4,5,5-d
4
hydrochloride (146 μg/ml) for more than seven generations. The unlabeled and labeled HeLa cells were treated without or with 5 μM ML-792 for 12 h, respectively. The equal amounts of cells were then mixed and nuclear matrix fractions were prepared and subjected to proteomic analysis as above. We confirmed by WB analysis that ML-792 treatment markedly reduced the levels of SUMO-1 and SUMO-2/3-modified proteins in the nuclear matrix (
Fig. 5
A
). Subsequent proteomic analysis of three biological replicates identified 1021 and 993 nuclear matrix proteins from control and ML-792 treated cells, respectively (
Fig. 5
B
). Principal component analysis (PCA) analysis revealed the good reproducibility of the data (
Fig. S5
B
). Using a cutoff of more that 0.8-fold decrease and 1.25-fold increase for differentially expressed proteins (DEPs), we found that ML-792 treatment resulted in change of 92 proteins or 8.6% of identified nuclear matrix proteins (
Fig. 5
C
). Among 92 altered proteins, 35 showed variable increase and 57 decrease upon ML-792 treatment (
Fig. 5
D
). We further analyzed whether ML-792 treatment preferentially affected SUMOylated proteins. As shown in in
Figure 5
E
, ML-792 did not appear to preferentially affect SUMOylated proteins. Together, these results indicate that potent inhibition of SUMOylation did not appear to significantly affect the nuclear matrix composition, nor the preferentially association of SUMOylated proteins with nuclear matrix.

Among the nuclear matrix proteins that exhibited a significant reduction upon ML-792 treatment was Sin3A, a scaffold protein for HDAC1/2-containing Sin3A complex (
41
). In fact, Sin3A was one of the four proteins that were detected in either all three controls but not three ML-792 treated samples or opposite (
Fig. 5
D
, lower panel and

). This prompted us to further investigate if SUMOylation regulates the nuclear matrix association of Sin3A complex and possibly the NuRD complex (

Fig. 5

UBC9 KO results in chromosome segregation defect
Studies from lower eukaryotic cells demonstrated that SUMOylation is essential for cell proliferation and chromosome segregation (
9
). The concept that SUMOylation is required for mammalian cell viability, cell cycle progression, nuclear integrity, and chromosome segregation is largely derived from the study of UBC9 KO mouse early embryonic cells. Interestingly, UBC9 KO in chicken DT40 cells indicated that SUMOylation is required for cell viability but does not lead to considerable accumulation of cells in mitotic (M) phase or chromosome missegregation (
44
). These inconsistent results raise the question whether there is a consensus function of SUMOylation in higher eukaryotic cells. We thus made use of CRISPR/CAS9 technology and three sgUBC9 to KO UBC9 gene in HeLa cells (
Fig. S6
A
). HeLa cells were transfected with the mixture of three sgUBC9 plasmids together with a CAS9 expression plasmid that carries also a puromycin selective marker. Two days after transfection, puromycin was added to select for transfected cells, and cells were collected at various time points (72 h, 96 h, and 120 h after transfection) for evaluating UBC9 KO and effect on SUMOylation. As shown in
Figure 6
A
, UBC9 protein level was markedly reduced 96 h after transfection and was nearly undetectable after 120 h. Notably, histone H3 serine 10 phosphorylation (H3S10P), a marker of M phase, was progressively accumulated from 72 h to 120 h, suggesting a M phase arrest upon UBC9 KO. However, we observed neither significant increase of γH2AX (a marker for DNA damage), nor significant increase of cleaved poly-ADP ribose polymerase (PARP) or cleaved, active caspase-3 (
Fig. 6
A
), indicating that UBC9 KO in HeLa cells led to M arrest but not apoptosis.

+

To further assess the role of SUMOylation in mitotic progression and chromosome segregation, we treated HeLa cells with 5 μM SUMO E1 inhibitor ML-792 for up to 4 days. We confirmed by IF staining that ML-792 treatment drastically downregulated the levels of SUMO-2/3 modified proteins after 1 day and remained low throughout the entire experiments (
Fig. 6

Interestingly, lamin B and DAPI staining revealed that ML-792 treatment did not lead to the accumulation of M phase cells and obvious chromosome missegregation as UBC9 KO, but appeared to exhibit a severe defect in cytokinesis. Consistent with this observation, WB analysis revealed that ML-792 treatment did not result in accumulation of H3S10P (
Fig. 6

Also differed from UBC9 KO, ML-792 treatment resulted in elevated γH2AX, cleaved poly-ADP ribose polymerase, and cleaved Caspase-3, indicating that ML-792 treatment leads to increased DNA damage and cell apoptosis. Thus, inhibition of SUMO E1 and KO of SUMO E2 appear to generate distinct but related defects in cell cycle.
Inactivation of SUMOylation by UBC9 KO did not significantly alter nuclear matrix composition
Although we failed to observe significant change of nuclear matrix composition upon inhibition of SUMOylation by ML-792, one caveat for such an approach could be incomplete inhibition of SUMOylation. Having established protocol for knocking out UBC9 in HeLa cells, we next assessed how UBC9 KO affected nuclear matrix. HeLa cells were transfected without or with sgUBC9 and CAS9 plasmids as above, and 5 days after transfection the cells were collected and subcellular fractionation and nuclear matrix preparation were performed as above. As shown in
Figure 7

Fig. 7

We then carried out label-free proteomic analysis of the resulting nuclear matrix fractions and identified 2003 common proteins from three sets of control cells and 2061 common proteins from three sets of UBC9 KO cells, with 1894 proteins overlapped between two sets (
Fig. 7

). The three independent data were of high quality based on PCA analysis (
Fig. S6

). Using a cutoff of more that 0.8-fold decrease and 1.25-fold increase for DEPs, we found that UBC9 KO resulted in level change for 219 proteins, which represents about 10.1% of the identified nuclear matrix proteins (
Fig. 7

). Among these 219 proteins, 104 were reduced and 115 increased in protein levels (
Fig. 7
,
D

Discussion
SUMOylation has been shown to play roles in virtually all biological processes (
2
). In principle, SUMOylation exerts its pleiotropic biological functions
via
modulating the properties of its substrate proteins (
1
,
3
). Accumulative studies have identified nuclear proteins and in particular chromatin-related proteins as the major SUMOylation substrates (
7
,
33
). In this study, we unravel that SUMOylated proteins are highly enriched in the nuclear matrix rather than chromatin (
Figure 1
,
Figure 2
,
Figure 3
). However, SUMOylation appears neither required for nuclear matrix formation nor for selective nuclear matrix association (
Figs. 5
and
7
). We provide further evidence that SUMOylation is required for chromosome segregation in higher eukaryotic cells (
Fig. 6
).
Although nuclear matrix is gaining recognition with the increasing attention on nuclear architecture (
16
), it is surprising so far that there are very limited proteomic studies on nuclear matrix (
29
,
30
), which hinders the comprehensive understanding of this mysterious cellular structure. Here, we carried out proteomic analysis of nuclear matrix in HeLa cells and from three independent experiments a common set of 1735 nuclear matrix proteins were identified (
Fig. 4
). The nuclear matrix proteins are likely highly conserved in evolution, because a comparison with the
Drosophila
S2 and embryonic cell nuclear matrix proteins revealed a 71.7% and 80.5% overlap respectively when considering only the
Drosophila
proteins that are nuclear and conserved between
Drosophila
and human (
Fig. 4
B
).
GO analysis reveals that HeLa nuclear matrix is highly enriched of proteins involving in translation and ribosome assembly, mRNA splicing, chromosome segregation, protein stabilization, mitotic spindle assembly checkpoint, and histone deacetylation (
Fig. 4
C
). Enrichment of translation and ribosome assembly could be explained by nucleolus as being nuclear matrix associated. Notably, the nuclear matrix is also enriched of proteins involved in histone deacetylation, in particular the HDAC1/2-containing Sin3A and NuRD complexes (
Fig. 4
,
C
and
E
). Thus, our proteomic analysis of nuclear matrix composition is in further support of nuclear matrix being the framework orchestrating various nuclear processes including ribosome assembly, RNA processing, chromosome regulation, protein stability, and transcriptional regulation, especially repression
via
histone deacetylation.
We provide evidence that although SUMOylated proteins are highly enriched in the nuclear matrix, SUMOylation is unlikely required for nuclear matrix formation. By either inhibition of SAE1/SAE2 or KO of UBC9, we could nearly completely block SUMOylation (
Figure 5
,
Figure 6
,
Figure 7
). However, subsequent proteomic analysis revealed that inactivating SUMOylation only resulted in a mild change of nuclear matrix composition. In fact, this mild change was obtained without considering the potential effect on protein expression levels upon inactivating SUMOylation. In addition, blocking SUMOylation appears to affect the nuclear matrix association of both SUMOylated and non-SUMOylated proteins (
Figs. 5
E
and
7
F
). Thus, SUMOylation does not appear to provide an advantage for nuclear matrix association.
Taking together, we provide a working model in
Figure 7
H
explaining the selective enrichment of SUMOylated proteins in the nuclear matrix. This selective enrichment is probably not due to SUMO as a molecular glue or binding module that preferentially interacts with nuclear matrix. Rather, the selective enrichment of SUMOylated proteins in nuclear matrix is likely a result of selective nuclear matrix enrichment of SUMOylation machinery (
18
,
20
,
45
). We and others have previously reported the preferential enrichment of SUMO E1 activating enzymes SAE1/SAE2 (
20
) and PIAS family (
18
,
20
,
45
) in the nuclear matrix. Despite of its selective enrichment, SUMOylation is unlikely required for the formation of nuclear matrix. However, SUMOylation is clearly required for appropriate chromosome segregation, a function that is conserved in evolution. Our proteomic analysis supports nuclear matrix as the framework orchestrating various nuclear processes. Future work is needed to elucidate how SUMOylation regulates chromosome segregation.
Our study also supports a critical role of SUMOylation in cell cycle progression and nuclear architecture. UBC9 KO led to enlarged nuclei and accumulation of M phase cells with chromosome missegregation (
Fig. 6
B
). Interestingly, inhibition of SUMO E1 enzyme by ML-792, which potently inhibited SUMOylation, did not lead to accumulation of M phase cells but resulted in defect in cytokinesis, producing giant cells containing multiple divided and/or undivided nucleus (
Fig. 6
C
). Indeed, WB analysis showed accumulation of H3S10P in UBC9 KO but not ML-792-treated cells (
Fig. 6
,
A
and
D
). The reason for this phenotypic discrepancy is unknown, although we surmised the lack of M phase blockage for ML-792 could be due to incomplete inhibition of SUMOylation. However, we could not rule out the possibility that UBC9 may have function beyond SUMOylation. Nevertheless, both ML-792 treatment and UBC9 KO experiments support a role of SUMOylation in cell cycle progression and nuclear architecture.
Experimental procedures
Cell culture and transfection
Human embryonic kidney 293T (HEK293T) cells, human cervical cancer HeLa cells, and human colorectal cancer HCT116 cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific). All cells were cultured in medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (HyClone), 100 U/ml penicillin, and 100 μg/ml streptomycin (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific) in 5% CO
2
at 37 °C. Primary hepatocytes were isolated from 8 to 12 weeks old male C57BL/6 J mice by collagenase perfusion as previously described (
46
). Hepatocytes were seeded at a final density of 0.5 × 10
6
cells/well in 6-well plates and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Cell transfection was performed using LipoFiter (Hanbio) according to manufacturer’s instruction.
Antibodies and reagents
The commercial antibodies used in this study and their sources are as follow: histone H3 (ab1791), UBC9 (ab33044), Sin3A (ab129087), histone H3 phospho S10 (ab14955), γH2AX (ab81299), lamin B1 (ab16048) and lamin A/C (ab133256) from Abcam; SUMO-1 (ET1606-53) and SUMO-2/3 (ET1701-17) from HUABIO; H3K4me3 (07-473) from Sigma-Aldrich; HDAC1 (A0238) and HDAC2 (A2084) from ABclonal; p62 (CY9081) from Abways; RARP (#9542), Caspase-3 (#9662) from CST; FLAG, HA, and GAPDH from Abmart. RbAp46 and acetylated H3 antibodies were homemade. The following secondary antibody were used: Alexa Fluor 680 goat anti-rabbit IgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch, 111-625-144); Alexa Fluor 790 goat anti-mouse IgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch, 115-655-146); Alexa Fluor 594 goat anti-rabbit IgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch, 111-585-003); Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-mouse IgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch, 115-545-003). ML-792 (HY-108702) was purchased from MCE.
Plasmids
FLAG-SUMO-1 and FLAG-SUMO-2 expression plasmids were as described (
20
,
47
).
KO of UBC9 in HeLa cells using CRISPR–Cas9
UBC9 KO cell lines were generated by using CRISPR–Cas9 system, as described previously. Briefly, an online CRISPR design tool (
http://tools.genome-engineering.org/
) was used to select three suitable guide RNA sequences against human UBC9 (sgUBC9-1: CACCGACATTCGGGTGAAATAATGG; sgUBC9-2: CACCGACCCGAATGTGTACCCTTCG; and sgUBC9-3: CACCGAAGCCCTCCTTACAAATGGG). The three sgRNA sequences were cloned into CRISPR-Cas9-V2 sgRNA vector. To KO UBC9 in HeLa cells, three CRISPR-Cas9-V2 sgUBC9 plasmids were mixed together and transfected into HeLa with LipoFiter (Hanbio) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Forty-eight hours after transfection, puromycin was added at a final concentration of 1 mg/ml to select transfected cells. The cells were collected at various time points and the efficiency of UBC9 KO was verified by WB analysis using antibodies against UBC9, SUMO-1, and SUMO-2/3 as indicated.
Subcellular fractionation and preparation of nuclear matrix
To separate cell extracts into cytosol, soluble nuclear extract and chromatin plus nuclear matrix fractions as illustrated in
Figure 1
A
, cultured cells were washed with cold 1× PBS twice. Cells were then resuspended with 250 μl Buffer A (10 mM Hepes-NaOH, pH 7.9, 10 mM KCl, 1.5 mM MgCl
2
, and 0.5 mM β-Mercaptoethanol) and incubated on ice for 15 to 20 min. NP-40 was added to a final concentration of 0.1%, vortexed, and incubated on ice for 10 min. The samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 to 15 min and the resulting supernatants were designated as cytosols. The nuclear pellets were washed with cold 1× PBS twice, and then were resuspended with 60 to 80 μl Buffer B (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.6, 420 mM NaCl, 0.5% NP-40, 2 mM MgCl
2
plus 1× protease inhibitor cocktail, 50 mM
N
-Ethylmaleimide and 1 mM DTT). The mixtures were incubated on ice for 20 min and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 to 15 min. The supernatants were designated as nuclear extracts, whereas the pellets washed with Buffer B once and designated as the chromatin plus nuclear matrix.
To prepare nuclear matrix depleted of chromatin as illustrated in
Figure 1
C
, cells were collected after wash in PBS, and lysed in CSK buffer (10 mM Pipes, pH 6.8, 100 mM NaCl, 300 mM sucrose, 3 mM MgCl
2
, 1 mM EGTA, supplemented with 1× protease inhibitor cocktail, 50 mM
N
-Ethylmaleimide, 1 mM DTT, and 0.5% Triton X-100). After 10 min incubation on ice, the pellets with chromatin plus the nuclear matrix were separated from soluble proteins by centrifugation at 5000
g
for 3 min. The pellets were resuspend in CSK buffer and chromatin was digested with 5 units of RNase-free DNase I (Promega) for 30 min at 37 °C. Then ammonium sulfate was added in CSK buffer to a final concentration of 0.25 M and samples were pelleted again after 5 min incubation on ice. The pellets were further extracted with 2 M NaCl (1× protease inhibitor cocktail, 50 mM
N
-Ethylmaleimide, and 1 mM DTT) in CSK buffer for 15 min on ice, and then centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min. This treatment removed all the DNA and the histones from nuclear pellets. The remaining pellets were designated as nuclear matrix and was solubilized in Urea buffer (8 M urea, 0.1 M NaH
2
PO
4
, 0.01 M Tris, pH 8.0, 1 × protease inhibitor cocktail, 50 mM
N
-Ethylmaleimide, and 1 mM DTT).
To prepare nuclear matrix depleted of chromatin as illustrated in
Figure 1
E
, cells were processed as the protocol in
Figure 1
A
upon to the step of resuspension of nuclear pellets in Buffer B. The samples were then divided into two halves, with one added with 5 U of Benzonase and incubated for 30 min on ice. The samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min to obtain nuclear extracts and chromatin plus nuclear matrix for samples not treated with Benzonase and nuclear extracts plus chromatin and nuclear matrix for samples treated with Benzonase.
Immunofluorescence analysis of the nuclear matrix-associated proteins
Immunofluorescence assay of the nuclear matrix-associated proteins was performed as described (
48
). In brief, HeLa cells grown on cover slips were washed with cold 1× PBS twice and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 in CSK Buffer (100 mM NaCl, 300 mM sucrose, 10 mM Pipes, pH 6.8 and 5 mM MgCl
2
) for 10 min at room temperature. After washing twice with CSK with 1×protease inhibitor cocktail, cells were incubated with 50 U or 250 U Benzonase (Merck, 71,205-3) in CSK with 1×protease inhibitor cocktail for times as indicated at 37 °C. After two washes with CSK buffer, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at 4 °C, washed with cold 1× PBS twice and then permeabilized again with 1% Triton X-100 in 1× PBS for 20 min on ice. After washing with cold 1× PBS twice, the cells were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin in 0.2% PBST (0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS) for 1 h at 37 °C, washed with cold 1×PBS twice, and incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4 °C followed by incubation with fluorescently conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. The images were acquired with a confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss).
Proteomic analysis of nuclear matrix by label-free quantification LC-MS
For LC-MS analysis, the nuclear matrix fractions solubilized in 8 M Urea buffer were clarified by centrifugation at 16,000
g
for 10 min. The resulting supernatants were loaded into 10 kD Microcon filtration devices (Millipore) and centrifuged at 12,000
g
for 20 min to remove solvents. The samples were washed twice with urea lysis buffer (8 M urea, 100 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0) and twice with 50 mM NH
4
HCO
3
by centrifugation. Then the samples were digested using trypsin at an enzyme to protein mass ratio of 1:25 overnight at 37 °C. Peptides were extracted and dried. For the proteome profiling samples, peptides were analyzed on a Q Exactive HF-X Hybrid Quadrupole-Orbitrap Mass Spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled with a high-performance liquid chromatography system (EASY nLC 1200, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Dried peptide samples redissolved in Solvent A (0.1% formic acid in water) were loaded to a 2-cm self-packed trap column (100-μm inner diameter, ReproSil-Pur C18-AQ, 3 μm; Dr Maisch) using Solvent A and separated on a 150-μm-inner-diameter column with a length of 15 cm (ReproSil-Pur C18-AQ, 1.9 μm; Dr Maisch) over a 75 min gradient (Buffer A: 0.1% formic acid in water; buffer B: 0.1% formic acid in 80% acetonitrile) at a constant flow rate of 600 nl/min (0–75 min, 0 min, 4% B; 0–10 min, 4–15% B; 10–60 min, 15–30% B; 60–69 min, 30–50% B; 69–70 min, 50–100% B; 70–75 min, 100% B). The eluted peptides were ionized under 2 kV and introduced into mass spectrometry. Mass spectrometry was operated under a data-dependent acquisition mode. For the MS1 full scan, ions with m/z ranging from 300 to 1400 were acquired by Orbitrap mass analyzer at a high resolution of 120,000. The automatic gain control was set as 3E + 06. The maximal ion injection time was 80 ms. MS2 acquisition was performed in a top-speed mode. Precursor ions were selected and fragmented with higher energy collision dissociation with normalized collision energy of 27%. Fragment ions were analyzed by ion trap mass analyzer with automatic gain control at 5E + 04. The maximal ion injection time of MS2 was 20 ms and the dynamic exclusion was 12 s (
49
).
Proteomic analysis of nuclear matrix by SILAC
To quantitatively determine the effect of blocking SUMOylation on nuclear matrix, the SILAC strategy was used essentially as described (
50
). In brief, L-arginine
13
C
6
hydrochloride and L-lysine-4,4,5,5-d
4
hydrochloride–labeled HeLa cells were designated as control groups, whereas L-arginine and L-lysine labeled HeLa cells were treated with ML-792 for 12 h. The same quantity of control and ML-792-treated cells were collected and mixed. The mixed cells were then subjected to preparation of nuclear matrix using the protocol illustrated in
Figure 1
C
. The proteomic analyses were performed as described above with three independent biological replicates.
Proteome data analysis
MS raw files were processed using the Maxquant software (1.5.3.8 version) (
http://maxquant.org
) and searched against the Uniprot human reviewed protein database (released on 202006). Default searching parameters were used with brief modifications. Trypsin specificity was used and up to two missed cleavages were allowed. Oxidation(M) and acetyl (N-term) were considered as variable modifications, while carbamidomethyl of cystine was set as a fixed modification. Unique and razor peptides were selected for protein quantification. IBAQ algorithm was used in HeLa nuclear protein identification. Peptides were validated using decoy protein sequences at a threshold of 0.01 false discovery rate at both PSM and protein levels.
Raw intensities without imputation were used for further analysis. The upper quantile normalization for each sample and the row median normalization for each protein were used to normalize the expression data. DEPs were determined using two-sided student
t
test. The fold change threshold for up and downregulated proteins was 1.25 and 0.8, and a
p
value cutoff of 0.05 was required. Genome-wide functional analysis was performed using the PANTHER tool and visualized using R (version 4.2.1) (
https://www.r-project.org
). R was also used to visualize the result of proteome data analysis, including quality control, heatmap and volcano plot of DEPs, and PCA. Venn diagram and histogram were generated with Excel software. HomoloGene database was used to map the homologous genes from proteome study of different spices. Only proteins identified in all three biological replicates and annotated as nuclear protein using genome-wide functional analysis were used in differentially expression and bioinformatic analysis.
