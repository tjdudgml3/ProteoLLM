Introduction
Nitrogenase is the only enzyme capable
of reducing dinitrogen gas
(N
2
) into ammonia (NH
3
). It consists of two
component proteins, the molybdenum iron protein (MoFeP) and the iron
protein (FeP). MoFeP is an (αβ)
2
dimer of heterodimers
and contains two metal clusters, the FeMoco, a [7Fe:9S:1Mo:1C] cluster
that is the site of catalysis, and the [8Fe:7S] P-cluster.
1
,
2
FeP is a γ
2
homodimer, containing a [4Fe:4S] cluster
that bridges the two subunits (
Figure
1
). During nitrogen reduction, FeP sequentially delivers
electrons to MoFeP in an ATP-dependent process (
eq
1
).

Molybdenum nitrogenase, the most common isoform
of the enzyme,
is inactivated by the environmental gas carbon monoxide (CO), which
acts as a mixed inhibitor.
3
,
4
CO is produced anthropogenically
during the combustion of fossil fuels, through natural geochemical
and biological processes in the soil,
5
−
7
and by plants that use
it is a signaling gas.
8
,
9
While CO potently inhibits nitrogenase
in vitro, diazotrophs tolerate the gas quite well. The inhibition
constants,
K
is
and
K
ii
, are in the range of 1–2 × 10
–4
atm and 1–2 × 10
–3
atm, respectively,
in vitro.
3
,
10
,
11
In contrast,
when nitrogenase activity is measured
in vivo
in
diazotrophs, the constants are an order of magnitude higher.
3
In many diazotrophs, nitrogenase is protected
by a small protein
called CowN.
Rhodobacter capsulatus
(
13
) and
Rhodospirillum rubrum
(
14
) are able to grow diaoztrophically in
1 and 0.15% (0.01 and 0.0015 atm) CO, respectively, without apparent
growth inhibition. However, when
cowN
is knocked
out in either bacterium, diazotrophic growth is nearly nonexistent,
suggesting that
cowN
is vital in maintaining nitrogenase
activity under CO. CowN-based CO protection was demonstrated in
R. capsulatus
and
R. rubrum
; however, most diazotrophs do not contain CowN, suggesting it is
not a necessary protein for nitrogen fixation. The presence of
cowN
in the genomes of diazotrophs does not appear to be
correlated with any specific metabolic trait (e.g., the ability to
utilize CO in the Wood-Ljungdahl pathway) or with the absence or presence
of alternative nitrogenases. Furthermore, while CowN is found in several
bacterial phyla, it is most widespread among proteobacteria.
Our group recently described the mechanism of CowN protection in
the diazotroph
Gluconacetobacter diazotrophicus
(
Gd
). CowN binds to MoFeP and increases the inhibition
constant for CO-binding about 5- to 10-fold.
10
There was no evidence that CowN alters the structure of FeMoco,
nor does CowN have any activity of its own, leading to the hypothesis
that CowN prevents CO from reaching FeMoco. CowN appears to specifically
prevent CO binding as it does not prevent the alternative substrate
acetylene (C
2
H
2
) from reaching the active site.
While we were able to determine that CowN and MoFeP interact, the
binding surface on either protein is unknown. This goal of this work
is to identify structural features in
G. diazotrophicus
CowN that are important for its function and for MoFeP binding.
Mutagenesis and cross-linking data suggest that a glutamate residue
near CowN’s C-terminus is necessary for CowN activity; however,
a conserved cysteine residue is not essential for CO protection.

Nitrogenase Activity Assays with N
2
For
N
2

2

and the reaction volume was 1.12 mL. When added, CowN was at 2 μM.
Assays were conducted in 10 mL stoppered glass vials in a solution
containing 50 mM Hepes, pH 8, 50 mM NaCl and 13 mM sodium dithionite.
The solution contained an ATP regeneration system consisting of 1.2
mg/mL sodium ATP, 4 mg/mL creatine phosphate, and 4 × 10
–5
mg/mL creatine kinase. Reactions were quenched after
20 min by adding ethylene glycol using the method described by Hales
16
to a final percentage of 45%. Ammonia was detected
using the indophenol method. Prior to measuring ammonia, the interreferences
were removed in two chromatography steps. First, the sample was passed
through a Dowex 1 × 2 column that had been prepared to be in
its chloride form. Next, the reaction product was passed through a
Dowex 50WX8 column that had been equilibrated to be in its sodium
form. The indophenol protocol was based on Dilworth et al.
17
The following reagents were freshly prepared
prior to the assay: A solution containing 5% (w/v) phenol and 2.5%
(w/v) NaOH, a 0.02% (w/v) sodium nitroprusside solution, and a 1.34%
bleach solution. To measure ammonia, 100 μL of reaction solution
that has been chromatographically cleaned up, as described above,
was mixed with 30 μL of alkaline phenol reagent in a 96-well
plate. Next, 45 μL of sodium nitroprusside solution was added,
followed by the immediate addition of 45 μL of the 1.34% bleach
solution. Samples were incubated for 40 min, and color development
was measured at 400 nm using a Tecan Spark plate reader with a baseline
correction at 800 nm. Ammonia concentrations were calculated based
on a standard curve constructed with 0–0.25 mM NH
4
Cl
2
.

Results and Discussion
Our group previously examined
nitrogenase protection by CowN using
the common model substrate C
2
H
2
.
10
Since the mechanisms of C
2
H
2
and N
2
reduction are different,
18
−
20
we wanted to
determine if
G. diazotrophicus
CowN
protects nitrogenase under N
2
turnover. As shown in
Figures
2
and
S1
, CowN prevents CO inhibition when N
2
is the substrate. The amount of protection during N
2
turnover
is nearly identical to that reported during C
2
H
2
turnover,
10
suggesting that CowN’s
protective mechanism is likely the same for both gases. The result
is consistent with our proposal that CowN prevents the access of CO
to FeMoco and thus protects nitrogenase turnover for all substrates.
Furthermore, these results directly explain the observations by Masepohl
and co-workers,
13
and Kerby and Roberts
14
who demonstrated that CowN sustained diazotrophic
growth in the presence of CO.

CowN is predicted to be a compact, 13 kDa 4-helical

primary sequence is not particularly conserved, however, it commonly
features a negatively charged C-terminal end that contains several
Glu and Asp residues (

A,B). In

a negatively charged C-terminus and a more positively charged N-terminus

residue (

CowN features a sulfur containing amino acid as either the penultimate
or the final residue. The residue is usually a Cys. In some cases,
there are two thiol containing residues at the C-terminus, as is the
case in
Gd
-CowN which ends in Cys90-Met91.

The conservation of acidic and sulfur-containing
residues at CowN’s
C-terminus suggests functional relevance. To examine the importance
of the negative C-terminus, we targeted Glu87, the only fully conserved
residue in CowN, for mutagenesis, generating the E87A, E87D, and E87Q
mutants. Furthermore, we investigated the role of the C-terminal Cys
and Met residues by making C90A, C90S, and M91A mutants as well as
a C-terminally truncated version of CowN that lacks the final two
residues (termed C-trunc CowN).
Mutant expression and purification
were identical to those of WT
CowN. All three E87 mutants and both C90 mutants expressed to similar
levels as wild type, yielding approximately 3–4 mg of protein
per liter of growth media. Circular dichroism (CD) analysis suggests
that the Glu87 and Cys90 mutants are folded α-helical proteins,
as expected, (
Figure
4
A) and are identical to WT CowN. In contrast, M91A and C-trunc CowN
expressed at lower levels (0.5–1 mg/L of growth media). Most
of M91A and C-trunc CowN was present as a misfolded aggregate; however,
a small amount of monomeric protein could be separated from the aggregate
by gel filtration chromatography. CD spectra of monomeric M91A and
C-trunc CowN indicates that the protein is less folded than wild-type
CowN (
Figure
4
B). This
suggests that residue Met91 is important for CowN stability. However,
the reason for this is unclear. Met91 is not conserved. Structural
modeling suggests that M91A CowN should be nearly identical to WT
CowN (
Figure S2
). CowN has no predicted
post-translational modifications or ligand binding sites that would
depend on Cys90 or Met91, and we did not detect modifications to either
residue by mass spectrometry. Met91 is predicted to point toward the
hydrophobic center of CowN (
Figure S2
).
We therefore hypothesize that it may play a role in stabilizing the
hydrophobic packing between CowN’s α-helices.

To test the role of conserved residue Glu87, we
carried out turnover
experiments with acetylene. These experiments were conducted with
a CO concentration of 0.02 atm, which abolishes approximately 90%
of nitrogenase activity in absence of CowN. Consistent with previous
reports,
10
WT CowN leads to a partial rescue
of activity to 30% of the uninhibited level (
Figure
4
C). E87A, E87D, and E87Q CowN do not protect
nitrogenase from CO. Their levels of protection are within error of
samples that do not have CowN and of an unfolded CowN negative control
(
Figure
4
C). Additionally,
we investigated if E87 CowN mutants are able to recover nitrogenase
activity when they are present at higher concentrations. Recovery
increased slightly when the E87Q CowN concentration was increased
from 2 to 4 μΜ, however, the difference was not statistically
significant at a
p
-value of 0.05 (
Figure S3A
). We also tested if E87A CowN and E87Q CowN were
able to rescue nitrogenase activity under lower CO conditions of 0.001
atm (compared to 0.02 atm of CO in previous experiments) (
Figure S3B,C
). Here too, very little activity
recovery was observed (
Figure S3B
), even
when the CowN concentration was increased to 8 μM
(
Figure S3C
). Together, these experiments
suggest that a mutation of residue E87 is deleterious enough that
it is unlikely to be easily counteracted by increasing the CowN concentration.
Both M91A and C-trunc CowN do not protect nitrogenase from CO inhibition
(
Figure
4
D). This result
was expected given the fact that both mutants are partially unfolded.
In contrast, both C90S and C90A CowN protect nitrogenase to the same
degree as WT CowN (
Figure
4
D). Taken together, these data suggest that CowN function
depends on residue Glu87 and that even mild substitutions to Gln and
Asp are not tolerated. At the same time, the data suggest that the
conserved C-terminal Cys is not required for CO protection.
We hypothesize that the loss of CO protection in the Glu87 mutants
is caused by a decreased affinity for MoFeP, as Glu87 may form a salt
bridge with a positively charged MoFeP residue. To test this hypothesis,
we carried out cross-linking experiments with diazirine labeled MoFeP.
In this experiment, MoFeP is first labeled at Lys residues with a
photoactivatable diazirine cross-linker using NHS-ester chemistry.
The diazirine cross-linker then binds covalently to interacting proteins
under illumination. We had previously used this method to determine
that MoFeP interacts with WT CowN.
10
As
shown in
Figures
5
A
and
5
B, WT CowN and MoFeP form a diazirine-mediated
cross-link band at 70 kDa. A representative MoFeP and CowN peptide
peak from an excised cross-link band that was tryptically digested
and analyzed by mass spectrometry is shown
Figure
5
C. Furthermore, a list of peptides belonging
to WT CowN and MoFeP from the excised cross-link band and a sequence
coverage map are shown in
Tables S1–S3
. The cross-linking band intensity for E87D and E87Q CowN is weaker
than for the wild type, and E87A CowN does not cross-link at all (
Figure
5
A). These data support
the hypothesis that Glu87 is important in mediating protein–protein
interactions with MoFeP.

Although diminished E87D and E87Q CowN binding
to MoFeP was consistent
with the hypothesis that Glu87 is important for CowN function, the
observation that E87A CowN displayed less cross-linking than E87D
and E87Q CowN was surprising since all three mutants are equally inactive.
The results are likely explained by the different experimental conditions
for turnover and cross-linking experiments. The protein concentration
in cross-linking experiments is much higher than in turnover experiments
(1.25 μM vs 0.2 μM for MoFeP and 15 μM vs 2 μM
for CowN). The diazirine cross-linking data suggests that some complex
formation occurs for E87D and E87Q CowN at protein concentrations
of 15 μM CowN but not for E87A CowN. One might therefore expect
partial CO protection to occur at very high E87D and E87Q CowN concentrations.
However, it was not possible for us to test this by carrying out activity
assays at high CowN concentrations since E87D and E87Q CowN could
not be obtained in sufficient quantities.
We also examined cross-linking
of C90A and M91A CowN with diazirine-labeled
MoFeP (
Figure
5
B).
M91A did not cross-link with MoFeP, suggesting that its partially
unfolded state prevents it from interacting with MoFeP. In contrast,
C90A CowN bound to MoFeP, adding further evidence that mutations at
residue Cys90 do not impair CowN function. Taken together, the cross-linking
and turnover data are consistent with each other and suggest that
residue Glu87 is important for CowN function as it is required for
protein–protein interaction with MoFeP.
Aiming to find
the CowN-MoFeP interaction site, we carried out
additional mass spectrometry experiments with different mass detectors
and using methods for finding large peptides; however, we were unable
to find peaks corresponding to cross-linked peptide pairs. The most
likely explanation for the lack of detection is the low abundance
of cross-linked peptide fragments and their large size. A possible
solution to overcome this problem is to utilize a cleavable heterobifunctional
diazirine cross-linker to detect binding sites using MS
3
methods.
23
,
24
However, such cross-linkers are
not yet commercially available.
Glu87 likely enables CowN to
bind to MoFeP through ionic interactions
with positively charged residues on MoFeP. Such electrostatic interactions
may occur at the opening of a putative CO channel between the α
and β subunits, which is lined by four Lys residues in
Gd
-MoFeP (
Figure
1
B).
10
,
18
This potential binding site is
compatible with diazirine cross-linking results since the diazirine
cross-linker is bound to MoFeP at a Lys residue. While the hypothesis
that CowN blocks the mouth of a putative CO channel makes mechanistic
sense, additional work is needed to confirm this site. Furthermore,
it is important to point out that neither the CO nor the N
2
access pathway to FeMoco has been unambiguously determined. There
are several possible gas entry routes,
12
,
18
,
25
,
26
as illustrated in
Figure
1
B, as well as increasing
evidence that substrate binding and release may involve conformational
changes in the region of MoFeP surrounding FeMoco.
27
,
28
We therefore do not rule out the possibility that CowN may bind
near the entrance of a different gas access channel or in the vicinity
of FeMoco.
Conclusions
Overall, the data presented herein suggest
that CowN’s negatively
charged C-terminal is important for CowN binding to MoFeP and protection
against CO inhibition. At the same time, this work raises the question
of the role of the C-terminal Cys. We previously speculated, that
CowN may have additional roles in nitrogen fixation that are unrelated
to CO protection since CowN is upregulated under all nitrogen fixation
conditions, not just when CO is present.
10
Independently, other researchers have implicated CowN in nitrogenase
cold protection.
29
,
30
Our group intends to investigate
CowN further to uncover the full gamut of its roles in bacterial nitrogen
fixation and to determine where it interacts with MoFeP.
