Much of our understanding of
the molecular underpinnings of cellular function and the development
of therapeutics is based on knowledge of protein structure. Complementing
crystallography, NMR, and cryo-electron microscopy are enzymatic and
chemical structure mapping methods often called “footprinting”
that map the accessible surface of macromolecules by introducing modifications
at low doses to yield an ensemble of distributed reaction products
within a predominantly unmodified population of molecules.
1
,
2
The Fenton reaction describes the production of
•
OH by Fe(II)-EDTA-mediated catalytic degradation of H
2
O
2
.
3
,
4
Hydroxyl radical (
•
OH) footprinting using Fe(II)-EDTA-mediated Fenton chemistry was
introduced by Tullius and co-workers to map DNA structure and protein
binding
5
−
7
and has been extensively used to study DNA and RNA
structure, folding, and protein binding (e.g., refs (
8
−
10
)). This approach was extended to equilibrium and time-resolved
methods that report thermodynamic and kinetic transitions.
8
,
11
The widespread adoption of this approach for the analysis of nucleic
acids was facilitated by its utilization of inexpensive reagents and
common laboratory tools.
Protein footprinting approaches create
either reversible or covalent
modification. Reversible methods include hydrogen–deuterium
exchange (HDX)
12
and “protein painting”.
13
Among protein footprinting methods that introduce
covalent modification (e.g., refs (
14
−
18
)) oxidation by the hydroxyl radical (
•
OH) is most
common. Oxidation by
•
OH is promiscuous, with all
side chains but glycine detectable by mass spectrometry (MS).
19
The small Van der Waals volume of
•
OH makes it a fine probe of solvent-accessible surface
9
,
20
even in living cells
21
or 3D tumor spheroids.
22
The preeminent methods of
•
OH generation for
protein oxidative footprinting are synchrotron X-ray radiolysis
19
,
23
and UV laser photolysis of H
2
O
2
.
24
−
27
Other approaches use plasma
28

flash lamp
29

•
OH. These approaches emphasize using a short pulse of
•
OH to minimize the possibility of modification-induced protein structure
perturbation. These approaches also require either access to remote
infrastructure, sending samples to a contract research organization,
or the purchase of costly equipment.
29
The
impetus behind the development of the method detailed in this article
is the belief that a laboratory-based, robust, inexpensive, and straightforward-to-implement
approach to dose-dependent
•
OH generation will facilitate
broader access to protein oxidative footprinting.
That Fenton-generated
•

Materials and Methods
The workflow outlined in
Figure
1
A for an assembly
reaction is common among contemporary
methods of protein oxidative footprinting. Ideally, purified and well-characterized
proteins are analyzed under biologically relevant solution conditions.
In the absence of other experimental constraints, protein concentrations
of ∼1 μg/μL are utilized. Our workflow embraces
the quantitation of dose-dependent oxidation pioneered in synchrotron
radiolytic protein footprinting.
19
,
23
Ligand binding
and complex formation analyses are conducted as comparisons; target
proteins are identically oxidized in the absence and presence of their
ligand.
The key features of our protocol include (i) conducting
dose-dependent
oxidation,
19
,
23
(ii) high reproducibility due
to the concurrent addition of the two Fenton reagents to the samples,
(iii) simultaneously oxidizing a set of samples with a series of 
reagent concentrations (not time) to obtain a dose–response
curve (
Figure
2
, inset),
(iv) determining the efficacy of
•
OH generation
in a particular solution, (v) confirming successful oxidation prior
to initiating MS analysis of proteins, and (vi) use of multiwell plates
to simplify oxidation and subsequent processing of protein samples
(
Figure
2
). We summarize
below the essential steps of our protocol. The
Supporting Information
provides details.
Quantitating
the degradation of a fluorescent dye is a simple and
sensitive way to calibrate the generation of
•
OH
in solution (
Figure
1
C). First, 45 μL of the experimental buffer containing dye
is aliquoted into wells of the 96-well plate. In the experiment shown
in
Figure
1
C, triplicate
samples are oxidized by both Fenton reagents and Fe(II)-EDTA and H
2
O
2
alone (
Figure
2
, inset).
A loaded sample plate is covered and
set aside, while the Fenton
reagents are prepared. A quench stock solution is prepared that contains
thiourea, methioninamide, and catalase.
39
Just prior to initiating the oxidation reactions, working solutions
of the experimental buffer and 300 mM Fe(II)-EDTA and the experimental
buffer and 600 mM H
2
O
2
are prepared. For a full
dose–response series, the working solutions are aliquoted into
the first row of the plate and then serially diluted six times, as
depicted in
Figure
2
. The eighth row is plain buffer. The third column is plain buffer
that is used to maintain constant total volume for the single Fenton
reagent control reactions.
The sample plate is mounted on a
plate shaker. An 8-channel programmable
pipet is used to sequentially draw up 2.5 μL of Fe(II)-EDTA,
a 1.5 μL air gap, and 2.5 μL of H
2
O
2
from the columns containing the serially diluted Fe(II)-EDTA and
H
2
O
2
(
Figure
2
, inset). The reagents are purged into a sample column,
and the plate shaker is briefly pulsed. 50 μL of the quench
solution is drawn up into the eight tips of a multichannel pipet and
at 2 min, expelled into the samples. Residual H
2
O
2
is degraded by catalase in the quench solution. This process is
repeated for each sample column in the experiment. Oxidized samples
whose fluorescence is to be quantitated are transferred to an optical
plate and analyzed. Protein samples can be stored at −80 °C
or immediately processed, as described below.
The processing
of the oxidized proteins for analysis for MS utilizes
standard protocols, except that the Fe-EDTA is removed by solid phase
extraction. A complete description of the preparation and analysis
of oxidized protein samples is presented in the
Supporting Information
. In silico analysis of the protein
sequence aids the selection of the protease(s) that will best facilitate
the analysis of oxidized residues. The LC-MS analysis protocols are
instrument- and system-specific; we provide the protocols for our
studies. The detection and quantitation of oxidation is facilitated
in the Protein Metrics software.
38
For
mapping of a binding interface, the extent of oxidation of the free
protein is compared with that of a complex. The preparation of the
protein samples analyzed herein is described in the
Supporting Information
.

Dose-Dependent Oxidation of a Benchmark Protein, Lysozyme
We analyzed the oxidation of a small monomeric protein to demonstrate
the efficacy of our method and provide a benchmark system for the
use of our approach. In intact lysozyme, we observed
•
OH dose-dependent decrease in the unmodified protein and a proportional
increase in oxidized forms (
Figure S2A
).
The charge state distribution does not dramatically change with increasing
oxidation demonstrating that mild oxidation does not unfold the protein
(
Figure S2B
). Degradation products indicative
of peptide backbone cleavage are not observed. The dose-dependent
fraction of unmodified and oxidized protein closely matches fluorescent
dye degradation demonstrating that the dye assay is a valid proxy
for quantitating protein oxidation (
Figure S2C
).
The tryptic peptides of lysozyme (
Figure S3
) are similarly oxidized in a dose-dependent manner (
Figure
1
D). Only one peptide,
15–21, is not detectably modified. Analysis of individual residues
shows that 23 individual residues are detectably oxidized (
Figure
3
A). The
•
OH dose–response curves determined for individual residues
are precise and consistent over the entire range of doses (
Figure
3
B–G). This
observation supports the conclusion drawn from intact protein analysis
that mild oxidation does not perturb the native fold.
Since
Fe(II)-EDTA-mediated Fenton chemistry has not been extensively
used for protein oxidative footprinting,
31
,
41
we searched the data sets using a complex list of modifications
that have been observed using radiolytic or photolytic generation
of
•
OH. The modifications we detect are those previously
reported using these methods (
Table S1
).
19
,
23
,
24
,
42
The most common modification is the formation of hydroxyl group
formation (+15.9949). Mono- and dioxidation of tryptophan and mono-oxidation
of methionine are the most abundant residue modifications (
Table S2
).
The oxidation of individual
lysozyme residues was compared to the
accessible surface area (ASA) calculated by GETAREA
43
from PDB:
1E8L
. Two of the three phenylalanine (F) residues are within identified
peptides. F34 oxidation is 8 times greater than F38, proportional
to their respective ASA values of 61 and 7 Å (
Figure
3
B). All six tryptophan (W)
residues are within identified peptides. The three detectably oxidized
W residues also are oxidized proportionally to ASA: W62 (119 Å)
> W123 (76 Å) > W28 (0 Å) (
Figure
3
C). The three W residues not detectably oxidized
have ASA values ranging from 7 to 36 Å. W63 (36 Å) is adjacent
to W62; its oxidation may be suppressed by the solvent accessibility
of its neighbor. W108 and W111 are spatially close to M105, which
is reactive even though its ASA is zero, as is M12 (
Figure
3
F). Oxidation of buried methionine
and quenching of the oxidation of proximal residues by methionine
and tryptophan has been observed with all approaches to protein oxidative
footprinting.
18
All eight cysteine
(C) residues are disulfide-bonded and solvent-inaccessible
in the structure. Although C residues are intrinsically reactive,
six of the eight are not oxidized, which is consistent with their
inaccessibility. Of the remaining two residues, C30 is barely oxidized.
Although C76 is detectably oxidized, its disulfide partner C94 is
not (
Figure
3

Two solvent-accessible leucine residues (L75 and L84) are oxidized,
although the 2-fold difference in their ASA is not reflected in their
reactivity (
Figure
3

I98 (ASA = 8) are not detectably oxidized, consistent with their inaccessibility.
In contrast, the three isoleucine residues whose oxidation is detected

Figure

Overall, there is consistency between
levels of residue oxidation
and the ASA calculated from the structure. The observed differences

Mapping a Monobody Epitope
A common task in biomedical
research and pharmaceutical development is mapping protein interfaces.
To demonstrate the efficacy of our method at this task, we mapped
the epitope of monobody NS1 on its target HRAS; this complex is stable
and structurally characterized.
44
Precise
•
OH dose-dependent oxidation is achieved (
Figure S4A,B
). Protection of HRAS due to monobody
binding is revealed by the ratio of the percent total free HRAS oxidized
divided by the oxidation in the complex; ratios greater than 1 indicate
protection. Peptide 136–147 is most protected from oxidation
and thus is expected to contain residues within the macromolecular
interface (
Figure S4C
).
Of the 189
HRAS residues, 130 are within detected peptides, and of those, 32
are oxidized (
Figure
4

and 4 of the 9 tyrosine (Y) residues. The oxidation ratio of free
to complex shows that the most highly protected residues in the complex
are within peptide 136–147 (
Figure S4C
). We used these “hotspots” to focus the time-intensive
single amino acid analysis to residues 136–147 as this region
is likely involved in a molecular interface. This analysis shows that
residues P140, Y141, and I142 are protected in the complex (

Higher-order structure (HOS) characterization is important in the
development of monoclonal antibody (mAb) therapeutics in quality control
and in assessing biosimilarity.
45
To demonstrate
the efficacy of our method to map the ASA of a mAb, we conducted a
peptide-level analysis of the ∼150 kDa NIST IgG1k mAb standard
(NIST Reference Antibody RM 8671). While a solved full structure of
the NIST mAb is not available,
46
−
48
the antibody’s surface
has been mapped by radiolytic protein oxidative footprinting.
16
We used separate trypsin and pepsin digestions
to increase the sequence coverage and refine the sequence resolution
in this comparison. This is a rapid way to identify an oxidation “hotspot”
using just peptide-level analysis.
The NIST mAb peptides oxidize
in a
•

and HRAS (
Figure S5

of pepsin peptide quantitation is due to overlapping peptides resulting
from this enzyme’s lesser specificity.
49
A heat map shows comparable NIST mAb oxidation by radiolysis and
Fenton chemistry (
Figure
6

of the light chain are highly oxidized and no oxidation is detected

Discussion
Protein oxidative footprinting is a versatile
in-solution approach
to the structural analysis of proteins and protein complexes.
19
,
23
,
24
,
29
Our paper describes a readily deployable and reliable approach to
dose-dependent chemical generation of
•
OH that is
distinct from published studies.
30
−
32
A key feature of our
method is that the samples that constitute a dose response are oxidized
simultaneously rather than one by one. (
Figure
2
, inset). These simple innovations confer
experimental advantages: (i) Precise and reproducible
•
OH dose–response measures are achieved by combining the simplified
Fenton cocktail of only Fe(II)-EDTA and H
2
O
2
with consistent reagent delivery (
Figure S2C
). Measures of dose-dependent oxidation are likely to become the
primary reporter of side-chain accessibility as the field moves toward
methodological harmonization and validation. (ii) A complete 96-well
sample plate is oxidized in 15–20 min enhancing experimental
throughput, decreasing the time a set of protein samples sit in a
plate and ensuring that the proteins within each dose sit for an identical
length of time. In contrast, oxidizing 96 samples one by one using
Fe(II)-EDTA/H
2
O
2
/ascorbate (e.g., ref (
52
)) would take hours. This
length of time exceeds the stability of the Fe(II)-EDTA reagent (
Figure S1I
) and that of ascorbate
32
and likely the endurance of all but the hardiest
scientist. (iii) We have demonstrated that chemical generation of
•
OH is compatible with a range of solutes and conditions
commonly used to analyze proteins (
Figure S1
). (iv) Our approach lends itself to robotic implementation that
can further enhance experimental throughput, the precision of dose
delivery, and reduction of oxidation times (Paukner and Brenowitz,
unpublished results).
Since oxidation causes covalent modifications,
samples can be stored
for later analysis, and the entire workflow from sample preparation
to LC-MS analysis is consistent with general bottom-up proteomics
analysis. Thus, no modifications to the workflow or instrument configuration
are required for quantitative protein oxidation. Quantitating the
degradation of a fluorescent dye is a proxy for protein oxidation,
which allows new project protocols to be quickly developed. Conducting
the dye assay along with protein samples confirms the success of the
oxidation reaction prior to the initial protein MS analysis.
Robust dose-dependent oxidation is observed at the intact protein,
peptide, and residue levels. Our analysis of a commercially available
protein, lysozyme, provides a benchmark against which new users can
test the implementation of the protocol. Quantitation precision slightly
decreases at the residue level due to the low abundance of some oxidized
peptide species. The predominant modifications detected are hydroxyl
(+15.9949) formation. At a higher reagent dose, low abundance modifications
specific to histidine and tryptophan are observed consistent with
the modifications reported for radiolysis.
19
Modifications indicative of radicals other than
•
OH are not observed consistent with studies showing the oxidation
of Fe(II)-EDTA by H
2
O
2
at pH 8.2 generates
•
OH as the sole product.
37
Our protocol combines the determination of the
•
OH dose–response
19
,
23
with triplicate measurements
at each dose.
24
−

The companion program Byos generates extracted ion chromatograms
for all search results and integrates the area of all peptides for
all of the MS files. The chromatograms and MS2 spectra are viewed
in an interface, allowing rapid validation and adjustment of the integration
area for individual raw files and specific oxidation isoforms. This
visualization facilitates a rapid residue-level resolution footprinting
analysis. Using these semiautomatic data analysis and visualization
tools, peptide data search and analysis can typically be completed
within a day.
