Introduction
Traditionally, bioinformaticians searched for open reading frames (ORFs) limiting the annotation of proteins to a certain length. Therefore, small proteins comprising less than 100 amino acids (aa) have often been overlooked in bacterial genome annotations. The first surprise was that a handful of seemingly noncoding small regulatory RNAs—subsequently termed dual-function sRNAs—carry small ORFs that are translated into small proteins with a function in the same or a different pathway as/than the encoding sRNA (Gimpel and Brantl
2017
). About a decade ago, researchers started to investigate whole peptidomes systematically, and a broad variety of small proteins was discovered. Meanwhile, even small ORFs translated upstream or downstream of annotated ORFs or from alternative start codons within known ORFs were identified (Storz et al.
2014
). Nevertheless, in the majority of bacterial species, only a few small proteins have been characterized in depth. In the following, we will include proteins with a length up to 80 aa.
Small proteins have been found to interact with RNA, larger proteins or even metal ions (Orr et al.
2020
) or are embedded in the membrane where some of them act as toxins of type I TA systems. In this review, we will summarize our current knowledge on small proteins from Gram-positive bacteria with a special emphasis on
Bacillus subtilis
. We will not discuss antimicrobial peptides, which are described elsewhere (e.g. Ganesan et al.
2023
, Puan et al.
2023
).
Small proteins binding RNA include the general RNA chaperones Hfq and CsrA as well as
B. subtilis
FbpB, which might act exclusively on a single sRNA, FsrA, to help maintain iron homeostasis. Hfq, an RNA chaperone playing an important role in Gram-negative bacteria (Kavita et al.
2018
) is not expressed in all Gram-positives and does not play a decisive role in sRNA/mRNA interactions in Gram-positive bacteria. By contrast, CsrA promotes the interaction of the sRNA SR1 with its target,
ahrC
mRNA (Müller et al.
2019
), but also inhibits translation of
hag
mRNA encoding the major flagellin (Yakhnin et al.
2007
). Recently, KH domain proteins like KhpA came into focus as potential RNA chaperone (Olejniczak et al.
2022
).
Cytosolic small proteins in
B. subtilis
interact with other proteins, e.g. Sda, SR1P, and SR7P, and some, like MciZ, can disrupt large protein complexes. Sda inhibits KinA, the major kinase in the phosphorelay that regulates sporulation. MciZ binds to the C-terminal polymerization interface of FtsZ, blocks the assembly of higher order FtsZ structures, and thus inhibits cell division (Ul Haq et al.
2020
). SR1P and SR7P modulate RNA degradation by interacting with the glycolytic enzymes GapA and enolase, respectively, which moonlight as scaffolding components of the RNA degradosome (Ul Haq and Brantl
2021
). Other small proteins influence sporulation and spore morphogenesis in
B. subtilis
whereas small acid soluble spore proteins (SASPs) protect endospores from various deleterious conditions (Setlow
2006
).
Mycobacterium tuberculosis
KdpF binds the histidine kinase KdpD, and its overexpression reduces intramacrophage replication (Gannoun-Zaki et al.
2013
).
Representatives for membrane-located proteins are toxins of type I TA systems that affect the membrane potential, or—like
B. subtilis
BsrG and
Enterococcus faecalis
Fst—interfere with cell division.
These randomly selected examples alone show that the search for novel small proteins and their intensive characterization not only expand our knowledge of gene expression control in Gram-positive bacteria but help to elucidate new communication networks among the main components in bacterial cells.
Membrane-bound small proteins that act as toxins of type I TA systems
Toxin–antitoxin (TA) systems comprise a stable toxin the ectopic overproduction of which either kills cells or confers growth stasis, and an unstable antitoxin that neutralizes the toxin action (Brantl and Jahn
2015
, Jurenas et al.
2022
). In type I TA systems, an RNA antitoxin interacts with the toxin mRNA that encodes a hydrophobic small protein of 25–60 aa, which is inserted into the membrane (Table
1
). Toxins of type I TA systems have an identical predicted or experimentally confirmed secondary structure containing an α-helical transmembrane domain and are supposed to be localized in the membrane (e.g. Göbl et al.
2010
, Sayed et al.
2012
). It has been proposed that they can interact with each other to create pore-like structures that destroy the membrane potential leading to an inhibition of ATP synthesis (Brantl and Jahn
2015
). Since deletion of the antitoxin gene does not always yield phenotypical effects, information about the mechanism of toxin action and its cellular target(s) is—despite many years of research—still limited and frequently based on overexpression experiments, where the majority of the toxins entail cell death. In a number of type I TA systems from Gram-negative bacteria, overexpression of toxin genes leads to destruction of the membrane potential or the membrane itself (Brantl and Jahn
2015
). However, this is not always true for toxins from Gram-positive species. In addition, interactions of toxins with other cellular targets cannot be excluded.

In
B. subtilis
, 14 type I TA systems have been predicted (Durand et al.
2012
) and four of them have been confirmed and characterized in great detail:
txpA
/RatA,
bsrG
/SR4,
bsrE
/SR5, and
yonT
/as-yonT (Brantl and Müller

). So far, the other systems have not been shown experimentally to cause cell death upon antitoxin gene deletion or toxin overproduction. Whereas in both
txpA
/RatA and
bsrG
/SR4 deletion of the antitoxin gene allows toxin expression, which causes cell lysis on agar plates and growth arrest in growing cultures (Silvaggi et al.
2005
, Jahn et al.
2012
), in
bsrE
/SR5, only toxin
bsrE
overexpression leads to cell lysis on agar plates and impairs growth in liquid media (Müller et al.
2016
). In the case of
yonT
/
yoyJ
-as-yonT, it was neither possible to delete the antitoxin gene in the
B. subtilis
chromosome nor to overexpress
yonT
in either
Escherichia coli
or
B. subtilis
, even under a tightly regulated promoter, due to the high toxicity of YonT (Reif et al.
2018
). By contrast, the second toxin of this triple system, YoyJ, is extremely weakly expressed, and only transformation experiments revealed that the
yoyJ
gene could be only established in the
B. subtilis
chromosome in the presence of the antitoxin, i.e. supported that it is indeed a toxin (Reif et al.
2018
). The only
B. subtilis
toxin, for which the mode of action was elucidated, is BsrG comprising 38 aa: It neither dissipates the membrane potential nor affects cellular ATP levels, but strongly interferes with cell envelope biosynthesis, causes membrane invaginations together with delocalization of the cell wall synthesis machinery. Furthermore, it triggers autolysis and inhibits protein biosynthesis efficiently (Jahn et al.
2015
, Fig.
1A
). Mechanisms of action were neither investigated for the 59 aa TxpA, although it was hypothesized to affect the membrane potential (Silvaggi et al.
2005
), nor for the 30 aa BsrE or the 58 aa YonT.
Many
B. subtilis
toxin genes (
txpA, yonT, bsrG, bsrE
, and
bsrH
) are located on prophages and prophage-like regions and are proposed to be required for maintenance of these elements, reminiscent of PSK (plasmid-encoded postsegregational killing systems) (Gerdes and Wagner
2007
). In particular,
txpA
/RatA was suggested to sustain the
skin
element, which is excised from the genome by a DNA rearrangement during sporulation (Durand et al.
2012
). The removal of
skin
which interrupts the
sigK
gene allows the reconstitution of the gene encoding the sigma factor required for mother-cell-specific gene expression in late sporulation. Rapid toxin synthesis under defined conditions is ensured by transcription of all
B. subtilis
TA modules under control of the vegetative sigma factor σ
A
. Consequently, the identification of transcriptional activators that might alter the equilibrium between toxin and antitoxin gene expression could hint at the function of the individual toxins.
In
E. faecalis
, the plasmid pAD1-encoded type I TA system
fstI
/RNAII—a typical PSK required for plasmid maintenance—has been studied for many years, and later, chromosomal homologues have been discovered, too (Weaver
2012
). Overexpression of the plasmid-encoded
fst
gene encoding the 33 aa toxin Fst from
E. faecalis
did not only cause toxicity in its original host, but also in
Staphylococcus aureus
and
B. subtilis
as well as other bacilli (Weaver et al.
2003
,
2009
, Patel and Weaver
2006
). Even in
E. coli
, Fst was toxic after deletion of the 5′ stem-loop of the toxin mRNA (Shokeen et al.
2008
) suggesting the lack of a host-specific Fst target. In all analyzed species, condensation of the nucleoid was detected as the primary event. Furthermore, in both
B. subtilis
and
E. coli
, cell elongation was observed which was due to inhibition of cell division, while in
S. aureus
, division septa started to form but remained uncompleted. By contrast,
fst
overproduction in
E. faecalis
resulted in misplaced septa yielding daughter cells with a few or no DNA molecules, typical for a segregation defect. However, an increase of membrane permeability—as observed for membrane-bound toxins of Gram-negative bacteria—turned out to be a secondary effect, being the result of ongoing division and/or the impaired segregation (Patel and Weaver
2006
). A few years later it was demonstrated that the overproduction of Fst induces the upregulation of energy-requiring membrane pumps (Brinkman et al.
2013
). Cells could be rescued from Fst toxicity by chemically inhibiting these pumps (Fig.
1B
). An Fst resistant mutant unable to induce these pumps was localized to the
rpoC
gene encoding the RNA polymerase β' subunit. The authors concluded that exposure of cells to Fst has a cascading effect: it first causes membrane stress and subsequently, overexpression of certain transporters potentiate these effects (Brinkman et al.
2013
).
Enterococcus faecalis
produces in addition to the plasmid pAD1-encoded Fst
pAD1
the chromosome-encoded toxin Fst
EF0409
. A difference in two aa between both Fst toxins results in a differential transcriptional host response to the two toxins (Holmes et al.
2021
). Furthermore, a transporter protein genetically liked to the chromosomal version of the type I TA system limits the toxicity of Fst.

In the human pathogen
S. aureus
, a number of type I TA systems have been identified within pathogenicity islands (
sprA1
/SprA1AS,
sprA2
/SprA2AS, and
sprG1
/SprF1) as well as in the core genome (
sprG2
/SprF2,
sprG3
/SprF3, and
sprG4
/SprF4), raising the question on the redundancy and possible cross-regulation between these systems (Pinel-Marie et al.
2014
, Germain-Amiot et al.
2019
, Riffaud et al.
2019
).
For years it was speculated if toxins of type I TA systems might have extracellular effects, similar to those of antimicrobial peptides.
S. aureus
toxin SprA1 (30–33 aa) that belongs to the
fst
family (Fozo et al.
2010
) was reported to behave not only as bactericidal type I toxin in staphylococci but also, when applied as chemically synthesized molecule, to kill other bacteria like
E. coli
or
B. subtilis
in a mixed cell population and to lyse human erythrocytes (Sayed et al.
2011
). This would indicate that it might outcompete other
S. aureus
strains or other species and that it is released from the membrane upon infection and could act on human host cells. The latter properties would be contradictory to a toxin of a
bona fide
TA system, which should be only directed against its own cell.
In 2010, the solution structure of plasmid-encoded
E. faecalis
Fst was solved (Göbl et al.
2010
). Fst forms a membrane binding α-helix in the N-terminal part comprising aa 4–26 and contains an intrinsically disordered region near the C-terminus. It binds in a transmembrane orientation with the C-terminus likely pointing towards the cytosol. When the authors applied their chemically synthesized Fst to
B. subtilis, E. coli
, and human erythrocytes it showed neither antimicrobial nor haemolytic activity (Göbl et al.
2010
). However, despite structural similarity between
E. faecalis
Fst and
S. aureus
SprA1, their primary aa sequences differ: in contrast to Fst, SprA1 contains a central cysteine residue located at a flexible hinge as shown by NMR (Sayed et al.
2012
), which led to the assumption it might trigger dimerization (Sayed et al.
2012
). Therefore, the different extracellular effects of both toxins might be due to their diverging aa sequences.
Recently, 13 potential type I TA systems, eight of them prophage associated, the other CRISPR associated, were discovered in
Clostridium difficile
(now renamed
as Clostridioides difficile
). The hypothetical toxins share common properties with
B. subtilis
toxins of type I TA systems because they are all small hydrophobic proteins comprising 34–59 aa with a transmembrane domain and a charged C-terminal aa region, and the majority of them are located in prophage regions suggesting a role in prophage stability (Soutourina
2019
, Kreis and Soutourina
2022
, Peltier et al.
2020
). In
C. difficile
, σ
B
-dependent promoters were identified in the regulatory regions of the majority of toxin and antitoxin genes (Soutourina
2019
). New data on the coregulation of type I TA and CRISPR–Cas systems via the general stress response σ
B
in
C. difficile
could be relevant for responses to stresses encountered by this pathogen inside the human host (Maikova et al.
2018
). Interestingly, the depletion of the RNA chaperone Hfq resulted in a moderate destabilization of the CD0977.1-toxin mRNA and antitoxin RCd11-RNA as well as in a minor decrease in the half-life of antitoxin RCd9 and RCd10 in
C. difficile
(Maikova et al.
2018
, Peltier et al.
2020
). As we also observed a stabilization of the RNA antitoxin SR5 in the absence of Hfq, which had, however, no impact on the TA system
in vivo
(Müller et al.
2016
), it remains to be seen what is the impact of Hfq on toxicity in
C. difficile
.
The role of plasmid-encoded type I TA systems is evident: they are PSK systems, i.e. in plasmid-free cells, the short-lived antitoxin is rapidly degraded and thus, cannot prevent toxin expression anymore, whereas the stable toxin mRNA is translated, eventually causing cell death. This scenario has been verified for the plasmid pAD1-encoded
E. faecalis fst
/RNAII (Weaver
2012
, Clewell et al.
2014
).
Some chromosome encoded type I TA systems are induced under certain metabolic or oxygen stress conditions, and the function of their toxins could be to cause bacteriostasis to confine nutrient consumption or oxygen depletion (see Durand et al.
2012
). For instance,
txpA
mRNA expression seems to be phosphate dependent, and
txpA, bsrG
, and
yonT
RNA levels increase upon glucose exhaustion, linking these toxins to the metabolic state of the cell (Durand et al.
2012
). Interestingly,
B. subtilis
BsrG could play a role in response to temperature changes, as the half-life of
bsrG
mRNA is temperature-dependent, and the mRNA is rapidly degraded upon temperature shock at 48 or 55°C by RNases Y and J1 (Jahn et al.
2012
, Jahn and Brantl
2016
). By contrast, BsrE might play a role under anaerobic conditions, because an anaerobic shock altered the ratio between SR5 and
bsrE
mRNA in favour of the toxin mRNA (Müller et al.
2016
).
Small proteins that act as RNA chaperones
RNA chaperones
RNA chaperones are proteins that bind RNA and influence RNA structure and/or stability. Consequently, they can impact bacterial gene regulation by affecting mRNA translation as well as the regulatory functions of sRNAs. The majority of bacteria encode two major RNA chaperones composed of about 60–100 aa, Hfq and CsrA, which were investigated in detail (Kavita et al.
2018
, Vakulskas et al.
2015
). In
E. coli
and
Salmonella
, 20%–25% of the genome is regulated by Hfq, which is not the case in Gram-positive species. Recently, it was proposed that the presence of RNase J1, a 5′–3′ exoribonuclease, might have been a significant barrier to the evolution of 3′ UTR derived sRNAs in
B. subtilis
and
S. aureus
to present RNA ligands available to Hfq and other chaperones, depreciating their function in these model Firmicutes (Mediati et al.
2021
).
Hfq
In the majority of Gram-positive bacteria, the RNA chaperone Hfq does not seem to play a significant role. In
B. subtilis
, only about 150 transcripts could be identified that bind Hfq (Dambach et al.
2013
) and even less displayed a different abundance in
hfq
knockout compared to wild-type strains (Hämmerle et al.
2014
). The crystal structures of
S. aureus
Hfq (Schumacher et al.
2002
) and that of
B. subtilis
Hfq (Someya et al.
2012
) have been solved. Although the structures of
B. subtilis
and
E. coli
Hfq are similar,
E. coli
Hfq prefers binding to ARN-repeats and
B. subtilis
Hfq to AG-repeats instead (Someya et al.
2012
). Whereas in a multitude of Gram-negative bacteria, in particular in
E. coli
and
Salmonella enterica
, it has been demonstrated that Hfq can stabilize sRNAs and/or promote the interaction with their target RNAs, so far only one such case has been reported in Gram-positive bacteria:
Listeria monocytogenes
Hfq binds the sRNA LhrA, which controls translation and degradation of its target
lmo850
mRNA by an antisense mechanism. Thereby, Hfq facilitates the LhrA/
lmo850
mRNA interaction (Nielsen et al.
2010
).
In
B. subtilis
, by contrast, no growth differences between a wild-type and an isogenic
hfq
-deficient
B. subtilis
strain were detected in a screen with more than 2000 different growth conditions (Rochat et al.
2015
). The only known phenotypes in the absence of
hfq
were a decreased long-term survival of cells in stationary phase, independent of sporulation (Rochat et al.
2015
) and a significantly reduced motility and chemotaxis (Jagtap et al.
2016
). Notably, a number of
B. subtilis
type I toxin mRNAs as well as the corresponding antitoxins were bound by Hfq (Dambach et al.
2013
) and were less abundant in its absence (Hämmerle et al.
2014
). However, growth or survival were not impacted (Rochat et al.
2015
). Similarly, Hfq had no effect on the type I TA system
bsrE
/SR5, although it stabilizes the RNA antitoxin SR5 (Müller et al.
2016
). The expression of
hfq
is upregulated in the stationary phase and under several stress conditions (Dambach et al.
2013
, Rochat et al.
2015
, Jagtap et al.
2016
). Together this rather implies a role for Hfq as a general fine-tuning regulator in the stationary phase than as a globally acting RNA–RNA matchmaker in
B. subtilis
. Some time ago, we found that Hfq binds
in vitro
near the SD sequence of
ahrC
mRNA, refolds the 5′ UTR to allow access to the 30S SU and is required for
ahrC
translation
in vivo
(Fig.
2
, left). However, Hfq is dispensable for the control of
ahrC
translation by the sRNA SR1 (Heidrich et al.
2007
). This confirms that Hfq can act as RNA chaperone in
B. subtilis
, but its role may be restricted to mRNAs.

In
C. difficile
, Hfq might play a more important role than in

(Boudry et al.

In vitro
, RCd1 binds
CD1234

spoIIID
mRNA encoding the transcriptional regulator of σ
E
and σ

(Boudry et al.
2021
). RCd1 was less abundant in an
hfq
depletion strain (Boudry et al.
2014
) but was not stabilized by Hfq (Boudry et al.

). Although Hfq could bind RCd1 and its proposed
CD1234
or
spoIIID
target mRNAs
in vitro
efficiently with Kd values in the nanomolar range, it did not promote the sRNA/target mRNA complex formation (Boudry et al.
2021
). Future research will show if Hfq can facilitate sRNA/mRNA interactions in
C. difficile
or if it mainly binds mRNAs to affect their translation—like in the case of
B. subtilis ahrC
mRNA—or perhaps their stability.
In
S. aureus
, Hfq is involved in the regulation of pathogenicity, the oxidative stress response, and other cellular processes in some strains. In total, 16 genes were influenced in a
hfq
mutant in a transcriptomics study, and 49 mRNAs could specifically bind Hfq, much less than in
E. coli
. Moreover, only a few
S. aureus
strains encode Hfq (Liu et al.
2010
). The best-studied
S. aureus
regulatory RNA RNAIII (Bronesky et al.
2016
) does not need Hfq to exerts its inhibitory or activating effects.
CsrA
CsrA (carbon storage regulator protein A) has been discovered in
E. coli
in 1993 as regulator of glycogen biosynthesis, and later shown to be encoded in the majority of Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria where it can bind hundreds of RNAs. It is a small (60–70 aa) RNA chaperone that binds at GGA motifs in single-stranded or loop regions in the 5′ UTR of mRNAs. In many Gram-negative bacteria, CsrA and its Rsm homologues have been shown to inhibit or activate translation, prevent the access of endoribonucleases or facilitate Rho-dependent transcription termination by unfolding mRNAs (Vakulskas et al.
2015
). In 2005, the crystal structure of
Pseudomonas putida
CsrA (Rife et al.
2005
), in 2007 the NMR structure of a RsmA/
hcnE
mRNA complex (Schubert et al.
2007
) and in 2016, that of
B. subtilis
CsrA (Altegoer et al.
2016
) have been solved. CsrA proteins are homodimers of five intertwined β-strands of each polypeptide and protruding wing-like α-helices. Residues critical for RNA binding reside in the β1 and β5 strands of opposing polypeptides.
Although CsrA/Rsm molecules of Gram-negative bacteria and
B. subtilis
CsrA are structurally similar and share their binding preference, until 2019, only one major function for
B. subtilis
CsrA (74 aa) had been discovered and studied in detail (Yakhnin et al.
2007
): CsrA can bind
hag
mRNA encoding the major flagellin and is involved in a regulatory circuit that controls the Hag concentration: At low intracellular Hag concentrations, CsrA is sequestered by the FliW protein, and when Hag concentrations increase, FliW switches to Hag, releasing CsrA which in turn can bind
hag
mRNA near the RBS to inhibit further Hag synthesis (Mukherjee et al.
2011
,
2016
). Thereby, FliW allosterically antagonizes CsrA in a noncompetitive manner by excluding the 5′ UTR from the CsrA-RNA binding site (Altegoer et al.
2016
). Whereas CsrA and its homologues in Gram-negative bacteria can be sequestered by small RNAs (in
E. coli
CsrB and CsrC) presenting numerous GGA motifs in stem-loop structures to prevent CsrA binding to mRNA 5′ UTRs (Vakulskas et al.
2015
), no such competing sRNAs have been found in Gram-positive bacteria.
Recently, we discovered that CsrA can promote the interaction between an sRNA and its target mRNA (Müller et al.
2019
), which was previously only shown for Hfq.
Bacillus subtilis
CsrA is able to refold
ahrC
mRNA encoding the transcription activator of the arginine catabolic
rocABC
and
rocDEF
operons to promote the binding of its base-pairing regulatory sRNA SR1 (Fig.
2
, right). CsrA binds to both SR1 and
ahrC
mRNA with Kd values in the nanomolar range, but neither affects their stability nor translation of
ahrC
mRNA or any of the
roc
operon mRNAs directly. This is a novel function that had not been found for CsrA in Gram-negative species. Surprisingly, our assumption that CsrA might be also involved in the interaction of SR1 with its second target,
kinA
mRNA encoding the major histidine kinase in sporulation (Ul Haq et al.
2021b
), was not confirmed: although CsrA bound
kinA
mRNA in EMSAs (electrophoretic mobility shift assays), it was neither able to facilitate the
kinA
mRNA/SR1 interaction, nor to affect the half-life or translation of
kinA
mRNA or impact sporulation efficiency. This makes a more global role of CsrA in sRNA regulation in
B. subtilis
less likely.
FbpA
Three small basic proteins, FbpA (54 aa), FbpB (48 aa), and FbpC (29 aa) predicted to be RNA chaperones, were discovered to be involved in the regulation of the
B. subtilis
sRNA FsrA (Gaballa et al.
2008
). All three of them are regulated by the iron-dependent Fur repressor. Later, for FbpB, a concerted action with FsrA in the regulation of
lutABC
mRNA was demonstrated (Smaldone et al.
2012
): in the presence of FsrA and FbpB,
lutABC
RNA levels were about twofold increased, and FbpB had a more significant effect. Only modest effects on the protein levels of LutA, LutB, and LutC were measured. The authors proposed that FbpB might either promote the FsrA–
lutABC
mRNA interaction or recruit an RNase for the degradation of
lutABC
mRNA. So far, these hypotheses were not substantiated experimentally.
KhpA
Recently, so-called KH-domain proteins came into focus as potential RNA chaperones. KH domains are known to bind RNA and have been found in eukaryotic, but also bacterial proteins of diverse functions, e.g. RNase Y, transcription elongation factor NusA and ribosomal protein S3 (Olejniczak et al.
2022
). KH proteins were found in Grad-seq experiments performed for Gram-positive bacteria to be associated with mRNAs, tRNAs and sRNAs (e.g. Zheng et al.
2017
, Hör et al.
2020
, Lamm-Schmidt et al.
2021
). Two examples are KhpA and KhpB that are prevalent in Firmicutes and Actinobacteria, but not encoded in the genomes of α, β, or γ-proteobacteria like
E. coli
or
S. enterica
. This makes them particularly interesting, as they are also encoded in species that lack an
hfq
gene (e.g. Streptococci). KhpA (KH-domain protein A) only consists of a single KH domain, can comprise between 62 and 149 aa (in
B. subtilis
82 aa) and was initially identified as protein involved in
Streptococcus pneumoniae
cell elongation (Zheng et al.
2017
). By contrast, KhpB is larger (208 aa in
B. subtilis
) and comprises in addition to the KH domains one or more other domains. Streptococcal—and possibly also clostridial—KhpA and KhpB form heterdimers, and both proteins bind to an overlapping set of sRNAs and mRNAs
in vivo
(Zheng et al.
2017
, Lamm-Schmidt et al.
2021
). In
S. pneumoniae
, KhpA/B were bound by 170 RNAs (Zheng et al.
2017
), in
C. difficile
, Flag-tagged KhpB coeluted 1400 RNAs, mostly full mRNAs and full operons, but also 12 sRNAs (Lamm-Schmidt et al.
2021
). A total of eight of these 12 sRNAs also bound Hfq. In
S. pneumoniae
, the 5′ UTR of
ftsAZ
mRNA is required for KhpA/KhpB-dependent downregulation of FtsA levels, and either direct downregulation or indirect regulation through an sRNA chaperoned by the KH proteins were proposed. However, the role of the KH domains in this process is still unclear (Zheng et al.
2017
). Recently, KhpA was also shown to be critical for proper cell elongation in
Lactobacillus plantarum
(Myrbråten et al.
2019
).
More research on KhpA and KhpB is needed. RNA binding by both proteins has to be demonstrated in EMSAs, and it has to be investigated whether or not they can facilitate the interaction between sRNAs and their complementary target mRNAs. It has to be studied if both proteins always act together and if they exert different functions in different Gram-positive species.
In contrast to Gram-negative bacteria, where one of the two RNA chaperones, Hfq or ProQ (Holmqvist et al.
2020
), is involved in sRNA/target RNA regulation, this does not seem to be the case in Gram-positive bacteria. It is more likely that different RNA chaperones might be employed in different Gram-positive species. Future research will reveal the variety of these small RNA binding proteins.

Bacillus subtilis
Sda interacts with KinA and affects signal transduction
A notable example of small proteins situated in the inner membrane that engage with transmembrane sensor kinases is represented by the 46-aa Sda protein from
B. subtilis
. Sda binds to and inhibits KinA, which serves as the initial histidine kinase in the phosphorelay pathway responsible for regulating sporulation in
B. subtilis
(Burkholder et al.
2001
). During periods of nutrient scarcity and environmental stress, the kinases KinA and KinB undergo autophosphorylation and subsequently transfer their phosphate groups through the proteins Spo0F and Spo0B to the central sporulation regulator Spo0A.
The
sda
gene was discovered in a mutant strain with a suppressed
dnaA1
mutation, allowing it to sporulate. The promoter region of the
sda
gene contains multiple binding sites for the DnaA protein, and mutations in these regions or in the
dnaA
gene itself were found to impact the
sda
expression (Burkholder et al.
2001
). Structural investigations have revealed that Sda consists of a monomeric structure characterized by two α-helices interconnected by an interhelix loop, forming a helical hairpin (Rowland et al.
2004
). The specific interaction surface between Sda and KinA has been elucidated. Sda utilizes a hydrophobic region comprising aa L21 and F25 to interact with the dimerization/phosphotransfer (DHp) domain of KinA (Rowland et al.
2004
).
Several hypotheses have been proposed to explain the inhibitory mechanism of KinA by Sda. It was suggested that Sda may prevent the autophosphorylation of KinA by disrupting the transfer of phosphate groups between the ATP binding site in the catalytic domain and the DHp domain (Rowland et al.
2004
). Alternatively, Sda could induce conformational changes in the DHp domain, interfering with the phosphotransfer between KinA and Spo0F (Whitten et al.
2007
). A subsequent study proposed that Sda directly obstructs this phosphotransfer process, as its binding site on KinA overlaps with that of Spo0F (Cunningham and Burkholder
2009
). Using a variant of the protein that incorporated seleno-methionine, the crystal structure of Sda was solved (Jaques et al.
2009
). This structure closely resembles the previously determined NMR-based structure and the structure of a related protein from
Geobacillus stearothermophilus
when in complex with KinB. Contrary to earlier findings that suggested Sda to be monomeric or dimeric in solution, it maintains a trimeric arrangement without straightforward internal symmetry in solution.
A recent study observed that the disruption of genes
recA, sda, disA
, or
sirA
had a mitigating effect on the sporulation deficiency exhibited by a strain lacking YtkD, MutM, MutY, and Mfd proteins.
recA
and
sda
genes were upregulated in the strain lacking Mfd and the guanine oxidized (GO) prevention/repair system (Suárez et al.
2021
). The researchers proposed that the transcriptional coupling mechanism involved in the repair of 8-OxoG lesions activates checkpoint processes that rely on RecA, Sda, SirA, and DisA. These checkpoint processes directly influence the initiation and progression of sporulation in
B. subtilis
. The action of RecA, Sda, SirA, and DisA was found to occur in a hierarchical manner. This hierarchical regulation plays a significant role in alleviating the sporulation defect in a
B. subtilis
strain, i.e. deficient in transcription-coupled repair and prone to the accumulation of 8-OxoG lesions. Notably, among the set of genes related to sporulation,
sda
has been found to be conserved in various Bacilli species (Galperin et al.
2022
). The regulatory interaction between Sda and KinA holds significant importance for
B. subtilis
, as it contributes to the modulation of sporulation initiation at multiple levels in response to unfavourable environmental conditions.
Bacillus subtilis
MciZ regulates cell division
Bacterial cytokinesis, the process of cell division in bacteria, is initiated when a specialized molecular complex known as the divisome assembles at the midpoint of the cell. This divisome is composed of approximately 10 core proteins that play essential roles in attaching to and constricting the cell membrane during division (Lutkenhaus et al.
2012
). One crucial divisome component is FtsZ, which forms a structure called the Z ring in a GTP-dependent manner and generates the physical force required for cell membrane constriction (Shapiro et al.
2009
). Numerous small proteins that interact with FtsZ and other divisome components have been identified, including MciZ (mother cell inhibitor of FtsZ), which was first discovered in 2008 through a yeast two-hybrid screening using FtsZ as a bait protein (Handler et al.
2008
).
MciZ, a small protein consisting of 40 aa, is produced under the control of the σ
E
factor during sporulation (Handler et al.
2008
). It plays a role in inhibiting the Z-ring formation by interfering with FtsZ polymerization when present at moderate to low concentrations. The crystal structure of the FtsZ–MciZ complex has revealed that MciZ binds to a specific site on one of the surfaces involved in FtsZ polymerization, causing inhibition through steric hindrance (Bisson-Filho et al.
2015
). In the absence of FtsZ binding, the N-terminus of MciZ remains unstructured, but interaction with FtsZ induces the formation of two β-sheets (Bisson-Filho et al.
2015
). MciZ exhibits different effects on FtsZ depending on its concentration: at high concentrations, it sequesters FtsZ, while at substoichiometric concentrations, it acts by capping FtsZ filaments. Additionally, MciZ has been shown to induce filamentation of FtsZ
in vitro
. MciZ can influence sporulation in
B. subtilis
, as excessive levels of intracellular or exogenously added MciZ do not only reduce spore formation efficiency but also inhibit spore germination (Araújo-Bazán et al.
2019
).
In vitro
studies have characterized the effects of varying MciZ levels on FtsZ (Bisson-Filho et al.
2015
). However, conducting
in vivo
experiments to study these effects has been challenging due to FtsZ’s high sensitivity to even low concentrations of MciZ, necessitating a tightly controlled expression system. Recently, Stork et al. (
2021
) employed an innovative approach by utilizing nonstandard genetic code expansion, incorporating nonstandard aa (nsAAs) into proteins to create a pHyperspank–TAG–MciZ construct (Stork et al.
2021
). This construct allowed for the controlled production of MciZ. Through single-molecule lifetime imaging and cell length measurements, the researchers found that increasing the production of MciZ led to a dose-dependent decrease in the lifetimes of FtsZ subunits (Stork et al.
2021
). While reductions in FtsZ subunit lifetimes were observed even at low levels of MciZ induction, disruption of Z-ring formation only occurred at higher induction levels. A critical threshold for Z-ring assembly was identified at 3 μM of nsAA, indicating that beyond this point, FtsZ filaments became too short and dynamic to properly assemble a Z ring, corresponding to a mean FtsZ life-time ranging between 4 and 6 s (Stork et al.
2021
).
Mycobacterium bovis
and
M. tuberculosis
KdpF binds a histidine kinase
The Kdp complex, a high affinity ATP-driven potassium transport system, is composed of the four membrane-bound subunits, KdpA, KdpB, KdpC, and KdpF. KdpB is the catalytical subunit, KdpA the K
+
-translocating subunit and KdpF is a stabilizing small membrane protein. In
M. bovis
, the 30-aa long KdpF protein is cotranscribed with the
kdpABC
genes and regulated by the KdpDE two-component system. KdpF was supposed to stabilize the KdpABC potassium transporter complex. In BTHS (Bacterial two hybrid-system), KdpF interacts with the histidine kinase KdpD, but it does not play a critical role in
kdp
gene expression (Gannoun-Zaki et al.
2013
). Surprisingly,
kdpF
overexpression led to a significant reduction of
M. bovis
BCG growth in murine and human primary macrophages associated with altered colony morphology. Members of the MmpL family of membrane proteins involved in the biosynthesis and transport of various cell wall lipids were discovered as KdpF interaction partners (Gannoun-Zaki et al.
2013
) suggesting that the intramacrophage growth defect may be caused by a cell wall alteration. KdpF also interacts with membrane proteins Narl, NarK2, and Rv2617c, which are involved in nitrosative stress response (Rosas Olvera et al.
2017
). Surprisingly, the exogenous application of KdpF did not only affect the stability of its interaction partners in the membrane, but had the same effects as endogenous
kdpF
overexpression making KdpF a potent antiviral molecule (Rosas Olvera et al.
2017
).
Two small proteins modulate the
B. subtlis
degradosome
The proposed
B. subtilis
RNA degradosome is composed of the major endoribonuclease RNase Y, RNases J1 and J2, PNPase, helicase CshA, the glycolytic enzymes enolase and phosphofructokinase and, under certain circumstances, GapA (Commichau et al.
2009
, Gimpel and Brantl
2016
). Until now, two small proteins—SR1P and SR7P—have been discovered that interact with components of the RNA degradosome.
SR1P is a 39-aa protein derived from the dual-functional sRNA SR1 (Heidrich et al.
2006
,
2007
, Gimpel et al.
2010
, Gimpel and Brantl
2017
). Transcription of the
sr1
gene is mainly controlled by CcpN, and to a minor extent by CcpA (Licht et al.
2005
,
2008
,
2009
). SR1P plays a crucial role in the RNA degradation network of
B. subtilis
. Its primary function involves interacting with GapA, one of the two glyceraldehyde-3P-dehydrogenases (GAPDHs) in
B. subtilis
, and contributing to the stabilization of
gapA
mRNA. This stabilization is particularly significant during gluconeogenesis (Gimpel et al.
2010
).
In addition to its canonical role in glycolysis, GapA exhibits moonlighting properties by engaging with both RNases J1 and the main endoribonuclease, RNase Y (Gimpel and Brantl
2016
). SR1P acts as a facilitator in the interaction between GapA and RNase J1, leading to an augmentation of RNase J1’s catalytic activity
in vitro
, as shown for two substrates, SR5 and threonyl-tRNA. Furthermore, SR1P exerts an influence on SR5 stability
in vivo
, underscoring its multifaceted regulatory role. In essence, SR1P fine-tunes GapA’s moonlighting function (Gimpel and Brantl
2016
, Ul Haq and Brantl
2021
).
To elucidate the SR1P–GapA interaction, comprehensive analyses were undertaken, employing peptide mutants in coelution experiments, complemented by functional assessments through northern blotting (Gimpel et al.
2017
). These investigations successfully unveiled the molecular interface between SR1P and GapA. SR1P forms contacts with GapA through specific aa, within a binding pocket primarily constituted by GapA’s C-terminal helix 14. Additionally, SR1P establishes interactions with the N-terminal helix 1 of GapA (Gimpel et al.
2017
).
It is noteworthy that SR1 transcription occurs under gluconeogenic conditions, a metabolic phase characterized by the activation of the gluconeogenic GAPDH, GapB (Fillinger et al.
2000
), coinciding with reduced reliance on the role of the glycolytic GAPDH, GapA. This temporal correlation suggests a dynamic link between RNA degradation and the cellular metabolic state (Newman et al.
2012
). Similarly, it has been hypothesized that during periods of nutrient scarcity, glycolytic enzymes such as enolase and phosphofructokinase, which are integral components of the
B. subtilis
degradosome, along with GapA, may serve as sensors for nutritional stress. These molecular components, acting in concert, potentially transmit signals to the RNA degradation machinery (Gimpel and Brantl
2016
,
2017
). This intricate interplay likely regulates global RNA turnover rates, conserving cellular energy resources for essential biological processes.
Recently, we discovered that SR1P not only interacts with RNase J1 and RNase Y independently of GapA but also engages with another component of the degradosome, the 3′–5′ exoribonuclease PnpA (Ul Haq et al.
2023
). Only the interaction between SR1P and RNase Y was partially mediated by RNA. We observed that SR1P might form binary complexes with RNase Y and RNase J1
in vivo
, influencing their enzymatic activity toward different substrate RNAs (Ul Haq et al.
2023
). However, it is crucial to note that this binary complex formation appears to be substrate-dependent. Currently, it is unclear which specific substrate properties dictate SR1P’s contribution to RNase-mediated degradation.
SR7P, a 39-aa protein, is encoded by the dual-function antisense RNA SR7 (Ul Haq et al.
2021a
). The
sr7
gene which situated within the intergenic region flanked by the
tyrS
and
rpsD
genes is preceded by a σ
B
-dependent promoter (Irnov et al.
2010
, Mars et al.
2015
). SR7P is synthesized in response to five distinct stress conditions, ethanol, salt, acid, heat shock and Mn
2+
stress, arising from the σ
B
-dependent
sr7
transcription. In addition, it is produced constitutively from a processing product of the
tyrS
mRNA (Ul Haq et al.
2021a
).
One of SR7P’s significant functions involves its interaction with enolase, a scaffolding component of the RNA degradosome complex (Ul Haq et al.
2021a
). This interaction, in turn, enhances the binding affinity of enolase to RNase Y. Importantly, the SR7P-Eno-RNase Y interaction is not bridged by RNA (Ul Haq et al.
2021a
).
The activity of RNase Y is potentiated in the presence of the ternary complex comprising SR7P, enolase and RNase Y compared to the activity observed in the enolase/RNase Y complex alone. This regulatory effect of SR7P has been demonstrated both
in vivo
and
in vitro
(Ul Haq et al.
2021a
). Furthermore, SR7P exerts a discernible impact on cell survival under selective stress conditions, thereby hinting at its potential role in orchestrating specific aspects of the cellular stress response (Ul Haq et al.
2021a
). Notably, recent investigations have explored SR7P’s ability to interact with two other essential RNases, namely RNase J1 and PNPase, along with GapA, the scaffolding components constituting the
B. subtilis
RNA degradosome. Through far-western blotting experiments, it was established that purified SR7P does not interact with any of these components of the
B. subtilis
degradosome (Ul Haq et al.
2023
). This underscores the exclusive nature of the SR7P-mediated modulation, which is restricted to its interaction with the enolase–RNase Y complex (Ul Haq et al.
2023
).
Small proteins are part of the endospore coat and contribute to the resistance of spores against harsh conditions
Bacillus subtilis
SpoVM
Bacillus subtilis
endospores are highly resilient cellular structures enclosed by a dual protective barrier consisting of a peptidoglycan layer called the cortex and a protein-based layer known as the spore coat. The development of these protective layers relies on the correct assembly of a foundational coat layer comprised of two specific proteins, SpoIVA and SpoVM.
SpoVM is a 26-aa amphipathic α-helical small protein synthesized within the mother cell compartment. The
spoVM
gene is expressed under the control of the sigma factor σ
E
(Levin et al.
1993
). The absence of SpoVM leads to the formation of mutants that experience severe impediments in spore formation and are unable to generate a normal spore cortex (Levin et al.
1993
). SpoVM was demonstrated to interact with and inhibit the ATP-dependent protease FtsH
in vitro
(Cutting et al.
1997
, Prajapati et al.
2000
). Moreover, the presence of σ
E
triggers the localization of SpoVM in the outer forespore membrane (van Ooij and Losick
2003
). This localization is achieved through the distinction between the positive curvature of the membrane surrounding the inner cell and the negative curvature of the cytoplasmic membrane (Ramamurthi et al.
2009
). The recognition of a convex membrane curvature, possibly in a multimeric form, serves as a signal for the attachment of the endospore coat. Furthermore, SpoVM also functions as a connector, anchoring the endospore coat to the emerging forespore (Ramamurthi et al.
2009
).
SpoIVA, a structural protein with ATPase activity, directly engages with SpoVM and undergoes conformational changes after ATP hydrolysis, enabling it to polymerize and cover the forespore surface (Ramamurthi and Losick
2008
, Castaing et al.
2013
). SpoIVA also recruits SpoVID, a penicillin-binding protein, which, in conjunction with SpoVM, is indispensable for the encapsulation of the spore (Wang et al.
2009a
).
A study employing time-resolved imaging and flow cytometry reported that SpoVM utilizes a “dash-and-recruit” mechanism to selectively accumulate on the forespore. SpoVM exhibited a higher adsorption rate on membranes with greater convex curvature. This preferential adsorption was accurately characterized as a two-step process: initially, SpoVM binds more rapidly, followed by cooperative recruitment of additional SpoVM molecules (Kim et al.
2017
). Utilizing a two-dimensional ratchet model, Peluso et al. (
2019
) suggested in 2019 that SpoVM and SpoIVA rely on each other for their localization. Through an interaction with SpoVM, SpoIVA achieves the local concentration necessary for its polymerization on the outer forespore membrane. Initially, SpoVM’s association with the membrane is dynamic, but polymerized SpoIVA entraps SpoVM in its correct position on the forespore membrane (Peluso et al.
2019
).
A recent study has shown that SpoVM also directly interacts with SpoVD and plays a crucial role in the proper localization of SpoVD (Krajčíková et al.
2021
). Future studies will shed light on how SpoVM and other proteins involved in spore coat morphogenesis impact the formation and maturation of the spore cortex.
CmpA
Cortex Morphogenic Protein A (CmpA), a 37-aa protein, is synthesized in
B. subtilis
during the sporulation process (Schmalisch et al.
2010
, Ebmeier et al.
2012
). The identification of the
cmpA
gene was facilitated through an oligonucleotide microarray search specifically aimed at detecting small genes within intergenic regions of the
B. subtilis
genome that become active during spore formation (Schmalisch et al.
2010
). Expression of
cmpA
is regulated by sigma factor σ
E
and transcription factor SpoIIID, which are specific to the mother cell (Ebmeier et al.
2012
). CmpA localizes to the surface of the forespore early in sporulation, concomitant with the initiation of coat assembly, but becomes undetectable in cells that successfully progress through the spore formation process (Ebmeier et al.
2012
). Deletion of
cmpA
results in the suppression of the sporulation defect observed in a
spoVM
mutant allele. This particular
spoVM
mutation can initiate coat assembly but fails to initiate cortex assembly. Furthermore, while CmpA is degraded in wild-type cells during the late stages of sporulation, concurrent with the elaboration of phase-bright forespores, CmpA persists in cells carrying the mutant
spoVM
allele (Ebmeier et al.
2012
). Interestingly, overproducing CmpA interferes with key sporulation steps that necessitate peptidoglycan synthesis, ultimately reducing sporulation efficiency and causing cellular lysis (Ebmeier et al.
2012
, Tan et al.
2015
).
Initially, a model was proposed wherein CmpA acts to repress premature cortex assembly until coat assembly has successfully commenced (Ebmeier et al.
2012
). Later on, CmpA was found to interact with the AAA+ protease CplXP (Tan et al.
2015
). The spore envelope, which shields the dormant spore’s genome from environmental challenges, utilizes the protein SpoIVA as a framework for its assembly. Disrupting the assembly of this envelope triggers a cell death pathway. In this process, CmpA serves as an adaptor to ClpXP, leading to the degradation of SpoIVA. This event halts sporulation and ultimately results in the lysis of defective sporulating cells (Tan et al.
2015
).
Therefore, CmpA plays a pivotal role in ensuring the proper assembly of the spore envelope and participates in a quality-control pathway that selectively removes defective sporulating cells through regulated cell death (Tan et al.
2015
).
SASPs protect spore form the deleterious effects of UV and chemicals exposures
The SASPs are a group of proteins with less than 100 aa, displaying high conservation among all bacteria capable of forming endospores (Setlow
1988
). They constitute roughly 20% of the overall protein content within spores (Setlow
2014
). These proteins are produced during the later stages of sporulation, controlled by the forespore-specific sigma factor, σ
G
, and confer resistance to spores (Setlow
2014
). Most endospore-forming bacteria possess two major types of SASPs (SspA and SspB) falling under the α/β classification, while minor SASP types differ based on the particular organism (Setlow
1988
, Cabrera-Martinez et al.
1989
,
1991
, Cabrera-Hernandez and Setlow
2000
).
In
B. subtilis
, the α/β-type SASPs (SspA, 69 aa, and SspB, 67 aa) enhance spore resistance to various chemicals, including nitrous acid, formaldehyde, glutaraldehyde, iodine, and hydrogen peroxide (Setlow and Setlow
1993
, Tennen et al.
2000
, Setlow
2006
). These SASPs function by binding to DNA, promoting the formation of spore photoproducts rather than cyclobutane thymine dimers, thus safeguarding against UV radiation (Setlow
2006
, Moeller et al.
2008
). Notably,
B. subtilis
mutants lacking either SspA or SspB demonstrate increased sensitivity to UV light, with a double mutant displaying even more sensitivity than vegetative cells, highlighting the critical role of these proteins in spore survival (Mason and Setlow
1986
). Crucially, the UV sensitivity can be rescued by the introduction of
sspA
or
sspB
genes (Mason and Setlow
1986
,
1987
). Additionally, the interchangeability of SASPs in UV resistance is indicated by the complementation of phenotypes with a SASP from
Bacillus megaterium
, suggesting that SASPs may have overlapping functions in imparting resistance to UV radiation in different spore-forming organisms (Leyva-Illades et al.
2007
). Moreover,
B. subtilis
encodes a γ-type SASP, postulated to act as an aa reservoir upon the emergence of vegetative cells from germinated spores (Hackett and Setlow
1988
).
In the case of another endospore-forming organism,
C. difficile
, SspA and SspB are implicated in UV resistance (Nerber and Sorg
2021
). These putative SASPs exhibit 40% similarity to α/β-type SASPs found in other endospore-forming bacteria (Nerber and Sorg
2021
). Deletion of the
sspA
gene in
C. difficile
results in a significant reduction in spore survival post-UV exposure, while
sspB
has a more modest impact on UV resistance, though without contributing to spore outgrowth after germination (Nerber and Sorg
2021
). Intriguingly, the absence of both SspA and SspB results in the formation of phase-gray, unreleased spores, suggesting a previously unreported role of these SASPs in sporulation (Nerber and Sorg
2021
). Nonetheless, the lack of SASPs in
C. difficile
does not affect the emergence of vegetative cells postgermination, nor do these proteins substantially contribute to chemical resistance (Nerber and Sorg
2021
). A recent investigation has indicated that the deletion of both
sspA
and
sspB
affects the sporulation frequency (Nerber et al.
2023
). Both SspA and SspB are essential for the formation of the spore cortex layer (Nerber et al.
2023
). The overexpression of
B. subtilis sspA
was able to partially restore UV resistance in a Δ
sspA C. difficile
strain (Nerber et al.
2023
).
Listeria monocytogenes
small membrane protein Prli42
The stressosome, a cytoplasmic complex that relays external cues and activates the σ
B
regulon, is well-characterized in
B. subtilis
, but it was unknown how it senses stress. Therefore, it was an important step forward that in a genome wide N-terminomics study in
L. monocytogenes
, the 31-aa protein Prli42 was discovered and preliminarily characterized (Impens et al.
2017
). Prli42 is anchored in the membrane and interacts with
L. monocytogenes
orthologues of
B. subtilis
stressosome components RsbR (regulator of σ
B
) (Lmo0889), RsbS (Lmo0890), and three RsbR-like proteins. The authors reconstituted the
Listeria
stressosome
in vitro
and visualized its supramolecular structure by electron microscopy. They could show that Prli42 partially tethers RsbR to the bacterial membrane and is involved in σ
B
signaling following oxidative stress (Impens et al.
2017
; Fig.
3
). Prli42 aa K4, K5, and R8 were found to be involved in the interaction with RsbR and one monomer of Prli42 was predicted to interact with one RsbR monomer close to the dimer interface of the N-terminal RsbR domain. Later, the supramolecular organization of the
Listeria
stressosome was solved by cryoelectron microscopy at 3.38 Å resolution (Williams et al.
2019
). The stressosome comprises multiple copies of RsbR, RsbS, and RsbT, a kinase required for its activation. With E109, E110, and E113 of RsbR the authors could determine aa residues involved in Prli42 binding. The authors demonstrate that the N-terminal domain of RsbR is a stress sensing apparatus by virtue of its interaction with Prli42.
In contrast to the initial assumption in 2017, it was found in 2021 that after host infection, Prli42 plays no role in associating RsbR to the membrane. Apparently, in intracellular
Listeria
, the stressosome adopts a configuration, which is different from that in free-living bacteria (Dessaux et al.
2021
).
Prli42 is highly conserved in 42 Firmicutes, among them in 21
Bacillus
species and in six
Staphylococcus
species (Impens et al.
2017
). In living cells, RsbR1 is present in multiple states: in protomers with RsbS, large clusters of stressosome complexes, and in connection with the plasma membrane via Prli42. RsbL can freely diffuse in the cytoplasm but forms clusters when exposed to light. The clustering of RsbL is independent of the presence of Prli42 (Tran et al.
2023
).
Methods for the prediction and investigation of small proteins
In silico prediction of small ORFs
In general, short sequences yield lower alignment scores which makes it difficult to discriminate between true and random ORFs. Therefore, prediction methods have to be combined with other approaches, and subsequent experimental validation is necessary.
Small ORFs have divergent features, e.g. an atypical nucleotide composition, often noncanonical start codons and sometimes lack an obvious SD sequence, which make computer predictions more challenging. Since start and stop codons are AT-rich, the probability to predict ORFs that are nonfunctional
in vivo
is higher in GC-rich genomes. Meanwhile, researchers do not have to rely on BLAST or FASTA alone, but prediction programs are available that have been specifically developed for small proteins, like sORF finder (Hanada et al.
2010
) or µProteIns (de Souza et al.
2022
) (for others, see Fuchs and Engelmann
2023
).
In similarity-based predictions that utilize phylogenetic conservation as search criterion, several problems arise: short genes are less conserved, may lack conserved domains and functional motifs, and are frequently species-specific. They might not even be encoded in closely related bacteria as we observed for instance for SR1P and SR7P. These small proteins are confined to the genus
Bacillus
and
Geobacillus
(Gimpel et al.
2012
, Ul Haq et al.
2021a
) and are not encoded in any other firmicute.
So-called
ab initio
prediction methods, e.g. AUGUSTUS (Keller et al.
2011
) employ Markov models and are particularly appropriate for the identification of novel proteins because they do not necessitate any preceding knowledge of already existing proteins.
Machine learning-based methods for the prediction of small ORFs use algorithms to learn and recognize complex patterns and properties in annotated genomes. An example that has been employed successfully in different bacteria is RanSEP (Miravet-Verde et al.
2019
). It calculates aa composition, secondary structures and hydrophobicity, considers RBS information, start codon prevalence and the GC content to discriminate between coding and noncoding small ORFs. The program learns to adjust organism-specific parameters obtained from provided training sets to different species. By contrast, DeepRibo (Clauwaert et al.
2019
) is not only based on an artificial neural network that integrates recurrent memory cells and convolutional layers but also integrates experimental data from ribosome profiling studies. Balrog (Sommer and Salzberg
2021
) is a gene-finding algorithm for different bacterial species based on a temporal convolutional network. It uses a default minimal ORF length of 60 nt, but excludes hypothetical proteins from its training set, which makes it less suitable for uncharacterized gene families.
Hybrid methods integrate the strength of the three other methods to enhance the accuracy of small ORF predictions and to diminish the number of false positives. Some of them merge homology searches with synteny analysis or with statistical methods. Evidence-based hybrid methods integrate experimental ribosome profiling and proteomics data.
Transcriptomics
Over the past 15 years, comprehensive transcriptome analyses utilizing RNA-seq (Wang et al.
2009b
) have been performed in a plethora of bacterial species under different physiological and stress conditions and the results deposited in publicly accessible databases. Examples for low GC-Gram-positive bacteria have been reviewed previously (Brantl and Brückner
2014
). RNA-seq approaches allowed to identify all actively transcribed genes in a species, many of them very short and previously unknown. Since the discovery of dual-function sRNAs (Gimpel and Brantl
2017
) it had been clear that translation of short transcripts is more widespread than originally anticipated.
The current methods of choice for the experimental identification of small proteins are ribosome profiling and mass spectrometry (MS)-based proteomics. The sensitivity of ribosome profiling is much higher, but—as it only shows whether or not ribosomes protect a predicted ORF—it provides only indirect evidence for the existence of a small protein. By contrast, MS-based methods do not only directly detect small proteins accumulating to robust levels, but can also contribute additional information, e.g. about post-translational modifications or the presence of isoforms (Fuchs and Engelmann
2023
). Moreover, it is also possible to obtain information on the global subcellular localization of proteins and the structures of protein complexes.
Ribosome profiling
Ribosome profiling is a method that uses specialized deep mRNA sequencing of ribosome-protected mRNA fragments to determine which mRNAs are being actively translated (Ingolia
2014
, Ingolia et al.
2019
). In short, polysomes engaged in active
in vivo
translation are digested by RNases yielding ribosome-protected mRNA fragments, so called “ribosome footprints.” These footprints are extracted and employed for generation of a DNA library by ligation of a linker followed by reverse transcription and circularization PCR. Resulting cDNA libraries are then analyzed by deep sequencing (see Fig.
5
). Ribosome profiling can resolve translation down to a single-codon level. It can be employed for all bacterial species. A recent study shows how it can be adapted to search for small ORFs in different prokaryotic species, and which critical controls have to be used (Vasquez-Laslop et al.
2022
). Advanced profiling methods increase the density of ribosomes at start or stop codons: TIS (translation initiation site)-profiling can accurately identify translation start sites, as it stops ribosomes at initiation with retapamulin (also termed Ribo-RET), Onc12 or tetracycline. In TTS (translation termination site)-profiling, ribosomes are stalled at stop codons with apidaecin (e.g. Shell et al.
2015
, Weaver et al.
2019
). However, since antibiotics are not equally effective against all bacterial species, TIS and TTS profiling cannot be applied to all bacterial species. Sometimes, genetic modifications like deleting genes for major efflux pumps have to be used to increase the potency of antibiotics. Meanwhile, translation activity can be even determined during elongation (e.g. Fremin et al.
2021
). In a ribosome profiling analysis in
M. tuberculosis
which discovered 90 new ORFs, many of them with less than 50 codons (Smith et al.
2022
), the utilization of all three alternative start codons ATG, TTG, or GTG was observed. Ribosome profiling studies have also shown that leaderless transcripts can serve as templates for translation, and that RBS are not essential for initiation but required for high efficiency of translation. Interestingly, ribosome profiling allowed to identify multiple alternative start sites within the same ORF which may result in proteins with different N-termini, but the same C-termini. Furthermore, internal start codons belonging to alternative ORFs could be mapped precisely (e.g. Orr et al.
2020
). However, it remains to be seen, if alternative ORFs—so far excluded from annotations—have any relevance
in vivo
.

In the past few years, a number of algorithms have been developed to use ribosome-, TIS-, and TTS-profiling data to detect small bacterial ORFs, e.g. RiboReport (Gelhausen et al.
2022
) or smORFer (Bartholomäus et al.
2021
). The latter integrates structural features of the genetic sequence, in-frame translation data, TIS-profiling data and Fourier transform to convert these parameters into a measurable score to select small ORFs without relying alone on existing gene annotations or homology comparisons.
Proteogenomics
To identify novel small proteins or peptides that are not present in protein databases, “proteogenomics,” an area of research at the interface of proteomics and genomics, can be employed (Nesvizhskii
2014
). Based on genomic and transcriptomic information, customized protein sequence databases are generated, which are used to identify novel peptides or small proteins from MS-based proteomic data. Strategies for the generation of such databases can include stop- to stop translation, which creates databases in which, however, the majority of protein sequences are artificial and N-terminal peptides are missing. Alternatively, start-to stop translation can be employed, but in that case, seven potential start codons, ATG, TTG, GTG, CTG, ATT, ATC, and ATA found to function as start codons e.g. in
E. coli
have to be considered (Hecht et al.
2017
). Therefore, ATG could be preferred followed by “translation” of the longest possible ORF. In case there is no ATG, the next alternative start codon yielding the longest possible ORF could be considered, but many artificial protein sequences would be obtained as well. Start-to stop translation with ATG and all other start codons would include all N-terminal peptides, but result in a huge, extremely redundant database. However, for qualified MS analyses, nonredundant databases are recommendable (Fijalkowski et al.
2022
). Such an example is iPtgxDB (Omasits et al.
2017
), an integrated proteogenomics database with a significantly diminished amount of nonexistent protein sequences that has been applied successfully to identify 16 proteins comprising less than 51 aa in
B. subtilis
(Bartel et al.
2020
). The present standard method for the selective enrichment of N-terminal peptides in conjunction with high-throughput MS is N-terminomics. It relies on blocking free amino groups on intact proteins by acetylation followed by proteolysis of acetylated proteins, which yields a mixture on N-terminally acetylated (true N-terminal) and nonacetylated (internal and C-terminal) peptides. The latter are affinity-captured on an immobilized amine-reactive reagent. The nonbound fraction is highly enriched in N-terminal peptides which can be analysed without further treatment by LC-MS/MS (McDonald and Beynon
2006
). N-terminomics has been successfully utilized to identify six small proteins in
L. monocytogenes
, among them Prli42, see above (Impens et al.
2017
).
MS-based proteomics
Although MS has a high potential for the identification of novel small proteins, their low molecular weight makes it difficult to prepare MS-compatible peptides. In
Salmonella
, the peptide identification rate went down from 9% for proteins comprising more than 100 aa to 4.5% for proteins between 50 and 100 aa, and even 2.5% for proteins up to 50 aa (Fijalkowski et al.
2022
). Therefore, method development aims at increasing the number and intensity of MS-compatible peptides, and critical steps comprise sample preparation, protease digestion, liquid chromatography, MS data acquisition, peptide spectrum matching, MS-data analysis, and protein identification (Fuchs and Engelmann
2023
).
There are several approaches for the enrichment and digestion of small proteins. To separate small proteins from larger proteins prior to proteolytic digestion, different methods have been devised. One of them relies on solid-phase enrichment columns that combine reversed-phase binding and size-based separation, in which only small proteins can enter the pores and interact with the column material. For
B. subtilis
, this approach enhanced the number of identified small proteins about 2-fold (Bartel et al.
2020
). Another method, SDS-PAGE in a columnar cartridge, allows to separate proteins and collect soluble fractions according to their molecular weight. A third method, specific precipitation with organic solvents like acetone or different acetonitrile concentrations can remove proteins above 15 kD and has been used to identify small proteins in soluble protein extracts (e.g. Cassidy et al.
2019
). In most cases, several methods have to be combined. For protein samples with low concentration, protein digestion by Single-Pot Solid-Phase-Enhanced Sample Preparation can be conducted to increase the number of peptides detectable by MS (e.g. Froschauer et al.
2022
). To select endoproteases specifically suitable for small protein identification, peptide predictor tools can be applied. Often, multiprotease approaches are employed to enhance the number of MS-detectable peptides. For
S. aureus
Newman, proteases LysC, AspN, and trypsin were used and 175 proteins comprising up to 100 aa were identified, 24 of them novel and 144 highly conserved in the genomes of other
S. aureus
strains (Fuchs et al.
2021
). For small membrane proteins, which are deficient in charged aa in membrane-spanning regions, proteases like chymotrypsin, proteinase K, pepsin, elastase, or subtilisin are promising alternatives.
LC-MS/MS and data analyses
In the majority of analyses, classical proteomics approaches relying on electrospray ionization MS were employed for the identification of small proteins (e.g. Ahrens et al.
2022
). An alternative, especially for small membrane proteins or samples of low complexity, is matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization (MALDI) (e.g. Meier-Credo et al.
2022
). To prevent a loss of peptide information, i.e. especially critical for small proteins, the use of a data-independent acquisition MS-based proteomics may be beneficial for the future identification of small proteins (Zhang et al.
2020
). Additional fragmentation methods like higher-energy collision dissociation carried out e.g. in tandem time-of-flight mass spectrometers, electron-transfer dissociation (ETD), and electron-transfer combined with higher-energy collision dissociation can be employed to enhance peptide detection and sequence coverage (Fuchs and Engelmann
2023
). Collision-induced dissociation (CID) induces fragmentation of selected ions (molecular ions or protonated molecules) in the gas phase, which are accelerated applying an electrical potential, to increase the kinetic energy of the ions and allows them to collide with neutral molecules (helium, nitrogen, and argon). Some of the kinetic energy is converted into internal energy that leads to bond breakage and fragmentation into smaller fragments. Common CID selectively breaks labile bonds. ETD induces fragmentation of large, multiply-charged cations by transferring electrons to them and has significantly improved sequence coverage.
Small proteins are well-suited for top-down proteomics employing protein extracts but this requires mass spectrometers with high sensitivity, mass accuracy, and isotopic resolution as well as efficient peptide fragmentation. These top-down data can be combined with
de novo
sequencing, which is a database-independent method that deduces sequences from aa-specific mass increases between adjacent fragment peaks. A MALDI-MS/MS top-down method has been successfully used for membrane proteins (Meier-Credo et al.
2022
).
Small proteins that have been exclusively detected by MS-based approaches have to be confirmed by other methods, e.g. the expression of tagged proteins either from plasmids or their native location in the chromosome followed by western blotting (e.g. see Gimpel and Brantl
2016
, Ul Haq et al.
2021a
).
Vice versa
, small proteins predicted from transcriptomics or ribosome profiling data can be substantiated by MS-based methods.
