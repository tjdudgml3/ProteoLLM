G protein–coupled receptors (GPCRs) are involved in numerous physiological processes and respond to a wide array of ligands—from photons to peptides. Given that approximately one-third of marketed drugs act on GPCRs (
1
), understanding how ligands influence their structure and signaling profile remains critical in elucidating the diverse roles these receptors play.
Until recently, solving the structures of active GPCRs has been challenging because of difficulty purifying them in a functional form. Early chromatography techniques to isolate GPCRs include the use of hydroxyapatite for nonspecific ionic interactions (
2
,
3
) and the use of lectin concanavalin A-conjugated Sepharose to bind glycosylated receptor side chains (
4
,
5
,
6
). Subsequent ligand-affinity techniques use receptor-specific ligands tethered to a chromatography matrix to act as bait molecules to bind and stabilize a specific GPCR (
7
,
8
). While powerful, the latter approach requires an amenable ligand and is specific for a single receptor–ligand pair.
As monoclonal antibodies became more readily available, immunoaffinity techniques based on recognition of peptide epitopes—either native to the receptor sequence (1D4 epitope) (
9
,
10
) or appended directly to the receptor N and/or C termini (
e.g.
, HA, FLAG)—gained widespread popularity because of their generalizability (
11
). Unfortunately, with the exception of ligand affinity, none of these approaches necessarily targets the properly folded functional receptor. Thus, a combination of the aforementioned approaches has often been used.
An additional challenge for structural studies of GPCRs is the fact that they can adopt many distinct conformations (
12
). One of the most successful approaches to deal with this issue has been the use of nanobodies (Nbs) that specifically bind and stabilize the active receptor–G-protein ternary complex. Nbs have been used to solve active structures of GPCRs in complex with all the major Gα subtypes (
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
) as well as other biophysical studies (
17
,
18
). However, generation of Nbs can be costly and often results in highly specific recognition of only a single target or complex (
19
).
Binding of G-proteins alone can also stabilize active receptor conformations (
20
,
21
,
22
). This stabilization occurs, in part, because of the tight (submicromolar) affinity with which GPCRs bind heterotrimeric G-proteins until nucleotide exchange causes the Gα subunit to dissociate. Thus, mini-G-proteins—Gα subunits reduced to their GPCR-interacting region and nucleotide-binding (GTPase) domain—have recently been used in structural studies of a variety of different GPCRs (
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
). The binding interaction between GPCR and Gα subunit occurs through a highly conserved mechanism. First, upon activation, the receptor exposes its intracellular G-protein binding site by a concerted outward movement of transmembrane helices 5 and 6 (TM5 and TM6) (
27
,
28
,
29
,
30
). The extreme C terminus of the Gα subunit—called the α5 helix—then makes stabilizing contacts with residues in this binding site, which then links GPCR engagement to nucleotide exchange in the Gα subunit (
31
,
32
,
33
,
34
).
This key binding event can be replicated using mimetic peptides of the Gα C terminus (Gα-CT). Binding to Gα-CT peptides similarly stabilizes the active receptor conformation and thus has been used to define factors contributing to high affinity binding and to define the biophysics involved. This approach has been extensively used to study rhodopsin interactions with transducin C-terminal (Gα
t
-CT) peptides (
28
,
31
,
32
,
35
,
36
,
37
,
38
,
39
,
40
,
41
,
42
) and to determine active receptor structures of ligand-free bovine opsin (
43
) and active/meta-II conformations (
44
,
45
).
The entire Gα domain can be fused onto the C terminus of a GPCR to help stabilize and study G protein–receptor interactions and enhance agonist affinity (
46
,
47
). Amazingly, it was found this behavior can be replicated by fusing
only
the Gα-CT sequence to the receptor C terminus (
13
). Our laboratory has used the latter approach to study retinal binding to rhodopsin with Gα
t
-CT fusions (
48
,
49
) and used this approach to create a biosensor to detect active receptor conformation (
50
).
Here, we tested a simple but potentially powerful idea: could high-affinity Gα-CT mimetic peptides be used to purify GPCRs? Specifically, if Gα-CT peptides were attached to a chromatography column, could they be used to selectively bind and purify activated receptors? Given the conserved way Gα-CT peptides bind receptors (noted previously), this approach could provide a tag-free way to purify a wide variety of GPCRs.
We first tested this idea on rhodopsin in native source tissues (bovine retina). We found this method could rapidly purify activated rhodopsin, and by varying elution conditions, the method could elute it in two functionally distinct conformations: the active ternary complex and inactive dark-state conformation. Finally, we tested and found this approach works in principle for another model GPCR—the β
2
-adrenergic receptor (β
2
AR)—illustrating the potential as a rapid, simple, and universal GPCR purification method.

Estimation of yield and binding capacity of Gα
t
-CT resin
We analyzed numerous purification experiments (n > 20) to better define key parameters affecting receptor binding and recovery (
Table S2
). This analysis shows that while batch binding provides a slightly improved yield (60% of total receptor recovery), gravity flow-through binding can achieve ∼80% of this in a fraction of the time (∼15 min
versus
1 h) and thus would be especially preferable when receptor instability is of concern. The maximum binding capacity we observed was 1.4 mg Rho/ml resin (∼35 μM Rho) for samples bound in batch and was obtained using high initial receptor concentration ([R]
in
>10 μM). However, we routinely find the binding capacity to be closer to ∼0.7 mg Rho/ml resin for samples with a lower [R]
in
(
Table S2
).
It is also worth noting that by comparing the peptide content of precoupling/postcoupling fractions by SDS-PAGE and Coomassie staining suggests that coupling to the resin occurs with an efficiency of ∼60% under our conditions (data not shown). We estimate that since 60% of the initial 2 μmol peptide was coupled per ml resin, the valency of the resin slurry is ∼1.2 mM immobilized peptide and thus is in excess relative to [R]
in
.
Further analyses over a range of conditions indicate that the apparent resin-binding capacity as a function of [R]
in
can vary over a linear range spanning 1 to 10 μM [R]
in
when binding volume is held constant (
Fig. S2
). We suspect the variability occurs when the [R]
in
is similar to the
K
d
for receptor binding to the Gα-CT. Typically, concentrations much higher than the
K
d
([R]
in
>>
K
d
) are required to fully saturate an adsorbent with analyte binding in equilibrium (
58
,
59
). Supporting this idea, our titration studies find that >2 μM free Gα
t
-CT peptide are required for significant receptor recovery (
Fig. S3
), consistent with EC
50
values (200–920 nM) reported for the high-affinity Gα
t
-CT peptide from which ours are derived (
31
,
60
). However, titration studies examining [R]
in
concentrations below this linear range indicate a lower capture limit of ∼30 nM [R]
in
(
Fig. S4
), likely because of the high apparent concentration of immobilized peptides on the resin as noted previously.
From a practical standpoint, this observation suggests three avenues for improving total binding and recovery. One is to first enrich and concentrate receptors with lower expression levels before solubilizing (and applying to the Gα-CT resin); for example, using a membrane isolation step as in our β
2
AR experiments). The second is to develop and use higher affinity Gα-CT peptides and higher affinity receptor agonists. Finally, alternative coupling strategies or materials may improve the availability of Gα-CT for binding.

Discussion
The simple method for GPCR purification described here relies on the conserved binding mechanisms between an active GPCR and the Gα subunit C terminus (a region known as the α5 helix). This approach offers several unique advantages over traditional GPCR purification methods. One is speed—we could purify rhodopsin in less than an hour for peptide-eluted samples (∼45 min from receptor activation to eluted protein). Although the overall procedure was longer for β
2
AR purification (∼3 h total), as batch binding and additional incubation times were used during the elution step, this could certainly be optimized.
Gα
t
-CT resin is tag free and selectively binds active and functional receptors
Importantly, this approach does not require a purification tag—relying instead on a properly folded receptor tertiary structure and pharmacological activation of the receptor to drive the interaction with the column. Thus, the affinity matrix is conformationally selective for active receptors—only those capable of forming an active G-protein signaling conformation should bind to the resin. For example, rhodopsin with bound antagonist 11CR maintained in the inactive (dark) state does not bind to the Gα
t
-CT resin and is not purified (
Fig. 2
); nor is β
2
AR in the presence of the antagonist carazolol (
Fig. S6
).
Receptors can be selectively bound and eluted with appropriate ligands
The fact that the receptors required agonists to bind the Gα-CT resin illustrates another key point—receptors can be selectively recruited to the resin by using activating ligands. For example, opsin required adding agonist ATR to bind the column (
Fig. 3
) and β
2
AR required agonist ISO (
Fig. 6
, discussed later).
Interestingly, when eluted with Gα
t
-CT peptide, rhodopsin coelutes with ATR in a 1:1 ratio, with the ATR still attached
via
a Schiff base linkage (
Fig. 2
B
). This was unexpected, as this linkage is typically not stable for light-activated rhodopsin in detergents. We think it occurs here because the Gα-CT peptide stabilizes and maintains rhodopsin in an active ternary complex consisting of (Rho∗–ATR–Gα
t
-CT). Any ATR released from this complex can simply rebind and reattach, as we previously saw in our rhodopsin-based biosensors containing a C-terminal Gα
t
-CT sequence (
50
). Together, these results suggest Gα
t
-CT resin could prove useful for the study of less stable rhodopsins and mutants for whom there is a lack of structural information.
Receptor affinity for ligands and Gα-CT modulate interactions with the resin
Our initial studies of β
2
AR showed significantly more receptor eluted from Gα
s
-CT
cha
resin when the binding step included agonist (ISO) rather than inverse agonist (carazolol) (
Fig. S6
). This illustrates that Gα
s
-CT
cha
peptide preferentially binds to and stabilizes β
2
AR in an active state (
61
,
63
).
Encouraged by these results, we carried out a larger-scale purification. SDS-PAGE and silver stain analysis of the elution fractions indicated a high degree of purity, and the identity of β
2
AR-1D4 was confirmed with Western blots (
Fig. 6
) as well as mass spectrometry (
Fig. S8
,
Table S3
). Under both elution conditions, the β
2
AR-1D4 appears as a smeared band around ∼50 kDa, consistent with full-length receptor with heterogeneous glycosylation.
In peptide-eluted samples, a discrete ∼75 kDa band also appeared to coelute with β
2
AR-1D4, as indicated by silver staining (
Fig. 6
A
). This band was subsequently excised and analyzed by LC–MS
2

Table S4
). One possible cause for this observation is that the stress-related protein interacts with the immobilized Gα
s
-CT
cha
peptide and thus is eluted when excess Gα
s
-CT
cha
peptide is added during the elution process. Alternatively, the presence of this protein could simply be an artifact because of our transient overexpression system. Interestingly, while a similar band appeared when α1D4 immunoaffinity purification was done to first concentrate the β
2
AR-1D4, it was absent in the subsequent Gα
s
-CT
cha

Fig. S9
).
The elution efficiency between rhodopsin and β
2
AR showed both similarities and differences. Both were readily eluted by competitive displacement with Gα-CT peptides (
Figs. 2
,
3
,
5
and
6
). However, their ability to be eluted using antagonists/inverse agonists diverged (
Figs. 4
A
versus
6

). The β
2

Fig. 6
,
C

D

Fig. 4

t
-CT) complex (noted previously) or because Gα
t

54

Further improvements and additional limitations
How tightly the two receptors bind their respective Gα-CT peptides could also contribute to differences in their elution profiles. Nature has evolved these sequences to bind to R∗ specifically, but not too tightly, as the active G proteins must also be able to release R∗ in a timely manner so that they can go on to couple with downstream effectors. Thus, it is reasonable to assume that with effort, higher affinity variants can be generated that have not yet been identified in nature.
For example, the original Gα
t
-CT peptides were created using phage display experiments (
60
) and contain a wealth of information about substitutions that increase peptide binding affinity. A single unanticipated substitution (Lys341→Leu) identified in these experiments was shown to be sufficient to enable opsin crystallization with the Gα
t
α5 helix (
43
). In contrast, the Gα
s
-CT
cha
peptides were rationally designed; thus, even higher-affinity Gα
s
peptides might possibly be identified using a directed evolution approach or a combination of the two. Similar approaches may also prove fruitful in the development of high-affinity peptides of the remaining Gα subtype C termini.
Our preliminary tests using a Cha-labeled variant of the native Gα
i
C terminus (Gα
i
-CT) to isolate a Gα
i
-selective receptor, CB1i, indicate significant room for improvement as the inability to isolate native cannabinoid receptors from brain extracts under similar conditions (
Fig. S7
) points to the current limitations of the method.
Beyond improvements to the affinity of the interaction, alternative resin coupling materials may improve the overall yield. In studies not included here, our preliminary results suggest that the Gα
t
-CT purification method also works when peptide is attached to cyanogen bromide beads (without any additional linker region); yet appears to benefit from the short 6-atom spacer that
N
-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS)-activated beads (data not shown). Thus, even longer linkers may further improve the overall yield and should be explored.
Another drawback to the method in its current form is that it requires prior knowledge about the receptor of interest. Specifically, the G protein selectivity must be known to ensure the proper Gα-CT resin is used and receptor-specific agonists must be available. This would be an additional challenge in using Gα-CT resin for the study of orphan receptors. In addition, the nature of the conserved Gα-CT mechanism may limit the applicability toward receptors favoring noncanonical signaling pathways. While many receptors can signal through both G protein and alternative pathways, certain instances may be further skewed from our traditional understanding of G protein coupling.
This is certainly the case for angiotensin II type 2 receptor (AT
2
R), which has been shown to function largely independent of both G protein and arrestin-mediated pathways (
64
). Surprisingly, structures of the active AT
2
R show that H8 of the receptor binds within its own intracellular binding cleft, which likely occludes the interaction of G proteins and arrestins (
65
). Whether AT
2
R represents a unique case in GPCR function remains to be seen, yet other receptors may adopt similar mechanisms to tune their signaling profile that render them incompatible for Gα-CT-based purification.
Conclusions and future directions
The Gα-CT resin approach exploits the conserved binding mechanism between GPCR and Gα α5 helix and thus should prove applicable to most GPCRs with further development. While ligand affinity–based techniques can successfully purify GPCRs (
7
,
8
,
66
)—they are tailored toward a single GPCR–ligand pair and require a different column matrix for each ligand. In contrast, by making the Gα-CT the affinity “ligand,” many different receptors of interest could be induced to bind Gα-CT resins, simply by using their specific agonists. This selectivity could be further enhanced using inverse agonists targeted toward the receptor of interest to drive specific elution from the resin (an expansion of the approach demonstrated for β
2
AR in
Fig. 6
).
The Gα-CT resins also allow receptors without purification tags to be purified and thus could be used as an enrichment step in an existing protocol to facilitate isolation of WT receptors from native tissues (as demonstrated in
Figure 2
,
Figure 3
,
Figure 4
). If coupled to analytical techniques such as tandem mass spectrometry, trace amounts of WT receptors could be analyzed for post-translational modifications or interactions with affiliate signaling proteins.
Finally, this approach could be used in a myriad of ways to identify novel compounds that promote (or prevent) binding of a receptor of interest to Gα-CT resin. Putative GPCR agonists could be used to “fish out” orphan receptors directly from crude lysate. Compound libraries could be screened for high-affinity drugs that promote capture of a specific GPCR or class of GPCRs. Alternatively, prebound GPCRs challenged by compound libraries could help identify ligands that disrupt the active ternary complex, presumably those of high-affinity for the inactive state, or which act allosterically.

Materials

Peptide coupling to NHS-activated resin
Peptides were coupled to NHS-activated Sepharose 4 Fast Flow resin (Cytiva; catalog no.: 17-0906-01) by reacting with the free N-terminal amino group of the peptide. In brief, the resin was preactivated in a Buchner funnel with cold ∼1 mM HCl (pH 2.5) by slowly passing 15 column volumes (CVs) over a resin bed using a vacuum manifold over the course of 30 min and then washed with 1 CV of coupling buffer. The resin was reacted with the desired peptide (predissolved in coupling buffer) at a ratio of 2 μmol peptide per milliliter of resin for 2 h at room temperature with nutation in an appropriately sized screw cap polypropylene tube to yield a final coupling mixture of approximately 1:1.5 (v/v) resin:peptide solution. Excess peptide was removed by repeatedly washing the resin with 5 CV of coupling buffer; each wash was performed in batch for 5 min at room temperature with nutation for a total of five washes. Unreacted functional groups on the resin were blocked with a solution of 200 mM glycine (pH 8) overnight at 4 °C with nutation. The next day, the resin was transferred to a vacuum manifold and washed with 5 CV of resin wash A (pH 4) followed by 5 CV of resin wash B (pH 8). This process was repeated for a total of five cycles, alternating between wash buffers A and B for each cycle. Finally, the resin was washed with 10 CV of storage buffer and stored in the same buffer as a 50% slurry (v/v) at 4 °C.
Regeneration of resin for reuse
Resin was regenerated for reuse as follows. Resin is first washed with 5 CV of the respective purification buffer (either Rho or β
2
AR buffer) containing 1 M NaCl; then 10 CV acid regeneration buffer; and then 10 CV storage buffer. It was then transferred as a 50% slurry (v/v) in storage buffer and stored at 4 °C until further use. We have routinely reused batches of resin >10 times without a noticeable loss of yield. See
Table S1
for additional details on buffers.

Gα

The Gαt-CT resin bed was prepared by transferring 0.4 ml of Gαt-CT resin (as ∼50% slurry in storage buffer) to a clean 1.5 ml polypropylene chromatography column (Bio-Rad; catalog no.: 7326008) and pre-equilibrated with 5 CV of cold binding buffer. Binding of rhodopsin to the resin was initiated by light activation (>500 nm light for 30 s while on ice), whereas control samples were maintained under dim-red light for the duration of the experiment. Gravity-flow binding was performed on ice by passing the DDM-solubilized ROS directly over the resin. Batch binding was performed by applying 1 CV of solubilized ROS supernatant directly to the resin-containing column (capped), diluted to 1.5 ml with PBSSC, and nutated at 4 °C for 30 min. After binding, the resin was washed with 20 CV of Rho purification buffer.
For peptide displacement elutions, rhodopsin was eluted immediately after washing by subsequent addition of 0.5 CV fractions of Rho purification buffer containing 200 μM exogenous Gαt-CT peptide. For rhodopsin eluted with 11-

-retinal, additional washes containing 0.6 μM 11-

After capping, 0.5 CV of the same buffer was carefully applied to the resin and allowed to sit for >2 h at 4 °C in darkness before collection. Purification of apo-opsin was performed as described previously, with the exceptions of exogenous ATR supplied in ethanol at final concentrations ranging from 10 μM, 1 μM, 0.1 μM, 0.01 μM, or VEH during the batch binding step.
