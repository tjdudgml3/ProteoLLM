Introduction
Dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM), one of the most common forms of cardiomyopathy and the main cause of heart failure, is characterized by ventricular chamber enlargement and systolic dysfunction. Despite a great deal of progress in the treatment in recent years, the prognosis of DCM patients remains poor, with high risks of death, hospital readmission, or need for cardiac transplant, which causes an enormous burden to families and society.
1
Therefore, early diagnosis and precise risk prediction are crucial for the clinical management of DCM patients.
In recent years, significant advances in imaging technology and genetic tests have provided useful tools for clinical practice.
2
Additionally, circulating biomarkers are convenient and important tools that can assist in decision making. Well‐known indicators (e.g., cTnT and NT‐proBNP) and emerging biomarkers (e.g., soluble ST2 and galectin‐3) have been employed to monitor the disease.
3
Nevertheless, the diagnosis and risk prediction of the disease remain challenging. Therefore, more valuable biomarkers for the diagnosis and prognosis of DCM are urgently needed.
In this study, we presented a series of lines of evidence from the discovery and verification of candidate protein biomarkers through proteomics and enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) analyses.
Methods
Study population and protocol
The diagnosis of DCM was defined according to the latest European Society of Cardiology proposal.
4
A total of 238 DCM patients with New York Heart Association functional class II‐IV who were consecutively admitted to the Department of Cardiology in our hospital between January 2017 and January 2019 were included in the study. Patients, when not contraindicated, were receiving standard medical treatment consisting of diuretics (93.2%), β‐blockers (91.2%), angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers (89.1%), digitalis (37.2%), amiodarone (22.7%), trimetazidine (50.9%), and anticoagulants (23.6%).
The participants without a previous history of heart failure with normal left ventricular ejection fraction by echocardiography and normal level of NT‐proBNP were defined as control subjects. Sixty‐five control subjects were consecutively enrolled from Department of Health Examination in our hospital between January 2017 and January 2019.
DCM patients and control subjects were excluded if they had (1) coronary heart disease; (2) congenital or valvular heart disease; (3) myocarditis and pericarditis; or (4) other diseases such as malignant tumours, autoimmune diseases, acute infection, severe hepatic disease, or end‐stage renal disease.
Blood analysis and echocardiography of all subjects were examined by physicians at the time of study entry. Fasting whole blood was obtained from each participant at the time of study entry, and the sera were stored at −80°C for later proteomic analysis and ELISA. All samples were anonymized. The study design is shown in

Proteomic analysis
Data‐independent acquisition (DIA) detection and data processing: Serum protein was extracted and digested with trypsin according to the FASP protocol.
5
After high pH reverse‐phase separation, the peptides separated by liquid phase chromatography were sprayed into the nanoESI source and then entered into a tandem mass spectrometer Q‐Exactive HF X (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA) for DDA (data‐dependent acquisition) mode detection. Subsequently, the same nano‐LC system and gradient were used for DIA analysis. MaxQuant (Max Planck Institute of Biochemistry version 1.5.3.30) and Spectronaut (
https://biognosys.com/software/spectronaut
) were used for DDA and DIA data analysis.
6

MSstats version 4.1.2.1 (MacCoss Lab, Washington DC, USA) screened differentially expressed proteins according to a difference multiple ≥2 and
P
< 0.05 as the criteria for determining significant difference.

Multiple‐reaction monitoring assay and data processing
Samples were digested with Trypsin Gold (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). Multiple‐reaction monitoring was performed on a QTRAP 6500 mass spectrometer (SCIEX, Framingham, MA, USA) equipped with LC‐20AD nanoHPLC system (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) as described in previous investigation.
9
Skyline software (
http://skyline.maccosslab.org/
) was used to integrate the raw file generated by QTRAP 6500. We use MSstats version 4.1.2.1 (MacCoss Lab, Washington DC, USA) with the linear mixed‐effects model. The
P
values were adjusted to control the false discovery rate at a cutoff of 0.05. All proteins with a
P
value below 0.05 were considered significant.
8
,

ELISA detection
The levels of peripheral S100A4 and S100A8/A9 complex were measured by ELISA (Ray‐Biotech, Norcross, USA; R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA), and the concentrations of serum S100A12, IL‐1β, and TGF‐β were measured by ELISA (Boster, Wuhan, China) following the manufacturer's instructions. All samples were measured in duplicate.
Follow‐up and outcome ascertainment
Follow‐up was initiated when the patients were enrolled and ended in February 2022. The median follow‐up time was 33.5 months (interquartile range 11–49 months). The follow‐up was conducted through periodic outpatient interviews and scripted telephone interviews with the patients or their families every month. Major adverse cardiac events (MACEs), including cardiac death or readmission for heart failure, were documented. Cardiac death was defined as death directly resulting from failure of the cardiac system. The endpoint was defined as the first occurrence of at least one of the abovementioned adverse events.
Statistical analysis
Analyses were performed with SPSS version 16.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Owing to nonnormality, NT‐proBNP was Lg‐transformed for subsequent analyses. Continuous variables were tested for normal distribution by the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and are presented as the mean ± SD. Statistical analysis of normally distributed data was performed using an unpaired Student's
t
‐test between two groups. Nonnormally distributed data were analysed using the Mann–Whitney
U
test for continuous variables and are presented as median (interquartile range). Categorical variables were expressed as percentages and analysed using the chi‐square test. Spearman rank correlation was performed to study the association between S100A4, S100A8/A9, S100A12, and other parameters. Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared by the log‐rank test. Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was used to examine independent factors for predicting MACEs in DCM patients. In addition, the receiver operator characteristic (ROC) curve was depicted by the area under curve (AUC) with 95% confidence interval (CI), and the comparison of ROC curve of was performed with MedCalc Statistical Software version 14.8.1 (MedCalc Software Ltd, Ostend, Belgium). A value of
P
< 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Identification of the differential serum proteins between the dilated cardiomyopathy and control groups
To find novel discriminative markers, DIA proteomics was performed in the screening set and identified 1561 proteins and 7271 peptides. Among the identified proteins, based on two filtration criteria (fold change ≥2 and
P
value <0.05), a total of 76 proteins were differentially expressed in the DCM group compared with the control (
Table

). To validate these differentially expressed proteins from DIA, multiple‐reaction monitoring analysis was further conducted and succeeded in identifying five proteins in total. < 0.05) (

Discussion
DCM is a complex disease with high mortality and readmission. Therefore, finding more valuable biomarkers would give great impetus to improve strategies for optimal clinical management of the disease. In this study, we detected protein expression profile in the sera of patients with DCM by using proteomic analysis and verified significantly elevated S100A4, S100A8/A9, and S100A12 levels in patients with DCM. Moreover, the combination of S100A4, S100A8/A9, and S100A12 was superior to any single indicator in the diagnosis of DCM. Furthermore, S100A4 rather than S100A8/A9 and S100A12 exhibited a significant prognostic value for MACEs among patients with DCM.
The S100 protein family consists of 25 members and shares a common structure defined in part by the Ca
2+
binding EF‐hand motif. They could appear in cells and the circulating blood. Extracellular S100 proteins interact with the pattern recognition receptors Toll‐like receptor 4 and receptor for advanced glycation endproducts and have been implicated in the control of a wide number of intracellular and/or extracellular functions, including regulation of cell death, proliferation, differentiation, migration/invasion, inflammation in different cell types.
10
Emerging evidences have demonstrated that elevated levels of S100A4, S100A8/A9, and S100A12 in the patients with inflammatory disorders, such as arthritis,
11
,
12
inflammatory bowel disease,
13
,
14
and systemic lupus erythematosus.
15
Circulating S100A8/A9 levels are significantly elevated in acute myocarditis.
16
Increased S100A12 is present in the patients with coronary artery disease.
17
Serum levels of S100B are higher in chronic heart failure patients.
18
These findings reveal that S100 proteins may serve as additional tools in the diagnostic workup of cardiovascular diseases. In present study, serum proteomic analysis and elisa assay displayed that serum levels of S100A4, S100A8/A9, and S100A12 were significantly higher in DCM patients than in control subjects, indicating these three proteins may be novel biomarkers for DCM. ROC analysis showed the combination of these three indicators had a superior ability to distinguish DCM patients from controls than any singer indicator, suggesting that the combination of these three indicators has a greater diagnostic value for DCM.
In addition, S100 proteins provide important prognostic information in patients with cardiovascular diseases. S100A1 is believed to be a potential prognostic biomarker or therapeutic target to improve the myocardial damage induced by exhaustive exercise.
19
The S100B rs9722 locus SNP is associated with chronic heart failure risk in a Chinese Han population.
20
Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis patients with higher serum S100A4 levels have significantly worse prognosis than those with low serum levels.
21
High S100A12 expressers exhibited significantly shorter survival and increased mortality than low S100A12 expressers in the patients with pulmonary hypertension.
22
In this study, our findings showed that the DCM patients with elevated S100A4 levels had worse prognosis and that S100A4 was an independent predictor of MACEs. However, we failed to confirm S100A8/A9 and S100A12 as significant predictors of MACEs within the whole follow‐up duration.
It remains unclear regarding the effects of S100A4, S100A8/A9, and S100A12 and underlying mechanisms in the pathogenesis of DCM. It is known that myocardial fibrosis is common in patients with DCM. S100A4 has recently been discovered as a potential factor implicated in fibrotic diseases. S100A4 is involved in transcriptional regulation of matrix metalloproteinases and E‐cadherin
23
and modulates cardiac fibrosis via p53 function and Wnt/β‐catenin pathway.
24
,
25
It has been reported that S100A4 improves the proliferation of mouse embryonic fibroblasts by activating epidermal growth factor receptor/ErbB2 receptor signalling
26
and increases the expression of α‐SMA through activating of c‐Myb and S1P pathway.
23
Besides, oxidative stress are believed to be potential contributing factors to DCM.
27
S100A8/A9 and S100A12 have reported to be associated with oxidative stress. S100A8/A9 induces mitochondrial respiratory dysfunction in cardiomyocytes by downregulating NDUF gene expression with subsequent mitochondrial complex I inhibition.
28
S100A12 up‐regulates the production of inflammatory cytokines (TNF‐α, IL‐1β, and IL‐6), disrupts the balance of oxidation‐antioxidant factors (malondialdehyde, superoxide dismutase, and glutathione), and then activates cardiomyocytes pyroptosis upon hypoxia‐reoxygenation induction.
29
Nevertheless, the exact mechanisms require further exploration.
Limitations
Our study has limitations. First, all patients were from a single center, so the results may not be universally generalized. Second, serum samples were collected at the beginning of the study and were not dynamically collected during the period of follow‐up, which may induce other significant trends not captured in the current analysis. Last but not least, we assessed diagnostic utility of S100A4, S100A8/A9, and S100A12 in discriminating DCM from healthy control rather than from the population without DCM, which may reduce the clinical practice value. More high‐quality, large‐scale studies are needed to confirm the diagnostic and prognostic utility of S100A4, S100A8/A9, and S100A12 among the patients with DCM.
Conclusions
DCM patients had increased S100A4, S100A8/A9, and S100A12. S100A4, S100A8/A9, and S100A12 may be additional diagnostic tools for human DCM recognition, and the combination of these three indicators helped to improve the accuracy of a single index to diagnose DCM. In addition, S100A4 was identified as a significant predictor of prognosis in patients with DCM. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to reveal that circulating S100A4, S100A8/A9, and S100A12 should be promising biomarkers for the management of DCM.
