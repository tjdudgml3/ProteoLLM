1
Introduction
Electronic cigarettes (ECs) emerged in the early 2000 s as a safer alternative to traditional cigarettes, with global patent protection granted in 2007(
Regan, Promoff, Dube, & Arrazola, 2013
). These devices were promoted as a safer alternative to traditional cigarettes, intended to aid smoking cessation efforts (
Caponnetto et al., 2012
,
Sapru et al., 2020
). A global study reveals that approximately 68 million individuals currently use ECs (
Jerzyński, Stimson, Shapiro, & Król, 2021
). In Saudi Arabia, the prevalence of EC use is concerning, with 26 % of young adults reporting EC use at least once in their lifetime(
Althobaiti & Mahfouz, 2022
).
Electronic cigarettes (ECs) are electronic devices powered by batteries that aerosolize a liquid containing flavored nicotine(
Cao et al., 2021
). Apart from nicotine, the liquid in ECs also contains glycerol, propylene glycol, and flavoring agents, all of which have been identified as potential contributors to EC-induced toxicity. (
Caponnetto et al., 2012
,
Sapru et al., 2020
).
Electronic cigarettes (ECs) expose users to a reduced number of toxic substances compared to conventional cigarettes (
Cao et al., 2021
), However, numerous studies have documented adverse health effects associated with EC use, particularly in the respiratory and cardiovascular systems(
Espinoza-Derout et al., 2022
,
Jonas, 2022
). EC exposure has been linked to respiratory conditions such as bronchitis, asthma, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease(
Garcia-Arcos et al., 2016
). Common respiratory symptoms associated with EC use include chronic cough, sputum production, wheezing, dyspnea, chest pain, hemoptysis, and pleuritis(
Blagev et al., 2019
). Several case reports have demonstrated a strong association between EC use and respiratory illnesses (
Sommerfeld et al., 2018
,
Thota and Latham, 2014
,
Wolf and Richards, 2020
). In the United States, an outbreak of over 2,500 cases of lung injury related to electronic cigarette or vaping product use was documented within a five-month period, leading to the term “electronic cigarette or vaping product use-associated lung injury” (
Krishnasamy et al., 2020
,
Layden et al., 2020
). These reports have spurred scientific investigations to study the toxicity of EC products across various biological systems.
Animal studies have demonstrated that exposure to electronic cigarettes (ECs) leads to respiratory damage. Rats exposed to ECs exhibit increased susceptibility to laryngeal mucosa hyperplasia and metaplasia(
Salturk et al., 2015
). Additionally, ECs induce oxidative stress and inflammation in mouse lung tissues by reducing glutathione levels and increasing pro-inflammatory cytokine levels. (
Lerner et al., 2015
).
In vitro
studies have demonstrated that ECs have toxic effects comparable to traditional cigarettes, particularly on apoptosis, necrosis, and DNA damage in epithelial cells (
Yu et al., 2016
). The effect of EC exposure on the microRNA transcriptome reveals activation of cellular oxidative stress genes, such as GPX2, NQO1 and HO-1(
Solleti et al., 2017
). The presence and concentration of specific components in EC solutions significantly modulate the release of pro-inflammatory mediators and cytokines in cells, including HaCaT and A549 cells. (
Cervellati et al., 2014
). Exposure of H292 human bronchial epithelial cells to various EC solution components disrupts cellular metabolic activity and increases cytokine release, such as IL-1β and IL-6 (
Leigh, Lawton, Hershberger, & Goniewicz, 2016
). Primary human epithelial cells exposed to flavored EC liquid with and without nicotine revealed that nicotine-free EC liquid promoted the release of pro-inflammatory mediators, such as IL-6, while the addition of nicotine magnified this effect(
Wu, Jiang, Minor, & Chu, 2014
). Additionally, the flavoring agent cinnamaldehyde, commonly used in EC solutions, causes significant damage to respiratory immune cells(
Clapp et al., 2017
). Collectively, these findings highlight the substantial influence of EC components on the toxicological responses associated with EC use.
Metabolomics is a comprehensive analytical approach for studying metabolic alterations in biological systems, providing detailed insights into the physiological status and responses of organisms to toxicants. This powerful tool has been extensively utilized in toxicology to gather mechanistic data on toxicity(
Alasmari et al., 2022
,
Robertson et al., 2011
). Metabolomics facilitates the identification of disrupted biochemical pathways caused by toxicants (
Assiri et al., 2019
), and plays a crucial role in discovering novel biomarkers of exposure or effects(
Wei et al., 2022
). Despite its utility, limited research has investigated the impact of electronic cigarettes (ECs) on the human metabolome. A study demonstrated that exposure to EC liquids impairs glucose metabolism and ATP synthesis in lung epithelial cells, while significantly altering the levels of histidine, arginine, xanthine, and certain lipids(
Aug et al., 2015
,
Woodall et al., 2020
). However, there is a lack of comprehensive studies characterizing the global effects of EC exposure on cellular metabolism. In this study, our aim was to elucidate the specific metabolic changes associated with specific and regulated EC exposure in human lung epithelial cells, identifying potential biomarkers of exposure or toxicity and providing a foundational mechanistic understanding of EC-induced toxicity. Untargeted metabolomics approach was employed using high-resolution liquid chromatography quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (LC-QTOF-MS/MS) to detect and quantify specific metabolic changes associated with EC exposure.
2
Materials and Methods
2.1
Cells and growth
A549 human alveolar basal epithelial cells were obtained from ATCC and grown in a DMEM complete media (Gibco) containing 10 % fetal bovine serum (Gibco). The cells were incubated at 37 °C in a 95 % air, 5 % CO2 incubator.
2.2
Vapor extraction
To mimic the available marketed EC liquid, an EC refill liquid was prepared by dissolving 25 mg of nicotine salt (Sigma) in 1 mL of a 50/50 vegetable glycerin/propylene glycol-flavored vehicle. EC vapor was extracted using a cigarette smoking machine (CSM-STEP, CH Technologies) to mimic physiological exposure using the CORESTA protocol, which delivers one puff of vaporized EC liquid every 30 s for 15 min. The EC vapor was extracted using serum-free DMEM. The concentration of the extract was set to 100 %. Serial dilutions of these extracts were performed in serum-free medium (10–100 %).
2.3
GC–MS analysis
To measure the nicotine concentration, EC vapor was extracted in HPLC water for 0, 15, 30, and 45 min. The nicotine concentration of the extracts was analyzed using GC/MS (single quadrupole Clarus 600 T). An elite 5MS column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 µm film thickness) was used, with helium as the gas carrier at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. The compound peaks were identified by comparing their spectra with those of a standard pure nicotine solution.
2.4
Cell viability
Cell viability was determined using a previously described protocol(
Yang, Liu, Zhao, Ma, & Qi, 2020
). Briefly, cells were seeded in 96-well plates for 24 h and treated with increasing concentrations of the EC extract for 24 h; then, the treatment medium was removed. MTT reagent was added and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. The MTT reagent was removed, the cells were washed, isopropyl alcohol was added, and the cells were incubated for 15 min at room temperature. The absorbance was read at 450 nm using a plate reader (BioTek). Cell viability was determined as a percentage of the control group.
2.5
Reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation
ROS generation was detected using 2′,7′-dichloro-dihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCF-DA). Following the manufacturer’s protocol (MedChemExpress), the intensity of DCF-DA fluorescence was measured at an excitation wavelength of 485 nm and an emission wavelength of 535 nm using a plate reader (Biotek). Hydrogen peroxide was used as the positive control for ROS production. Data are presented as fold changes compared to the control group.
2.6
Metabolomics
2.6.1
Metabolite extraction
At 80 % confluency, A549 cells were treated with increasing concentrations of the EC extract for 24 h in serum-free medium. Cellular metabolites were extracted as described previously (
Sapcariu et al., 2014
). Briefly, the medium was removed, and the cells were washed with 0.09 % sodium chloride. An ice-cold extraction solution containing methanol/water at a 1:1 ratio was added; then, cells were scraped on ice and transferred into Eppendorf tubes containing equal amounts of chloroform. Cells were vortexed for 20 min at 4 °C and centrifuged at 16,000 g for 5 min, until polar, non-polar, and interphase layers formed. Then, 300 µL of the polar layer was transferred into a new tube, vacuum dried, and saved at −80 °C for analysis of polar metabolites.
2.6.2
LC-MS analysis
LC-MS analysis was performed using LC-QTOF-MS/MS connected to an electrospray ionization source. Compact II ESI/QTOF/MS (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany) was used for MS analysis, and small metabolites were detected according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Prior to the experiment, the Q-TOF instrument was calibrated by directly injecting a 10 mM sodium formate solution. Additionally, the instrument was internally and externally calibrated during sample analysis. Internal calibrations using a combination of three lock-mass compounds (C5H4N4, C12H18F12N3O6P3, and C18H19O6N3P3F24, with accurate masses of 121.043, 621.022, and 921.002, respectively) was infused from the source reservoir directly into the MS, and external calibrations were used. The mass accuracy was < 0.5 ppm. An Acquity UHPLC CSH (1.7 μm 2.1 mm 100 mm) C18 column (Waters, USA) on a UHPLC system (UltiMate 3000, Thermo Scientific, Germany) was used to separate 5 μL of samples. The column temperature was kept at 40 °C, and the autosampler temperature was maintained at 4 °C. Mobile phase A contained 0.1 % formic acid in water, and mobile phase B contained 0.1 % formic acid in acetonitrile. The flow rate was set at 0.35 mL/min and elution conditions for UPLC were run as follows: 0–0.5 min 1 % of solvent A, 0.5–3.5 min of 1–53 % A, 3.5–7.5 min of 53–70 % A, 7.5–9 min of 70–90 %, then maintained for 9–13 min of 90 % A, and 13.1 to 15 min returned to 1 % A. MetaboScape software version 4.0 (Bruker), was used to obtain raw MS and MS/MS data. The metabolites were annotated based on the exact mass of the molecules, molecular formula, and patterns of MS/MS fragmentation in relation to available libraries, such as Mona Export LC-MS-MS. The annotated metabolites were obtained from the human metabolome database metabolite library. The RSD% of CUDA (the internal standard in LC) was 4 %, and was used to validate the method and the reproducibility of the instrument.
2.6.3
Metabolomics analysis
Multivariate statistical analysis of the metabolite data was performed using MetaboAnalyst 5.0. Partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA). Variable importance in projection (VIP) analysis was performed to identify differential metabolites between the groups and to rank the metabolites based on their importance. Venn diagrams were generated for metabolites with > 1,000 peak intensities found in 2/3 of the samples using the Bioinformatics & Evolutionary Genomics website. Volcano plots were constructed, and metabolites with fold change > 2 and FDR < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Pathway analyses were performed using the 'Homo sapiens' library, with hypergeometric test parameters for over-representation analysis and relative betweenness centrality for topological analysis. Pathway analysis provided the raw
p
-value calculated from the enrichment analysis, and was adjusted using the False Discovery Rate.
2.7
Statistical analysis
Statistical analyses were performed using the GraphPad Prism software. All data are expressed as means ± SEM values. ANOVA was performed to determine statistical significance. Statistical significance was set at
p
< 0.05.

Results
3.1
Assessment of vapor extracts
To validate the smoking machine extraction method and the other parameters involved, EC liquid containing 25 mg/mL nicotine in a flavored vehicle containing 50/50 glycerol/propylene glycol was vaporized as described in the Methods section. EC vapor was extracted in HPLC water at four time points (0, 15, 30, and 45 min). vapor extract was assessed for nicotine concentration using GC–MS. The extraction method successfully delivered nicotine to HPLC water, and the concentration increased in a time-dependent manner (

3.2
Cell viability and ROS production
To study the cytotoxicity of EC extracts, A549 cells were grown until they reached 80 % confluency and then treated with increasing concentrations of EC vapor extracts (10 %-80 %) for 24 h; then, cell viability was evaluated via the MTT assay. The results demonstrated that EC exposure increased the cytotoxicity of A549 cells in a concentration-dependent manner. (
Fig. 2
A). The effect of EC exposure on ROS generation was studied using DCF-DA. Exposure to EC for 24 h increased ROS production, especially at 40 % EC (
Fig. 2
B). However, ROS production decreased by 80 %, and which could be due to the increased number of dead cells at EC concentrations > 40 %. These results revealed the role of ROS production as a potential mechanism of cytotoxicity induced by EC exposure.

3.3
Metabolomics
Investigating the effects of EC on disturbing the metabolic homeostasis is essential for understanding the impact of EC on human health. To comprehensively assess the metabolic effect of EC on cellular metabolism, A549 cells were grown to 80 % confluence and exposed to 0, 10 %, 40 %, and 80 % EC extracts for 24 h. Polar metabolites were extracted, purified, and subjected to LC-MS/MS using an untargeted approach.
In total, of 1,468 metabolic features were identified, normalized, and auto- scaled for further processing. PLS-DA score plots showed a complete separation of the treatment groups and adequate classification (
Fig. 3
A), implying that exposure to each concentrations of EC significantly induced unique changes in metabolic features. The VIP scores (
Fig. 3
B) demonstrated that the top 40 metabolites had a substantial impact on group segregation in the PLS-DA. Higher VIP values indicated that the metabolites significantly drove group segregation. Some metabolites, such as isoleucine and guanosine, showed a dose- dependent increase in abundance. In contrast, riboflavin, and N-acetyl-L-aspartic acid showed dose-dependent decreases in their relative concentrations. These metabolic changes could be employed as sensitive biomarkers for EC vapor exposure and explain the primary toxic insult induced by EC exposure. Detailed changes in the top 40 metabolites were presented via heatmap analysis, demonstrating the effect of increasing EC concentrations on the VIP metabolites (
Fig. 3
C).

Pathway analysis
To investigates the relevance of identified metabolites to known biological pathways, 122 metabolites were annotated via the MetaboScape software, and their details are listed in
Table 3S
. Pathway analysis was performed using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway mapping tool. A total of 47 metabolic pathways were identified, including aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis, glutathione metabolism, arginine and proline metabolism, pyruvate metabolism, nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism, fatty acid biosynthesis, phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan biosynthesis, and D-glutamine and D-glutamate metabolism. (
Table 1
). For example, 13 metabolites were linked to the aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis, including L-histidine, L-phenylalanine, L-arginine, glycine, L-aspartate, L-serine, L-methionine, L-lysine, L-isoleucine, L-tryptophan, L-tyrosine, L-proline, and L-glutamate. 5/38 metabolites in the arginine and proline metabolic pathways were identified. Additionally, the four metabolites involved in fatty acid biosynthesis were hexadecenoic acid, tetradecanoic acid, dodecanoic acid, and octanoic acids. Two metabolites were linked to the nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism: nicotinamide and L-aspartic acid. Six metabolites were identified for glutathione metabolism: reduced glutathione, oxidized glutathione, glycine, L-glutamate, pyroglutamic acid, and spermidine. Pyruvate metabolism included pyruvate, (S)-malate, and (S)-lactate. Three metabolites each were listed in D-glutamine and D-glutamate metabolism (D-glutamine, L-glutamate, pyroglutamic acid), as well as and phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan biosynthesis (L-phenylalanine, phenylpyruvic acid, and L-tyrosine). These data show that exposure to EC could induce cytotoxicity by disrupting fundamental metabolic pathways.

Discussion
Smoking is a risk factor for a wide range of illnesses, including cardiopulmonary diseases(
Castaldelli-Maia, Ventriglio, & Bhugra, 2016
). Owing to this concern, ECs have been marketed as safer alternatives to traditional cigarettes. EC use has increased significantly over the past several years(
Patel et al., 2016
). Studies have shown that inhaling EC vapor causes toxicity by increasing oxidative stress and inflammatory reactions(
Lerner et al., 2015
). Furthermore, xenobiotic compounds produced by ECs or traditional cigarettes may affect the metabolomic profile in humans(
Górna et al., 2020
,
Gu et al., 2016
,
Hsu et al., 2013
,
Sun et al., 2012
). Therefore, emerging evidence suggests a possible risk link between exposure to ECs and several pathological lung conditions, including lung cancer(
Bracken-Clarke et al., 2021
). Taken together, it is crucial to understand the mechanism of EC toxicity; therefore, we conducted a comprehensive study to evaluate the effect of EC vapor on A549 human lung epithelial cells by investigating general toxicity mechanisms, such as cellular proliferation and ROS production. We performed an untargeted metabolomic analysis to gain a better understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying EC toxicity.
Oxidative stress is one of the primary mechanisms underlying EC-induced toxicity(
Ji et al., 2016
,
Lerner et al., 2015
). The increase in oxidative stress is due to increased production of ROS, decreased antioxidant capacity, or both. Glutathione is an essential component of the antioxidant system, as it maintains the integrity of cellular functions and homeostasis(
Meister & Anderson, 1983
). For instance, glutathione prevents protein oxidation by reversibly glutathionylating the active thiols in proteins(
Kwon et al., 2019
; R. M.
Liu & Gaston Pravia, 2010
). Decreased glutathione levels negatively affect cellular functions, including mitochondrial bioenergetic parameters and ATP synthesis(
Ferrington et al., 2017
,
Qin et al., 2014
,
Sreekumar et al., 2016
). In this study, EC exposure increased ROS production and affected the relative concentrations of glutathione-associated metabolites. These findings support the notion that EC-induced cellular toxicity is primarily caused by augmented oxidative stress. Notably, elevated ROS levels are a step within the sequence of cellular toxicity, and the use of antioxidants such as N-acetylcysteine to intervene with ROS can hinder subsequent toxic responses.
A recent study showed that exposure to EC liquids inhibited glucose metabolism and ATP synthesis in lung epithelial cells(
Woodall et al., 2020
). Additionally, unlike cigarette smoke condensate, ECs induce distinctive metabolic changes in human bronchial epithelial cells. For instance, ECs significantly increased histidine, arginine, and xanthine levels; however, this effect was not observed with cigarette smoke condensate, suggesting that ECs have a specific effect on metabolism(
Aug et al., 2015
). Importantly, every EC liquid contains ingredients distinct from nicotine. Consequently, it is challenging to reach a broad conclusion regarding EC toxicity. In this study, we evaluated the composition of EC liquids and determined their nicotine concentrations. However, it is important to note that real-world situations are more complex than the findings presented here, as the EC liquid components vary significantly with the type of EC device and patterns of usage. Interestingly, each concentration of the EC extract in our study led to a distinctive pattern of metabolic changes. For instance, 13, 29, and 20 unique metabolites were identified in 10, 40, and 80 % EC samples, respectively. These results should be further elucidated to clarify the relationship between increasing concentrations of EC vapor and the presence of different metabolites. This study highlights the importance of metabolomics in identifying minute changes in metabolism that may play significant roles in toxicological responses.
In the present study, several essential metabolic pathways, including glutathione metabolism, fatty acid biosynthesis, amino acid metabolism, aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis, and nicotinate and nicotinamide levels, were affected by EC exposure. However, the relationship between EC exposure and these pathways requires further investigation. For example, aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases are a key group of enzymes involved in translating the genetic code, as they attach tRNA to their cognate amino acids(
Martinis, Plateau, Cavarelli, & Florentz, 1999
). These enzymes are ubiquitously expressed in all cell types and sustain protein synthesis and cell viability(M. C.
Park et al., 2012
; S. G.
Park et al., 2005
). Several studies have shown that oxidative stress dysregulates aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis and causes amino acid mismatches, which significantly contributes to the pathogenesis of numerous diseases(
Lant et al., 2018
,
Netzer et al., 2009
). In the present study, EC exposure increased ROS levels, with aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis being the most significantly affected pathway. Therefore, we suggest that EC exposure affects aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis by increasing cellular ROS production.
Our study also showed that EC exposure dysregulated numerous amino acids, including arginine and proline metabolism; phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan biosynthesis; and D-glutamine and D-glutamate metabolism. However, the role of these amino acids in EC toxicity is largely unknown. Previous reports have indicated that amino acids are crucial components of the redox system. For instance, it has been suggested that the proline metabolic pathway regulates the redox balance and cell viability(
Phang, 1985
). Tryptophan is another key amino acid involved in controlling oxidative stress, inflammation, and immune responses(J.
Liu, Zhang, Li, Yan, & Zhang, 2019
). Glutamine prevents oxidative stress-induced apoptosis and damage by enhancing glutathione synthesis(
Zhang et al., 2018
). We believe that targeted metabolic analysis of amino acids, especially those contributing to oxidative stress, will lay the foundation for expanding our knowledge of the mechanisms of EC-induced cytotoxicity.
To gain additional and specific insights into the mechanism of EC toxicity, further research may explore multi-omics approaches, such as proteomics and genomics. Targeted metabolomics is needed to investigate the absolute changes in key metabolites found in this study, such as proline, glutamate, and pyruvate, to better characterize the effect of EC-induced cytotoxicity.
5
Conclusions
In conclusion, this study demonstrates that exposure to electronic cigarette (EC) vapor leads to a concentration-dependent decrease in cell viability and an increase in reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. Metabolomic analysis reveals significant changes in essential metabolic pathways, particularly those involved in glutathione and energy metabolism, highlighting the impact of EC vapor on cellular function. These findings underscore the sensitivity of metabolomics in elucidating the mechanisms underlying cellular toxicity and lay the foundation for targeted metabolomic studies focusing on specific metabolites associated with oxidative stress and energy metabolism. Overall, this research highlights the urgency of investigating the health risks associated with EC use and provides valuable insights for researchers and regulators in understanding the adverse effects of EC vapor exposure on cellular metabolism.
