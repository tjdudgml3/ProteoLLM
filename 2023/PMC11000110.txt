5
Introduction
The Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV) is a ubiquitous gamma herpesvirus implicated in the pathogenesis of lymphoproliferative disorders and several human cancers. These include the undifferentiated form of nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC), Burkitt lymphoma (BL), classical Hodgkin lymphoma (cHL), several non-Hodgkin lymphomas (NHL), and a small fraction of gastric carcinomas [
1
]. Epithelial and B-cells are the primary targets of EBV infection, and the virus latently infects memory B cells to establish a life-long, persistent infection in human hosts. Several viral products expressed during the EBV latent cycle have known oncogenic activities and contribute to EBV-associated malignancies, including the latent membrane proteins (LMPs) 1 and 2, the Epstein–Barr virus nuclear antigen 1 (EBNA1), the Epstein-Barr virus-encoded small RNAs (EBERs), and, more recently, EBV-encoded microRNAs (miRs) [
2
].
EBV was the first human virus identified to encode miRNAs, clustered in two regions of the viral genome: the BamHI fragment H rightward open reading frame 1 (miRs-BHRF) and the BamHI-A rightward transcripts (miRs-BART) [
3
]. EBV encodes 25 precursor miRs (pre-miRs), producing 48 mature viral miRs [
4
]. The EBV miRs-BART is located within intronic segments of the genomic locus encoding the BART transcripts, an extensively spliced region expressed in all viral latency programs. Although EBV-encoded miRNAs are not required to produce new virions, they play critical roles in regulating the viral lytic cycle and the pathogenesis of some EBV-associated cancers [
5
,
6
].
The EBV miRs-BART are highly expressed in nasopharyngeal and gastric carcinomas, contributing to malignant transformation and tumor progression [
7
]. Conversely, the expression levels of miRs-BART in lymphoid malignancies are lower than in epithelial cells, and their impact on lymphomagenesis needs to be properly elucidated [
8
]. In 2011, Ramakrishnan and colleagues reported that the expression of EBV miR-BART9 was associated with increased levels of LMP1 (both mRNA and protein), promoting cell proliferation in nasal NK/T-cell lymphomas (NKTL) [
9
]. Furthermore, EBV miRs-BART 7 and 9 reportedly reduce the expression of the
BCL6
gene in EBV-associated Diffuse Large B cell Lymphoma (DLBCL), implicated in the modulation of cell cycle and differentiation, apoptosis, and the levels of local inflammation [
10
]. In this study, we initially assessed EBV miRs BART7 and BART9 expression levels across a panel of lymphoid cells constitutively infected by EBV. Subsequently, we examined the
in vitro
effects of knocking down the viral miRNAs in EBV-positive Akata cells. We also evaluated cells harboring edited and non-edited (wt) EBV genomes regarding their global proteome profiles. Overall, the results demonstrate that knocking down EBV miR-BART7 and 9 influences the behavior of Akata cells in cultivated
in vitro
and affects cellular processes that mirror crucial mechanisms of lymphomagenesis and cancer progression
in vivo
.

6.2
CRISPR/Cas9 single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs)
The sgRNAs sequences to target EBV miRs-BART 7 and 9 were designed using the online tool CHOP-CHOP (
http://chopchop.cbu.uib.no
) [
11
]. The hairpin sequences containing the PAM sequences were retrieved from miRbase (
http://www.mirbase.org
), and the assembled viral genome from EBV-infected Akata cells (
GenBank Acc#: GCA_900004315.1
) was used as a reference to design the sgRNA targeting the EBV miR-BART7 or EBV-miR-BART9. The sequences, on-target and off-target analyses conducted by CHOP-CHOP algorythms are provided in the
Supplementary Material Figs. S1 and S2
. Customized DNA oligonucleotides with extra nucleotides for restriction enzyme-mediated cloning (sequences in Supplementary Material,
Table S3
) were synthesized by Thermo Fisher. Annealing of sgRNAs resuspended in Tris-EDTA buffer (TE, 10 mM Tris, 0.1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0) was performed using the T4 Ligase using the reaction buffer and conditions suggested by the manufacturer (New England Biolabs, NEB, Ipswich, MA, USA).
6.3
Lentiviral vectors for sgRNA expression
The recombinant vectors LentiGuide-Puro-BART7 and LentiGuide-Puro-BART9 were prepared to perform CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genomic editing of EBV miRs-BART 7 and 9. These constructs were generated by cloning the respective sgRNAs into the LentiGuide-Puro backbone (
Addgene #52963
), a gift from Feng Zhang [
12
]. Briefly, the backbone vector was digested with
BsmBI
(New England Biolabs), followed by gel purification using the QIAquick PCR & Gel Cleanup Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA). The linearized vector and the sgRNAs heteroduplexes were ligated overnight at 16 °C with the T4 DNA ligase (New England Biolabs), and the ligation product was used to transform One Shot™ Stbl3™ cells (Thermo Fisher). The plasmids were isolated from transformed bacteria, following Sanger sequencing with the hU6.F primer and the 3500 Series Genetic Analyzer device (Thermo Fisher) to verify the constructs.
Lentiviral particles harboring the sgRNAs for CRISPR/Cas9 edition of EBV miRs-BART 7 and 9 were obtained after transfection of HEK293-FT (
RRID: CVCL_6911
) cells with the Lentiviral Puro-based constructs and the packaging vectors psPAX2 (
Addgene #12260
) and VSVG (
Addgene #14888
). The transfection was performed with Lipofectamine™ 3000 (Thermo Fisher) in 6-well plates. DNA-liposome complexes were prepared in OptiMEM™ (Gibco), homogenized, and added dropwise into each well containing attached HEK293-FT cells cultivated in DMEM medium (Gibco). The transfection media was replaced with fresh RPMI after 24h incubation at 37 °C in a 5 % CO
2
atmosphere. The lentiviral particles were collected at 48 and 72h post-transfection, filtered through 0.45 μM PES syringe filters, and directly used for lentiviral transduction.
The Akata-EBV/Cas9 cells were transduced with fresh stocks of lentiviral particles harboring the sgRNA vectors targeting EBV miRs BART 7 or 9 for CRISPR-Cas9-mediated viral genome edition. After 48h post-transduction, the cells received fresh RPMI medium supplemented with 10 μg/mL puromycin (Invitrogen) for eukaryotic selection. Stably transduced cells were obtained after 2 weeks of selection, propagated, and then routinely maintained in culture with RPMI medium supplemented with 10 % FBS, 1 μg/mL puromycin, 500 μg/mL geneticin, and 10 μg/mL blasticidin.
6.4
Validation of CRISPR/Cas9-mediated edition of the EBV genome
The T7 endonuclease I (T7EI) assay was used to assess the efficiency of the CRISPR/Cas9 edition of the EBV genome [
13
]. Specific primers for amplification of a region flanking the predicted target sites for CRISPR/Cas9 edition were designed and used for PCR amplification (Supplementary Material,
Table S3
) with the Platinum™ High Fidelity Taq DNA Polymerase (Thermo Fisher). The PCR product was separated using 1.5 % agarose gels and purified using the QIAquick PCR & Gel Cleanup Kit (Qiagen). Purified PCR products (300 ng) were then denatured at 95 °C for 5 min and cooled at room temperature to form heteroduplexes using NEBuffer2.1 1x (New England Biolabs). The T7 endonuclease I (New England Biolabs) was then added to the heteroduplexes and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C. DNA fragments were separated on 1 % agarose gel, and the cleavage products were evaluated using a Reverse Mass DNA Ladder in Gel Loading Dye Orange (New England Biolabs).
We used the Tracking of Indels by DEcomposition (TIDE) analysis using the online tool available at
https://tide.nki.nl
[
14
] to evaluate the spectrum and frequency of targeted mutations generated in the Akata-EBV/Cas9 cells by CRISPR/Cas9-mediated edition of the EBV genome. Briefly, the total DNA extracted from Akata-EBV/Cas9 cells was amplified (primers and experimental conditions on
Supplementary Material Table S2
), and the PCR product was purified as previously described and then subjected to Sanger sequencing. The samples were sequenced in parallel using the forward and reverse primers, and their sequence trace files were uploaded to the online TIDE tool to analyze their indel/HDR spectrum.
6.5
Assessment of expression of selected human and EBV genes
Total RNA was isolated using the TRIzol™ (Invitrogen) reagent, followed by purification with the Qiagen RNeasy column (Qiagen), treatment with DNAse (Sigma-Aldrich), and quantification by spectrophotometry using the NanoDrop® device (Thermo Fisher). The complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher), according to the manufacturer's instructions. Custom reverse transcription primers (Canopy Biosciences, St. Louis, MO, USA) were added to total RNA samples before cDNA synthesis to amplify the EBV miRs-BART under evaluation.
The assessment of gene expression was performed by quantitative real-time Polymerase Chain Reaction (qPCR) using the GoTaq® qPCR Mix (Promega) in the 7500 Fast Real Timer PCR system (Applied Biosystems®). SNORD47 and SNORD48, or HSP90 and RPS13, were used as reference endogenous controls to assess the expression of miRs and mRNAs, respectively. The comparative Ct method [
14
] was used to estimate the relative expression levels. The expression of the viral lytic proteins BZLF1 (EBV Zta protein; NCBI gene ID: 3783744) and BLLF1 (EBV gp350 protein; NCBI gene ID: 3783713) was assessed in parental Akata-EBV/Cas9 (wt) and its EBV miRs-BART mutants. All primers sequences and the qPCR conditions for these assays are indicated in the Supplementary Material (
Table S3
).
6.6
Luciferase reporter assays
EBV miRs-BART 7-3p and 9-3p activity was evaluated in Akata-EBV/Cas9 cells by luciferase reporter gene assay with constructs based on the psiCheck-2 backbone (Promega). 3’-UTR sequences with miRNA-binding sites (
Supplementary Material Table S3
) and the respective negative controls (scramble, SCR) were cloned into the psiCheck-2 vector according to the manufacturer's instructions. Furthermore, we conducted a luciferase reporter assay to assess the NF-κB activity using a pcDNA3.1-based vector expressing the firefly luciferase under the control of NF-κB elements (pcDNA3.1-NFκB-luc gifted by Prof. Ethel Cesarman), and the commercial vector pGL4.73, which constitutively express
Renilla
luciferase (Promega). Akata-EBV/Cas9 cells expressing psiCheck-2-BART7-3p, psiCheck-2-BART9-3p, pcDNA3.1-NFκB-luc, and the pGL4.73 were generated by transfection with Lipofectamine™ 3000 (Thermo Fisher). The assessment of luciferase activity was performed 48h post-transfection using the Dual-Glo® Luciferase Assay System (Promega), according to the manufacturer's protocol in the GloMax® Discover Microplate Reader device (Promega).
6.7
Cell viability and proliferation measurements
An MTS-based assay using the CellTiter 96® Aqueous One Solution (Promega, Madison) was performed to assess the viability and proliferation rates of Akata-EBV/Cas9 cells
in vitro
, following the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, 2 × 10
3
cells were seeded onto 96-well plates in complete medium and 20 μL of the CellTiter reagent. After incubation, the formazan dye produced by viable cells was quantified by measuring the absorbance at 490 nm on the Bio-Rad Model 680 microplate reader device (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA).
6.8
Proteomic analysis
Whole-cell lysates from Akata-EBV/Cas9 (wt and mutants for EBV miRs-BART 7 or 9) were extracted using a detergent-free buffer containing 8 M urea, 75 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.2, 50 U/mL benzonase, 2 mM MgCl
2,
and the cOmplete™, EDTA-free Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Roche Life Science, Indianapolis, IN, USA), as previously reported [
15
]. Briefly, cell pellets were washed twice with DPBS, resuspended in lysis buffer (100 μL for 1 × 10
6
cells), and sonicated (30s, 5s on, and 5s off) at 20 % amplitude with the Vibra-Cell™ VCX 750 device (Sonics & Materials Inc., Newtown, CT, USA). Afterwards, the samples were incubated at 4 °C for 30min and centrifuged at 16,000×
g
for 30 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected and quantified using the Bio-Rad Protein Assay Kit and the Quick Start Bovine Serum Albumin Standard Set (Bio-Rad) and stored at −80 °C until use.
Protein samples (50 μg) were diluted in 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate buffer (AmBic, NH
4
HCO
3
; Sigma-Aldrich), 0.2 % (m/v) RapiGest SF (Waters™, Milford, MA, USA), and incubated for 60 min at 37 °C. After surfactant incubation, 100 mM dithiothreitol (DTT, 2.5 μL; Sigma-Aldrich) and 300 mM 2-iodoacetamide (IAA, 2.5 μL; Sigma-Aldrich) were added to the samples, followed by incubation for 30 min at room temperature. Samples were digested with Sequencing Grade Modified Trypsin (0.1 μg/μL, Promega) for 16 h at 37 °C. Upon digestion, reactions were acidified with 5 % trifluoroacetic acid (TFA, v/v) and incubated for 90 min at 37 °C to stop proteolysis. Samples were centrifuged at 16,000×
g
at 4 °C for 30 min to remove insoluble debris. The supernatant containing soluble peptides was collected and dried overnight using the Vacufuge® Concentrator Plus (Eppendorf, Hamburg, HH, Germany). According to the manufacturer's instructions, peptides were desalted, purified, and concentrated using the Pierce™ C18 Spin Columns (Thermo Fisher).
Mass spectrometry (MS) was performed to identify and quantify differentially expressed proteins in Akata-EBV/Cas9 miRs-BART 7 and 9 mutants. The obtained peptides were subjected to MS, followed by sequencing analysis using a liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry system consisting of the UltiMate 3000 LC liquid nanochromatography system (LC Packings DIONEX, Sunnyvale, CA, USA) coupled to the Q-Exactive™ device (Thermo Fisher). LC-MS/MS was conducted according to the parameters previously described [
16
]. Proteins expressed were identified with the PatternLab software version 4.0.0.84, using the UniProt proteome of
Homo sapiens
(UniProt ID: UP000005640) as a reference [
17
]. Fragment mass tolerance was set to 40 ppm with a false discovery rate (FDR) of ≤1 %. Identified proteins were submitted to parsimony analysis, and quantitation analysis using extracted-ion chromatogram (XIC) was processed using the MetaboAnalyst (
https://www.metaboanalyst.ca
) web-based tool, with FDR of ≤5 %. Also, we used the RawVegetable software for quality control of raw MS files [
18
,
19
].

Results
7.1
The CRISPR/Cas9-mediated edition of EBV miRs-BART efficiently knockdowns the expression of the viral microRNAs in Akata-EBV/Cas9 cells
The EBV miR BARTs 7 and 9 are located within the intronic regions of the BART cluster 2 in the viral genome (
Fig. 1
a). To disrupt the generation and maturation of the targeted EBV miR-BARTs using CRISPR/Cas9 technology, two sgRNAs for each EBV miR-BART were designed to target different regions within the 5’-Dicer processing site of the precursor viral miRNA (pre-miRNA) sequences (
Fig. 1
b and
c
). Akata-EBV/Cas9 cells transduced with the lentiviral vectors (LentiGuide-Puro-BART7 and LentiGuide-Puro-BART9) were evaluated for insertion and deletion mutations (
indels
) caused by CRISPR/Cas9 edition of the EBV genome. The T7 endonuclease I mismatch detection assay revealed two expected cleavage bands (300 and 200 bp) for both sgRNAs, identifying the indels generated in the EBV genomes (
Fig. 1
d and
e
). DNA sequencing of a flanking region encoding ebv-miR-BART7 and ebv-miR-BART9 also showed several deletions within the predicted sites (
Fig. 1
d and
e
). The
indels
profile obtained by TIDE analysis showed that the most frequent mutations found in the viral DNA of Akata-EBV/Cas9 cells were deletions spanning 1–6 nucleotides, indicating a CRISPR/Cas9 genome edition efficiency above 70 % for the targeted EBV miRs-BARTs (Supplementary Material,
Fig. S1
).

7.3
Knocking down EBV miRs-BART induces viral lytic genes
Given the known influence of EBV BART miRNAs on the viral lytic cycle and their significant implications for viral persistency and immune escape
in vivo
[
20
], we aimed to specifically investigate the effects of knocking down EBV miR-BARTs 7 and 9 on crucial viral regulators involved in the latent-lytic switch. To achieve this, we evaluated the transcriptional levels of the EBV immediate-early lytic gene BZLF1 (encodes the viral transcription factor Zta) and the late lytic gene BLLF1 (encodes the structural viral protein gp350) in Akata-EBV/Cas9 cells infected with EBV-wt or the miR-BARTs mutants by RT-qPCR. As shown in
Fig. 3
, panels
C
and
D
, Akata-EBV/Cas9 cells with EBV ΔBART7 and ΔBART9 showed a robust increase in the expression of both lytic genes compared to cells infected with the non-edited viral genome (EBV-wt).
7.4
EBV miR-BART7 and BART9 knockdown mutants show distinct protein expression signatures
To comprehensively delineate the impact of suppressing EBV BART 7 and BART9 in our Akata-EBV/Cas9 experimental model, we thoroughly examined the overall cellular protein expression via LC-MS/MS. Subsequently, we performed pathway enrichment (PE) analyses, focusing on proteins exclusively expressed in cells infected with EBV miR-BART7 or miR-BART9 mutants compared to the cell line infected with wt EBV genomes (control).
Overall, 2118 and 2065 proteins were identified in ΔBART7sg2 and ΔBART9sg2 mutants, respectively (Supplementary Material, Datasets 1 and 2). The analysis revealed 59 significantly up or down-regulated proteins in cells infected with EBV ΔBART7sg2 and 44 in cells infected with EBV ΔBART9sg2, compared to the control (
Table 1
,
Table 2
). Notably, cells infected with the EBV ΔBART7sg2 mutant exhibited enrichment of proteins involved in RNA-binding, nucleotide biosynthesis, and nucleotide metabolism pathways, while the EBV ΔBART9sg2-infected cells demonstrated enrichment in proteins associated with oxidoreductase and catalytic activities. (
Fig. 4
).

Discussion
In this study, we sought to investigate the effects of knocking down miRs BART 7 and 9 in Akata cells constitutively infected by EBV to get insights into the importance of viral miRNAs in the pathogenesis of EBV-associated lymphomas. We opted to investigate Burkitt's lymphoma (BL) as our model due to its robust connection with EBV infection and epidemiological significance, considering its African variant. Overall, the influence and mechanisms of EBV-encoded miRs in lymphomas associated with EBV infection remain largely unknown. Upon screening various human cell lines derived from EBV-associated lymphoproliferative diseases, we observed significant heterogeneity in the expression levels of EBV miRs BART 7 and 9 (
Supplementary Fig. S2
). The variations are likely due to the inherent biological characteristics unique to each cell line since the distinct expression levels of miR-BARTs in EBV-associated lymphomas probably reflect the different growth stimuli and the cellular microenvironment profiles [
6
,
21
]. For instance, in a study comparing the molecular profile of endemic, sporadic, and immunodeficiency-associated BL, EBV-positive tumors that express high viral miRs levels were correlated with increased activation of proliferation pathways [
22
].
We created mutant EBV genomes within Akata-EBV/Cas9 cell lines using CRISPR/Cas9 technology to study the effects of miRs BART7 and BART9 in these BL-derived cells. The EBV genome edition was highly efficient, as demonstrated by the T7EI mutation assays, Sanger sequencing, and TIDE analysis. Furthermore, the specificity for EBV miRs BART7 and 9 was further validated by both qPCR and the luciferase reporter assay, showing that we successfully knocked down the viral microRNAs, as shown in
Fig. 2
.
Although CRISPR/Cas9 edition is commonly used to knock genes out, complete abrogation of EBV miRs BART7 and BART9 in the current experimental setting is not feasible because Akata cells can harbor multiple copies of the EBV genome [
23
]. As assessed by RT-qPCR and luciferase reporter assays, we observed a drastic reduction in miRs BART7 and BART9 expression in Akata-EBV/Cas9 mutants. Our findings align with existing literature, indicating that editing multicopy EBV episomes with CRISPR/Cas9 typically results in a low percentage of unedited viral genomes [
24
]. However, utilizing CRISPR/Cas9 to suppress the expression of EBV miRs BART provides a more enduring and efficient inhibition than other methods, like RNA interference technologies, frequently used for repressing miRs expression [
25
].
Downregulating the expression of EBV miRs BART7 and BART9 in Akata-EBV/Cas9 cells led to a considerable decrease in cell viability and proliferation rates compared to cells containing the unaltered wild-type EBV genome. Our results align with existing literature on NPC- and BL-derived cell lines, suggesting that the expression of BART7 and BART9 enhances cell proliferation and apoptosis resistance [
9
,
26
,
27
]. We also found that both BART7 and BART9 mutants display an increased expression of the viral transcripts for the lytic proteins Zta and gp350 (EBV genes BLZF1 and BLLF1, respectively). EBV BART9 has been documented to modulate the EBV lytic cycle by transcriptionally inhibiting multiple elements within the BCR and FOXO3 signaling pathway. Furthermore, it's worth noting the considerable sequence homology shared between EBV BART9 and the human miRNA miR-141 [
28
,
29
], a crucial regulator of various signaling pathways implicated in the development and progression of human cancers [
30
]. In summary, these results support the hypothesis that the expression of EBV miRs BART-7 and 9 regulate cell proliferation and apoptosis and maintain viral latency in malignant B-cells derived from BL.
The global proteomic analysis performed in this study revealed that the inhibition of EBV miR-BART7 resulted in a significant reduction in the expression of ribosomal proteins, notably members of the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS), such as RPS27A and UBA52, as well as the RPS21 and EIF3L translational initiation factors. Intriguingly, previous reports have highlighted the role of RPS27A in promoting LMP1-mediated cell proliferation, acting as a regulator of this viral oncoprotein by stabilizing through a proteosome-dependent pathway [
31
]. The modulation of LMP1 by EBV miRs likely plays a crucial role in enhancing the aggressiveness of EBV-associated cancers by regulating signaling pathways linked to cell survival and proliferation and tumor progression phenomena [
32
]. Overall, EBV can exploit the UPS pathway by inhibiting proteasomal degradation to avoid the loss of critical viral proteins or inducing the degradation of unwanted cellular proteins [
33
]. For instance, the UBS may be inhibited in an EBNA1-mediated way, aiding immune evasion of EBV-infected cells by impeding the generation of antigenic peptides presented to T cytotoxic cells via MHC-I molecules [
34
].
Additionally, knocking down the EBV miR-BART7 leads to a notable increase in the expression of RNA-binding proteins (RBPs), pivotal regulators of mRNA biogenesis and maturation, crucially involved in both normal hematopoiesis and hematological malignancies [
[35]
,
[36]
,
[37]
]. Proteins directly linked to ribosome biogenesis are notably affected, including STRAP, WDR12, GAR1, and ISG20. The pathway enrichment analysis showed several hits for ribonucleoprotein complex binding, RNA binding, and nucleic acid binding. Previous reports have indicated that EBV commandeers several host RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) and ribosomal proteins during the lytic cycle, allowing the translation, maturation, and regulation of viral proteins [
38
,
39
]. In our model, the suppression of miR-BART7 led to a significant increase in the expression of the Zta and gp350 lytic genes. This data suggests that EBV miR-BART7 might selectively target human RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) to regulate lytic replication, potentially contributing to maintaining persistent infection.
Conversely, suppressing EBV miR BART9 led to a significant decrease in the expression of several mitochondrial enzymes, including ACSF3, ALDH5A1, HADHB, IDH2, OXCT1, and PDHB. These enzymes are particularly enriched in fatty acid metabolism, primarily associated with oxidoreductase activity pathways. The reprogramming of fatty acid metabolism plays a critical role in hematological malignancies, promoting tumorigenesis and cell survival. Previous studies showed that knocking down HADHB led to reduced proliferation of LCL-K and MD901 cells; furthermore, in different cancer cell models, the overexpression of IDH2 and OXCT1 has been linked to an increase in cell survival, proliferation, autophagy, and chemoresistance [
[40]
,
[41]
,
[42]
,
[43]
]. Our findings reveal a potential involvement of EBV BART9 in modulating lipid metabolism, suggesting that EBV products might manipulate these metabolic pathways to enhance the survival and proliferation of transformed B-cells [
44
,
45
].
While the data provided here yields valuable insights, it is essential to acknowledge three aspects in our experimental design that limit drawing broad conclusions about the role of EBV miRs BART7 and 9 in lymphomagenesis. First, the CRISPR/Cas9 approach aimed for knock-down rather than complete suppression of viral miRs expression. As a result, the observed effects reported here occur within the context of residual expression of the viral miRNAs in the Akata cells. Secondly, due to resource constraints, this study focused on a single BL cell line instead of a set of diverse BL cell lines. Thirdly, the Akata-EBV/Cas9 cells subjected to viral genome editions targeting the EBV mir-BARTs 7 and 9 were assayed without selection of single cell clones. Consequently, our analysis is based on a pool of cells with a varied mixture of edited and non-edited EBV genomes within each cell. Even taking these limitations upfront, the data generated in this study demonstrate that the miR-BARTs 7 and 9 have the potential to modulate critical phenomena involved in the development and progression of cancers, paving the way to characterize further the biological effects of viral microRNAs in EBV-associated diseases.
Our findings indicate the potential involvement of EBV miRs BART7 and BART9 in Akata EBV-positive cells. They demonstrate an association with increased cell proliferation and viability, modulation of the lytic cycle and expression of immune checkpoints, and manipulation of essential cellular pathways such as RBPs, ubiquitin/proteasome, and fatty acid metabolism.
