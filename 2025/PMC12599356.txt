Introduction
The autosomal recessive disorder cystic fibrosis (CF) is the most common life-shortening genetic condition in the Caucasian population. While CF affects multiple organ systems, the leading cause of mortality is respiratory failure due to a persistent chronic pulmonary infection [
1
]. One of the predominant respiratory pathogens in this context is
Pseudomonas aeruginosa
, alongside
Haemophilus influenzae
and
Staphylococcus aureus
. Chronic infection with
P. aeruginosa
affects an estimated 25–30% of the adults with CF in Europe and the United States [
2
,
3
].
The management of pulmonary infections in pwCF is advancing through improved airway clearance techniques, the use of antibiotic combinations, and the introduction of CFTR modulators, all of which impact the incidence and prevalence of respiratory infections. Although recent studies have reported a decline in
P. aeruginosa
prevalence among pwCF following the increased availability of CFTR modulators, complete elimination of
P. aeruginosa
has not been achieved, underscoring the need for continued vigilance [
2–6
]. Ledger et al. for example, reported that chronic
P. aeruginosa
infection in pwCF perseveres after CFTR modulation: clonal lineages of
P. aeruginosa
persisted for up to 21 months after treatment with elexacaftor-tezacaftor-ivacaftor or tezacaftor-ivacaftor, especially in pwCF with severe lung disease [
7
].
Chronic pseudomonal infection is a major contributor to airway inflammation, a key factor in the progression of CF lung disease. Infection with this bacterium is associated with reduced pulmonary function and an
increased risk of severe respiratory complications [
8
,
9
]. The host–pathogen interactions are very complex, involving bacterial virulence factors and various host cell types, triggering inflammation [
10
].
P. aeruginosa
produces virulence factors that enhance its fitness and survival within the host. Among these factors, proteases can cleave key host protein substrates and influence various biological processes, including modulation and evasion of the host immune responses to favor bacterial survival [
11
,
12
]. These proteases have been mainly studied for their role in causing direct tissue damage and are primarily recognized as virulence factors in the pathogenesis of acute infections [
13–15
]. Most of the evidence supporting the role of pseudomonal proteases comes from standard
in vitro
experimental settings or animal models of acute infection with laboratory strains of
P. aeruginosa
. However, the use of laboratory strains, such as PAO1, may not accurately represent the genetic background of strains isolated from chronically infected pwCF, which may lead to (lack of) expression of key mediators that contribute to inflammation. These laboratory strains do not reflect the adaptations and phenotypic changes that occur during long-term colonization in CF airways [
16
,
17
]. Common adaptations to the chronic lifestyle include loss of flagellar motility, overproduction of the exopolysaccharide alginate resulting in a biofilm lifestyle and reduced virulence (reviewed in [
10
,
18
]).
Accurately replicating key aspects of the host microenvironment during infection is essential to improving the accuracy and relevance of experimental models [
19
]. In this regard, a synthetic CF sputum medium (SCFM2) has been designed to simulate the physicochemical properties of the CF lung environment.
P. aeruginosa
grown in SCFM2 displays a gene expression profile that closely resembles that of
P. aeruginosa
in the sputum of pwCF [
17
,
20
]. Furthermore, traditional two-dimensional cell cultures fall short in capturing the structural and functional complexities of the human airway epithelium. To overcome this limitation, physiologically relevant three-dimensional (3D) cell culture models have been developed to better mimic the airway microenvironment [
19
,
21
]. Due to their higher throughput compared to air–liquid interface cultures, which are often considered the gold standard in respiratory research, these models represent a good alternative [
22
]. In the present study, a rotating wall vessel (RWV)-derived 3D lung alveolar epithelial A549 cell model was used (
Figure 1
).

The RWV-derived 3D A549 model recapitulates
in vivo
-like phenotypic and functional features of the parental lung epithelium – including a single-cell layer, cell polarity, barrier function, and physiologically relevant cytokine production in response to pro-inflammatory stimuli [
23–25
]. By employing well-established and reliable tools, such as clinical isolates, SCFM2, and an
in vivo
-like cell culture model, the
in vivo
lung environment can be mimicked, thereby enhancing the study of chronic CF infections.
The role of proteases in establishing and maintaining chronic infections is not well understood, with some studies suggesting that proteases induce inflammation, while others indicate that they compromise the inflammatory response [
14
,
15
,
26–39
]. Therefore, this study aims to assess the role of
P. aeruginosa
proteases in inflammation during chronic lung infection.
Materials & methods
Bacterial species, culture conditions, and filtrate preparation
P. aeruginosa
isolates were previously collected from sputum samples of two pwCF (patients 1 and 6) [
40
]. Patient 1 and patient 6 had an early (1 year) and late ( >19 years)
P. aeruginosa
chronic infection, respectively. Throughout this paper, isolates are designated
using the format X/Y, where X indicates the isolate number and Y corresponds to the patient number. For instance, 6/1 indicates isolate 6 from patient 1.
In addition, two reference strains of
P. aeruginosa
were used, i.e. PAO1 wild-type (WT) and AA44 (a late CF sputum isolate) [
41
,
42
]. For select studies, PA14 WT, PAO1 lasB:Tn, and PA14 lasB:Tn were used. The mutants were derived from a transposon (Tn) mutant library [
42–44
].
P. aeruginosa
isolates were cultured in the Luria-Bertani (LB) broth for 16–18 h at 37°C with shaking at 250 rpm. All overnight
P. aeruginosa
cultures (PAO1 WT, PAO1 lasB:Tn, PA14 WT, PA14 lasB:Tn, AA44, and the 14 clinical isolates) were diluted in SCFM2 to an OD
590
of 0.005. This OD
590
is equivalent to a bacterial inoculum of 5 × 10
7
colony-forming units per mL (CFU/mL). The diluted overnight
P. aeruginosa
suspensions were further diluted 10:1 to obtain a working suspension with a bacterial inoculum of 5 × 10
5
CFU/mL. The inoculum was confirmed for all
P. aeruginosa
clinical isolates. There were no significant inoculum differences between strains (
p
= 0.46, Kruskal–Wallis test, Figure S1A). To prevent dilution of the SCFM2 medium, a maximum volume of 20 µL of the overnight suspension was added to 0.5 mL of the SCFM2 medium. SCFM2 was prepared as described previously [
20
], with the modification that mucin was autoclaved instead of UV-sterilized. The final pH of the medium was adjusted to 6.8 by adding sterile 1 M NaOH [
45
,
46
]. Given that
P. aeruginosa
growth under oxygen-restricted conditions has been observed in pwCF with advanced lung disease, the bacterial suspensions were incubated for 48 h in micro-aerophilic conditions using a hypoxia chamber, and growth was assessed by plating (Bactrox, Sheldon Manufacturing Inc., Cornelius, OR, US) (3% O
2
, 5% CO
2
, and 92% N
2
at 37°C) [
47
]. The medium control, i.e. an equal volume of SCFM2 medium without bacteria, was incubated under the same conditions.
To obtain the cell-free supernatant after 48 h incubation, the bacterial suspensions and the medium control were centrifuged (3500 rpm, 10 min) and the resulting supernatants were passed through a 0.22 µm filter. Supernatants were stored at −20°C. No repeated freeze/thaw cycles were performed for all samples.
Quantification of
P. aeruginosa
virulence factors
Proteolytic activity
The azocasein assay was used to test the proteolytic activity of the clinical isolates and reference strains [
48
]. Azocasein (Sigma-Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO, USA) solution (5 mg/mL) was prepared freshly in 0.1 M Tris-HCl buffer with pH 8.0. Azocasein solution (400 μL) was mixed with filtered cell-free supernatant (400 μL) and incubated at 37°C for 1 h under shaking conditions (250 rpm). The reaction was stopped by adding 100 μL of 10% (w/v) trichloroacetic acid, followed by centrifugation (13000 rpm, 15 min). From the resulting supernatant, 100 μL was mixed with 625 nM NaOH (100 μL) in a flat-bottom 96-well plate. The OD was measured at 420 nm using the EnVision Multilabel Plate Reader (Perkin Elmer, Shelton, CT, USA). The same protocol was completed with a medium control mixed with azocasein solution, which was used as negative control. PAO1 WT was used as a positive control. Four technical replicates were conducted for each biological replicate.
Elastolytic activity
Elastase activity was assessed with the Elastin-Congo red assay. Elastin-Congo red (Elastin Products Co., Owensville, MO, USA) suspension (20 mg/mL) was prepared freshly in a 0.1 M Tris-HCl buffer, pH 7.6. Elastin-Congo red suspension (400 μL) was mixed with filtered cell-free supernatant (400 μL) and incubated at 37°C for 24 h under shaking conditions (250 rpm), followed by centrifugation (13000 rpm, 15 min). From the resulting supernatant, 200 μL was transferred to a flat-bottom 96-well plate, and the absorbance was measured at 492 nm with the EnVision Multilabel Plate Reader. To avoid saturated OD measurements, the supernatant was diluted in Milli-Q (MQ) water when necessary. The same protocol was completed with medium control mixed with Elastin-Congo red suspension, which was used as a negative control. PAO1 WT was used as a positive control. Four technical replicates were conducted for each biological replicate.

Bacterial genomic DNA was extracted using a glass-bead protocol as previously described [
49
]. Genomic DNA was extracted from 5 mL overnight cultures of the
P. aeruginosa
isolates grown in LB broth (Lab-M) at 37°C and 250 rpm.

Cytokine degradation assays
A degradation assay of interleukin-8 (IL-8), IL-1 beta (IL-1β), IL-6, IL-10, granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFα), and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1) was performed by exposing recombinant cytokine to
P. aeruginosa
cell-free culture supernatants.
A recombinant cytokine solution containing IL-8, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, GM-CSF, TNFα, or MCP-1 (Biolegend, San Diego, CA, USA) at a concentration of 10 ng/mL was prepared by resuspending the lyophilized powder in sterile distilled water. The reconstituted stock solution was aliquoted and stored at −80°C for later use. A working solution of 1 ng/mL was freshly prepared prior to each experiment and the wells of a 96-well flat-bottom plate (VWR International, Radnor, PA, USA) were filled with a recombinant cytokine solution (100 µL). Cell-free
P. aeruginosa
supernatant (100 μL) was added to the wells resulting in a final concentration of 500 pg/mL in each well. A mixture of recombinant cytokine with medium control was used for positive control. The plate was incubated for 4 h in micro-aerophilic conditions at 37°C. After incubation, Halt
TM
Protease Inhibitor Cocktail and 5 mM EDTA (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) were added to each well.
Samples were collected and stored at −80°C until assayed. No repeated freeze/thaw cycles were performed for all samples and cytokine stock solutions.
Three-dimensional lung epithelial cell culture model
The human adenocarcinomic alveolar epithelial cell line A549 (ATCC, CCL185) was cultured as an organotypic three-dimensional (3D) cell culture model using the rotating wall vessel (RWV) bioreactor, as described previously [
24
,
25
]. Briefly, A549 cells were cultured in GTSF-2 medium (Hyclone™, Logan, UT, USA) supplemented with 1,5 g/L sodium bicarbonate (Sigma-Aldrich), 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 2,5 mg/L insulin transferrin sodium selenite (Sigma-Aldrich) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich) as monolayers in T75 flasks. When confluent growth was obtained, the A549 cells were washed with Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and trypsinized with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cell viability and counting were performed using a 0.4% Trypan Blue solution (Sigma Aldrich) and a hemocytometer. A suspension containing 2 × 10
6
cells in a supplemented GTSF-2 medium was mixed with 0.25 g dextran beads coated with porcine-skin collagen (Cytodex®-3 microcarrier beads, Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA), transferred to a RWV (slow turning lateral vessel (STLV) and high-aspect rotating vessel (HARV), Synthecon, Houston, TX, USA), and cultured for 11 to 14 days to obtain differentiated 3D lung epithelial cultures, termed 3D aggregates. All cultures were incubated at 37 °C, 5% CO
2
.
In vitro
cell exposure assay
The experiment was set up by transferring 3D cell aggregates to a flat-bottom 96-well plate at a density of 2.5 × 10
5
cells/well in GTSF-2 medium without FBS or antibiotics. Cell viability and counting were performed on a small volume of cell aggregates as described above. The 3D cells (150 μL) were exposed to 100 µL cell-free supernatants of
P. aeruginosa
and filtered medium control for 4 h at 37°C in micro-aerophilic conditions (3% 0
2
, 5% CO
2
, 37°C). Supernatant (25 μL) from
P. aeruginosa
strain AA44 and recombinant human TNFα (InvivoGen, Toulouse, France) (10 ng/mL) was used as a positive control for inflammation. Based on preliminary assays showing degradation of recombinant TNFα (500 pg/mL) by isolates with proteolytic and
elastolytic activity, we used a 20-fold higher concentration (10 ng/mL) in 3D aggregates. This ensured a robust pro-inflammatory response while minimizing the potential signal loss due to degradation (IL-8 release was significantly higher in 6/1 + TNFα and 14/1 + TNFα compared to isolates alone (one-way ANOVA, Benjamini–Hochberg FDR 5%; data not shown)).
After incubation, supernatants from the 3D cells were collected and stored at −20°C until assayed. No repeated freeze/thaw cycles were performed for all samples.
Cytotoxicity assay
Previous research by our group observed
P. aeruginosa
interference with lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) quantification due to protease production. To solve this issue, Van den Bossche et al. developed and validated a modified protocol to assess cytotoxicity [
50
]. In this protocol, the release of LDH from the viable cell fraction adherent to the beads at the end of the exposure assay is used to determine cell viability. Briefly, cell viability is determined by first removing the supernatant of the culture, followed by rinsing twice with HBSS and lysing the viable attached cells with 1% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich). Subsequently, the Lactate Dehydrogenase Activity Assay Kit (Sigma-Aldrich) was used to determine the activity of LDH following the manufacturer’s instructions.
Inflammatory marker quantification
For the interleukin degradation experiments, IL-8, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, GM-CSF, TNFα, and MCP-1 levels were measured by a Human ELISA MAX
TM
Standard or Deluxe Set (Biolegend) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Optimal dilutions of the samples were made in MQ water. Afterward, values were expressed as a percentage of the signal obtained for positive control (i.e. the mixture of recombinant cytokine with medium control).
For
in vitro
cell exposure experiments, IL-8, IL-6, GM-CSF, and MCP-1 release was measured in the cell culture supernatant by a Human ELISA MAX
TM
Standard or Deluxe Set according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Optimal dilutions of the samples were prepared in GTSF-2 medium without FBS and antibiotics.
Proteomics
Sample preparation
We used a protocol based on amphipols for proteomics sample preparation, with in-house adaptations [
51
]. For each sample, 500 µL of bacterial supernatant was transferred into 1.5 mL tubes, and 55.55 µL of lysis buffer containing 10 mg/mL Amphipol A8-35 (APols, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 1 M triethylammonium bicarbonate (TEAB, pH 8.5, Sigma) was added. After vortexing, the samples were placed on ice for 30 min. Reduction and alkylation were carried out by adding 33.3 µL of 0.167 M tris(2-carboxyethyl) phosphine hydrochloride (TCEP) and 44.4 µL of 0.5 M 2-chloroacetamide (CAA), followed by incubation at 95°C for 10 min in the dark, while shaking at 750 rpm.
To precipitate the APols protein complexes, 10% formic acid was added until the pH was lower than 3.0, and the samples were centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 10 min at room temperature. The resulting pellet was resuspended in 55.55 µL of 1 M TEAB and transferred to a 96-well plate. Protein concentration was determined using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (Thermo Scientific), and 50 µg of protein was isolated from each sample for further processing.
Digestion began with a pre-digestion step using 0.5 µg LysC for 4 h at 37°C, followed by overnight digestion with 0.5 µg trypsin at 37°C. The reaction was stopped by acidification to 1% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA), and a final purification of peptides was performed using OMIX Pipette Tips with C18 resin (Agilent, CA, USA).
The OMIX tips were first equilibrated with 0.1% TFA in water/acetonitrile (ACN, 20:80, v/v) and then washed with 0.1% TFA in water. Peptides were captured on the tips by pipetting the sample up and down 20 times. The tips were subsequently washed 3 times with 0.1% TFA to remove impurities. Finally, the peptides were eluted twice from the tips by pipetting up and down 10 times in 100 µL of 0.1% TFA in water/ACN (40:60, v/v). The eluted peptides were transferred to MS vials and dried completely using vacuum centrifugation. After completing the sample preparation, the samples were stored at −20°C until liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis.
LC-MS/MS analysis
The LC-MS/MS analysis was done as described previously [
52
], with modifications. Briefly, the dried peptides were re-dissolved in 20 µL loading solvent A (0.1% TFA in water/ACN (99.5:0.5, v/v)) of which 5 µL was
injected for LC-MS/MS analysis on an Ultimate 3000 ProFlow nanoLC system in-line connected to an Orbitrap Exploris 240 mass spectrometer (Thermo). Trapping was performed at 20 μL/min for 2 min by loading solvent A on a 5 mm trapping column (Thermo scientific, 300 μm internal diameter (I.D.), 5 μm beads). The peptides were separated on an in-house produced column (75 µm × 250 mm), equipped with a laser pulled electrospray tip using a
p
-2000 Laser-Based Micropipette Puller (Sutter Instrument, CA, USA), packed in-needle with ReproSil-Pur basic 1.9 µm silica particles (Dr Maisch HPLC GmbH, Ammerbuch, Germany) kept at a constant temperature of 45°C. Peptides were eluted by a gradient reaching 26.4% MS solvent B (0.1% FA in ACN) after 80 min, 44% MS solvent B at 95 min, 56% MS solvent B at 100 min followed by a 5-min wash at 56% MS solvent B and re-equilibration with MS solvent A (0.1% FA in water). The flow rate was set to 250 nL/min.
The mass spectrometer was operated in data-independent acquisition mode, with automatic alternation between MS and MS/MS scans. Full-scan MS spectra were collected across a m/z range of 400–900 at a resolution of 60,000, using a normalized target value of 300% and a maximum injection time of 25 ms. Subsequently, 30 sequential quadrupole isolation windows, each 10 m/z wide, were applied for higher-energy collisional dissociation fragmentation at a normalized collision energy of 30%. For each isolation window, the trap was filled to a normalized target value of 2000% with a maximum injection time of 45 ms. MS/MS spectra were acquired in the Orbitrap analyzer at a resolution of 15,000 with a scan range of 200–1800 m/z without multiplexing. The isolation windows covered the 400–900 m/z range, with a width of 10 m/z using window placement optimization.
The polydimethylcyclosiloxane background ion at 445.120028 Da was used for internal calibration (lock mass), and QCloud has been used to control the instrument longitudinal performance during the project [
53
,
54
].
Raw spectral data analysis
LC-MS/MS runs of all samples were searched together using the DiaNN algorithm (version 1.9.1), library free. The search criteria included a false discovery rate (FDR) of 1% at both the precursor and protein level. Spectra were matched against the
Pseudomonas aeruginosa
PAO1 (Proteome ID: UP000002438) protein sequences from the UniProt database (release version June 2024), containing 5,563 sequences (
www.uniprot.org
). Enzyme specificity was set as C-terminal to arginine and lysine, also allowing cleavage at proline bonds with a maximum of two missed cleavages. Variable modifications were set to oxidation of methionine residues and acetylation of protein N-termini. Mainly default settings were used, except for the addition of a 400–900 m/z precursor mass range filter, and MS1 and MS2 mass tolerances were set to 10 and 20 ppm, respectively. Further data analysis of the shotgun results was performed with an in-house script in the R programming language, version 4.2.2 [
55
]. Protein expression matrices were prepared as follows: the DIA-NN main report output table was filtered at a precursor and protein library q-value cutoff of 1% and only proteins identified by at least one proteotypic peptide were retained. After pivoting into a wide format, iBAQ intensity columns were then added to the matrix using the DIAgui’s R package get_IBAQ function [
56
]. In total, 2,225 protein groups were reliably quantified by retaining only those protein groups with at least three valid PG.MaxLFQ values in at least one experimental condition.
Functional annotation
The gene names corresponding to the 2,225 protein groups were uploaded to The Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery (DAVID) [
57
,
58
]. Functional annotation was conducted using
Pseudomonas aeruginosa
PAO1 as the reference. A total of 79 proteases were identified based on the following annotation terms: Aminopeptidase, Proteolysis, Proteases, Metalloendopeptidase, and Endopeptidase Activity.
Prediction of protein subcellular localization
The subcellular localization of the identified proteases was retrieved from the Pseudomonas Genome Database (version 22.1) with
Pseudomonas aeruginosa
PAO1 (
Stover et al.
, 2000) as reference strain [
59
,
60
]. When multiple localizations were listed, the one with the highest confidence level was selected. In case of a tie, the localization closest to the extracellular region was selected.

Statistical analysis
ELISA data analysis was performed using GainData® Arigo’s ELISA calculator (Arigo Biolaboratories,
https://www.arigobio.com/elisa-analysis
). Standards were plotted using four-parameter logistic regression. Readings obtained within the linear region of the standard curve were used to calculate cytokine concentrations. After exploratory data analysis, it came to attention that the date of the degradation assay/
in vitro
cell assay and the date of the ELISA both resulted in systematic “batch” effects, i.e. all assays or ELISAs performed on a certain date were systematically higher or lower than the average. Before modeling, we combined the two dates (assay date and ELISA date) in a new categorical date variable.
To analyze the data of the interleukin degradation assays and
in vitro
cell assays, we ran a separate Tweedie generalized linear mixed model (with Tweedie index parameter restricted between 1 and 2) for each cytokine. These models are capable of modeling outcome data with a mean–variance relationship and with exact zeros in the data. They do so without having to resort to zero-inflated or hurdle approaches, which treat the data as a mixture of two distributions. To model the effect of strain on the cytokine concentration, we used strain as a (categorical) fixed effect and the new date variable as a random intercept. To analyze the effect of proteolytic and elastolytic activity on cytokine concentrations, we used the (log transformed) proteolytic and elastolytic activity as a continuous variable and the new date variable as a random intercept.
The models were fitted in R, with the glmmTMB package [
55
,
62
]. The residuals were checked with the DHARMa package, and the car package was used to assess the overall main effect (type II Wald Chi-square tests) [
63
,
64
]. Multiple testing, adjusted post hoc tests for contrasts between individual isolates, were performed using the multcomp package (which corrects for multiple tests by using a multivariate t-distribution) [
65
].
Statistical analyses of virulence factor quantification, the LDH assay, and the ELISA measuring the inflammatory response of the 3D lung model exposed to PA14 WT and PA14 lasB:Tn were performed using GraphPad Prism (Version 10;
https://www.graphpad.com
). The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to evaluate the normality of the data. If the data followed a normal distribution, an independent
t
-test, or a one-way ANOVA followed by a Dunnett’s Post Hoc test was subsequently performed. Non-normally distributed data were analyzed using a Mann–Whitney test or a Kruskal–Wallis test. To control for multiple testing, the Benjamini–Hochberg correction was applied with an FDR of 5% [
66
]. The 3D lung model’s response to a pro-inflammatory stimulus quantified via IL-8 ELISA was analyzed using a non-parametric permutational multivariate ANOVA (PERMANOVA), followed by post hoc permutation t-tests. Multiple test corrections were applied using Tukey’s Honest Significance Difference (THSD).
All experiments were conducted on at least biological triplicates. Graphical representations were created using GraphPad Prism and R (Version 4.4.2), utilizing R packages such as ggplot2 and pheatmap [
55
,
67
,
68
].
Results
Proteolytic activity of
P. aeruginosa
CF isolates and the role of lasR mutations in proteolytic activity
We first assessed the overall proteolytic activity of the culture supernatant of 14 clinical
P. aeruginosa
isolates from two pwCF and two reference strains (AA44, a late CF isolate; PAO1, a well-characterized protease-producing strain) (
Figure 2(A)
). In addition, the elastolytic activity (i.e. ability to degrade the host protein elastin) of these strains was assessed, which is for the main part mediated by the
P. aeruginosa
Elastase B (LasB) (

Discussion
P. aeruginosa
strains colonizing the CF lung face intense selective pressure from the host immune system and are exposed to variable nutrient and oxygen conditions and high antibiotic concentrations, leading to an accumulation of mutations over time [
72
]. Eventually, the lungs are colonized by a genetically heterogenous population adapted to the chronic infection lifestyle [
18
,
73
]. It is not fully understood which virulence factors of CF-adapted
P. aeruginosa
play a role in the chronic infection process. In this study, we focused on the role of proteases in the host inflammatory response given contradictory findings in literature [
14
,
15
,
26–39
].
The CF isolate collection tested here was found to contain both proteolytically active and non-active isolates, and this activity was mostly associated with a functional
lasR
gene. Although the selection of
lasR
-defective strains by the chronic CF lung environment is a well-established observation, these mutants are frequently found alongside isolates with a functional
lasR
gene [
70
,
71
]. For five out of the eight isolates without proteolytic/elastolytic activity in our study, a missense mutation was observed in
lasR
(i.e. Tyr56Cys). This mutation likely results in impaired LasR activation, as the highly conserved Tyr56 residue plays a crucial role in forming a hydrogen bond with the amide 1-oxo group of its N-acyl-homoserine lactone autoinducer [
74
,
75
]. For three isolates (i.e. 9/1, 13/6, and 14/6), no mutations in
lasR
were observed despite reduced proteolytic and elastolytic activity. To investigate this in more detail, we performed genotyping of the
lasB
gene to determine whether mutations or deletions resulting in an absent or dysfunctional LasB protein were responsible for the reduced activity. Only isolate 9/1 contained a missense mutation (Lys141Arg) in the
lasB
gene. Due to the conservative nature of this substitution and its location in the propeptide of LasB, the impact on the protein’s overall structure and function may be limited [
76
]. With isolates 13/6 and 14/6 harboring wild-type
lasR
and
lasB
, and isolate 9/1 wild-type
lasR
, a different reason must be underlying the absence of proteolytic activity in the culture supernatant of these isolates. Possible explanations for this phenotype are mutations in the
rhI
quorum sensing system,
lasI
synthase, or in regulatory elements affecting
lasR
expression [
13
,
77
,
78
].
In our study, we found that proteolytic and elastolytic activity results in an immunosuppressive phenotype of
P. aeruginosa
, hereby reducing the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines likely through degradation, even in the presence of the culture supernatant of a strongly pro-inflammatory
P. aeruginosa
strain (i.e. AA44) or TNFα. Vice versa, most isolates without proteolytic/elastolytic activity showed pro-inflammatory properties in the 3D lung epithelial model. Through a comparative proteomics analysis between proteolytically active versus non-active
isolates – which identified 79 proteases in the secretome of
P. aeruginosa
clinical CF isolates cultured in SCFM2 – and confirmatory experiments with mutants, LasB was found to be at least in part responsible for the immunosuppressive activity of isolates with proteolytic/elastolytic properties. Consistent with these findings,
lasR
mutants have been shown to trigger an excessive neutrophil-dominant hyperinflammatory response in a chronic airway mouse infection model due to the absence of proteolytic activity [
37
]. The inflammatory response was largely attributed to the loss of
LasR
-regulated proteases, including LasB [
37
,
79
]. LasB has been reported to degrade various cytokines, including IL-6, IL-8, IFN-γ, and TNFα [
15
,
28
,
37
,
38
]. The recombinant degradation assay used here revealed that PAO1 WT significantly reduced cytokine levels compared to PAO1 lasB:Tn, confirming the role of LasB in the degradation of recombinant IL-6, MCP-1, GM-CSF, and IL-1β. Interestingly, in contrast to our findings, earlier studies reported that LasB activates IL-1β by converting pro-IL-1β into its active form without further degrading it [
28
,
39
]. Additionally, while TNFα degradation by LasB has been previously reported, this was not observed in our study [
28
]. Beyond cytokines, LasB has a broad range of targets. A recent study by Cai et
al
. identified over a hundred protein targets for this metalloprotease [
80
]. For example, LasB has been shown to disrupt the endothelial barrier by degrading extracellular matrix proteins, including laminin and collagen IV [
35
,
81
,
82
]. Furthermore, a study by Saint-Criq
et al
. demonstrated that LasB degrades epithelial CFTR and decreases its activity across different airway epithelial cell lines [
15
].
The top 10 most differentially expressed proteases between proteolytically active and non-active
P. aeruginosa
isolates were for the most part secreted, with LasB expression being the strongest differentiator between proteolytic/elastolytic active and non-active isolates. Besides LasB, the extracellular proteolytic arsenal of
P. aeruginosa
includes AprA, LasA, LasD, PrpL, PASP, ImpA, and Lap. These proteases play a role in pathogenicity by modulating the host immune responses, facilitating immune evasion, and promoting tissue invasion [
11
]. Individual proteases have been implicated in diverse functions, such as disrupting of complement pathways, degrading cytokines, impairing phagocytosis, and supporting biofilm formation, although these roles have been characterized in only a limited number of studies for each protease [
28–33
,
83–89
]. Most research to date has focused on general immune interactions, with some studies exploring specific contexts such as corneal virulence or pulmonary infection [
30–32
,
89
]. Notably, the
P. aeruginosa
-derived large extracellular protease (LepA), previously reported to induce IL-8 production in a human bronchial epithelial cell line via NF-kB activation, was not detected in any of the tested isolates in our study [
34
,
90
].
In addition to extracellular proteases that are actively secreted and were found in the bacterial supernatants, other proteases were detected that may play a role in inflammation and immune evasion. For example, the periplasmic serine endoprotease DegP-like (MucD) is upregulated in the isolates with activity and is known to contribute to the establishment of infection by evading immune responses through degradation of IL-8 [
91
]. Whether MucD leaks from the periplasm into the extracellular space to directly degrade cytokines or instead modulates the production of extracellular virulence factors, indirectly contributing to cytokine degradation, remains unresolved [
91
,
92
]. Also, the formation of outer membrane vesicles (OMVs), which arise either by blebbing of the outer membrane or through biofilm associated cell lysis, have been shown to play a role in cell-to-cell communication, adaptation, and the transport of virulence factors [
93–95
]. Our proteomics analysis identified several proteases that were reported to be released via OMVs. For example, EprS, an autotransporter protein with serine protease activity localized in the outer membrane, was upregulated in isolates with activity. This protease, known as one of the major proteins present in OMVs, has, however, been reported to induce IL-8 production in a HBE cell line through activation of the NF-kB pathway, whereas we observed a strong reduction in IL-8 production in our 3D cell culture model [
35
,
36
,
93
].
Based on the proteases identified with the proteomics analysis and their described effects in the literature, the observed immunosuppressive response in 3D lung cells can likely be attributed to the combined (potentially additive or synergistic) cytokine degradation by multiple proteases. Nevertheless, given the observed strong elastolytic activity (which is primarily mediated by LasB) of isolates associated with reduced cytokine levels (
Figure 7(B,D)
), along with LasB being the most upregulated protease in active compared to non-active isolates, we focused on the specific contribution of LasB to the observed effects.
Key to our experimental approach was the use of
P. aeruginosa
strains isolated from chronically infected pwCF,
in vivo
-like culture conditions for these strains (i.e. SCFM2 and micro-aerophilic conditions) and a differentiated 3D lung cell culture model in order to generate translationally relevant data. While the RWV-
derived 3D A549 alveolar epithelial model used in this study offers a more physiologically relevant environment compared to traditional 2D cultures, it presents limitations when applied to CF research. Bronchial epithelial cell lines carrying mutations in the CFTR gene are generally preferred for CF research, yet the 3D A549 model has previously been used to study bacterial immunomodulation and reflected results obtained with 3D models of CF cell lines [
96–98
]. Furthermore, the cancerous origin of A549 cells raises concerns regarding their suitability as a surrogate for non-cancerous airway epithelia, yet it is noteworthy that 3D culture conditions reduce cancer-specific marker expression [
25
]. Another limitation of this study is the use of laboratory strains PAO1 and PA14, rather than clinical isolates, to investigate the contribution of LasB to cytokine decline. However, a study by Cornforth
et al.
[
17
]. showed that CF isolates are not always essential to capture key aspects of
P. aeruginosa
physiology, as PAO1 cultured in SCFM2 closely mirrored transcriptomic patterns from CF sputum, including lasB expression. Lastly, the clinical isolates used in this study were obtained from a relatively small number of pwCF. While our work primarily addresses the immunomodulatory activity of extracellular proteases, particularly LasB, analyzing a larger cohort will enable the generalization of our findings. For example, in our study proteolytic/elastolytic activity was primarily associated with isolates from early chronic CF infection, consistent with previous reports linking such activity with early-stage infection [
13
,
99
]. However, as our isolates were obtained from only two pwCF (representing early and late chronic infections), definitive conclusions cannot be drawn. Nevertheless, our dataset does capture key aspects of the CF lung environment, including the intra-patient genetically diverse population of
P. aeruginosa
strains adapted to chronic infection [
40
,
100–106
]. Specifically, the isolate collection in this study encompasses both proteolytically active isolates with immunosuppressive properties and non-active isolates that may promote hyperinflammatory responses. The coexistence of these distinct phenotypes within one patient will likely contribute to the complex and persistent inflammatory landscape observed in pwCF.
Cigana
et al
. were the first to highlight the contribution of LasB during chronic infection in pwCF by demonstrating that this metalloprotease compromises inflammation in a mouse model of chronic lung infection, thereby facilitating
P. aeruginosa’s
adaption to a persistent lifestyle [
27
]. Our findings support these observations by demonstrating that LasB attenuates multiple key inflammatory mediators known to be involved in chronic respiratory infection, indicating that LasB may play a critical role in
P. aeruginosa
immune evasion. Furthermore, we identified other proteases that – together with LasB – could strengthen this phenotype. Interestingly, the loss of proteolytic activity paradoxically intensifies inflammation by allowing pro‑inflammatory mediators to persist, while other
P. aeruginosa
virulence factors are often downregulated during chronic CF infection [
10
,
18
,
37
]. To understand how the combined outcome of virulence factor downregulation and reduced protease-mediated immune evasion influences net CF lung inflammation needs further research. Overall, our findings advance the understanding of host–pathogen interactions in chronic CF lung infection and lay the groundwork for future research exploring how proteolytic activity shapes immune evasion and how these mechanisms contribute to the complex and persistent inflammatory environment characteristic of CF lung disease.
