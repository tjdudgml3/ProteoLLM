1
Introduction
Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are membrane‐delimited particles released by all cells, including those in the central nervous system (CNS). EVs can be released through several different cellular processes, including multivesicular body fusion with the plasma membrane, direct budding from the cell surface and membrane blebbing (Gurung et al.
2021
). They comprise a heterogeneous population of vesicles that can be operationally classified based on their physical characteristics, including small EVs (typically <200 nm) and large EVs (typically >200 nm), though these size ranges may vary depending on the characterization methods used (Welsh et al.
2024
). They have a multitude of functions, maintaining homeostasis and promoting healthy CNS function, but can also mediate pathological processes such as neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration (Gassama and Favereaux
2021
).
In the field of neuroimmunology—and particularly in multiple sclerosis (MS) research—EVs have emerged as both candidate biomarkers and potential effectors of disease (Palacio et al.
2023
; Marostica et al.
2020
; Zamboni et al.
2023
). Recent studies have increasingly focused on using brain‐derived EVs from blood plasma to non‐invasively monitor CNS‐intrinsic neurological disease processes (Bhargava et al.
2021
; Torres Iglesias et al.
2023
; Torres‐Iglesias et al.
2025
; Duan et al.
2024
; Anandan et al.
2025
). However, despite the expanding literature on brain‐derived EVs in MS, the proteomic landscape of EVs directly isolated from CNS tissue remains poorly characterized. This limits our understanding of how well circulating brain‐derived EVs reflect the molecular changes occurring within the brain.
To address this research gap, we separated and profiled EVs from postmortem normal‐appearing white matter (NAWM) tissue of people with MS and controls. Our findings present a comprehensive bioinformatic analysis of pathways dysregulated in CNS‐derived EVs in MS and thereby provide novel insights into the potential role of EVs as mediators of MS pathological processes, including compartmentalized inflammation, synaptic pathology and mitochondrial dysfunction. Further, this study is a valuable resource to identify new candidates for EV biomarker studies and to align changes observed in brain‐derived plasma EV biomarker studies with changes in the CNS.
2
Methods
EV characterization followed MISEV2023 guidelines using orthogonal approaches to address the inherent heterogeneity of tissue‐derived EVs (Welsh et al.
2024
). This strategy is essential because EVs can vary greatly in size, surface marker expression, binding affinity to various surfaces and stability, exceeding any single method's ideal detection range (Arab et al.
2021
). Different techniques capture distinct subpopulations due to their specific limitations.
We used four complementary characterization methods: (1) Nanoflow cytometry analyzed bulk size distributions from larger sample volumes, with the limitation that it cannot distinguish EVs from non‐EV particles, has reduced sensitivity below 40 nm (Tian et al.
2018
) and detects particles up to 200 nm (Yim et al.
2023
). (2) ExoView single‐particle interferometric reflectance imaging (SP‐IRIS) captured tetraspanin‐positive EVs and characterized their expression/co‐expression at a single‐particle resolution but only captures EVs expressing (sufficient) tetraspanins. (3) TEM assessed morphology and size from multiple fields of view per sample, although small sampling volumes and sample preparation may introduce selective bias, as certain subpopulations might preferentially bind to grids or resist the staining/washing steps. (4) Proteomics provided comprehensive molecular profiling.
EV purity was validated through five approaches: cellular component enrichment comparison between EVs and tissue using the top 10% most abundant proteins; cellular component enrichment of unique proteins in EVs and tissue; cellular component distribution as percentages of the whole proteome; overlap with ExoCarta/Vesiclepedia databases; and assessment of known EV markers and contaminants (Figure
S1G
). While each approach has limitations (e.g., annotation completeness, abundance considerations, database biases), their convergent results provide robust validation of EV enrichment.
Below is a brief description of each method. For more detailed methods, please refer to the
Supporting Information File
.
2.1
Tissue Collection and Processing
MS patients were diagnosed according to the revised McDonald criteria (Thompson et al.
2018
). Postmortem brain tissues from 4 MS patients were obtained from the Cleveland Clinic MS brain donation programme described in more detail elsewhere (Dutta et al.
2019
). Control postmortem tissues (
n
= 4) were obtained from the Department of Anatomic Pathology at the Cleveland Clinic. Patient demographics are listed in Table
1
, with further details in Table
S3
. For the MS patients, postmortem in situ MRIs were acquired prior to brain removal using standardized MRI acquisition protocols. Following scanning, the brain and spinal cord were removed in a refrigerated autopsy room. The brain was hemisected, with one hemisphere immediately placed in a slicing box and cut into alternating 1cm coronal slabs. Alternate slabs were either snap‐frozen by immersion in dry‐ice‐chilled 2‐methylbutane (isopentane) or fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. Frozen tissue (used in this study) was sealed in freezer bags, transported on dry ice and stored at −80°C until use. For each case, the postmortem interval was recorded at the time of autopsy. There was no significant difference in the postmortem interval between control and MS patients. Cryostat sections (14 µm) were cut from the fresh frozen tissue slabs containing subcortical white matter and stained with proteolipid protein (PLP) to determine the presence of demyelinating lesions. Based on the staining results, normal‐appearing white matter (myelinated) regions were identified and scored on the frozen block with a scalpel and then collected for EV separation.

2.2
Extracellular Vesicle Separation
EVs were separated using a previously published protocol with slight modifications (Huang et al.
2020
). Briefly, frozen tissue samples were diced on dry ice. A portion was preserved for brain tissue proteomics. The remaining tissue (∼400 mg) was digested in Hibernate‐E medium containing 75 U/mL collagenase III for 20 min at 37°C, then immediately chilled and supplemented with protease/phosphatase inhibitors. Samples were then subjected to differential centrifugation (300 ×
g
for 10 min, 2000 ×
g
for 15 min and 10,000 ×
g
for 30 min), filtered (0.22 µm) and concentrated to 700 µL. Samples were loaded onto qEV 70 nm columns (IZON) and EV‐rich fractions (F7‐10) were collected, pelleted at 110,000 ×
g
for 90 min and gently resuspended in 70 µL Dulbecco's phosphate‐buffered saline (DPBS) with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Aliquots (50 µL for proteomics, 20 µL for other characterization) were stored at −80°C.
2.3
Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)
EVs were adsorbed onto glow‐discharged carbon‐coated copper grids (EMS CF400‐CU‐UL), washed with TBS and distilled water and negatively stained with 1% uranyl acetate. Grids were dried and imaged using a Hitachi 7600 TEM with the voltage set to 80 kV. A pooled EV sample (consisting of all four samples) was imaged for each group across eight fields at magnifications of 30,000× and 100,000× (Figure
1C
and Figure
S1C
). EV particle size was quantified from ∼100 particles per group using the TEM ExosomeAnalyzer tool (Kotrbová et al.
2019
) (Figure
S1E
). To assess EV enrichment, three negative control fractions were also processed and imaged using the same protocol at 80,000× magnification: (1) the 10,000 ×
g
pellet, (2) pre‐EV elution SEC fractions F1‐6 and (3) post‐EV elution fractions F11‐14 (Figure
S1D
).

2.4
Nanoflow Cytometry
EV size distribution and concentration were measured using a Flow NanoAnalyzer (NanoFCM) following the manufacturer's protocol. Calibration was performed using 250 nm fluorescent silica particles and a 68–155 nm size standard cocktail. EVs were diluted 1:1000 in DPBS and acquired for 1 min at 1 kPa (1500–10,000 events/min). Data was processed with the NanoFCM Professional Suite V2.0 software, correcting for dilution and subtracting the background of a DPBS‐only control.
2.5
Single‐Particle Interferometric Reflectance Imaging Sensor (SP‐IRIS)
Tetraspanin profiling was carried out using the ExoView R100 platform with EV‐TETRA‐C ExoView kits, as previously described (Arab et al.
2021
). EVs (10 µL) were diluted with 35 µL incubation buffer and loaded onto chips for 16 h. After thorough washing, chips were stained with fluorescent antibodies targeting CD9, CD63 and Syntenin‐1. Chips were imaged using SP‐IRIS and analyzed with NanoViewer 2.8.10.
2.6
Proteomic Sample Preparation and LC‐MS/MS
Proteomics was performed as previously described (Jank et al.
2024
) with minor changes. EVs were lysed in 8 M urea and 50 mM TEAB, reduced with 10 mM TCEP and alkylated with 40 mM CAA for 1 h. Proteins were digested with Lys‐C (1:100, 3 h, 37°C), followed by trypsin digestion (1:50, overnight, 37°C). Peptides were desalted using C18 StageTips, labelled with 18‐plex TMTpro reagents and fractionated into 24 concatenated fractions via high‐pH reverse‐phase chromatography. Samples were vacuum‐dried and reconstituted in 0.5% formic acid. LC‐MS/MS was conducted using an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos coupled with an Ultimate 3000 RSLCnano system. Peptides were separated over a 120 min gradient, with MS1/MS2 resolution at 120,000/50,000. HCD was applied at 35% NCE. AGC targets were set to 1 million (MS1) and 50,000 (MS2), with injection times of 50/100 ms. The isolation window was 1.6 Da, and lock mass calibration (m/z 445.12002) was used.
2.7
LC‐MS/MS Data Analysis
MS/MS data were searched against the UniProt human database using MSFragger via Proteome Discoverer (Kong et al.
2017
). Top 10 peaks per 100 m/z were selected. Parameters included trypsin digestion with up to 2 missed cleavages, fixed carbamidomethylation on cysteine and TMTpro tags on peptide N‐termini and lysine residues, variable methionine oxidation and MS1/MS2 tolerances of 10/20 ppm. FDR was set at 1% using Percolator. Reporter ion quantification used centroid mode, S/N >10, and both unique/razor peptides. Protein grouping applied strict parsimony, excluding non‐unique groups.
2.8
Bioinformatic and Statistical Analysis
UniProt IDs were mapped to Entrez IDs and gene symbols using AnnotationDbi and org. Hs.eg.db (
2023
,
2023
, org.Hs.eg.db). PCA was performed using the stats package and visualized with ggplot2 (Wickham
2016
). Data were log2‐transformed and median‐centred. Limma (Ritchie et al.
2015
) was used for differential abundance analysis. Pathway analysis included Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) and Over‐Representation Analysis (ORA) using ClusterProfiler (Wu et al.
2021
) with GO.db (
GO.db
), KEGG (Kanehisa and Goto
2000
) and Reactome (Milacic et al.
2024
) databases. We used CompareCluster from ClusterProfiler to assess enriched cellular components in EVs compared to tissue, using the top 10% most abundant proteins from tissue, and EV samples as the input for this analysis. Shared/distinct pathways changed in tissue and EVs were identified by ORA of the top up‐/downregulated (with MS) proteins in each sample type using CompareCluster.
Deconvolution analysis was performed using CIBERSORTx (Newman et al.
2019
) with a human glial deconvolution matrix (Sutton et al.
2022
; Zhang et al.
2016
) and validated with BRETIGEA (McKenzie et al.
2018
). Limma was used to compare the estimated cell type of origin proportions. Synaptic subsets were explored using GSEA of GO terms related to presynaptic/postsynaptic and excitatory/inhibitory proteins. SynGO (Koopmans et al.
2019
) was used for pathway analysis and sunburst visualization. To assess ageing signatures in MS EVs, we performed GSEA with the SenMayo dataset (Saul et al.
2022
) and a CSF proteomics ageing signature (Melendez et al.
2024
) and used a proteomic clock tool based on the CSF dataset. Finally, MS EV profiles were compared to chronic experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) EV proteomic data (Jank et al.
2024
). Protein‐wise fold changes were correlated, and shared/distinct pathways were identified by ORA of the top 150 up‐/downregulated proteins using CompareCluster. All figures were generated in R and finalized in Adobe Illustrator.
3
Results
3.1
There Is No Significant Difference in Median Size, Yield or Morphology of NAWM‐Derived EVs From People With MS Compared to Controls
EVs were separated from the NAWM of four controls (no known neurological disease) and four postmortem human MS brains using differential centrifugation followed by size exclusion chromatography and ultracentrifugation based on a previously published protocol (Huang et al.
2020
) (experiment flowchart in Figure
1A
and sample details in Table
1
with additional information in Table
S3
). Control and MS EVs were characterized using nanoflow cytometry, ExoView single‐particle interferometric reflectance imaging sensor and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) according to the MISEV criteria (Welsh et al.
2024
). Multi‐modal characterization confirmed typical EV properties in both groups. There was no significant difference in EV particle yields per tissue mass from MS or control brains. The particles in both the MS and control EV samples showed a wide particle size distribution in the nanoflow cytometry size histogram. While there was also no significant difference in median particle size, MS EVs showed a trend towards larger particles and a slightly different size distribution pattern (Figure
1B
, Figure
S1E
). In TEM, EVs from both groups had a typical “cup‐shaped” morphology (Figure
1C
, further images in Figure
S1C
). The TEM images confirmed the size heterogeneity of EVs seen in nanoflow cytometry, but in TEM, the EV diameters ranged from 40 to 460 nm. The size difference between TEM and nanoflow cytometry most likely reflects inherent limitations of each technique (discussed in Methods) alongside the heterogeneous nature of the EVs, which leads to different EV subsets and non‐EV particles being measured with each method. Lastly, Exoview analysis revealed that CD9+ EVs were the most abundant tetraspanin‐positive EVs in both MS and Ctrl EVs (Figure
1D
). Overall, the distribution of tetraspanin‐positive EV subpopulations was similar in both groups, with the only significant difference being fewer CD63+ CD9+ double‐positive particles in MS (Figure
1D
, Figure
S1A,B
).
EV purity was validated through five complementary approaches (Figure
1E,F
, Figure
S1G
): (1) GO:CC analysis of the top 10% most abundant proteins showed enrichment of exocytotic vesicle proteins and depletion of cytoskeletal and mitochondrial proteins compared to tissue. (2) Analysis of proteins unique to each fraction revealed that EV‐unique proteins were enriched for membrane‐associated and secretory compartments, while tissue‐unique proteins were predominantly from mitochondrial and cytoplasmic compartments, confirming effective separation of EVs from intracellular organelles. (3) Cellular component distribution analysis showed EVs were enriched for plasma membrane/ECM/secreted proteins (18.6% vs. 14.6% in tissue) and vesicular proteins (9.8% vs. 7.7%), while nuclear proteins were depleted (13.8% vs. 20%), with mitochondrial proteins comprising 8.4% of the EV proteome versus 9.8% in tissue. (4) 88% of Vesiclepedia (Chitti et al.
2024
) and 91% of ExoCarta (Keerthikumar et al.
2016
) top 100 EV markers were detected in our samples. (5) Established surface and cytosolic EV markers (CD9, CD63, FLOT1/2, PDCD6IP/ALIX, ANXA proteins) were enriched in EVs, while potential unwanted co‐isolates, including nuclear histones, ribosomal proteins, ER proteins (with the exception of calnexin), Golgi apparatus‐related proteins and most mitochondrial proteins (with the exception of some proteins of the inner mitochondrial membrane like IMMT, OPA1, SLC25A11), were either depleted or not present in the EV samples. These convergent results, despite individual method limitations, confirm successful EV separation with minimal co‐isolates.
Proteomic analysis of NAWM‐EVs reveals changes in inflammatory, synaptic and mitochondrial proteins, as well as differences in the contribution of astrocytes and neurones to the EV pool in MS EVs compared to controls.
Proteomic analysis of the MS and control NAWM EVs detected 4029 proteins, of which 291 were differentially abundant (in this exploratory study, defined as
p < 0.05
). Proteomics of the corresponding tissue detected 7663 proteins, of which 267 were differentially abundant (Tables 1 and
2
). Principal component analysis distinguished EV samples into disease and control groups, despite one control sample (Ctrl4) differing from the remaining controls (Figure
2A
). The top upregulated proteins in MS EVs compared to control EVs included proteins involved in lipid metabolism (ADIRF, FABP5), reactive astrocytosis (CD44, VIM), inflammatory activation (S100A1, A6, A13, CP, FSTL4) and negative regulation of inflammation/cell activation (ANXA1, SZT2). Meanwhile, mitochondrial (LRPPRC, ATP6V0C) and synaptic (SLC6A12, SYT12) proteins were among the top downregulated proteins in MS EVs (Figure
2B
). These changes were confirmed in pathway analysis: pathways related to homeostatic neuronal and mitochondrial functions were downregulated, and inflammatory pathways, particularly the complement cascade, were upregulated in MS EVs compared to control EVs (Figure
2C
). These results remained after adjusting for the postmortem interval (Figure
S2E
).

In tissue, the top upregulated proteins in MS compared to controls (Figure
2C
) included extracellular matrix components (COL21A1, ELN) and serpins (SERPINA3, SERPINC1) that were not observed in EVs. Meanwhile, many of the mitochondrial, synaptic and inflammatory proteins that were among the top dysregulated in EVs were less prominent in tissue. The pathway analysis of the tissue proteomes of MS and control NAWM showed some overlap with the pathways dysregulated in EVs, for example, downregulation of some mitochondrial and upregulation of complement pathways, but also showed key differences—most notably, several extracellular matrix‐related pathways were among the top upregulated pathways in tissue (Figure
S2B,C
). To more directly identify which pathways are more strongly dysregulated in EVs compared to tissue, we further carried out pathway overrepresentation analysis comparing the dysregulated pathways between EVs and tissue (MS vs. Ctrl for each). This analysis was carried out using the entire proteome for the EV and tissue datasets and repeated only using the overlap of the two datasets. While MS‐induced changes in neuronal/synaptic pathways were more pronounced in EVs, MS‐induced changes in cytoskeletal pathways and the complement cascade were more pronounced in the tissue (Figure
2D
). Direct correlation analysis of MS‐induced protein changes between EVs and tissue showed overall correlation of changes but confirmed the pathway‐specific differences: Synaptic and mitochondrial proteins showed notable downregulation in EVs with fewer and less consistent changes in tissue, while complement proteins demonstrated stronger EV‐tissue correlation (Figure
S2D
).
To determine if the cells contributing to the EV pool change in MS, we carried out CNS cell deconvolution analyses on the EV proteomics data using CIBERSORTx (Newman et al.
2019
; Sutton et al.
2022
; Zhang et al.
2016
) and BRETIGEA (McKenzie et al.
2018
). In both analyses, a trend of increasing astrocyte and decreasing neuronal contributions (driven mainly by synaptic proteins) was observed, but this did not reach significance in either analysis (Figure
2E
and Figure
S2F
). The trends observed in the CIBERSORTx deconvolution analyses aligned with the results in the BRETIGEA analysis (Figure
S2F
) and with changes in commonly used CNS cell markers (Figure
2G
). Astrocyte cell markers for reactive subsets (VIM, CD44, S100B) were particularly strongly upregulated in MS EVs.
3.2
Pathways Related to Synaptic Signalling, Mitochondrial Function, Complement Activation and Ageing Are Dysregulated in MS Tissue EVs Compared to Controls
Neuronal system, mitochondria/respiratory electron transport chain and complement cascade‐related pathways were among the top dysregulated pathways in MS tissue‐derived EVs. All three of these pathways have been shown to play a role in MS disease progression and white matter pathology (Reich et al.
2018
; Muñoz González et al.
2025
). Therefore, we next characterized which proteins contribute to the changes in these pathways.
The most strongly dysregulated proteins within the neuronal system pathway were synaptic proteins (Figure
3A
). Consistent with our earlier analyses, these changes were more pronounced in EVs than in the corresponding tissue samples (Figure
S2H
). To investigate whether specific subsets of synaptic proteins were particularly affected, we categorized proteins as uniquely pre‐ or postsynaptic by identifying those annotated with Cellular Component Gene Ontology (CC GO) terms containing either ‘presynaptic’ but not ‘postsynaptic’ or ‘postsynaptic’ but not ‘presynaptic’. Both pre‐ and postsynaptic proteins were enriched at the bottom of the ranked list of EV proteins, ordered by decreasing fold change, suggesting they are downregulated. In direct comparison, presynaptic proteins exhibited a lower (more negative) enrichment score compared to postsynaptic proteins (NES pre: −1.94; post: 1.70), indicating more pronounced downregulation of presynaptic proteins (Figure
3B
). To compare changes in inhibitory and excitatory synaptic proteins, we identified these proteins by searching the GO terms for either ‘GABA‐ergic’ and ‘inhibitory’ or ‘glutamatergic’ and ‘excitatory’. The enrichment analysis revealed downregulation of both inhibitory and excitatory synaptic proteins, with inhibitory proteins having a lower enrichment score (NES inhib.: −2.19; exci.: −1.53), suggesting an overall more pronounced downregulation compared to excitatory synaptic proteins (Figure
3B
). These findings were further confirmed using the SynGO synapse function enrichment tool (Koopmans et al.
2019
). There was an overall enrichment of synaptic proteins, with a stronger enrichment of presynaptic proteins compared to postsynaptic proteins among the differentially regulated proteins in MS EVs (Figure
3C
).

The top dysregulated proteins in the respiratory electron transport pathway were all mitochondrial proteins, many of which, interestingly, have previously been shown to be present in mitovesicles (D'Acunzo et al.
2021
) (Figure
3D
; top dysregulated mitovesicle proteins are shown in Figure
S2I
). Axons in MS NAWM contain fewer mitochondria (unless undergoing remyelination) and show signs of mitochondrial dysfunction (Zambonin et al.
2011
). Based on this, we hypothesized that the reduction of mitochondrial proteins may be partially driven by changes in neurone‐derived EVs. Our dataset supports this hypothesis, revealing a reduction in neuronal‐specific mitochondrial proteins (Fecher et al.
2019
) in MS EVs (Figure
3E
; astrocyte‐specific mitochondrial proteins showed no consistent changes, Figure
S2J
).
The complement cascade pathway upregulation in MS EVs was driven by both initiators (immunoglobulins), components (C1s, C4B, C5 and C9), and inhibitors (SERPING1) of the complement cascade (Figure
3F
), some of which have been studied as EV‐based biomarkers in MS (Bhargava et al.
2021
; Torres Iglesias et al.
2023
).
All three pathways discussed above are associated with brain ageing. Therefore, we next tested for enrichment of ageing‐related proteins in MS EVs. We found that proteins known to be up‐ or downregulated in the CSF with ageing (Melendez et al.
2024
) are enriched at the upper and lower ends, respectively, of the ranked list of EV proteins, ordered by decreasing fold change. This suggests that these ageing‐related proteins are dysregulated in MS EVs (Figure
3G
). This finding was further corroborated by calculating the predicted age of MS EV samples using a clock tool based on the CSF proteomics dataset mentioned above (Melendez et al.
2024
). While MS EVs exhibited signs of accelerated brain ageing compared to control EVs, the difference did not reach statistical significance, given our sample size. Using the SenMayo dataset (Saul et al.
2022
), we further found that proteins associated with senescence, a hallmark of ageing, are also upregulated in MS EVs compared to control EVs.
Finally, we assessed whether the proteomic changes observed in human MS‐derived EVs were conserved in the mouse EAE model. Indeed, we found a significant correlation in the log2‐fold changes of EV proteins between MS and EAE. Comparison of the overrepresented pathways among dysregulated proteins revealed that both synaptic and mitochondrial pathways were consistently downregulated in EVs from MS and EAE. In contrast, there was less overlap in the upregulated pathways. Specifically, inflammatory pathways, including antigen presentation and phagocytosis, were overrepresented in EAE EVs but were either unchanged or only slightly changed in MS EVs. This might be due to differences in the tissues analyzed in mice and humans: while EAE EVs were separated from whole spinal cords, likely containing active demyelinating lesions, human MS EVs were separated from NAWM, which lacks lesional tissue.
4
Discussion
Overall, there were no significant differences in the median size, yield and morphology of EVs from MS compared to control NAWM. As expected, the separated EVs were heterogeneous, as seen in the spread peaks in both the nanoflow cytometry and TEM size histograms. The size discrepancy between these methods most likely reflects both this heterogeneity and each technique's inherent limitations (discussed in Section
2
), which may lead to each method preferentially detecting different subsets of the heterogeneous EVs. The slight shift towards larger EVs in MS may suggest alterations in the mechanisms of EV release or changes in the cell of origin in MS. This might be linked to the inflammation in NAWM, as the size and number of EVs released by some glial cells have been shown to increase under inflammatory conditions (Yang et al.
2018
; You et al.
2020
; Datta Chaudhuri et al.
2020
).
Cellular deconvolution analyses revealed a trend towards increased astrocytic and decreased neuronal contributions to the EV pool in MS compared to controls. This shift may reflect multiple factors: changes in the cellular composition of the NAWM (e.g., more astrocytes and/or neuronal loss), altered EV production by these cell types, different EV clearance dynamics or changes in cellular marker packaging into EVs. Supporting a tissue composition effect, tissue deconvolution analysis revealed similar MS‐associated shifts in predicted cell type proportions, suggesting that EV changes may partially mirror tissue composition. Additionally, increased EV production by reactive astrocytes may also contribute. In vitro studies have shown that reactive astrocytes release more EVs under inflammatory conditions (You et al.
2020
; Datta Chaudhuri et al.
2020
), which aligns with our observation of upregulated reactive astrocyte markers, such as CD44 and vimentin, in MS‐derived EVs. While the consistency of the changed neuronal and astrocytic contributions/markers across all three analysis approaches (selected markers, CIBERSORTx and BRETIGEA) strengthens these findings, these analyses remain indirect estimations that cannot distinguish between the potential underlying mechanisms. Definitive investigation of the change in EVs’ cell of origin with MS will require studies that pair region‐matched histopathology with single‐EV phenotyping to directly correlate cellular composition and activation states with EV populations.
In MS‐derived EVs, synaptic signalling and mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation pathways were downregulated, while complement cascade‐related pathways were upregulated compared to controls. These changes are particularly interesting given that these pathways are key features of MS pathology (Reich et al.
2018
; Muñoz González et al.
2025
). Interestingly, when comparing changes in EVs to those in the tissue, synaptic and mitochondrial alterations were more strongly dysregulated in EVs, whereas complement changes were correlated between EVs and the tissue, suggesting that EVs might play a unique role in the MS‐induced synaptic pathology and mitochondrial dysfunction.
MS involves synaptic pathology, even in the absence of demyelination and axonal loss (Jürgens et al.
2016
), highlighting its relevance as an independent pathological feature of MS. Under homeostatic conditions, neurones release EVs containing synaptic proteins, which support synaptic plasticity and provide trophic signals to stressed neurones (Solana‐Balaguer et al.
2023
) (reviewed in more detail elsewhere [Hermann et al.
2024
]). The reduced synaptic cargo in MS EVs may therefore not only reflect ongoing synaptic pathology but could also perpetuate synaptic loss and neuronal stress in MS. Notably, the more pronounced loss of inhibitory and presynaptic proteins aligns with the pattern of synaptic loss observed in MS tissue and animal models of MS (Huiskamp et al.
2022
; Zoupi et al.
2021
; Werneburg et al.
2020
). However, what was surprising was that these changes in synaptic proteins were observed in NAWM EVs, despite synaptic pathology in MS primarily being described in the grey matter. One possible explanation is that some of the EVs detected in NAWM originate from other brain regions, including the grey matter. Alternatively, these findings may suggest that synaptic pathology also occurs within the white matter itself. Although classical synapses are rare in the white matter, axon‐glial synapses have been described in white matter (Ziskin et al.
2007
; Bergles et al.
2000
; Kukley et al.
2007
). These synapses have been shown to contain both inhibitory and excitatory synaptic proteins and regulate remyelination. Disruption of these synapses in MS—perhaps due to Wallerian degeneration, reduced proximal input secondary to synaptic pathology in the GM or compartmentalized inflammation—could also explain the changes in synaptic proteins in MS NAWM EVs.
The MS‐associated synaptic pathology and neurodegeneration described above are linked to mitochondrial dysfunction (Dutta et al.
2006
), which we also saw in MS EVs. There was a loss of mitochondrial proteins with a marked downregulation of complex I‐associated proteins, particularly those of neuronal origin. This mitochondrial dysfunction is well described in neurones in the grey matter (Dutta et al.
2006
). In white matter, especially NAWM, the patterns are less well understood, in part because most studies in MS white matter focus on changes in MS lesions, only including NAWM as internal control tissue. In close proximity to MS lesions, axons (demyelinated and remyelinated), microglia and astrocytes have increased mitochondrial density and complex I activity, which sustains neuroinflammation (Zambonin et al.
2011
; Peruzzotti‐Jametti et al.
2024
; Witte et al.
2009
). The MS NAWM, on the other hand, has fewer mitochondria in myelinated axons and shows lower levels of the neuronal mitochondrial metabolite N‐acetylaspartate in progressive disease stages compared to healthy control WM (Zambonin et al.
2011
; Aboul‐Enein et al.
2010
). The reduced mitochondrial proteins in MS may therefore reflect mitochondrial dysfunction in NAWM axons, especially since we saw a pronounced change in neurone‐specific mitochondrial proteins. However, besides merely reflecting disease pathology, the loss of mitochondrial proteins in EVs might also contribute to MS disease processes. EVs can shuttle mitochondrial proteins and even entire functional mitochondria (D'Acunzo et al.
2021
; Peruzzotti‐Jametti et al.
2021
; Hayakawa et al.
2016
). Mitochondrial cargo in CNS cell EVs, including neuronal progenitor and astrocyte EVs, has been shown to reduce the inflammatory activation of mononuclear phagocytes and promote neuronal cell survival (Peruzzotti‐Jametti et al.
2021
; Hayakawa et al.
2016
). Furthermore, depolarized mitochondria contributing to intracellular oxidative stress can be released in EVs and taken up by recipient cells, which then restore the mitochondria's metabolic functions (Phinney et al.
2015
). The reduced mitochondrial cargo in MS EVs is indicative that MS tissue lacks the metabolic reserve/regenerative capacity these EVs offer. Hence, the loss might perpetuate mitochondrial damage and downstream oxidative stress in MS. Enhancing EV‐mediated mitochondrial protein transfer could then be a potential therapeutic strategy in MS. A critical consideration when interpreting these mitochondrial changes is distinguishing between EV cargo and potential contamination from cellular mitochondrial fragments. However, our comprehensive purity assessment using five different approaches demonstrates overall depletion of EVs from mitochondrial proteins in EVs compared to tissue. Further evidence supporting that the mitochondrial proteins detected likely represent EV cargo is that (1) many identified proteins have been previously reported in mitovesicles (D'Acunzo et al.
2021
); (2) the altered mitochondrial proteins show divergent patterns between EVs and tissue, with some changing in opposite directions; and (3) we observe specific reduction of neuronal mitochondrial proteins rather than global contamination, indicating biological relevance rather than technical artefact.
Complement and triggering of the complement cascade were among the top up‐regulated pathways. In MS NAWM pathology, CNS complement activation plays diverging roles, promoting debris clearance through phagocytosis but also contributing to the compartmentalized inflammation and tissue damage when activated excessively or continuously (reviewed in more detail elsewhere [Saez‐Calveras and Stuve
2022
]). The complement cascade is thought to mediate many of these effects through extracellular signalling of the soluble complement components. However, there is increasing evidence that the complement components can be activated and can execute their functions intracellularly as part of the complosome (Liszewski et al.
2013
; West and Kemper
2023
). In this context, our findings of changed complement pathway‐related proteins in MS EVs are particularly interesting because they suggest that, besides its extracellular and intracellular functions, complement might also play a role in EVs. There is some evidence for this in C3‐containing fibroblast‐derived EVs, which have been shown to induce inflammatory activation and metabolic reprogramming when taken up by macrophages (Kumar et al.
2021
). Further microglial EVs have been shown to bind C1q on their surface, shuttling it to synapses to promote synaptic pruning (D'Arrigo et al.
2025
). Should a similar mechanism be taking place in MS, the upregulation of complement proteins in EVs could contribute to CNS compartmentalized inflammation and (excessive and/or protective) synaptic pruning in MS. Whether the complement‐related proteins detected in EVs are within EVs or bound to their surface externally or possibly even part of the EV biocorona is unclear and should be subject of future investigations.
Complement deposition, synaptic pathology and mitochondrial dysfunction are all associated with brain ageing (reviewed in more detail here [Mattson and Arumugam
2018
]). We therefore hypothesized that EVs might show signs of accelerated brain ageing, which we saw trending. Further, senescence‐associated proteins were upregulated in MS EVs, including ANXA1 and S100 proteins, which have previously been shown to be markers of senescent EVs (Meng et al.
2023
). Senescent EVs can contribute to inflammation (reviewed in more detail [Hanley et al.
2024
]) and more recently have also been suggested as mediators of paracrine senescence independent of the SASP (Wallis et al.
2021
; Borghesan et al.
2019
).
Interestingly, several of our findings in MS tissue EVs align with what has been shown in EVs derived from the CSF and blood of MS patients (Bhargava et al.
2021
; Torres Iglesias et al.
2023
; Welton et al.
2017
). We found a trend of increased astrocytic and decreased neuronal EV contribution to the tissue EV pool in MS compared to controls. In line with this, astrocytic EVs (identified either as GLAST+ EVs or EAAT2+ EVs) have been shown to be increased both in blood and CSF (Bhargava et al.
2021
; D'Ambrosio et al.
2024
), and neuronal EV particle counts and the expression of neuronal markers are decreased in circulating EVs from MS patients compared to healthy controls (Torres Iglesias et al.
2023
; Bhargava et al.
2019
). Though this is not consistent among all studies (Bhargava et al.
2021
), which may be due to differences in the cellular markers used, EV particle quantification methods and patient cohort characteristics across studies. Besides the cellular contribution, the loss in synaptic proteins we observed in MS tissue EVs also aligned with findings in circulating EVs from MS patients, where synaptic protein levels are reduced in neuronal EVs compared to healthy controls, and the rate of synaptic protein loss is associated with brain and retinal atrophy (Bhargava et al.
2021
; Ladakis et al.
2024
). Lastly, the increase in complement‐related proteins we saw in MS tissue EVs has also been reported in both plasma and CSF EVs. Circulating astrocyte‐enriched EVs from MS patients contain higher levels of several complement proteins, including those identified in this study, and complement pathways are enriched in RRMS CSF EVs compared to control EVs and whole CSF (Bhargava et al.
2021
; Welton et al.
2017
). Our study provides compelling evidence that the MS‐related changes in EVs in the brain may be detectable in circulation, which is an exciting prospect because CNS‐derived EVs in the circulation might provide a unique opportunity to monitor progressive disease stages, which are driven by CNS‐internal processes, rather than peripheral immune activation. To better characterize the overlap of CNS tissue, CSF and circulating EVs, it will, however, additionally be necessary to carry out studies with samples from all three compartments. Since it is hard to come by these samples in humans, these experiments could also be carried out animal models of MS, in which we found similar changes in the CNS EV proteome (Jank et al.
2024
).
The primary limitation of this study is the small sample size, which restricts the statistical power and generalizability. Future studies should aim to expand the sample size, allowing for the breakdown of samples according to MS subtypes or possibly even novel glia biology‐driven MS subclassifications (Macnair et al.
2025
). Additionally, comparisons between different lesion types and NAWM within the same patient would provide deeper insights into how EVs might be impacted by and possibly even contribute to the distinct molecular processes taking place in these regions. The EV separation method used, while effective for separating EVs from human brain tissue, does not capture the full range of particles and, like every separation method, has its unique set of disadvantages (reviewed in more detail here [Brenna et al.
2021
]). Complementary studies using different separating methods may yield slightly different results and further enrich our understanding of EVs in MS. Another limitation of this study is that our proteomic approach cannot distinguish whether proteins, such as complement components, are located within EVs, bound to their surface or part of the EV biocorona, which has important implications for understanding their functional roles and is therefore an interesting direction for future studies. Moreover, the bioinformatics tools used in this study, such as the cell deconvolution analyses, the pathway analyses and the ageing clock tool, were not specifically developed for EV proteomics and may therefore not fully capture changes in EV‐specific pathways or cellular markers. Finally, an inherent limitation of many human CNS tissue studies is the reliance on postmortem tissue, since biopsies of NAWM are not ethically feasible in people with MS. Terminal events and the postmortem interval (PMI) may influence tissue biochemistry and EV cargo. In our cohort, the PMI did not differ significantly between MS and controls, and sensitivity analysis including PMI as a covariate confirmed that our main findings are not driven by PMI. Importantly, we also observe the same pathway signatures in EVs from the EAE mouse model, providing independent support that these alterations reflect disease‐related biology rather than postmortem artefacts.
In conclusion, this exploratory study of four MS and four control brains found no significant differences in NAWM CNS‐tissue EV median size, yield or morphology in MS compared to controls, though there was a slight shift towards larger EVs in MS. There was a trend in increased astrocytic EVs and decreased neuronal EVs, accompanied by alterations in pathways related to synaptic signalling, mitochondrial function, complement activation and ageing in MS tissue EVs compared to controls. These findings are consistent with MS pathology and suggest that EVs may play a role in mediating these disease processes, though validation in larger cohorts is needed in the future. The similarities with changes seen in circulating MS EVs confirm that circulating CNS‐derived EVs are a promising platform to monitor CNS disease progression. Our study provides a novel resource to select interesting biomarker candidates.
