Introduction
Major depressive disorder (MDD) represents one of the most common types of mental illnesses, affecting millions of individuals worldwide [
1
]. As a debilitating disease, MDD is characterized by a depressed mood, diminished interests, impaired cognitive function and vegetative symptoms, such as disturbances in sleep or appetite [
2
]. Unfortunately, no effective strategies currently exist for the treatment of depression, a deficit which, in part, can be attributable to the lack of an understanding of the underlying pathological mechanisms of this disorder.
Chronic stress appears to be a trigger or exacerbator of MDD, and individuals with an increased sensitivity to stress have been shown to exhibit a higher risk for MDD [
3
,
4
]. In results from a majority of studies, it has been demonstrated that chronic stress promotes significant structural remodeling of microglia [
5
–
7
], which can then enhance the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines from microglia, the innate immune cells of the central nervous system [
8
,
9
]. Microglia play important roles in clearing pathogens and in maintaining brain homeostasis [
10
,
11
]. In addition, it is also becoming increasingly apparent that they are involved in a host of non-immunological activities. For example, microglia play a crucial role in sensing and regulating neuronal activity [
12
–
14
], and activated microglia can influence neuronal activity through direct engulfment of spines [
15
,
16
]. Moreover, a critical role for microglial Triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (TREM2) in restricting complement-mediated synaptic elimination has been demonstrated in an Alzheimer’s disease (AD) mouse model [
17
] and, acute stress induces microglial phagocytosis in a manner that later promotes the extinction of anxiety-like behaviors in mice [
18
]. In addition, microglia mediate forgetting via complement-dependent synaptic elimination [
19
]. Accordingly, these studies provide substantial evidence for the participation of microglia in neuronal diseases and, more specifically, they suggest that microglia can exert long-term deleterious impacts on synaptic plasticity, effects which might underlie mental disorders. Interestingly, in patients with MDD, synaptic deficits and microglial activation are observed in the brain, especially in the hippocampus [
20
]. One approach to model MDD can be achieved with chronic exposure to stress, effects which can lead to several long-lasting adaptive changes and decreased activity in stress-sensitive brain regions such as in the hippocampus of mice [
21
,
22
]. When collating these findings, it seems reasonable to conclude that chronic stress-induced reductions in microglial-neuronal interactions can contribute to MDD, however, the molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying this process remain unclear.
In this study, by combining three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction, molecular docking, electrophysiology, optogenetics and chemogenetic methods, we demonstrate that the Dkk3 protein is associated with the development of depressive- and anxiety-like symptoms in mice. Specifically, we show that CUMS exposure decreases the expression of Dkk3 in CA1 pyramidal neurons, an effect which activates the Wnt signaling pathway to increase the release of CX3CL1. This activation of the CX3CL1/CX3CR1 pathway induces microglial activation, which in turn increases the engulfment of neuronal spine, thereby reducing CA1 neuronal activity to contribute to behaviors deficits. Accordingly, these results provide novel insights into the mechanisms involved with the functional regulation of Dkk3 in depression-like phenotypes and, notably, identify Dkk3 as a potential target for the development of strategies for use in the prognosis and treatment of MDD.
Results
Chronic stress induces depressive- and anxiety-like behaviors along with microglial activation within the hippocampal CA1 region
The CUMS protocol used to generate a rat model of depression is presented in Fig.
1A
. Following exposure to 6 weeks of CUMS, rats were assessed for depression using four different behavioral tests. In the sucrose preference test (SPT), CUMS rats exhibited a decreased preference for the sucrose solution as compared to that of control rats (Fig.
1B
), suggesting anhedonia-related behavior, which is considered a core symptom of depression in humans. Results from the open-field test (OFT) showed that CUMS exposure produced a decrease in the time exploring center area of the arena (Fig.
1C
), indicating depression-related fear and anxiety. Forced swim test (FST) results demonstrated that CUMS rats showed increased immobility times, results indicative of behavioral despair (Fig.
1D
). Finally, CUMS rats spent significantly less time in the open arms and fewer entries into the open arms of the elevated plus maze (EPM) as compared to that of non-stressed control rats (Fig.
1E
), results which indicate increased levels of anxiety. Taken together, the collective results from these four behavioral assays demonstrate that these rats developed a depressive- and anxiety-like phenotype in response to CUMS exposure. The statistically significant differences in the data as obtained from these four behavioral tests were then used to generate receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, which represent an objective method extensively used in clinical epidemiology for the evaluation of binary classifiers [
23
]. The ROC curves (Fig.
1B–E
) were then used to calculate optimal cutoff values based on the Youden J index [
24
]. With this index, it is possible to arrive at an unbiased determination as to whether an individual animal is positive for a given behavioral phenotype, as revealed by the degree of variability in the display of depressive-like behaviors for individual rats in the different groups (Fig.
1F
). The cutoff values that have the combination of highest true-positive and lowest false-positive rates were 64.91% for the SPT, 27.15 s for the EPM and 43.18 s for the FST. These results suggest that CUMS exposure serves as a reliable model for investigations involving depression and anxiety.

Results from previous studies have shown that human stress disorders, such as MDD, with their resultant behavioral pathologies, in part, involve aberrant microglia activation and an increase in a local production of proinflammatory cytokines [
6
,
7
]. Stress has been reported to increase the expression of several cytokines and chemokines or other complement factors within brain regions [
25
]. Therefore, we determined levels of a panel of several inflammatory factors, chemokines and complement factors with use of qPCR analysis. Our results demonstrated that the expression levels of pro-inflammatory factors IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α and INF-γ were significantly increased, while the expression levels of anti-inflammatory factors IL-4 and IL-10 were significantly decreased in hippocampal CA1 region of CUMS group (Fig.
1G
). In addition, the expression levels of chemokines CXCL1, CXCL2, CCL2, CCL5, CX3CL1 and CX3CR1 and the complement factors C1q and C3 also showed an increasing trend in CUMS rats (Fig.
1H
). As the hippocampal CA1 region is known to be implicated in the pathogenesis of depressive- and anxiety-like disorders [
26
], we assessed the effects of CUMS on microglial activation within the CA1 region of rats, as achieved with use of immunofluorescent detection of the microglia-specific marker, ionized calcium-binding adapter molecule 1 (Iba1). We found that CUMS significantly increased the number of Iba1
+
cells (Fig.
1I, J
). As microglial morphology is correlated with their activation status [
27
], we also analyzed microglial morphology using multi-layer scanning and three-dimensional reconstruction. With these procedures we demonstrated that a significantly decreased number of intersections and branch points, as well as shorter lengths of processes, were present in microglia after CUMS exposure (Fig.
1I, K–M
).
CUMS exposure increases microglial engulfment of neuronal spines and accelerates synaptic loss
Chronic stress has been shown to activate microglia by changing their morphology and, will usually increase microglial engulfment of neuronal spines in limbic brain regions, such as the hippocampus [
28
–
30
]. To delineate the interactions between microglial processes and dendritic spines within CA1 neurons, we used viral sparse labeling to visualize CA1 pyramidal neurons in rats. Results from our immunofluorescent staining and three-dimensional reconstruction assays showed that the size of microglial-dendritic contact were increased in CUMS rats (Fig.
2A, B
). We further evaluated microglial engulfment of synaptic elements by quantifying the amount of PSD95. Significantly increased numbers of PSD95 puncta were found in the major histocompatibility complex-II (MHCII), a marker often expressed on activated microglia, and in CD68, the macrophage marker, within microglial structures following CUMS exposure, effects which were accompanied with increased levels of MHCII and CD68 (Fig.
2C–E
,
S1A–C
). As microglial engulfed synapses are eventually digested by lysosomes, we next quantified PSD95 engulfment by co-staining PSD95 and lysosome-associated membrane protein 2 (Lamp2) microglial, with the results of this staining providing further support for the reconstruction of microglia and neuronal dendrites (Fig.
2F, G
).

To further assess the effects of synaptic alterations upon microglial engulfment, we examined the co-localization of pre-synaptic vGluT1, and post-synaptic PSD95, with the results of this assay revealing a significant loss of synapses. In addition, there was a significant decrease in the number of functional synaptic contacts, as indicated by the density of vGluT1-PSD95 co-localization in CUMS rats (Fig.
2H, I
). We next evaluated the remodeling of CA1 neuronal spines by bilaterally infusing pAAV-PTRE-tight-NLS-Cre-WPRE and pAAV-hSyn-DIO-WPRE viruses into the CA1 region to specifically label dendritic spines of pyramidal neurons in rats (Fig.
2J
). To this end, we quantified the changes in densities of three types of dendritic spines (mushroom, stubby and thin). Typically, stubby and thin spines are considered as immature subtypes, while mushroom spines represent a more mature and stable subtype. Compared with control rats, CUMS exposure decreased the number of dendritic mushroom spines (Fig.
2K, L
). As chronic stress can contribute to synaptic loss, we assessed functional synaptic plasticity by measuring spontaneous excitatory post-synaptic currents (sEPSC) and spontaneous inhibitory postsynaptic current (sIPSC) within the hippocampus. With whole-cell patch clamp recordings we showed that a significant impairment in the amplitudes and frequencies of sEPSC or sIPSC were present in CA1 pyramidal neurons of CUMS rats (Fig.
2M–P
,
S1D–G
). We also found that the spontaneous firing frequency of action potentials (APs) were significantly decreased in these neurons of CUMS rats (Fig.
S1H–I
). These results reveal that CUMS exposure leads to an increase in microglial phagocytosis of neuronal synapses, thus further contributing to synaptic loss and hypoirritability, which suggesting that this microglial phagocytosis may be involved with the development of depressive- and anxiety-like behaviors.
To further verify that CUMS-induced microglial priming is required for depressive- and anxiety-like symptoms, we conducted a loss-of-function study using a genetic approach to selectively ablate microglia. To achieve this goal, cre-dependent AAVs encoding a modified procaspase 3 (AAV-FLEX-taCasp3-TEVp) [
31
] were injected into the CA1 region of CX3CR1-creERT2 mice to target microglia (Fig.
S2A
). As activation of taCasp3 has been shown to induce cell apoptosis, we employed TUNEL staining (Fig.
S2B
). We also stained microglia using Iba1 to determine the efficiency of microglial deletion (Fig.
S2C, D
). As based on results from our behavioral assays, we found that mice lacking microglia exhibited an antidepressant phenotype (Fig.
S2E–M
) and the density of synaptic vGluT1 and PSD95 co-localization in these microglia ablation mice were significant increased (Fig.
S2N, O
). These results led us to speculate that activation of microglia in the CA1 region in response to chronic stress may be involved in the development of depressive- and anxiety-like behaviors.
Manipulation of CA1 pyramidal neuronal activity modulates depressive- and anxiety-like behaviors
As an approach to provide a more comprehensive verification regarding the role of neuronal activity within the CA1 region in depression, we determined whether an artificial activation of CA1 pyramidal neurons could rescue the depressive- and anxiety-like behaviors in a CUMS mouse model. To achieve this goal, neurons were optogenetically stimulated by virally expressing ChR2 in CaMKII-cre mice, which exposure to CUMS for 6 weeks, followed by an optic fiber implant directly above the injection site (Fig.
3A
). The efficacy of ChR2 activation was initially verified from results of whole-cell recordings of hippocampal slices. Three weeks after virus injection, spiking responses to current stimulation ranging from 0–100 pA in ChR2-expressing neurons were significantly increased, while the rheobase of spike generations was reduced in these hippocampal brain slices (Fig.
3B, C
). Activation of CA1 pyramidal neurons, as resulting from optogenetically stimulated ChR2-expression in CUMS mice, also showed behavioral responses indicative of reduced depression and anxiety. Specifically, decreased immobility times in the FST and TST were observed as compared with that observed in control mCherry-expressing mice (Fig.
3D–G
). Moreover, these ChR2-expressing mice showed increases in the amount of time exploring the center area in the OFT and increased times in the open arms of the EPM (Fig.
3H–J
,
S3A–C
). These results suggest that activation of CA1 pyramidal neurons could relieve depressive- and anxiety-like behaviors.

To further assess whether CA1 neurons are involved in behavioral disorders resulting from chronic stress, we inhibited the activity of these neurons by expressing the cre-dependent inhibitory DREADD receptor, hM4Di, in CaMKII-cre mice (Fig.
3K
). Whole-cell slice recordings verified that the application of clozapine-N-oxide (CNO, 10uM) significantly suppressed activity in hM4Di-expressing neurons (Fig.
3L
) as demonstrated by significant increases in spike thresholds and decreases spike numbers in response to step injections ranging from 0–200 pA, while administration of a CNO washout could reverse these changes (Fig.
3M, N
). Effects of CA1 neuronal suppression were next examined in mice expressing hM4Di or mCherry in response to an in vivo injection of CNO (i.p. 5 mg/kg). Strikingly, we observed that these hM4Di-expressing mice displayed depressive- and anxiety-like behaviors (Fig.
3O–T
,
S3D–F
). Together, these results show that CA1 neuronal hypoactivity is associated with depressive-like behavioral disorders in mice, suggesting that CA1 neurons may represent a functional link through which CUMS exposure affects microglia in a manner that eventually manifests as depressive-like behaviors.
Downregulation of Dkk3 activates Wnt signaling in the hippocampal CA1 region of CUMS rats
The Wnt (wingless-type mouse mammary tumor virus integration site family) signaling pathway plays a critical role in neuronal spine morphogenesis and neurotransmission during development and in mature neurons [
32
]. To examine the potential molecular mechanisms through which microglial activation may affect the engulfment of synapses which result in depressive-like behaviors, a proteomic analysis was performed within hippocampal CA1 tissue from control and CUMS rats (Fig.
4A
). Heatmap diagrams revealed that differential protein expression levels were present between the two groups (Fig.
S4A, B
). Results from our gene ontology (GO) analyses showed that the function of complement activation, phagocytosis and immune responses were enhanced and accompanied with upregulated genes in the CUMS group, findings which were consistent with the results of our qPCR assays (Fig.
S4C
). The differentially expressed proteins obtained between the control versus CUMS groups are displayed with use of volcano plot filtering. Notably, the Dkk3 protein, dickkopf Wnt signaling pathway inhibitor 3, was significantly decreased in CUMS rats (Fig.
4B
). A GO enrichment analysis was then performed on potential target genes of Dkk3, with the results revealing that the target genes identified were found to be associated with Wnt signaling pathways (Fig.
4C
). When examining the expression of Dkk3 in several emotion-related brain regions with use of western blot analysis we found that Dkk3 was mainly expressed in the hippocampus (Fig.
4D
).

With regard to the intracellular function of Dkk3, cytoplasmic Dkk3 may bind to β-Trcp, which can then facilitate transcription factor β-catenin degradation [
33
,
34
]. β-catenin levels are regulated by the ubiquitin-dependent proteolysis system [
35
]. Therefore, we next examined whether β-Trcp and β-catenin are target molecules of Dkk3. The protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis showed that there may exist a direct interaction among Dkk3, β-Trcp and β-catenin (Fig.
4E
). According to the molecular docking, interaction between Dkk3 and β-Trcp as derived from rats, which binding energy is −37.8 kcal/mol (Fig.
4F
). Co-immunoprecipitation (CO-IP) analysis showed that Dkk3 interacted with β-Trcp. Notably, CUMS exposure reduced this interaction between Dkk3 and β-Trcp (Fig.
4G, H
). In addition, the level of ubiquitinated β-catenin, as measured using anti-β-catenin antibody to precipitate β-catenin followed by Western blot analysis with an anti-ubiquitin antibody, was dramatically decreased in CUMS rats (Fig.
4I
). This result is consistent with the observation of an increased expression of β-catenin. Taken together, the findings from these last series of experiments suggest that the CUMS-induced decrease of Dkk3 expression, may activate the Wnt signaling pathway via reducing the ubiquitination levels of β-catenin.
Neuronal-specific knockdown of Dkk3 enhances Wnt-CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling, microglial engulfment and depressive phenotypes
As results obtained from our co-staining with Iba1, microtubule-associated protein 2 (MAP2), glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) or Oligodendrocyte transcription factor 2 (Olig2) assays demonstrated that Dkk3 was decreased in both neurons and microglia of CUMS rats, but almost had non expression in astrocytes and oligodendrocytes (Figs.
4J–M
,
S5A, B
), we next investigated whether it was neuronal or microglial Dkk3 that is involved in depressive-related phenotypes. To accomplish this goal, we first infused a cre-dependent adeno-associated virus (AAV) vector expressing Dkk3 short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) into the CA1 region of CX3CR1-creERT2 mice to specifically downregulate microglial Dkk3 expression (Fig.
S6A–C
). Interestingly, we found that a microglial-specific knockdown of Dkk3 in mice failed to produce any behavioral deficits in any of our behavioral tests (Fig.
S6D–L
), nor were there any changes in the co-localization of vGluT1 and PSD95 (Fig.
S6M, N
). Therefore, to specifically knockdown the Dkk3 in neurons, we bilaterally infused pAAV-Syn-shDkk3-eGFP into the CA1 region of rats (Fig.
5A, B
). In contrast to that observed with the knockdown of microglial Dkk3, depressive- and anxiety-like behaviors were observed in these rats receiving a neuronal-specific knockdown of Dkk3. In specific, these rats showed significant decreases in sucrose consumption in the SPT (Fig.
5C
), significant increases in immobility times in the FST (Fig.
5D
) and exhibited significant anxiety-like behaviors within the OFT and EPM (Fig.
S7A–F
). Accordingly, these data demonstrate that a selective knockdown of Dkk3 in neurons, but not microglia, produces depressive-like behaviors.

Microglial activation is usually regulated by the chemokine fractalkine CX3CL1 and its receptor, CX3CR1 [
36
]. In the brain, CX3CL1 is a unique neuronal chemokine, which can be cleaved to yield a soluble isoform, and is widely involved in activity-dependent synaptic pruning [
37
,
38
]. CX3CR1 is a G-protein-coupled receptor exclusively expressed by microglia [
39
]. The CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling pathway has been shown to play an important role in the maintenance of neural-immune communication, as well as in the bidirectional interaction between neurons and microglia in health and disease [
40
,
41
]. Therefore, we focused on the CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling pathway as related to the regulation of interactions between neurons and microglia. As expected, western blot assay results showed that CUMS exposure or a neuronal-specific knockdown of Dkk3 decreased Dkk3 levels and activated the Wnt signaling pathway. Such effects may then promote transcription factor β-catenin translocation to the nucleus and activate the transcription of the target gene, CX3CL1, by interacting with TCF/LEF, ultimately activating the CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling pathway (Figs.
5E, F
,
S8
). In addition, mRNA levels of CX3CL1, CX3CR1, CCL2 and CXCL2 were also found to be significantly increased in rats with a neuronal-specific knockdown of Dkk3 (Fig.
5G
).
Next, we evaluated the effect of a neuronal-specific knockdown of Dkk3 on microglial engulfment of neuronal spines. In rats with a neuronal-specific knockdown of Dkk3, three-dimensional reconstructions showed that microglia were activated (Fig.
S9A–E
) and there was a significant increase in microglial engulfment of PSD95 (Fig.
5H–J

Results from the sholl analysis revealed that knockdown of Dkk3 decreased the number of dendritic intersections as a function of radial distance from the soma, decreased the number of dendritic spines (Fig.

To further determine whether the neuron-specific knockdown of Dkk3 regulates synaptic structure and neuronal excitability through microglia rather than its own direct effects, we performed microglia depletion experiments on mice with neuron-specific Dkk3 knockdown in the hippocampal CA1 region. Together, these findings collectively support the conclusion that a downregulation of Dkk3 can promote microglial phagocytosis within CA1 neuronal synapses. Such effects impair synaptic functions, apparently via activating the Wnt-CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling pathway, thereby contributing to depressive-like behaviors.
Dkk3 overexpression rescues depressive- and anxiety-like behaviors through inhibiting microglial engulfment
As a complementary approach to evaluate the function of Dkk3 in the CA1 neuronal network as related to depression, the pAAV-Dkk3-eGFP-WPRE virus was infused bilaterally into CA1 neurons of CUMS rats to overexpress Dkk3 (Fig.

) and reductions in anxiety-like behavior in the OFT and EPM (Fig.

) as compared with that observed in CUMS rats receiving a control virus infusion. These findings suggest that an upregulation of Dkk3 within CA1 neurons rescues the core symptoms of depression in CUMS rats. Whether Dkk3 overexpression mediated microglial engulfment of neuronal spines through the Wnt-CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling pathway was examined in our next series of experiments. This Dkk3 overexpression reversed the CUMS-induced pathway activation and upregulated protein expressions of the transcription factor β-catenin, CX3CL1 and CX3CR1(Fig.

Discussion
There is growing evidence indicating that a dynamic communication exists between the brain and the neuroimmune system, which can result in the depressive-like behavioral responses observed to physical stress [
42
,
43
]. The primary goal of this study was to identify some of the specific mechanisms of neuroimmune signaling involved with these stress-induced behavioral disorders. Specifically, with use of the CUMS rodent model of depression, we investigated the antidepressant and anxiolytic effects of Dkk3 and its underlying mechanisms. Therefore, our results suggest that Dkk3 serves as a significant modulator of immune activation and a potential target for use in the treatment of stress-induced depression.
The pathogenesis of depression is complex, and the mental and emotional manifestations associated with depression can result from biological, psychological and/or social environmental factors. Among these, chronic and low-intensity stressors represent one of the notable pathogenic factors identified as contributing to depression [
44
]. Accordingly, we chose the CUMS rodent model to induce depression-like behaviors. A battery of behavioral tests were then used to assess and verify this depression. The SPT was used to assess anhedonia, a core symptom of depression. FST and TST were used to evaluate depressive symptoms such as hopelessness. In addition, the EPM and OFT were used to assess anxiety. The results obtained from these tests demonstrated that the CUMS treatment employed clearly produced behavioral responses indicative of depression.
As a higher central nervous system center involved with the regulation of emotion, the hippocampus has attracted considerable attention for its relationship with depression. In particular, the CA1 region of the hippocampus, which is composed of densely arranged large pyramidal neurons [
45
], represents a crucial site for cognition and spatial learning, as well as playing a key role in memory and emotional information processing. In patients with depression the hippocampal CA1 region shows signs of damage and degeneration with these effects then contributing to neurological disorders and the aggravation of depressive symptoms [
21
,
46
,
47
]. These findings are consistent with those as obtained with our animal models since the CUMS-induced depressive-like behaviors were accompanied with a decrease in CA1 neuronal activity. To further verify the significant role exerted by these CA1 pyramidal neurons, we used optogenetic and chemogenetic methods to manipulate CA1 pyramidal neuronal activity. The results from these experiments showed that activation of CA1 neurons was required for coping to stress and prevention of the progression of depressive symptoms, while depressive-like behaviors were present following a chemogenetic-induced inhibition in the activity of these neurons.
In our current study we found that CUMS resulted in a significant activation of CA1 microglia, with associated changes in cellular morphology and numbers, along with increased expressions of both inflammatory and phagocytic markers. Microglia are the resident macrophages of the central nervous system and their functions have been extensively studied in various brain pathologies. Microglia play a role in surveillance and respond to a variety of stimuli indicative of changes in homeostatic conditions. Thus, it is not surprising that they are involved in many pathological conditions, including responses to stress. It has been reported that microglial processes are often in close proximity to neuronal somata and dendritic spines, and that the dynamics of microglial processes are regulated by sensory experience and/or neuronal activity [
48
,
49
]. In the present study we show that microglia in the CA1 region are activated in mice subjected to CUMS, effects which were accompanied with increased engulfments of CA1 neuronal dendritic spines and larger contact areas within these neurons. Moreover, these depression-promoting effects were blocked following an experimental deletion of microglia, which further reveal the significance of microglial contributions to CUMS-induced hypoactivity of CA1 neurons. In addition to microglia, other neuroglial cells play important roles in the CNS. Astrocytes, which provide trophic support for neuronal growth and regulate synapse formation and signaling in the CNS, are also involved with regulating synaptic elimination during development [
50
,
51
]. And, oligodendrocytes are mainly responsible for the regulation of axonal growth and the production and maintenance of myelination. Although oligodendrocyte progenitors exert phagocytic effects upon synapses during circuit remodeling, mature oligodendrocytes are not directly involved in synaptic pruning [
52
]. Here, our results from immunofluorescent staining experiments showed that little expression of Dkk3 in astrocytes and oligodendrocytes. Thus, we hypothesized that these two types of cells do not play a role in neuronal spines engulfment in our current study.
The Wnt signaling pathway, which regulates diverse developmental processes in the nervous system, has been implicated in the modulation of neurogenesis, dendritic morphogenesis and synaptic function [
32
,
53
]. Dkk3 is an atypical member of the Dkk family of Wnt inhibitors, which also includes Dkk1, Dkk2 and Dkk4 [
54
]. Dkk1, Dkk2 and Dkk4 directly bind to LRP6 and serve as Wnt antagonists [
55
]. Via interaction with Kremen receptors, Dkk1 and Dkk2 induce endocytosis of LRP6, thereby suppressing the Wnt–β-catenin-dependent signaling pathway [
56
]. Interestingly, Dkk3 does not interact with Kremen to induce internalization of LRP6, which suggest that functional, and notable, differences might exist between Dkk3 and the other Dkk family members [
57
,
58
]. There are reports indicating that Dkk3 antagonizes canonical Wnt–β-catenin signaling [
59
–
61
]. Dkk3 is abundant in the liver and brain, and the expression of Dkk3 had been detected by in situ hybridization in the lateral ventricular zone, pyramidal neurons of the hippocampus and in cortical neurons [
62
]. Moreover, Dkk3 has also been detected in the cortex and pyramidal cells in the human brain and has been reported to be downregulated in elderly schizophrenic subjects [
63
]. Whether, and how, Dkk3 interferes with the Wnt signaling pathway in MDD has not been systematically examined thus far. Here, we provide evidence that Dkk3 is widely distributed in emotion-related brain regions, including the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), nucleus accumbens (NAc), basolateral amygdala (BLA), lateral habenular nucleus (LHb) and, is particularly prominent within the hippocampus. Notably, Dkk3 is decreased in CA1 neurons and its overexpression in CUMS rats can alleviate CUMS-induced CA1 neuronal engulfments and synaptic transmission, thus reversing the depressive-like behaviors that would normally result from CUMS exposure. Dkk3 has been reported to bind to β-Trcp, thereby promoting the degradation of β-catenin [
33
] and, we verified this interaction as demonstrated using molecular docking and CO-IP assays. Levels of ubiquitinated β-catenin are decreased by CUMS exposure, suggesting that the decreased expression of Dkk3 can activate the Wnt signaling pathway.
Synaptic elimination represents an important process for the formation of appropriate/normal neural circuits and is crucial for brain development and maintenance of neuronal homeostasis in depression [
64
,
65
]. However, the molecular mechanisms regulating microglial-mediated synaptic elimination remain unclear. In part, this lack of knowledge can be attributable to the complexity resulting from the variety of molecules involved, including CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling, classic complement cascade-dependent phagocytic signaling, chemokine signaling and BDNF, that can both promote and inhibit the removal of specific synaptic connections [
66
–
70
]. Our current results show that in CA1 neurons the transcription factor β-catenin regulated CX3CL1 transcription can lead to microglial activation and increased microglial engulfment via binding with CX3CR1. In CUMS mice, CX3CL1 McAb treatment reversed their depressive-like behaviors via improving the phagocytic function of microglia. Notably, Dkk3 overexpression can directly inhibit the Wnt-CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling pathway that mediates neuronal-microglial interactions to decrease microglial activation and engulfment of CA1 neuronal spines, consequently improving CA1 neuronal activity. The classical complement signaling pathway, C1q, is involved in synaptic pruning during development and such classical pathways can induce C3 activation, which can also act through alternative pathways [
71
]. In fact, microglia are considered to be the dominant cell types that synthesize C1q in the central nervous system [
72
]. Our results also revealed that CUMS exposure increased mRNA expressions of the complement factors C1q and C3, however the exact function of these factors as related to CUMS requires further investigation.
In this study, although we demonstrated that the expression of Dkk3 was decreased in both neurons and microglia in CUMS rodents, only a neuronal-, but not microglial-specific, knockdown of Dkk3 promoted phagocytosis in the hippocampal CA1 region. While our results demonstrated that a microglial-specific knockdown of Dkk3 in mice didn’t display of depressive- and anxiety-like behaviors, whether it plays a role in other aspects of hippocampal functions, such as spatial cognition or memory, requires further investigation. In addition to CX3CL1 and CX3CR1, expression levels of other chemokines, including CXCL2, CCL2 and CCL5, were also increased in our experimental paradigm. Whether these other chemokines promote microglial phagocytosis through other pathways in MDD represent other areas of investigation to be addressed in future studies.
In summary, the collective findings of these experiments reveal an important role for Dkk3 in stress-induced depression. In specific, here we report that Dkk3 regulates the Wnt-CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling pathway, mediates microglial activation and enhances microglial engulfment in these stress-induced depression models. Moreover, we show that treatment with a CX3CL1 monoclonal antibody produces significant therapeutic effects that can mitigate depression. Notably, Dkk3, the dickkopf Wnt signaling pathway inhibitor 3, which we found to play a critical role within the chronic stress-induced depression model, may serve as a promising biomarker and therapeutic target for use in treating stress-related disorders.
Materials and methods
Animals
The male Sprague Dawley rats (160–180 g body weight, 5–6 weeks) and C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks) used in these experiments were obtained from the Shandong University Animal Centre, while CaMKII-cre and CX3CR1-creERT2 mice were obtained from Yangzhou Biological Technology. Animals were housed 3 per cage under standard laboratory conditions (12-h light/dark cycle with lights on at 0700 h; 23 °C ± 1 °C ambient temperature; 55% ± 10% relative humidity) with free access to food and water for one week prior to use in the experiments. All experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of Shandong University (ECSBMSSDU-2022-2-65) and strictly abided by regulations of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC).
Chronic unpredictable mild stress (CUMS) model
The CUMS procedure adopted was that as described in a previous study [
73
]. Briefly, following one week period of acclimation to the laboratory conditions, stressed animals were housed individually and randomly exposed to various stressors once a day over a 6-week period. None of these stressors were administered successively and consisted of 24 h water/food deprivation, 24 h soiled/empty cage, reversed light/dark cycle, overnight illumination, 2 h confinement, 1 min tail clip and 3 min of cold swimming. No stressors were applied in control, non-stressed, animals that were housed under identical conditions.
Behavioral assays
All behavioral tests were performed during the light phase (1100 –1700 h). Each animal was allowed 15 min acclimation to the various test conditions before the start of the behavioral test. Behavioral analyses were performed blinded as to the experimental condition.
For optogenetic experiments, a fiberoptic implant was connected to a 473 nm DPSS laser diode (Laserglow) using a fiberoptic cable and rotary adaptor. Laser output was controlled using a Master-8 pulse stimulator (A.M.P.I.). Power output for the cable was tested using a digital power meter (Thorlabs) and was checked before and after the testing of each animal. Animals expressing ChR2 (or mCherry) received 473 nm light stimulation (10 Hz, 5–8 mW/mm
2
, 5 ms pulses) during the experiment. The optogenetic equipment was provided by RWD.
For the chemogenetic experiments, animals expressing DREADDs (or mCherry) were injected intraperitoneally with 5 mg/kg CNO at 30 min before the start of the experiment.
Sucrose preference test (SPT)
The SPT was performed as a means of assessing anhedonia symptoms [
74
]. Animals were individually housed and were trained to drink from two bottles of water for 24 h, followed by two bottles with 1% (W/V) sucrose for 24 h. Rats or mice were then water deprived for 24 h and then exposed to one bottle containing 1% sucrose and one bottle with water, rats were tested for a 2 h period and mice for 24 h. Total consumption of each fluid was measured and a sucrose preference was calculated by dividing the total consumption of sucrose by the total consumption of both water and sucrose.
Forced swim test (FST)
The FST is a method used to evaluate behavioral despair. Prior to the test day, the rats were subjected to a pre-test during which they were individually placed in a cylinder (50 cm high × 20 cm diameter) filled with water (23–25 °C, 30 cm deep) for 15 min under standard lighting conditions (mice did not require this pre-test) [
74
]. On the test day, animals were individually placed in the cylinder under the conditions described above and permitted to swim for 5 min, with their behaviors videotaped from a camera located on the side of the cylinder. Immobility times during the last 4 min of the test were recorded offline by an observer. Immobility was defined as the absence of movements required to maintain their head above water.
Tail suspension test (TST)
This test was based on a previous report with minor modifications [
75
]. Only mice were subjected to the TST which consisted of suspension by their tails while being secured to a horizontal bar with tape. The immobility time was recorded for 5 min. Mice were considered immobile only when they hung passively and were completely motionless. The behavioral apparatus was thoroughly cleaned with 75% ethanol between animals.
Open field test (OFT)
The OFT was used to analyze spontaneous exploratory activity and evaluate the level of anxiety. Rats were placed in the center of a dimly lit arena 100 cm × 100 cm × 50 cm and mice in a 50 cm × 50 cm × 50 cm arena for a 5 min period. Spontaneous locomotor activity was recorded using a video camera [
76
]. Total distance traveled (cm) and time (s) spent in the center area during the 5 min test period were analyzed automatically using a video tracking software program (SMART, Panlab).
Elevated-plus maze test (EPM)
The EPM test was used to assess anxiety-like behavior and was conducted as described previously [
77
,
78
]. The maze consisted of a plus-shaped maze, including two “open” and two “closed” arms (each 60 cm × 12 cm) with walls in the closed arms being 30 cm in height. Each animal was placed in the central area of the maze, oriented toward an open arm and was allowed to explore the maze for 5 min. The room was maintained in a quiet state during the entire test and the apparatus was cleaned with 75% ethanol after each trial. The total distance traveled, along with time spent and number of entries into the open arms were recorded and analyzed.
Drug administration
Clozapine-N-oxide (CNO) (MCE, HY-17366A) was dissolved in DMSO (10 mg/ml) as a stock solution and diluted in 0.9% saline for the chemogenetic experiments. It was injected (5 mg/kg, i.p.) at 30 min before behavioral tests.
Tamoxifen (TAM) (Sigma-Aldrich, H6278), which was used to activate the Cre activity within CX3CR1-creERT2 mice, was dissolved in 100% ethanol (20 mg/ml) as a stock solution and suspended in corn oil (Selleck, S6701) to make a final concentration of 10 mg/ml. To activate the Cre activity within CX3CR1-creERT2 mice before virus injection, 100 ul of 10 mg/ml TAM was injected in these mice on 4 sequential days.
XAV-939 (Selleck, S1180) is a small molecular inhibitor of the Wnt/β‑catenin signaling pathway. XAV-939 specifically inhibits TNKS1/2, thereby blocking their PARsylation modification of Axin. The accumulated Axin promotes β-catenin degradation, con-sequently suppressing Wnt signaling pathway. The dose and route of XAV-939 administration was based upon previous studies with minor modifications and consisted of a 40 mg/kg intraperitoneal injection for 7 days, with DMSO (0.1%) serving as the vehicle control [
79
,
80
].
BML-284 (Selleck, S8178) is an agonist of the Wnt signaling pathway which activates Wnt signaling dependent on beta-catenin transcriptional activity [
81
]. Each animal was injected intraperitoneally (i.p.) with 5 mg/kg for 7 days. Rats in the control group were injected with the same volume of DMSO (0.1%).
PLX3397 (Selleck, S7818) is an orally available CSF-1R inhibitor. that can be used to deplete microglia. To eliminate microglia, PLX3397 was mixed into standard diet at a concentration of 600 mg/kg. Following stereotactic injection of AAV-shDkk3 into the hippocampal CA1 region, mice were provided ad libitum access to the PLX3397-supplemented diet for 3 weeks. Ctrl group received standard chow without PLX3397.
Infusion of a CX3CL1 McAb
The CX3CL1 monoclonal antibody was infused into the brain ventricle through a pre-implanted cannula. Mice were anesthetized and positioned in a stereotaxic apparatus (RWD Instruments) and the cannula was implanted using the following coordinates: anteroposterior −0.3 mm, lateral +0.8 mm and at a depth of +2.5 mm. The cannula (RWD, Shenzhen) was secured with a small amount of dental cement and the mice were returned to their home cage for recovery. For 3 sequential days prior to behavioral testing, a 5 ul liquid form of the CX3CL1 antibody (500 ug/ml, GeneTex, GTX53291) was infused through the cannula. For the the control group, 5 ul of IgG (Bioworld,
B00051
) was infused through the cannula.

Animals were then placed on a heating pad for their recovery from the surgery/anesthesia. The hippocampal CA1 region coordinates relative to bregma were: rats - AP, –3.2 mm from bregma; ML, ±1.94 mm; DV, –3.24 mm; mice - AP, –1.94 mm from bregma; ML, ±1.0 mm; DV, –1.3 mm. Infection efficiency assays were performed 21 days later and injection sites verified, with only data from animals showing correct injection sites being included in the analyses.

Western blotting
Animals were euthanized immediately after behavioral tests, the brains quickly removed and frozen at −80 °C. Golgi staining
The FD Rapid Golgi Stain Kit (PK401, FD NeuroTechnologies) was used for Golgi staining. All procedures were strictly conducted according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Following anesthesia, intact brains were rapidly removed and maintained on ice. They were rinsed with distilled water to remove surface blood and then each brain was submerged into the mixed impregnation solution comprised of an equal volume of solutions A and B for 2 weeks at room temperature in the dark. After this period, the brains were transferred to solution C and stored at 4 °C for 3 days in the dark. Brain sections (100 μm) were then cut using a cryotome chamber at −23 °C and each section was mounted onto gelatinized slides with solution C and dried at room temperature. The brain sections were stained according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Images of hippocampal CA1 regions were thoroughly reviewed using an Olympus microscope (VS120, Japan) and dendritic spines were assessed using a 100 × objective. On average, three to four pyramidal neurons per animal from six rats were used for the Golgi stain study. Dendritic spine densities and number of branches were determined using Sholl analysis and Image J (v.7.4.2) software.
TUNEL assay
Apoptosis of cells was determined with use of the one-step TUNEL apoptosis assay kit (Vazyme, A113-01) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, brain sections were treated and subsequently fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 30 min, then incubated with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min and washed three times in PBS for 5 min each. The sections were then incubated with 50 μl of TUNEL reaction mixture for 1 h at 37 °C in the dark, with nuclei stained using DAPI. Finally, the expression of apoptotic cells was observed with use of Dragonfly 200 (Andor, UA) and processed by IMARIS software.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves
In order to determine whether individual animals were positive or negative for a specific behavioral phenotype as measured in the EPM, SPT and FST, we used receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves [
86
]. ROC curve analysis has been used extensively in clinical epidemiology for the assessment of diagnostic ability of biomarkers and imaging tests in the classification of diseases. ROC curves are uninfluenced by decision biases and they depict a classifier’s performance over the range of thresholds for sensitivity and specificity. A Youden J Index was calculated from the ROC curves in order to identify the optimal cutoff value that provides the lowest false positive rate (FPR) and the highest true positive rate (TPR). The Youden J index maximizes the differences between TPR (sensitivity) and FPR (1 - specificity): Youden J Index = TPR-FPR = Sensitivity + Specificity - 1. Thus, by maximizing (Sensitivity + Specificity) across various cutoff points, the optimal cutoff point was calculated.
