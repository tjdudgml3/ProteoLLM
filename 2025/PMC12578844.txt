Introduction
Obesity is a chronic metabolic disorder affecting millions of individuals worldwide and is associated with an increased risk of developing conditions such as type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and cardiovascular diseases
1
. Although Body Mass Index (BMI) is commonly used to classify obesity, it has notable limitations. BMI does not distinguish between fat mass and lean mass and fails to consider fat distribution, which is a crucial factor in assessing metabolic risk. For example, visceral fat, which encases vital organs, is closely linked to chronic inflammation and insulin resistance. In contrast, subcutaneous fat has a relatively less detrimental metabolic impact. Consequently, relying solely on BMI for diagnosis is inadequate, and more advanced methods are necessary to more accurately classify obesity types and tailor therapeutic strategies
2
In response to this issue, researchers have redirected their efforts towards identifying obesity phenotypes that incorporate factors such as adipose tissue distribution, inflammation, and insulin sensitivity
2
,
3
. This refined methodology facilitates the categorization of patients based on specific metabolic characteristics, thereby enabling more personalized and effective treatment strategies to be developed. For instance, differentiating individuals with metabolically healthy obesity, characterized by excess adiposity without significant metabolic abnormalities, from those with metabolically unhealthy obesity, who are at an elevated risk of complications, allows for tailored interventions. Such classifications contribute to the optimization of dietary and pharmacological treatment plans in accordance with each individual’s distinct metabolic profile.
In recent years, extracellular vesicles (EVs) have emerged as promising tools for addressing the limitations inherent to conventional diagnostic methodologies. Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are lipid bilayer-bound nanoparticles ranging from 30 to 1000 nm in size, released by nearly all cell types, and are unable to self-replicate. They are present in the extracellular environment and in various body fluids, including blood, urine, saliva, cerebrospinal fluid, and tears. These vesicles function as carriers of proteins, lipids, and genetic material, facilitating intercellular communication and also acting as significant reservoirs of biomarkers. Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are gaining prominence as liquid biopsy tools, particularly for tissues that are difficult to access, owing to their straightforward isolation from biofluids and robust stability of their cargos during circulation. Based on their biogenesis and size, EVs can be categorized into microvesicles (100 nm to 1 μm), which originate from the blebbing of the plasma membrane; exosomes (30–100 nm), which are formed within multivesicular endosomes (MVEs) and secreted via exocytosis; and larger vesicles (50–5000 nm), which include apoptotic bodies released by cells undergoing apoptosis
4
,
5
. Within adipose tissue, EVs reflect the metabolic state of adipocytes and transport bioactive molecules, such as adipokines, which play crucial roles in processes, including energy regulation, inflammation, and insulin sensitivity
6
–
9
.
Recent evidence indicates that adipose tissue exhibits distinct molecular profiles that can distinguish metabolically healthy obesity from unhealthy obesity. Furthermore, these profiles may serve as valuable biomarkers for monitoring disease progression and evaluating responses to therapeutic interventions
10
,
11
. The utilization of extracellular vesicles (EVs) as biomarkers may constitute a pioneering strategy in the enhancement of obesity phenotyping
12
. These vesicles may provide vital insights into an individual’s metabolic condition and associated risks, which are crucial for the development of personalized treatment strategies
13
. Furthermore, EVs may facilitate the monitoring of the efficacy of specific interventions, such as dietary modifications or pharmacological therapies, by offering real-time data on improvements in parameters such as inflammation and insulin sensitivity. Recent advancements in obesity phenotyping underscore the significance of integrating personalized dietary and pharmacological interventions with the use of molecular diagnostic tools. For example, individualized diets can specifically target inflammation and enhance insulin sensitivity, whereas pharmacological treatments can address the distinct metabolic pathways altered in each patient. This comprehensive approach, which combines molecular analysis with tailored therapies, will improve clinical outcomes while reducing the risks associated with generalized treatments
2
. However, the application of extracellular vesicles (EVs) for obesity biomarker screening remains limited, encountering significant challenges related to the difficulty in obtaining both high yield and purity of EVs, as well as in extracting cell- or tissue-specific EVs from biological fluids or cell culture supernatants. Although there have been considerable advancements in the isolation of extracellular vesicles (EVs), distinguishing adipose tissue EVs in circulation remains challenging, necessitating the development of novel technologies for their precise identification
14
,
15
.
To advance the analysis of tissue-specific extracellular vesicles (EVs), sophisticated methodologies such as the Single-Particle Interferometric Reflectance Imaging Sensor (SP-IRIS)/Exoview (Unchained Labs) have been developed. This innovative technique is a chip-based system that captures EVs based on their surface marker expression (tetraspanins) on antibody-capture spots printed in triplicate. ExoView employs SP-IRIS technology to facilitate the high-resolution measurement and quantification of extracellular vesicles (EVs) through immunocapture, paralleling other methodological strategies that leverage the abundant presence of tetraspanins (CD9, CD63, and CD81) on the surface of EVs. Recognizing the limitations of tetraspanin immunocapture in isolating cell- or tissue-specific vesicles, ExoView introduced an innovative feature that facilitates customized vesicle capture through the integration of user-selected antibodies (Exoflex chips). Thus, Exoflex chips are specifically engineered to enable a tailored data acquisition. Compared to other technologies, this method enables the precise detection and characterization of individual extracellular vesicles (EVs), facilitating the analysis of their size, concentration, and molecular cell/tissue-specific composition with exceptional accuracy
16
. In the context of obesity research, SP-IRIS has revealed distinct molecular patterns in EVs derived from steatotic hepatocytes, which can serve as significant repositories of real-time biomarkers for the early stages and progression of Metabolic dysfunction–Associated Steatotic Liver Disease (MASLD)
17
.
In our study, we used SP-IRIS technology to design an adipose tissue-specific chip to examine previously mass-spectrometry identified EVs biomarkers for obesity disease
6
,
7
. This innovative approach facilitated the identification of proteins and other molecular components associated with specific obesity phenotypes, thereby enhancing patient classification and enabling personalized treatment strategies for obesity management. The integration of SP-IRIS with advancements in clinical phenotyping represents a significant advancement toward precision medicine tailored to the individual needs of patients with obesity.

Design of an adipose tissue-specific capture SP-IRIS chip: adipo-chip
The core of SP-IRIS technology is an Si/SiO
2
chip featuring a microarray of functionalized antibody dots
18
,
19
. These dots can capture up to six distinct types of extracellular vesicles (EVs) using common EV tetraspanin antigens (CD81, CD63, and CD9) for EV capture, along with up to two custom capture dots to isolate specific EV populations. Building upon this technology and informed by the existing literature and our prior proteomics analysis, we developed an adipose tissue-specific capture chip, termed the adipo-chip. The selection criteria for candidate proteins to serve as personalized capture agents for the adipo-chip include their classification as membrane proteins and their consistent expression under various conditions, including the constitutive presence in visceral and subcutaneous shed EVs as previously demonstrated by us
6
. Caveolae-associated protein 1 (CAVN-1) and Perilipin-1 (PLIN1), both membrane proteins specific to adipose tissue, have been previously documented as vesicular biomarkers of adipose tissue. Importantly, Perilipin-1 has been identified as a biomarker for the detection of adipocyte-derived extracellular vesicles (EVs) in circulation
6
,
20

Discussion
Given the considerable potential of extracellular vesicles (EVs) analysis in biomedical research and its application in clinical practice, it is essential to develop precise and high-throughput technologies to facilitate the clinical integration of EVs, particularly in relation to disease-associated biomarkers. In this regard, novel methodologies such as the single-particle interferometric reflectance imaging sensor (SP-IRIS) have emerged as valuable tools, enabling the analysis of individual EVs without the need for prior purification. The fundamental principle of SP-IRIS is the capture of EVs using functionalized chips with antibodies that target standard exosomal tetraspanins. Notably, it allows the use of custom antibodies against proteins of interest, thereby circumventing the inherent bias associated with the uneven distribution of tetraspanins in disease contexts
22
. In this study, we describe, for the first time, the design and validation of an SP-IRIS chip functionalized with two capture antibodies to assess adipose-specific secreted vesicles, which may have potential applications in clinical settings through liquid biopsy at the circulating level. Given that body fluids may contain a heterogeneous mixture of EVs derived from various cells and tissues, and considering that blood samples have the potential to collect EVs from any cell within the organism, including those from blood cells and platelets, the separation of cell- or tissue-specific vesicles carrying biomarkers of interest poses a significant challenge
23
. In this study, we addressed this challenge by developing an adipose-specific SP-IRIS chip designed to capture tissue-specific extracellular vesicles (EVs) for biomarker assessment. By these means, we tested and validated the designed chip by analyzing EVs directly secreted by whole adipose tissue of patients with obesity who underwent bariatric surgery, demonstrating for the first time the capacity of the adipo-chip to enrich the capture of adipose secreted EVs, permitting the detection of changes in the described biomarkers.
A key issue in the design of an adipo-chip is the selection of adipose tissue specific proteins for the capture of EVs through capturing antibodies. Under the premise that capture proteins should be present in the membrane of the extracellular vesicle and should be constitutively present to avoid bias, we selected caveolae-associated protein 1 (CAVN-1) and Perilipin-1 (PLIN1). Caveolin-1 (CAV1) is an essential membrane protein that, in conjunction with proteins from the Cavin family, is integral to the formation of caveolae on the cell surface. Caveolae are highly abundant organelles in adipocytes, constituting up to 30% of the cell surface area
24
,
25
.Conversely, perilipin-1 envelops lipid droplets within adipocytes and foam cells (macrophages laden with lipids) and is integral to the metabolism of triglycerides
26
,
27
. In our previous quantitative proteomics (DIA-SWATH) analyses of vesicles secreted by human subcutaneous and visceral obese adipose tissue explants, as well as in murine adipocyte cell models, we identified both proteins and observed their presence in both depots
6
,
7
. Furthermore, perilipin 1 has been proposed as a biomarker for vesicles secreted by adipose tissue
20
.
To preliminarily validate the design of the adipo-chip before its future application at the circulating level through liquid biopsy extraction, we assessed the device’s performance by analyzing secretome samples obtained from human whole adipose tissue explants to directly isolate EVs from the tissue of interest. The objective was to evaluate our previously identified biomarkers in analogous samples, wherein extracellular vesicles (EVs) were isolated through ultracentrifugation, to facilitate subsequent quantitative data-independent acquisition (DIA)-SWATH proteome analysis via liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS)
6
. Considering the detrimental health impact of visceral adipose tissue in comparison to subcutaneous within the context of obesity and acknowledging its secretion profile as being closely associated with obesity-related comorbidities, we selected four proteins of interest that are significantly elevated in vesicles derived from obese visceral adipose tissue as opposed to those of subcutaneous origin
6
. This is the case for transforming growth factor beta-induced (TGFBI)
28
, fatty acid-binding protein 4 (FABP4)
29
, and mimecan
30
, all of which are associated with obesity, visceral adipose tissue, inflammation, and insulin resistance. Our earlier research indicated that circulating EV-TGFBI could be helpful in assessing the status of type-2 diabetes in individuals with obesity, while EV-mimecan might be effective for monitoring adiposity and particularly visceral obesity. Additionally, we selected ceruloplasmin, an adipokine implicated in angiogenesis, that has been identified as a novel adipokine secreted at higher rates in obese adipose tissue
31
. Notably, we also discovered that mimecan, ceruloplasmin, and FABP4 were elevated in vesicles secreted by lipid-hypertrophied murine adipocytes in vitro, and TGFBI was strikingly elevated in EVs shed by insulin-resistant cells
7
.
Through independent analysis of secretomes derived from subcutaneous and visceral adipose tissue explants of consecutive patients undergoing bariatric surgery, utilizing the designed adipo-chip, we demonstrated its potential as a high-throughput technology for the assessment of EV biomarkers associated with disease. We successfully isolated vesicles from all secretomes and observed a higher concentration of EVs in the secretomes derived from visceral adipose tissue explants, as anticipated. This finding aligns with others
32
and our previous analyses of whole adipose tissue explant secretomes using nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) and quantitative mass spectrometry
6
,
11
,
21
,
33
. In addition, we have previously described a higher EV secretion rate in adipocytes under pathological insulin resistance and lipid hypertrophy
7
. In the present study, while no size differences were observed among the vesicles captured at all functionalized spots, we successfully identified the same biomarker variations as those previously detected by LC–MS/MS and validated by western blot/ELISA of ultracentrifuged EVs using specific antibodies
6
. This finding underscores the potential application of the SP-IRIS platform in clinical practice, as it does not require the previous isolation of EVs. Notably, we detected the same significant differences in the biomarkers assessed in EVs captured by both tretaspanins and the specialized adipo-chip functionalized with antibodies against CAV-1 and PLIN1. This outcome is both favorable and expected because direct tissue secretome samples are more concentrated and less complex than body fluids such as blood. Thus, the observed parallel biomarker differences in the adipo-chip and tetraspanin-captured vesicles demonstrate the efficacy of the adipo-chip design, facilitating the detection of consistent differences. From our perspective, these findings suggest that the designed adipo-chip may be useful for enriching the capture of adipose tissue-secreted vesicles at the circulating level, avoiding tissue-unspecific capture through tetraspanins, which may also introduce bias due to variations in their presence and profile linked to pathological conditions
22
. One aspect to be considered in this research is the low colocalization rate of the biomarkers analyzed, as they were predominantly detected in individual vesicles, with mimecan being the most prevalent biomarker across all analyzed vesicles, including those captured with tetraspanins or with CAV-1 and PLIN1 in both tissue types. The function of mimecan as an adipokine within adipose tissue may explain its abundance in adipose EVs, particularly given that direct tissue secretomes were employed in this study. Furthermore, our findings suggest that EVs captured by PLIN1 exhibit a higher proportion of TGFBI than those captured by CAV-1 or tetraspanins. This suggests that the selection of tissue-specific capture antibodies must be conducted with precision to ensure reliable results in the future. Moreover, in this analysis we detected a positive correlation between the levels of mimecan and FABP4 in visceral adipose tissue secreted vesicles captured by tetraspanins and the adipo-chip. However, only the adipo-chip showed a positive correlation between mimecan and TGFBI in captured vesicles from visceral and subcutaneous fat, indicating that the population of captured vesicles is different for the adipo-chip compared to spots functionalized with anti-tetraspanins. Overall, we assert that the findings presented here lay the groundwork for subsequent investigations into tissue-specific EVs, which may have significant clinical implications in the future.
The limitations of this study refer to the sample utilized; while it is optimal for validating the adipo-chip, as it contains directly secreted EVs from adipose tissue, it does not capture the complexity of blood or other liquid biopsies. Consequently, the subsequent step involves evaluating the adipo-chip using plasma samples from individuals with obesity and various comorbidities. However, our designed chip has been effectively utilized by other researchers in different clinical settings involving complex samples. Specifically, the adipo-chip was evaluated using plasma samples from mouse models of metabolic syndrome (MetS), demonstrating its specificity and efficacy in isolating adipose-origin extracellular vesicles (EVs) and detecting biomarkers at the circulating level
34
. Furthermore, the absence of co-localization of the biomarkers under investigation suggests that the captured EVs may originate from various cell types within adipose tissue. Additionally, it is important to consider that although PLIN1 is an adipocyte specific protein, the presence of CAV1 should not be disregarded in vesicles from other cell types and tissues beyond adipocytes, such as endothelial cells or alveolar cells among others, which also express CAV1
35
,
36
.
Methods
Human AT explants samples
Visceral adipose tissue (VAT) and subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT) samples were collected from 13 consecutive patients with severe obesity (BMI ≥ 35 kg/m
2
) who underwent laparoscopic surgery at the Obesity Treatment Unit of the Hospital Clínico Universitario de Santiago, Spain. This study was conducted following the acquisition of written informed consent in compliance with the World Medical Association’s Code of Ethics (Declaration of Helsinki). The Clinical Ethical Committee of Galicia (CEIC), Spain, approved all procedures under code number 2020/497 (clinical data in Suppl. Table
1
). VAT explants were meticulously dissected from the hypogastric region adjacent to the internal organs, whereas SAT was obtained from the mesogastric abdominal area. All procedures received approval from the relevant institutional ethics committee, and informed consent was secured from all participants prior to tissue collection.
The protocol for processing adipose tissue was conducted as previously outlined
6
,
7
. In summary, fresh tissue explants were thoroughly washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Corning, NY, USA) to eliminate blood contaminants and residual debris. Using sterile number 10 surgical blades, tissues were gently scraped to remove the remaining capillaries and cauterized areas before being minced into uniform 1 g fragments. These fragments were then suspended in 25 mL PBS (Lonza) and centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min at room temperature (Sorvall™ ST16, Fisher Scientific) to pellet erythrocytes and cellular debris. For secretome collection, each gram of processed tissue was cultured in 5 mL serum-free DMEM (phenol red-free, low glucose, without L-glutamine; Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Biowest). Following an initial 2-h incubation to allow tissue stabilization, the medium was replaced with fresh medium. After 24 h, the medium was changed again using at a ratio of 3 mL per gram of tissue. After a total incubation period of 48 h, the conditioned medium (secretome) was collected, centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min (Micro Start 17R, VWR), and filtered through 0.22 µm PES membranes (Biofil) to remove cellular debris and larger vesicles. Aliquots were immediately frozen at − 80 °C for subsequent analysis.
Single particle—interferometric reflectance imaging sensor (SP-IRIS) analysis
Extracellular vesicle (EV) characterization was conducted utilizing the SP-IRIS/ExoView® R200 platform (Unchained Labs, USA), which incorporates Single-Particle—Interferometric Reflectance Imaging Sensor (SP-IRIS) technology. This sophisticated system facilitates high-resolution, single-vesicle analysis by integrating interferometric size measurement with multiplexed fluorescence detection of surface markers. The SP-IRIS platform employs antibody-functionalized Si/SiO
2
chips for the specific capture of EVs. In this study, we utilized custom ExoFlex chips (NAV251-1047, Unchained Labs, USA). conjugated with standard tetraspanin spots pre-coated with anti-CD9, -CD63, and -CD81 antibodies and antibodies targeting adipose tissue-specific markers (perilipin-1 and caveolin-1)
6
,
20
. This dual approach facilitated both the comprehensive characterization of EVs and the analysis of tissue-specific subpopulations.
The chips were initially prescanned following a specific protocol to detect particles that may have adhered during the manufacturing process. During the incubation phase, the chips were positioned in the designated plates to prevent contact between the chip corners and the sides of the wells. The secretomes were diluted to a concentration of 10⁹ particles/mL, as determined by nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA Malvern NanoSight NS300 (v3.3 Malvern Panaytical), utilizing the specified incubation buffer. A volume of 50 μL of the diluted sample was incubated overnight without agitation. The chips were processed using an automated Chip Washer (Unchained Labs) the following day, according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, the chips underwent sequential washing with solution A (provided in the kit), followed by fixation with solution C and permeabilization with solution D when intravesicular protein detection was required. Subsequently, additional washes with solution A were performed with gentle agitation (Table
3
).
Prior to characterization via ExoView, antibodies targeting the proteins of interest (Table
1
) were labelled using various fluorophore conjugation kits corresponding to the excitation field. The Alexa Fluor® 488—Lightning-Link® (Abcam), Alexa Fluor® 594—Lightning-Link® (Abcam), Alexa Fluor® 647—Lightning-Link® (Abcam), and DyLight® 755-Lightning-Link® (Abcam) conjugation kits were utilized in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. All reagents were equilibrated at room temperature before use. Subsequently, 1 µL of the modifier reagent was added per 10 µL of antibody (10 µg) and gently agitated. To achieve the desired antibody concentration (1 mg/mL), it was necessary to concentrate some of the antibodies using centrifugation filtration units (Amicon® Ultra-0.5 mL 3 K, Millipore) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The antibody and Modifier mixture was then transferred to a vial containing the lyophilized conjugation mixture and carefully resuspended. The mixture was then incubated in the dark at room temperature for 15 min. Following the incubation period, 1 µL of quencher reagent was added to every 10 µL of the antibody initially used. The fluorophore-conjugated antibodies were stored at 4 °C in the dark. Antibody mixtures were prepared in the provided blocking buffer and incubated with the chips for 1 h at room temperature, with gentle agitation. After extensive washing with solutions A and B (provided in the kit), the chips were rinsed with distilled water, air-dried, and scanned using the Exoview R200 equipment and the ExoView® Scanner software.
Data acquisition and analysis
Fluorescence images were obtained utilizing the ExoView® Scanner with excitation wavelengths of 488, 555, 640, and 750 nm. Image analysis was conducted using the ExoView Analyzer software (v3.2, Unchained Laboratories). Fluorescence thresholds were set at 200 arbitrary units (a.u.) for the red, far-red, and green channels, and 400 a.u. for the blue channel, ensuring that background signals on control spots remained below 50 events for the red, far-red, and green channels, or 100 events for the blue channel, as per the manufacturer’s recommendations. This rigorous thresholding facilitated specific detection, while minimizing nonspecific background signals.
Statistical analysis
Statistical significance was assessed using GraphPad Prism 10.0.3 software employing the Mann–Whitney U test. The normality of the data distribution was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test, with statistical significance defined as
P
≤ 0.05. Pearson Winsorized correlation was employed due to its efficacy in small sample sizes, particularly when the data do not satisfy the bivariate normality assumption required by the conventional Pearson correlation.
P
-values less than 0.05 were deemed statistically significant (*), while ** indicates
p
< 0.01, *** signifies
p
< 0.001, and **** denotes
p
< 0.0001.
