Introduction
Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the most prevalent cause of dementia and a progressive neurodegenerative disorder. Approximately 6.7 million Americans aged 65 and older are currently affected by AD, with projections indicating a rise to 13.8 million by 2060 due to environmental and socioeconomic factors [
1
]. Patients with AD experience a gradual decline in cognitive function, including memory and thinking skills, as well as noticeable changes in social and behavioral interactions, limiting their ability to function independently [
2
].
The pathogenesis mechanisms of AD are still elusive, with hallmark features such as the extracellular accumulation of amyloid‐beta (Aβ) plaques [
3
] and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles (NFT) primarily composed of hyperphosphorylated tau protein [
4
]. These misfolded proteins are suggested to disrupt neuronal communication and elicit chronic neuroinflammation, leading to progressive neuronal damage and cognitive decline [
5
,
6
,
7
]. The intricate interplay between these pathological hallmarks and other AD‐linked biological processes, such as neuroinflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, dysregulated bioenergetics, RNA splicing dysregulation, and disrupted proteostasis, further complicates the disease's progression [
8
].
To unravel the intricate interplay, a global‐scale understanding of dynamic protein dysregulation during AD pathogenesis is essential, as the proteins are workhorses and building blocks in the cell [
9
,
10
]. Proteomics offers a powerful approach to unraveling the molecular mechanisms underlying AD by enabling the comprehensive analysis of protein expression, degradation, modifications, and interactions [
11
,
12
]. Recent technological advancements in proteomics have uncovered the complex interplays of AD‐involved proteins in greater depth, providing increased opportunities to discover new therapeutic targets and biomarkers [
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
,
20
].
In this review, we delve into the proteomic insights that have advanced our knowledge of AD. By synthesizing the latest literature and integrating our own perspectives, we examine how advanced proteomic technologies have advanced our understanding of key biological pathways associated with AD pathology. We also discuss the proteomics technologies that are useful in better understanding the AD pathogenesis mechanisms. Finally, we discuss the future direction of proteomics in advancing AD research.
2
AD‐Related Pathways Discovered by Proteomic Studies
2.1
Aβ and Tau
Aβ plaque and tau NFT are typical neuropathological hallmarks in AD [
21
]. The amyloid hypothesis posits that Aβ peptides, derived from the cleavage of the amyloid precursor protein (APP), play a central role in AD pathogenesis by aggregating into plaques that disrupt neuronal communication, trigger inflammation, and lead to neuronal death [
22
,
23
,
24
]. This aggregation is considered to disrupt neuronal function and initiate neurodegenerative processes. The accumulation of Aβ in the brain is an early event in dementia, occurring years before clinical symptoms appear [
25
]. Its early accumulation is considered a key event in the disease's pathogenesis, making it a critical prognostic biomarker for AD [
26
]. Tau protein plays a critical role in the stability and assembly of microtubules within neurons, which are essential for intracellular transport and cellular structure [
27
]. In AD, tau undergoes abnormal hyperphosphorylation, leading to the formation of NFTs, one of the hallmark pathologies of AD [
28
]. These tangles are considered to disrupt normal neuronal function, leading to synaptic loss, neuronal death, and cognitive decline [
29
]. A growing number of evidence has been gradually revealing that Aβ and tau are working together, showing a synergistic effect [
21
,
30
]. Therefore, there could be many mediator proteins/pathways between Aβ and tau, and understanding the mediator proteins is critical in understanding the pathogenesis mechanism and early diagnosis of AD (Table
1
).

To address this question, Johnson et al. created a panel of 59 proteins from the large‐scale protein co‐expression analysis of AD patients' brains [
31
] and quantified the abundances of the 59 proteins in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) from 286 autosomal‐dominant AD (ADAD) mutation carriers and 184 noncarriers, which has a time scale spanning six decades, using selective reaction monitoring‐mass spectrometry (SRM‐MS). According to their study, Aβ plaque/matrisome proteome changed first about 30 years before the onset, and the changes of synapse function/glycolytic metabolism/stress response‐related proteins followed ∼25 years before onset. After that, white matter/axonal integrity‐related proteins, which include tau‐related proteins, changed ∼20 years before onset, followed by immune activation/metabolic failure/cognitive decline about 5–10 years before onset. Finally, cortical atrophy, synaptic/neuronal loss, glycolytic metabolism, and functional decline‐related proteins changed around the onset. This study suggests that the pathways related to synaptic function change/glycolytic metabolism/stress response are mediators between Aβ change and subsequent tau protein changes, and also neuronal death is mediated by immune activation, although further validation is required. They also found that proteins like SMOC1 and SPON1, associated with Aβ plaques, were elevated nearly 30 years before AD symptoms. This study shed light on the interplay between Aβ and tau in AD pathogenesis. The identified proteins could serve as early biomarkers for AD before its onset [
32
].
2.2
Apolipoprotein E (APOE)
APOE is one of the well‐known genetic risk factors for AD. The APOE genotype, determined by combinations of the ε2, ε3, and ε4 alleles, significantly influences AD risk [
33
]. ε4/ε4 greatly increases the risk, while ε2/ε2 provides protective effects by lowering the risk of AD. These genotypes impact AD risk differently across age, sex, and population ancestry, necessitating tailored research and clinical approaches [
34
].
Using 62 post‐mortem brain tissue samples, Odenkirk et al. investigated how the APOE ε4 genotype influences proteomic and lipidomic alterations in AD across different brain regions. The samples consisted of frontal cortex and cerebellum tissues, all of which were free from AD lesions and other neurological diseases. The study revealed that mitochondrial dysfunction and cellular proteostasis are key aspects of AD pathophysiology, significantly affected by APOE ε4 genotypic differences and brain region‐specific factors. This research provides crucial insights into the impact of the APOE ε4 genotype on protein and lipid changes in AD, contributing to a better understanding of the disease's mechanisms [
35
]. Dai et al. conducted a quantitative proteomic analysis of post‐mortem cortical tissues from 10 control cases with the ε3 allele and 24 AD cases, which were equally distributed among ε2/3, ε3/3, and ε4/4 genotypes. Aβ levels did not change, but neurotrophic factor (NTF) levels were reduced in ε2/3 AD brains compared to other AD brains. Weighted gene co‐expression network analysis (WGCNA) results showed that the modules with significant differential expression patterns by APOE genotypes were highly correlated with synaptic transmission and inflammation. APOE ε2/3 also exhibited resilience to homeostatic and disease‐associated cell type changes in astrocytes, microglia, oligodendroglia, and endothelial cells [
36
]. Frick et al. conducted a large‐scale high‐throughput serum proteomics study using the SomaLogic platform to identify proteins associated with late‐onset AD (LOAD) in the prospective population‐based cohort of 5127 Icelandic adults (mean age, 76.6 ± 5.6 years). The study identified 303 proteins associated with LOAD using serum proteomics. Among them, 17 proteins showed strong APOE‐ε4‐dependent associations and were enriched for lipoprotein‐related pathways [
37
].
These proteomics analysis studies suggest that APOE genotypes can affect various cellular pathways, such as mitochondrial dysfunction, cellular proteostasis, synaptic transmission, inflammation, cell type changes, and lipoprotein‐related pathways, although further investigation is required to understand their involvement in AD.
2.3
Protein Degradation
Protein degradation is critical for neuronal functions, especially learning and memory [
38
]. When clearance of damaged proteins is dysregulated, it can lead to neuronal damage. Thus, increasing evidence suggests that proteostasis can contribute to neurodegenerative diseases, including AD [
39
,
40
]. The main molecular pathways involved in proteostasis are composed of the mammalian target of the rapamycin (mTOR) pathway, ubiquitin‐proteasome system (UPS), autophagy, and unfolded protein response [
39
].
Hark et al. conducted a pulse‐chase proteomic experiment with an APP knock‐in mouse model to explore the synaptic protein turnover changes when Aβ is overexpressed. They uncovered selective impairment of protein degradation at presynaptic terminals during the early stages of Aβ accumulation. This impairment was characterized by the increased amount and misfolding of synaptic vesicle‐associated proteins, which interacted with APP and Aβ. These findings suggest that synaptic dysfunction in AD may originate from these early presynaptic alterations, providing new avenues for early intervention strategies [
41
]. Dammer et al. conducted proteomic analyses for CSF and plasma samples from 18 AD and 18 control individuals using three different platforms composed of mass spectrometry, SomaScan, and Olink. The median correlation values across three different platforms were relatively higher for CSF (
r
= ∼0.7) compared to plasma (
r
= ∼0.6). They could identify > 7000 proteins from CSF and ∼9500 proteins from plasma across the three platforms. WGCNA results showed that the modules related to autophagy, ubiquitination, and sugar metabolism exhibited the most significant changes [
19
]. Hsieh et al. explored the genetic influences on UPS. The research found that genetic variations can affect basal proteasome activity in neurons, with some individuals exhibiting lower UPS activity. This lower activity makes neurons more vulnerable to tau accumulation, a key feature of AD. The study also noted that neurons with chronic proteasome inhibition upregulated proteins involved in proteostasis, such as HSPA6 and BAG3, as a compensatory response. Moreover, neurons from individuals with inherently lower proteasome activity were more susceptible to protein aggregation, particularly tau, under conditions of mild UPS inhibition, indicating a genetic predisposition to AD‐related pathologies [
42
].
Together, these studies underscore the vital role of proteostasis in maintaining neuronal integrity and the consequences of its dysregulation in AD.
2.4
Neuroinflammation
Neuroinflammation is a key contributor to the pathogenesis of AD, significantly influencing the progression of neurodegeneration [
6
]. The accumulation of Aβ plaques and tau NFTs triggers an inflammatory response in the brain, primarily mediated by microglia, the resident immune cells of the central nervous system, and astrocytes, which also play a significant role in neuroinflammation [
22
,
23
,
24
,
43
]. Activated microglia, upon encountering Aβ deposits, adopt a pro‐inflammatory phenotype, releasing cytokines such as interleukin‐1 beta (IL‐1β), tumor necrosis factor‐alpha (TNF‐α), and interleukin‐6 (IL‐6), which exacerbate neuronal damage and contribute to synaptic dysfunction [
44
]. Building on this understanding, several proteomic studies have revealed the intricate relationship between neuroinflammation and AD, discovering specific protein changes that occur during the inflammatory response [
6
,
13
,
37
].
Ragaranju et al. analyzed the microglia proteome in 5xFAD (a transgenic mouse model with five familial Alzheimer's disease mutations) mice, Lipopolysaccharide (LPS)‐injected wild‐type (WT) mice, and untreated WT mice by isolating microglia using magnetic‐activated cell sorting (MACS). They found that the microglia proteome in 5xFAD mice closely resembled that of LPS‐injected WT mice. Furthermore, proteins like Aβ peptide, APOE, HTRA1, COTL1, and CLU, which are elevated in the AD brain, were also increased in 5xFAD mice. This study underscores the significant role of inflammatory pathways in AD [
45
]. Building on previous findings, Rayaprolu et al. conducted a follow‐up study using tandem mass tag (TMT)‐based proteomics with MACS and fluorescence‐activated cell sorting (FACS) to explore microglial responses in AD further. The study identified moesin as a highly abundant protein in microglia surrounding Aβ plaques in 5xFAD brains. This discovery highlights moesin as a key regulator of microglial activity in AD, reinforcing the importance of inflammatory pathways identified earlier [
20
]. Van der Spek et al. examined changes in global protein levels in the hippocampal synaptosome fraction of the APP/PS1 mouse model at 6 and 12 months of age. They found the most significant regulation at 12 months, particularly in proteins involved in Aβ homeostasis and microglial‐dependent processes such as synaptic pruning and the immune response. This study underscores the dynamic changes in microglial activity over the course of the Aβ‐mediated pathogenesis process, particularly as the disease progresses from early to later stages [
46
].
Together, these studies provide a comprehensive view of the role of neuroinflammation in AD, from the early activation of microglia to the sustained inflammatory response that drives neurodegeneration.
2.5
RNA Splicing
Dysregulation in RNA splicing plays a significant role in the progression of AD [
47
]. Abnormal splicing can lead to the production of pathological isoforms of key proteins, such as tau and APP, which contribute to NFTs and Aβ plaques, respectively. For example, the inclusion of specific exons in the APP gene, regulated by RNA binding proteins (RBPs) like RBFox, can enhance Aβ production, a key factor in AD pathology. Additionally, tau protein's alternative splicing results in different isoforms that are implicated in NFT formation, a hallmark of AD [
48
].
Guo et al. used targeted MS to quantify detergent‐insoluble RNA‐binding proteins (RBPs) in human brains from individuals with asymptomatic AD (AsymAD), AD, and Parkinson's disease (PD). They found that RBPs, particularly splicing factors, co‐aggregate with tau and Aβ in AD and AsymAD, but less so in PD. Subsequently, they conducted unbiased proteomic analysis using sarkosyl‐insoluble fractions across the multiple neurodegenerative disease brain cohorts and conducted WGCNA, identifying protein modules related to spliceosome assembly and RNA splicing. This study revealed distinct RBP aggregation patterns in AD, offering insights into early neurodegenerative mechanisms [
49
]. Johnson et al. conducted TMT‐based mass spectrometry for 20 AD, 14 AsymAD, and 13 control cases, identifying 6533 proteins. Through the statistical analysis, they identified 350 proteins with altered levels between AsymAD and AD human brains, which were enriched for RBPs. They also investigated whether the altered RBPs in AD lead to alternative splicing of RNAs. They could identify many alternatively spliced proteins, including AD risk proteins like BIN1, PICALM, PTK2B, and FERMT2 [
50
]. Bai et al. conducted a label‐free mass spectrometry‐based proteomic analysis of insoluble proteomes extracted from AD patients' brains, identifying 4216 proteins, 36 of which had accumulated in AD. They found that the 36 proteins included U1‐70K and other U1 small nuclear ribonucleoprotein spliceosome components. They also found increased RNA misprocessing in the AD brains [
51
]. In the follow‐up study, Chen et al. created a mouse model transgenic with an N‐terminal 40 KDa fragment (N40K) of U1‐70K. They found that N40K showed a dominant negative effect on downregulated U1‐70K. The N40K‐transgenic mice also showed decreased GABAergic synapse component and postsynaptic hyperexcitability. Crossing the N40K‐transgenic mice with 5xFAD mice synergized with amyloid cascade, remodeling brain proteome and transcriptome along with cognitive decline [
52
]. Tijms et al. conducted mass spectrometry proteomics on CSF from AD patients and identified five distinct molecular subtypes based on 1058 proteins that exhibited different levels in individuals with AD (
n
= 419) compared to controls (
n
= 187). One of the five subtypes was related to RNA dysregulation, in addition to four other subtypes related to neuronal hyperplasticity, innate immune activation, choroid plexus dysfunction, and blood‐brain barrier impairment [
53
].
In summary, dysregulation of RNA splicing plays a critical role in AD by contributing to various pathways, including forming pathological protein isoforms, disrupting the normal cellular processes, accelerating amyloid cascade, postsynaptic hyperexcitability, and so forth. Further investigations are required to understand the pathogenesis mechanism mediated by dysregulated RNA splicing.
2.6
Metabolic Dysregulation
In AD, energy metabolism is profoundly disrupted in neurons, microglia, and astrocytes, each contributing to the pathophysiology of the disease in unique but interconnected ways [
54
]. The accumulation of Aβ and pathological tau impairs mitochondrial dynamics in neurons, reducing adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production and causing energy deficits. This leads to a significant energy shortfall, contributing to synaptic dysfunction and cognitive decline [
55
,
56
,
57
]. Astrocytes and microglia also undergo metabolic reprogramming, a shift that supports the activation and production of pro‐inflammatory cytokines [
43
,
54
,
58
]. The chronic activation of glial cells in AD perpetuates neuroinflammation and further disrupts the energy balance in the brain, contributing to neuronal damage. Additionally, abnormal lipid metabolism impairs membrane fluidity and synaptic function, negatively impacting neuronal connectivity [
58
].
Johnson et al. conducted a label‐free quantitative proteomic study on > 2000 human brain tissues from AD, AsymAD, and control individuals and a TMT‐based quantitative proteomic study on ∼400 CSF samples. They found that sugar metabolism was most significantly associated with AD pathology (such as NFT and Aβ) and cognitive decline. Another key finding in this study was that the protein module associated with sugar metabolism was enriched for astrocyte and microglia marker proteins. Especially, the proteins in the module were increased in AsymAD and strongly correlated with cognitive impairment. This data suggests that the energy metabolism changes in glial cells are early events that are highly correlated with cognitive decline in the later stage of AD. They also found that these energy metabolism‐related proteins were changed early in the analysis of CSF samples [
59
]. Johnson et al. also conducted selected reaction monitoring‐mass spectrometry (SRM‐MS) analysis for 59 proteins in CSF from 286 ADAD mutation carriers and 184 noncarriers, which had a time scale spanning six decades. They reported that glycolysis metabolism change is an early event that occurs about 25 years before the onset of AD symptoms. The metabolism changes also occurred about 0–5 years before onset. Although this study does not provide what cell type's metabolism changed during that event, it suggests that energy metabolism alteration is strongly associated with AD [
60
]. Adav et al. conducted isobaric tags for relative and absolute quantitation (iTRAQ)‐based quantitative proteome analysis for human medial frontal gyrus from AD patients and control, and they identified dysregulation in the electron transport chain and ATP synthase, particularly involving subunits NDUFA4 and NDUFA9, as contributors to AD‐specific pathology [
61
]. Shen et al. further corroborated these observations by using iTRAQ‐based quantitative proteomics to analyze mitochondrial, synaptosome, and myelin sheath alterations in 3xTg‐AD mice, highlighting early AD‐related pathological events such as impaired energy metabolism, neurotransmitter synthesis, and increased oxidative stress [
62
].
These proteomic studies have identified significant protein alterations related to mitochondrial damage, energy metabolism, and oxidative stress in the brains of both human patients and mouse models, highlighting the critical role of energy metabolism dysregulation in AD. Moreover, since these changes in energy metabolism appear to be relatively early events in the course of AD pathogenesis, the involved proteins could serve as biomarkers for early diagnosis, and targeting the dysregulated pathways could be a strategy for early intervention.
2.7
Cognitive Resilience
Although Aβ and NFT are the pathological hallmarks of AD, there are individuals who are cognitively normal with a high load of Aβ and NFT, and those are called cognitive resilience [
63
]. Some studies reported that high educational and occupational attainment, IQ, and leisure activities could affect cognitive resilience [
64
,
65
,
66
]. Comorbidities such as cerebrovascular disease pathologies, phosphorylated TAR DNA‐binding protein 43, hippocampal sclerosis, and Lewy bodies could also affect cognitive resilience [
67
,
68
,
69
,
70
]. Synaptic changes by synaptophysin or PSD95 dysregulation [
71
,
72
], growth factors such as brain‐derived neurotrophic factors, vascular endothelial growth factor A, nerve growth factor, fibroblast growth factor 2, and platelet‐derived growth factor [
73
,
74
,
75
,
76
], glial cell activation [
6
,
77
], and so on can also affect cognitive resilience. However, the exact underlying cellular and molecular mechanisms of cognitive resilience are not known [
67
,
78
]. Therefore, understanding the mechanism of this cognitive resilience would provide a decisive clue to understanding the pathogenesis mechanism and finding a cure for AD.
A few proteomics studies have investigated the mechanism of cognitive resilience in a systemic approach [
79
,
80
,
81
,
82
]. Yu et al. conducted a TMT‐based proteomic analysis on brain tissue samples of the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) from 391 individuals to identify cognitive resilience‐related proteins. They identified eight cortical proteins (NRN1, ACTN4, EPHX4, RPH3A, SGTB, CPLX1, SH3GL1, and UBA1) associated with cognitive resilience [
80
]. In a separate study, Hurst et al. conducted a TMT‐based quantitative proteomic analysis on brain tissues from 105 AsymAD (53 cases for Brodmann area (BA) 6 and 52 cases for BA37), 65 AD (32 cases for BA6 and 33 cases for BA37), and 48 Control (24 cases for BA6 and 24 cases for BA37) individuals. They found that NRN1, which was already reported as one of the cognitive resilience‐related proteins by Yu et al. [
80
], was identified as a key protein in the protein module that is associated with synaptic biology. In the validation experiment using primary rat hippocampal neurons, they discovered that NRN1 endowed dendritic spine resilience to Aβ [
79
].
NPTX2 is a synaptogenic immediate early gene, which is expressed by pre‐synapses of pyramidal neurons and is critical in controlling network excitability and rhythmicity through fast‐spiking parvalbumin (PV) interneurons. The dysfunction of the adaptive control of the pyramidal neurons‐PV circuit by a decreased level of NPTX2 is considered critical in cognitive decline in AD [
83
]. To understand the relationship between NPTX2 level and cognitive decline in AD patients, Soldan et al. conducted parallel reaction monitoring‐mass spectrometry (PRM‐MS) for CSFs collected longitudinally from 269 participants over 16.3 years on average. They found that the individuals who progressed to mild cognitive impairment (MCI) showed ∼15% lower NPTX2 levels compared to the group who did not progress to MCI, suggesting that NPTX2 can work as a predictive marker for cognitive decline [
84
]. Buchman et al. conducted a glycoproteome analysis of 366 brains composed of 121 AD, 93 MCI, and 152 non‐cognitively impaired individuals. After adjusting for age, sex, education, and AD and AD‐related disease pathologies, the glycopeptiforms for NPTX2a, NPTX2b, NECTIN1, NPTX2c, HSPB1, PLTP, NAGK, and VAT1 exhibited association with cognitive decline, highlighting that NPTX2 is critical for cognitive decline [
85
].
As such, proteomic studies discovered that NRN1 and NPTX2 are key proteins involved in cognitive resilience in AD. Further investigation of these proteins and identification of additional pathways using cell‐type‐specific and single‐cell proteomics (SCP) approaches will enable us to better understand the mechanisms of cognitive resilience in AD.
3
Useful Proteomics Technologies to Better Understand Biological Pathway Analysis in AD
We have introduced proteomics‐based studies on AD. However, most studies discussed here were conducted using tissue chunks, where multiple cell types are intermingled. Each cell type performs distinct functions in the brain, and even within the same cell type, individual cells can exhibit heterogeneity [
86
,
87
,
88
]. For instance, if a protein critical to AD is increased in neurons but decreased in astrocytes, this change may not be detected at the tissue chunk level (bulk proteomics), resulting in inaccurate interpretation. These limitations can be addressed through cell‐type‐specific, single‐cell, and spatial proteomics approaches.
3.1
Cell‐Type‐Specific Proteomics
To overcome the limitations of bulk proteomics, various cell‐type‐specific technologies have been developed so far, including physical separation methods such as FACS and laser capture microdissection (LCM) and chemical separation methods such as bio‐orthogonal non‐canonical amino acid tagging (BONCAT), cell‐type‐specific in vivo biotinylation of proteins (CIBOP), ascorbate peroxidase 2 (APEX2) and in situ cell‐surface proteome extraction by extracellular labeling (iPEEL) [
89
,
90
,
91
,
92
,
93
,
94
]. Cell‐type‐specific proteome analysis based on FACS involves selectively isolating specific cell populations using fluorescent markers for subsequent analysis [
95
]. Rayaprolu et al. isolated microglia from adult mouse brains using FACS and MACS and conducted microglia‐specific proteome analysis identifying 1791 proteins [
96
]. Davis et al. enhanced LCM‐based proteomics to specifically analyze neurons from post‐mortem human brain tissue, identifying up to 3600 proteins from cell types like Betz and Purkinje cells with high reproducibility [
89
]. The cell‐type‐specific proteome analysis using the BONCAT method utilizes the cell‐type‐specific expression of a mutant tRNA synthase (MetRS*) to incorporate a noncanonical amino acid, azidonorleucine (ANL) into proteins in the target cells, which can be retrieved through biotinylation in vitro using click chemistry [
91
]. Other methods, such as APEX2 [
92
,
97
] and iPEEL [
93
], use peroxidases to biotinylate nearby proteins in specific cell types or subcellular locations in the mouse brain. CIBOP, another proximity labeling method, allows for cell‐type‐specific biotinylation of proteins using cell‐type‐specific expression of TurboID, identifying more than 2000 proteins, and has been primarily applied to neurons and astrocytes [
94
]. More recently, Kumar et al. applied CIBOP to PV‐expressing interneurons (PV‐IN). This technique revealed unique proteomic signatures associated with AD risk and cognitive resiliency in PV‐INs [
98
].
Applying these cell‐type‐specific methods will enable us to have a deeper understanding of the AD pathogenesis mechanism. However, shallow proteome depth and the requirement of genetic modification of animal models for chemical tagging methods such as BONCAT, CIBOP, APEX2, and iPEEL are still limiting factors that need to be overcome.
3.2
Single‐Cell Proteomics
Although the cell‐type‐specific proteome analysis would provide more comprehensive proteome information compared to the bulk proteomics, the cell‐type‐specific proteome data does not take cell‐to‐cell heterogeneity among the same cell type into account, which is critical in better understanding the AD pathogenesis mechanisms [
99
,
100
,
101
].
Thus, significant efforts have been made to develop SCP methods [
102
,
103
,
104
]. Budnik et al. developed the single‐cell proteomics by mass spectrometry (SCoPE‐MS) system, which enabled protein quantification from a single cell through multiplexing technology, including a carrier channel [
105
]. Rosenberger et al. used artificial intelligence (AI) and deep learning to develop a SCP method (single‐cell Deep Visual Proteomics [scDVP]) that precisely cuts and analyzes individual cell shapes through LCM, enabling accurate mapping of cellular spatial distribution and function within tissues in single cell level [
90
]. Yang et al. developed CycMIST, a single‐cell cyclic multiplex in situ tagging technology that enables functional proteome profiling of individual cells, detecting 182 proteins, including surface markers, neuron function proteins, and neurodegeneration markers [
106
]. Tsai et al. developed the surfactant‐assisted one‐pot sample preparation with mass spectrometry (SOP‐MS) method, enabling label‐free global SCP by using a nonionic surfactant and low‐bind materials, allowing the detection of hundreds of proteins from single cells [
107
]. Gebreyesus et al. developed a SCP workflow that combines microfluidic chips with data‐independent acquisition (DIA) mass spectrometry, identifying an average of ∼1500 protein groups across 20 single cells with high sensitivity and reproducibility [
108
]. Woo et al. quantified approximately 1500 proteins from single mouse cells using a nanoPOTS (nested nanodroplet processing in one‐pot for trace sample analysis) (N2) chip, in which > 240 single cells can be processed including TMT labeling [
109
]. Ctortecka et al. developed an automated workflow using the proteoCHIP EVO 96 and the cellenONE system, which minimizes sample handling and enhances sensitivity and reproducibility, enabling the identification of up to 4000 proteins per single cell [
110
]. Recently, Ye et al. identified ∼6000 proteins in a single cell from early mouse embryos using the One‐Tip method, which integrates efficient one‐pot sample preparation with narrow‐window DIA (nDIA) [
111
].
The advancement of these SCP methods will enable us to understand single‐cell level proteome changes during the AD pathogenesis process, eventually uncovering the AD pathogenesis mechanism.
3.3
Spatial Proteomics
Animal brain cells have different physiology and functions depending on their location and microenvironment. Therefore, the proteome analysis preserving their spatial information is critical in understanding the pathogenesis mechanism of various diseases, including AD [
43
,
112
,
113
,
114
].
Desorption electrospray ionization‐mass spectrometry imaging (DESI‐MSI), matrix‐assisted laser desorption/ionization mass spectrometry imaging (MALDI‐MSI), multiplex ion beam imaging (MIBI), imaging mass cytometry (IMC), deep visual proteomics (DVP), and deep topographic proteomics (DTP) are all spatial proteomics/metabolomics techniques, each with distinct sample preparation, ionization methods, and applications [
112
,
115
,
116
,
117
,
118
]. DESI‐MSI is conducted by impacting a tissue section with high‐speed charged microdroplets with 50–200 µm of spatial resolution and the microdroplets will dissolve molecules on the tissue section followed by ionization and mass spectrometry analysis, making it suitable for analyzing the spatial distribution of biological molecules [
119
]. Recently, Nano‐DESI‐MSI has improved the precision of smaller area analysis by providing higher spatial resolution (≤ 10 µm) and sensitivity [
120
]. MALDI‐MSI employs laser‐based ionization of molecules on a tissue section covered with a matrix, which is effective for visualizing the distribution of various molecules, such as proteins, peptides, and lipids, on tissue sections at up to 10 µm or less of spatial resolution [
121
,
122
,
123
]. However, MALDI‐MSI struggles with simultaneous peptide identification and quantification. The high‐resolution informatics toolbox in MALDI‐MSI proteomics (HIT‐MAP) overcame this limitation using peptide mass fingerprint analysis and protein coverage analysis pipeline combined with automated annotation and visualization [
124
]. MIBI utilizes an ion beam for ionizing antibodies labeled with metal isotopes, enabling the simultaneous analysis of multiple proteins at the single‐cell level, making it particularly strong in immunology and cancer research for studying spatial tissue organization and cellular interactions [
43
]. IMC is an expansion of the mass cytometry technique, in which tissue sections are stained with panels of antibodies coupled with mass tags, and subsequently, the mass tags are ionized by laser ablation and detected by mass spectrometry to acquire spatial information of biological molecules on a tissue section [
125
]. Recently, IMC was expanded to 3D imaging, enabling 3‐dimensional cytometry on tissue sections at single‐cell resolution [
116
]. DVP combines AI‐assisted tissue section image analysis in submicron resolution with LCM‐based SCP. In DVP, A submicron‐resolution image of a tissue section can be acquired by analyzing the boundary of single cells using AI (biology image analysis software). Along the determined boundary, a single cell can be excised by LCM, followed by pooling > 100 cells per sample and conducting ultra‐high‐sensitivity mass spectrometry [
117
]. Although DVP was mostly conducted by pooling > 100 cells per sample, scDVP further advanced it to a single‐cell resolution with the proteome depth of ∼1700 proteins from a single cell [
90
]. In a recent study, DTP performed a systematic analysis of human tissue sections by excising them down to 40 µm of spatial resolution, followed by identifying proteins from each excised segment using mass spectrometry. This approach enables the generation of spatial proteome maps across tissue structures, identifying around 1500 proteins from a 40 µm‐sized segment composed of approximately 10 cells [
112
].
Applying these various spatial proteomics analysis tools will provide us with single‐cell level proteome changes, preserving their positional information in the brain. This will lead us to decipher pathway dysregulation during the AD pathogenesis process.
4
Future Direction and Conclusion
Proteomics research has substantially deepened our understanding of the multifaceted pathophysiology underlying AD. Identifying dysregulated pathways, particularly those involved in amyloid metabolism, tau pathology, neuroinflammation, protein degradation, RNA splicing, metabolic dysregulation, and synaptic dysfunction, underscores the critical role of proteomics in advancing our knowledge of AD pathology. These insights not only elucidate the molecular mechanisms driving disease progression but also highlight potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets. However, most proteomic studies on human or animal tissues have been conducted at the bulk proteomics level, which provides only limited insights into cell‐dependent proteomic changes. For instance, proteomics research has identified RNA splicing factors as potentially important in the pathogenesis of AD. However, it remains unclear in which specific cell types these RNA splicing factors are dysregulated. To address this, cell‐type‐specific proteomic analysis, as discussed earlier, needs to be applied to human and animal brain samples. Even within the same cell type, there can be diverse subtypes with distinct functions. Neurons can be classified into excitatory and inhibitory types, which can be further divided into various subclasses [
126
]. Similarly, astrocytes can be categorized as protoplasmic or fibrous astrocytes [
127
], and microglia can be divided into six subgroups, including Satellite microglia, KSPG‐microglia, and Hox8b‐microglia [
128
]. To capture this heterogeneity, single‐cell proteomic analyses, as previously mentioned, should be employed to explore differences even among subtypes within the same cell population. Moreover, the functions of identical cell types can vary depending on their location in the brain. Spatial proteomics, as outlined earlier, can address this regional heterogeneity. By leveraging these advanced approaches, it becomes possible to uncover critical signaling pathways that might be overlooked in bulk proteomics studies.
Additionally, integrating proteomic data with complementary omics approaches, including genomics, transcriptomics, and metabolomics, will be essential to construct a more comprehensive and systems‐level understanding of AD pathogenesis. Such efforts should be complemented by the development and refinement of bioinformatics tools and machine‐learning algorithms capable of handling the complexity and scale of proteomic data. These advancements will be pivotal in translating proteomic discoveries into clinical applications, ultimately informing the development of precision medicine strategies tailored to individual patient profiles.
In conclusion, while proteomics has already contributed significantly to our understanding of AD, the continued evolution of proteomic technologies and their integration with other omics disciplines will be vital in overcoming the remaining challenges in AD research. By advancing these areas, we can enhance the precision and efficacy of diagnostic and therapeutic approaches, bringing us closer to effective interventions for this devastating neurodegenerative disease.
