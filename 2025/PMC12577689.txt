INTRODUCTION
Microtubules, highly dynamic cytoskeletal filaments formed by α/β-tubulin heterodimers, play essential roles in a variety of cellular processes, including cell division and polarity establishment (
1
). The microtubule properties and functions are regulated by various microtubule-associated proteins, diverse tubulin isotypes, and multiple posttranslational modifications (PTMs) of tubulin. Among these regulators, tubulin isotypes and PTMs are vividly termed as “tubulin code” (
2
). Most PTMs occur at the C-terminal tails of tubulin exposed to the outer surface of microtubules including detyrosination (
3
,
4
), tyrosination (
5
), Δ2-tubulin (
6
), (poly)glutamylation (
7
,
8
), and (poly)glycylation (
9
), and others are located in the main structure of tubulin, such as acetylation (
10
,
11
), methylation (
12
), phosphorylation (
13
,
14
), polyamination (
15
), sumoylation (
16
), and lactylation (
17
). Among these PTMs, only trimethylation, acetylation, and lactylation on lysine-40 (K40) of α-tubulin are in the lumen of microtubules.
Trimethylation on K40 of α-tubulin (α-TubK40me3) is a recently identified PTM, emerging in the spindle and midbody of dividing cells (
12
). SET domain–containing 2 (SETD2), a methyltransferase of histone-3 (H3) K36 trimethylation (H3K36me3), is responsible for methylating α-TubK40me3 (
12
,
18
,
19
). α-TubK40me3 displays varied functions among different cell types. In mitotic cells, the loss of α-TubK40me3 caused by SETD2 deletion results in spindle and cytokinesis defects, driving genomic instability (
12
). Similar deleterious phenotypes are also observed in deficient cells of PBRM1 (polybromo-1) that has the ability to recognize and bind trimethylated α-tubulin (
20
). In postmitotic cells such as neurons, down-regulation of α-TubK40me3 by SETD2 knockdown disrupts neuronal polarization and migration by impeding microtubule formation during cortical development (
21
). Furthermore, heterozygous SETD2 mutant mice that disable microtubule methylation show defects in dendritic arborization and axon length (
22
). These data indicate that a normal α-TubK40me3 level is important for the regulation of microtubule and cell function. However, the knowledge about reversing α-TubK40me3 and its corresponding role remains unknown, and the importance of α-TubK40me3 balance is still not fully understood.
The JmjC domain–containing demethylase KDM4A (also known as JHDM3A) is an epigenetic “eraser” and capable of removing the methyl group from H3 K9/36 mono/di/trimethylation (H3K9me1/2/3 and H3K36me1/2/3), especially with high affinity for trimethylation to generate lower methylated states (
23
–
25
). As an epigenetic demethylase, KDM4A is generally regarded as a nuclear protein and associated with gene transcription and silencing (
26
–
28
). In this study, we reveal that KDM4A acts as the demethylase of α-tubulin, a nonhistone substrate, and regulates microtubule function and cell mitosis. KDM4A was localized in both the nucleus and cytoplasm and associated with α-tubulin. In human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293T cells, KDM4A knockout (KO) or overexpression significantly increased or decreased the level of α-TubK40me3, respectively. Meanwhile, purified full-length KDM4A effectively demethylated α-tubulin in vitro. Moreover, both KDM4A KO by the CRISPR-Cas9 system and degradation by the degradation tag (dTAG) system caused more cell mitotic defects that could be rescued by overexpression of the α-tubulin mutant α-tubulin
K40A
for decreasing α-TubK40me3 or treatment with a low concentration of nocodazole. Together, these results suggest that KDM4A plays an important role in cell mitosis by regulation of microtubule polymerization as an α-tubulin demethylase.
RESULTS
KDM4A binds α-tubulin via its catalytic core domain
Previous study has shown that α-TubK40me3 was catalyzed by SETD2, a methyltransferase of H3K36me3 (
12
). To identify the possible demethylase of α-TubK40me3, we first focused on histone demethylases. KDM4A has been reported to play a role in the nucleus as an H3K9/K36me3 demethylase (
23
,
24
,
29
). Structural analysis reveals that the two glycine residues at +3 and +4 away from K9 of histone H3 (H3K9) and the proline residue at +2 in H3K36 are essential for KDM4A to demethylate H3K9me3 and H3K36me3 by providing the substrate with the flexibility required to fit into the catalytic pocket of KDM4A (
Fig. 1A
) (
25
). The K40 loop of α-tubulin is a well-known flexible region (
30
), and its corresponding amino acid sequence is conserved among various tubulin isotypes, with the exception of TUBA8 (tubulin alpha 8) (
Fig. 1B
). The sequence alignment of α-tubulin isotypes showed that K40 and its conserved sequence “KTIGG” conformed to the structural motif of the KDM4A substrate, which was similar to “GG” at +3 and +4 in H3K9 (
Fig. 1B
). Moreover, immunostaining and immunoblotting showed that KDM4A was located in both the nucleus and cytoplasm of HEK293T cells during the interphase (
Fig. 1, C and D
) and widely dispersed throughout the cells during mitosis when the nuclear envelope breaks down (fig. S1A). Three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction revealed that KDM4A was partially localized at the spindle and midbody during the metaphase and telophase (fig. S1B). Thus, KDM4A has both the molecular recognition capability and appropriate subcellular localization to potentially demethylate α-TubK40me3.

To verify the relationship between KDM4A and α-tubulin, we performed coimmunoprecipitation (co-IP) assay. With a specific antibody of α-tubulin, we detected that the endogenous KDM4A could be coimmunoprecipitated by α-tubulin in HEK293T cells (
Fig. 1E
). By overexpression of exogenously hemagglutinin (HA)–tagged KDM4A, we found that endogenous α-tubulin was able to be coimmunoprecipitated by HA-KDM4A as well (
Fig. 1F
). Moreover, co-IP assay with exogenous overexpression of Flag-tagged wild-type (WT) α-tubulin (α-tubulin
WT
), acetylation- and methylation-deficit α-tubulin (α-tubulin
K40A
) (
21
,
31
), acetylation-mimic α-tubulin (α-tubulin
K40Q
) (
32
), and trimethylation-mimic α-tubulin (α-tubulin
K40F
) (
21
,
33
) mutants showed that all of them were able to be coimmunoprecipitated by HA-KDM4A with a similar binding affinity (
Fig. 1, G and H
). Moreover, we performed cosedimentation assay with cytoplasmic fractions from HEK293T cells to detect whether KDM4A preferentially binds to polymerized microtubules or soluble tubulin. Immunoblotting showed that KDM4A exhibited a stronger binding affinity to polymerized microtubules than soluble tubulin (
Fig. 1I
). Thus, KDM4A is able to interact with α-tubulin, especially polymerized microtubules, regardless of the PTMs (trimethylation and acetylation) of K40.
We further inquire whether a direct interaction existed between KDM4A and α-tubulin. The recombinant Flag-KDM4A was incubated with purified mouse brain tubulin, followed by pull-down with a Flag antibody. Immunoblotting showed that KDM4A directly bound α-tubulin (
Fig. 1J
and fig. S1C). To further identify the α-tubulin binding domain of KDM4A, we constructed and purified a series of glutathione
S
-transferase (GST)–tagged truncated KDM4A mutants (fig. S1D). GST pull-down assay with purified mouse brain tubulin showed that the truncated KDM4A mutants containing a catalytic core JmjN/JmjC domain were required for KDM4A binding to α-tubulin (
Fig. 1, K and L
). Together, these data suggest that KDM4A directly binds α-tubulin through its catalytic core JmjN/JmjC domain in the cytoplasm, raising the possibility that KDM4A is a demethylase of α-tubulin.
KDM4A demethylates α-tubulin and decreases α-TubK40me3
Our previous study showed that our homemade antibody of α-TubK40me3 was suitable for immunoprecipitation but not for direct immunoblotting (
21
). To further confirm the specificity of the α-TubK40me3 antibody, we performed mass spectrometry and dot blot assay. Mass spectrometry analysis of the samples immunoprecipitated with the α-TubK40me3 antibody identified trimethylation at K40 of α-tubulin by calculating the molecular mass of amino acids in the peptide corresponding to the peaks (fig. S1E). Dot blotting revealed that our antibody only recognized the trimethylated K40 peptide of α-tubulin but not the nonmodified or acetylated peptide (fig. S1F). On the basis of the established method, we performed immunoprecipitation with the homemade antibody of α-TubK40me3, followed by immunoblotting with the α-tubulin antibody to detect the level of α-TubK40me3 (
12
,
21
). To explore whether KDM4A is able to demethylate α-tubulin, we expressed KDM4A or its catalytically inactive mutant KDM4A
H188A
in HEK293T cells and found that overexpression of KDM4A, but not KDM4A
H188A
, markedly reduced the level of both α-TubK40me3 and H3K36me3 (
Fig. 2, A and B
). Meanwhile, we constructed a KDM4A KO HEK293T cells by using the CRISPR-Cas9 system (fig. S2, A to C) and found that the levels of both α-TubK40me3 and H3K36me3 were significantly increased in these cells, while other PTMs of α-tubulin including acetylation at K40 of α-tubulin (α-TubK40ac) were unaffected (
Fig. 2, C and D
). Given that α-TubK40me3 was enriched at the spindle and midbody (
12
), we specifically quantified the fluorescence intensity of α-TubK40me3 at the mitotic spindle. Consistent with immunoblotting results, the fluorescence intensity of α-TubK40me3 in cells overexpressing KDM4A but not KDM4A
H188A
was decreased by about 40% compared to that in control cells (fig. S2, D and E), while in KDM4A KO cells, it was increased by ~50% compared to KDM4A WT cells (fig. S2, F to I). Moreover, we performed a transient knockdown of KDM4A by expressing short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) in murine-derived Neuro-2a cells, and similar results were observed, with a marked increase in α-TubK40me3 and H3K36me3 (
Fig. 2, E and F
). Therefore, KDM4A is involved in the demethylation of α-TubK40me3.

To confirm the change of α-TubK40me3 that resulted from the cytoplasmic role of KDM4A and explore the functional domain of KDM4A demethylating α-tubulin, we constructed several truncated KDM4A plasmids with tandem nuclear export signals (NESs) at the N terminus to sequester them in the cytoplasm (
Fig. 2G
). Overexpression of its catalytic core truncation HA-NES-KDM4A
1–350
, but not HA-NES-KDM4A
351–1064
, significantly reduced the level of α-TubK40me3 without affecting the H3K36me3 level in KDM4A KO cells (
Fig. 2, H and I
), suggesting that the cytoplasmic KDM4A is largely responsible for its demethylating effect on α-tubulin by its catalytic core domain. In vitro demethylation assay with purified mouse brain tubulin and synthesized trimethylated α-tubulin peptide showed that the level of α-TubK40me3 was markedly decreased by recombinant Flag-KDM4A (
Fig. 2, J and K
). Together, these data provide evidence to support the notion that KDM4A is a demethylase of α-tubulin.
Loss of KDM4A promotes microtubule polymerization and leads to mitotic defects
We next explore how α-TubK40me3 regulation mediated by KDM4A affects the microtubule function. Microtubules undergo dynamic changes between polymerization and depolymerization in cells. We first performed nocodazole washout assay to detect whether KDM4A affected microtubule polymerization. Immunostaining and quantitative data showed that the fluorescence intensity of microtubules in KDM4A KO cells was significantly higher than that in KDM4A WT cells after the removal of nocodazole, which could be restored by overexpression of HA-NES-KDM4A (
Fig. 3, A and B
). Meanwhile, we examined the efficiency of tubulin nucleation in KDM4A KO cells. We synchronized the KDM4A KO cells at the G
1
-S phase with thymidine treatment and released them into the prometaphase. Then, microtubules of cells were depolymerized with nocodazole (500 ng/ml), followed by the release of these cells into a fresh medium containing a low concentration of nocodazole (15 ng/ml) to inhibit centrosomal microtubule nucleation and induce acentrosomal microtubule nucleation (
34
,
35
). Immunostaining and quantitative data showed that the number of acentrosomal microtubule asters in KDM4A KO cells was significantly higher than that in KDM4A WT cells (fig. S3, A and B). Both results suggest that the loss of KDM4A increases the formation of new microtubules. Afterward, we separated the soluble tubulin fraction (S) and polymerized microtubule fraction (P) from KDM4A WT and KO cells, respectively. Immunoblotting and quantitative data showed that the P/S ratio in KDM4A KO cells was increased by ~25% compared to that in KDM4A WT cells, which was also restored by overexpression of HA-NES-KDM4A (
Fig. 3, C and D
). This result together with that of nocodazole washout assay implies that overpolymerized microtubules after KDM4A KO are induced by the KDM4A cytoplasmic function. Moreover, we expressed EB3-tdTomato to label microtubule plus-ends and performed time-lapse imaging in KDM4A WT and KO cells to investigate the effect of KDM4A on microtubule growth. Quantitative data showed that the velocity of EB3 comets was significantly increased in KDM4A KO cells, and this phenotype was rescued by overexpression of HA-NES-KDM4A (
Fig. 3, E and F
), suggesting that the loss of KDM4A promotes microtubule growth in cells. Together, these results indicate that KDM4A plays a negative role in microtubule polymerization.

Given that trimethylation and acetylation both take place on K40 and KDM4A KO, which does not affect the acetylation level, we immunostained with α-TubK40me3 and α-TubK40ac antibodies to examine their distribution in cells. Immunostaining showed that the distribution of most signals of these two PTMs was consistent in mitotic phases except for the interphase and mitotic telophase (fig. S3, C to E). The α-TubK40me3 level during mitosis was obviously higher than that during the interphase and mainly enriched in the spindle and midbody (fig. S3, F and G), which is essential for mitosis (
12
). Given that increased α-TubK40me3 affects microtubule polymerization, we hypothesize that excessive α-TubK40me3 affects cell division. In agreement with this, KDM4A KO cells with multipolar spindles were increased at the metaphase, about three times as many as KDM4A WT cells (
Fig. 3, G and H
). At the telophase and cytokinesis, KDM4A KO cells exhibited defects including lagging chromosomes (chromosomes lagging in the metaphase plate), chromosome bridges (a chromosome region simultaneously pulled to both poles of the mitotic spindle), and micronuclei (small perinuclear bodies formed by a nuclear membrane–like envelope) (
Fig. 3, I and J
) (
36
). Specifically, the cells with lagging chromosomes and micronuclei were significantly increased after the loss of KDM4A (fig. S4A). Furthermore, these defects after the loss of KDM4A were almost fully rescued by overexpression of HA-KDM4A and HA-KDM4A
1–350
but not HA-KDM4A
H188A
in KDM4A KO cells (
Fig. 3, K and L
, and fig. S4, B and C). These data suggest that KDM4A is required for cell mitosis.
dTAG-mediated degradation of KDM4A increases α-TubK40me3 and mitotic defects
As an epigenetic factor, KDM4A plays vital roles in regulating gene transcription by demethylating H3K9/K36me3 (
23
,
24
,
29
); we wonder whether the mitotic defects resulting from KDM4A KO are caused by its direct demethylation of α-tubulin or its indirect transcriptional regulation in cells. Given that KDM4A-regulated gene transcription took a long time to affect cell function, we constructed a dTAG system for rapid degradation of protein to dissect the effect of KDM4A-mediated α-TubK40me3 in mitotic defects. The dTAG system pairs a degrader of FKBP12
F36V
, such as dTAG-13, with expression of FKBP12
F36V
in frame with the target protein, thereby binding to the E3 ligase complex and ultimately inducing proteasomal degradation (
Fig. 4A
) (
37
). We generated KDM4A-FKBP12
F36V
-2×HA-T2A-Puro knockin HEK293T cells (termed as KDM4A-dTAG cells) by using the CRISPR-Cas9 system (fig. S5, A to C). Immunoblotting and immunostaining showed that the fusion protein KDM4A-FKBP12
F36V
-2×HA displayed similar expression levels and distribution pattern to that of WT cells (fig. S5, D to F). Then, we screened for the concentration and time of dTAG-13 inducing KDM4A degradation in KDM4A-dTAG cells. Immunoblotting showed that KDM4A was degraded in a dose-dependent manner after 4 hours of 50 to 200 nM dTAG-13 treatment (
Fig. 4B
). Temporal analysis detected that KDM4A was notably degraded as early as 0.5 hours after 100 nM dTAG-13 treatment, and the degradation effect continued to be stable after 2 hours (
Fig. 4C
). Moreover, similar results were observed by immunostaining after treatment of KDM4A-dTAG cells with 100 nM dTAG-13 from 2 to 12 hours (fig. S5G). Thus, a system for rapid KDM4A degradation is successfully constructed.

We selected 2 and 12 hours as the points for KDM4A degradation to explore their effects on α-TubK40me3 and cell mitosis. Immunoblotting and quantitative data showed that the level of α-TubK40me3 was increased by about 20 and 100% after 2 and 12 hours of dTAG-13 treatment, respectively (
Fig. 4, D and E
), which was also confirmed by the results of immunostaining (fig. S5, H and I). Meanwhile, the level of H3K36me3 was also increased by ~20 and 50%, respectively (
Fig. 4, D and E
). However, the α-TubK40ac level was unaffected (
Fig. 4, D and E
). As a rapid manner for depleting KDM4A in a different way than KDM4A KO cells, we further examined the effect of KDM4A decrease on cell mitosis, although the degradation of KDM4A also increased the H3K36me3 level. Immunostaining and quantitative data showed that the cell status after 100 nM dTAG-13 treatment for 2 hours was similar to that of the control group (
Fig. 4, F to I
, and fig. S5J). However, after 100 nM dTAG-13 treatment for 12 hours, significant mitotic defects were observed in KDM4A-dTAG cells with a higher percentage of multipolar spindles, lagging chromosomes, chromosome bridges, and micronuclei (
Fig. 4, F to I
, and fig. S5J), which were similar to KDM4A KO cells. These data suggest that degradation of KDM4A for 12 hours also leads to an increase in α-TubK40me3 as well as mitotic defects.
Given that both α-TubK40me3 and H3K36me3 were increased by acute degradation of KDM4A in KDM4A-dTAG cells, histone methylation mainly contributes to transcriptional regulation (
38
). We performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and label-free quantitative proteomics to evaluate the consequence of genetic alteration resulting from an increase in histone methylation in KDM4A-dTAG cells upon 12 hours of dTAG-13 treatment (fig. S6, A and B). Transcriptomic analysis showed that a total of 20,745 genes were detected in KDM4A-dTAG cells and only 23 genes showed more than 1.5-fold expression change after 12 hours of dTAG-13 treatment, with 13 up-regulated and 10 down-regulated genes (fig. S6, C and D). Meanwhile, gene ontology (GO) analysis revealed that differential genes were enriched in metabolic and developmental processes, such as the carbohydrate biosynthetic process and forebrain development (fig. S6E). Furthermore, label-free quantitative proteomics identified 5268 proteins in KDM4A-dTAG cells and only 16 proteins showed more than 1.5-fold expression alteration except KDM4A, including 10 up-regulated and 6 down-regulated proteins (fig. S6, F and G). GO analysis showed that differential proteins were related to intracellular transport and cilium assembly (fig. S6H). Notably, these differential proteins were completely distinct from the differential genes observed in the RNA-seq result (fig. S6I), implying that they did not result from the transcriptional changes. These data suggest that mitotic defects following degradation of KDM4A for 12 hours in cells are not mainly caused by the transcriptional changes from elevated histone methylation but stem from excessive α-TubK40me3.
Restoration of high α-TubK40me3 or microtubule overpolymerization rescues the mitotic defects by the loss of KDM4A
To further confirm the contribution of excessive α-TubK40me3 to mitotic defects in KDM4A KO cells, we overexpressed several α-tubulin mutants to modulate the level of α-TubK40me3 and detected their effects. All mutants were well incorporated into the microtubule network during the cell interphase and mitosis (fig. S7A). Immunoblotting and quantitative data showed that the level of α-TubK40me3 was effectively decreased by overexpression of α-tubulin
K40A
but not α-tubulin
WT
and α-tubulin
K40F
without affecting H3K36me3, α-TubK40ac, and KDM4A levels (

DISCUSSION
Tubulin PTMs play a crucial role in regulating microtubule properties and functions, which need a coordinated action among “writer,” “reader,” and “eraser.” The “writer” and “reader” of α-TubK40me3 have been uncovered (
12
,
20
). Here, we identify KDM4A as an “eraser” of α-TubK40me3, which is important for cell mitosis. As an epigenic factor, the catalytic core domain of KDM4A is able to bind and demethylate α-tubulin in the cytoplasm. The loss of KDM4A causes excessive methylation of α-tubulin, promoting microtubule polymerization and inducing the defects of cell mitosis (
Fig. 6
). This study establishes a link between cell function and α-TubK40me3 balance and expands our knowledge about the dual epigenetic and cytoskeletal remodeler.
Fig. 6.
Proposed model for the α-tubulin demethylase KDM4A in regulating microtubule polymerization as well as cell mitosis.
KDM4A, as an α-tubulin demethylase, can remove SETD2-catalyzed α-TubK40me3 in the cytoplasm, maintain the stability of the microtubule methylation level and the proportion of polymerized microtubules, and enable the normal mitotic process. When KDM4A is lost, excessive α-TubK40me3 promotes microtubule overpolymerization and causes mitotic defects in cells.
KDM4A serves as an α-tubulin demethylase
KDM4A used to be regarded as an epigenetic eraser for removing methyl marks on H3K9/K36 in nuclei, thereby regulating a number of cellular processes, such as DNA replication and gene expression. Further study reports that KDM4A is also localized to the cytoplasm and can interact with the translation initiation complex to regulate protein synthesis (
41
), suggesting that it has nonepigenetic functions. In the present study, we performed cellular co-IP assay and in vitro GST pull-down assay showing that KDM4A interacted with α-tubulin through its catalytic core JmjN-JmjC domain. Notably, the conserved K40 loop sequence of α-tubulin is similar to that of H3K9 containing two glycine residues at +3 and +4 of H3K9, which are essential for its demethylation reaction (
25
). Accordingly, KDM4A KO or knockdown markedly increased the level of α-TubK40me3 in cells and, together with in vitro demethylation of α-tubulin by KDM4A, supported the notion that KDM4A is a demethylase of α-tubulin. Considering that the JmjN-JmjC domain has also been found in another histone demethylase family, KDM4A may not be the unique demethylase for α-tubulin.
Both trimethylation and acetylation take place on K40, implying a competing relationship between these two PTMs. Our previous work has shown that SETD2 KO in HEK293 cells decreased α-TubK40me3 and increased α-TubK40ac, while elevating α-TubK40ac by overexpression of acetyltransferase MEC17 in HEK293 cells induced a significant decrease in α-TubK40me3 (
21
). However, we found that α-TubK40ac was not obviously changed after the loss of KDM4A to affect α-TubK40me3. We detected apparent differences in microtubule populations undergoing methylation and acetylation during the interphase and mitotic telophase, suggesting that these two modifications may be distributed in distinct subsets of microtubules in specific phases by some unknown mechanisms. Therefore, when KDM4A was knocked out and trimethylation levels were increased, methylation mediated by SETD2 did not or was insufficient to compete the resource of MEC17-driven acetylation, thereby preventing a significant reduction in the acetylation level of α-tubulin.
KDM4A-mediated α-TubK40me3 demethylation regulates cell mitosis
K40 is located in the lumen of microtubules and between two loops (the H1-S2 and H2-S3 loops) that could interact with the adjacent M-loop (S7-H9 loop) and affect the lateral contact of α/β-tubulin heterodimers (
42
,
43
). Although both trimethylation and acetylation take place on K40, their effect on microtubule properties is distinct. α-TubK40ac directly weakens interprotofilament interactions, enhances microtubule flexibility, and confers microtubule resilience to mechanical breakage (
44
,
45
). Thus, α-TubK40ac is proposed as a marker of stable microtubules. In this study, we found that excessive α-TubK40me3 caused by KDM4A KO increased the rate of tubulin nucleation, microtubule repolymerization, and the ratio of polymerized microtubule and microtubule growth, which was consistent with our previous study showing that SETD2-mediated α-TubK40me3 promoted tubulin nucleation and microtubule polymerization (
21
). Moreover, our previous study revealed that α-TubK40me3 was preferably distributed on polymerized microtubules (
21
). Our present study demonstrated that KDM4A preferentially associated with polymerized microtubules rather than soluble tubulin, which is beneficial for removing methylation on microtubules. However, our immunostaining and 3D reconstruction images did not show high colocalization between KDM4A and microtubules at the interphase and mitosis. Given that previous studies have shown that α-TubK40me3 is required for mitosis and can promote the nucleation and polymerization of microtubules (
12
,
21
), we speculate that cells may need to maintain a moderate level of α-TubK40me3 to complete various cellular functions, and excessive binding of KDM4A with microtubules would disrupt the balance of α-TubK40me3. In addition, the mechanism by which KDM4A accesses α-TubK40me3 within the microtubule lumen remains an important question. Structural analysis predicts that full-length KDM4A has a hydrodynamic radius of 6 nm (calculated from its AlphaFold-predicted structure using HullRad; UniProt:
O75164
) (
46
), suggesting spatial feasibility for accessing the microtubule lumen. We hypothesize that KDM4A may enter the microtubule lumen through two possible routes, similar to the established entry mechanism of the microtubule acetyltransferase MEC17 (
32
,
47
,
48
). First, the 24-nm-diameter microtubule ends could provide a sufficient space for enzyme entry. Second, natural defects in the microtubule lattice, which range from 2.6 to more than 20 μm in size (
49
), may serve as additional entry points. This proposed access mechanism would enable KDM4A to demethylate luminal α-TubK40me3, with the resulting demethylation promoting microtubule depolymerization—consistent with our observation of enhanced microtubule polymerization in KDM4A KO cells. Similarly, the antibody against α-TubK40me3 and fluorescently conjugated secondary antibodies may also adopt these routes to access the microtubule lumen to recognize this modification in cells. Nevertheless, how α-TubK40me3 affects the microtubule property is still unclear.
α-TubK40me3, as a PTM of α-tubulin, is often abundant in specialized area where a massive microtubule is formed such as a spindle and midbody (
12
). Our immunostaining results showed that the level of α-TubK40me3 during the interphase was lower than that in mitotic phases, which was consistent with previous studies (
12
,
50
). This may stem from predominant nuclear localization of the microtubule methyltransferase SETD2 (also histone methyltransferase) at the interphase (
21
,
51
,
52
), which constrains its methylation capacity on microtubules. Upon the entry into mitosis, nuclear envelope breakdown allows SETD2 to release into the cytoplasm, thereby markedly enhancing microtubule methylation levels. However, it remains unknown how SETD2 and KDM4A collaborate to regulate the α-TubK40me3 level. During mitosis, the chromosomes need to be delivered flawlessly to the daughter cells to maintain genomic stability, which requires proper activation of spindle assembly checkpoint and correct kinetochore-microtubule attachments (
53
–
55
). Previous studies have reported that KDM4A was related to genome instability, mainly attributed to its protective epigenic roles (
56
,
57
). Our experiments with KDM4A KO or degradation for 12 hours in cells exhibited that the loss of KDM4A caused more mitotic defects including multipolar spindles at the metaphase, lagging chromosomes, and micronuclei at the telophase and cytokinesis, whereas degradation of KDM4A for 2 hours in KDM4A-dTAG cells did not show notable mitotic defects, caused by the possibility that the slightly increased methylation of α-tubulin and histone is not enough to lead to mitosis errors or 12 hours of degradation of KDM4A caused some unknown alterations and made cells vulnerable to mitotic defects rather than a mitosis-specific role of KDM4A. RNA-seq and quantitative proteomic analyses revealed that the effect on the transcriptome was very minor after acute degradation of KDM4A for 12 hours, suggesting that abnormal mitosis, especially in the early stage of KDM4A depletion, was caused by the excessive α-TubK40me3 rather than the gene transcriptional change. Excessive α-TubK40me3–induced mitotic defects were also evidenced by overexpression of the trimethylation-mimic α-tubulin mutant α-tubulin
K40F
in cells. Moreover, overexpression of the α-tubulin mutant α-tubulin
K40A
for decreasing α-TubK40me3 and microtubule-depolymerized drug nocodazole treatment could restore the cell mitotic defects to the normal level in KDM4A KO cells. These findings provide evidence to support the notion that KDM4A-mediated α-tubulin demethylation is required for cell mitosis. Excessive α-TubK40me3 caused by KDM4A deletion promoted microtubule overpolymerization, which may compromise the spindle assembly checkpoint, form more misconnections between microtubules and kinetosomes, and lastly lead to events of genomic instability such as multipolar spindles, lagging chromosomes, and micronuclei. However, given that other alterations (such as microtubule-organizing center fragmentation) may also lead to the increased number of tubulin nucleation foci and microtubule overpolymerization (
58
), we cannot fully exclude the possibility that other changes induced by KDM4A KO contribute to mitotic defects. It should be noted that the loss of α-TubK40me3 by SETD2 deletion results in reduced microtubule polymerization and also causes mitotic defects (
12
,
21
), indicating that the balance of α-TubK40me3 is required for normal cell mitosis, and both excessive α-TubK40me3 and insufficient α-TubK40me3 lead to mitosis defects.

Cell culture, transfection, and drug treatment

RNA-seq and data analysis
Total RNA was isolated from KDM4A-dTAG cells treated with 100 nM dTAG-13 for 0 or 12 hours using the RNeasy mini kit (Qiagen, Germany). Paired-end libraries were synthesized by using the TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina) following TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation Guide. Briefly, the poly-A–containing mRNA molecules were purified using poly-T oligo–attached magnetic beads. Purified libraries were quantified by the Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Life Technologies) and validated by the Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies) to confirm the insert size and calculate the mole concentration. A cluster was generated by cBot with the library diluted to 10 pM and then sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq X Ten (Illumina).
Differentially expressed gene analyses were performed in R (version 4.3.3) using the DESeq2 (version 1.42.1) package for 0- and 12-hour samples (
63
). Correction for the overestimation of effect sizes for lowly expressed genes was done with a log
2
fold change shrink. Differentially expressed genes were defined by expression difference of 12-hour samples compared to the 0-hour samples larger than 1.5-fold and Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate–adjusted
P
< 0.05. GO enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes was conducted using clusterProfiler (version 4.10.1) of the R package (version 4.4.4) in conjunction with the “org.Hs.eg.db” package (version 3.18.0) (
64
). All detected expression genes are listed in table S2.
Label-free quantitative proteomics and data analysis
Total protein was extracted from KDM4A-dTAG cells treated with 100 nM dTAG-13 for 0 or 12 hours using RIPA buffer (containing cocktail) and precipitated with acetone. The protein pellet was dried by Speedvac and subsequently dissolved in 8 M urea and 100 mM tris-HCl (pH 8.5). Tris(2-chloroethyl) phosphate (5 mM; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and iodoacetamide (10 mM; Sigma-Aldrich) were added to the solution and incubated at room temperature for 30 min for reduction and alkylation. The protein mixture was digested overnight with trypsin at 1:50 (w/w) (Promega) and then stopped with formic acid. The peptide mixture was desalted by a monospin C18 column (SHIMADZU-GL) and loaded onto an Easy-nLC1200 system equipped with a homemade reversed phase C18 column (75 μm by 30 cm, 1.9 μm) with a 180-min gradient from 2 to 100% of buffer B (0.1% formic acid in 80% acetonitrile) at 300 nl/min. Data-dependent MS/MS analysis was performed with an Orbitrap Eclipse Tribrid mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Peptides eluted from the column were directly electrosprayed into the mass spectrometer. The acquired MS/MS data were analyzed against a human database using the software MaxQuant.
Limma package (version 3.58.1) was used to compare label-free quantitative intensity between 0 and 12 hours (
65
). Differentially expressed proteins were defined by expression difference of 12-hour samples compared to the 0-hour samples larger than 1.5-fold and Bonferroni-adjusted
P
< 0.05. The method of GO enrichment analysis was the same as RNA-seq data. All detected expression proteins are listed in table S3.
Statistical analysis
All experiments were performed at least three times. Data were obtained from at least three independent experiments and are presented as the means ± SEM. Statistical analysis was performed by Prism 8 software (GraphPad). Comparison between two groups with one variable was analyzed by two-tailed unpaired Student’s
t
test, and comparison among multiple groups with one variable or two variables was performed with a one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) test. Differences were considered as significant as at the level of
P
< 0.05.
