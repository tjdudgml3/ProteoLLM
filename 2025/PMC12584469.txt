Introduction
Bladder cancer is a high-incidence malignant tumor in the world, ranking in the top 10. The main symptom of bladder cancer is gross hematuria, which occurs in approximately two-thirds of bladder cancer patients [
1
]. For patients without hematuria, early and accurate diagnosis remains a challenge. Current diagnostic methods, including computerized tomography (CT), ultrasound, urine cytology and cystoscopy can solve this problem to some extent. However, these examinations have more or less some defects, such as low sensitivity of B-ultrasound, high radiation damage of CT scan and lower sensitivity and specificity of urine cytology and so on. Consequently, there is an urgent need for novel diagnostic approaches.
Biomarkers have emerged as a focal point in bladder cancer diagnosis and surveillance. In a retrospective study of 112 different types of bladder cancer biomarkers from 44 studies, current area under the curve (AUC) values for bladder cancer biomarkers ranged from 0.650 to 0.707, and nuclear matrix protein-22 (NMP-22), UroVysion, and uCyt + appeared in more than five studies [
2
]. Some important new biomarkers, such as fibroblast growth factor receptor 3 (FGFR3), telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT), HRAS gene, etc. have also been reported for several times. However, present potential biomarkers showed insufficient accuracy. Newer biomarkers still need to be developed.
Blood-based biomarkers offer advantages due to lower intra- and inter-individual variability and reduced susceptibility to dietary influences [
3
]. Lipids, which play critical roles in biological processes, have gained attention as potential cancer biomarkers [
4
,
5
]. In urological tumors, particularly prostate cancer, lipid metabolism has been extensively studied due to its association with obesity and disease progression [
6
,
7
]. Circulating lipids, including fatty acids [
8
] lecithin [
9
] hemolytic lecithin [
10
] etc., have been validated as diagnostic markers for prostate cancer. For instance, serum biomarkers dimethylheptanoyl carnitine and arachidonoyl demonstrated ROC values of 0.97 and 0.86 by mass spectrometry, respectively [
11
]. In a previous study in 2020, Liu et al. highlighted the potential of plasma lipidomics in distinguishing bladder cancer (BC) from renal cell cancer (RCC). It suggested that plasma lipids could distinct BC and RCC with satisfactory accuracy with the AUC of 0.85 [
12
]. However, few studies have explored lipidomic profiles in bladder cancer stratified by grade.
In our previous study, we utilized liquid chromatography-high resolution mass spectrometry (LC-HRMS) to perform comprehensive blood metabolomic profiling in non-muscle invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) patients across both low- and high-grade disease. These analyses yielded biomarkers demonstrating exceptional sensitivity and specificity [
13
]. Leveraging these foundational findings and synthesizing current literature, we have extended this research to systematically characterize lipid metabolite expression patterns in NMIBC patients, with ultimate goal of identifying clinically relevant lipid biomarkers for NMIBC diagnosis.
Methods
Sample collection and preparation
This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Institute of Basic Medical Sciences and ethics Committee of Peking Union Medical College Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences. All the patients signed the informed consent before the study. Based on the 2004 classification criteria of the World Health Organization/International Society of Urological Pathology, patients with NMIBC are divided into three categories: papillary urothelial proliferation of low-malignant potential (PUNLMP), low-grade papillary carcinoma and high-grade papillary carcinoma. The inclusion criteria include: (1) age 18–90 years old. (2) patients who were diagnosed with NMIBC, including low-grade NMIBC and high-grade NMIBC. (3) people who were diagnosed without NMIBC were included in the healthy group. The exclusion criteria include: (1) patients with any other non-malignant bladder diseases. (2) patients with more than one type of tumor; (3) patients who did not sign an informed consent. The blood samples were collected in the morning on an empty stomach before the operation of transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURBT) by nurses and centrifuged within 6 h.
Blood samples of patients and healthy controls was collected prior to surgery or during physical examination in the morning at 07:00 a.m.−09:00 a.m. after overnight fasting and was subsequently drawn into 10 ml Vacutainer Plasma Separator Tubes with a clot activator for plasma. Next, all samples were centrifuged according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Plasma samples were prepared within 2 h after blood collection and stored in a − 80◦C refrigerator until lipidomic analysis.
200 µL of plasma samples were mixed with 600 ul of isopropanol (IPA) precooled to − 20 °C. Samples were vortex mixed for 1 min. After 10 min of incubation at room temperature, samples were stored at − 20 °C for 2 h to improve protein precipitation and then centrifuged at 14, 000 g for 20 min. The supernatant was dried under vacuum and stored at − 80 °C refrigerator until analysis. The dried lipids were reconstituted with 100 µl of 50% IPA.
LC-HRMS analysis
Lipidomics analysis was conducted using a Waters ACQUITY H-class LC system coupled with an LTQ Orbitrap mass spectrometer velos (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA.
USA) in positive ion mode. Plasma lipids were separated with a 23 min gradient at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min. Mobile phase A was 10mM ammonium acetate in acetonitrile (4:6) and mobile phase B was 10mM ammonium acetate in isopropanol/acetonitrile (9:1). The gradient was set as follows: 0 min, 40% solvent B; 0–2 min, 40–43% solvent B; 2–8 min, 43–85% solvent B; 8–15 min, 85–99% solvent B; 15–18 min, 99% solvent B; 18–18.1 min, 99–40% solvent B;18.1–23 min, 40% solvent B. The column temperature was set as 45◦C.
The mass spectrometer was operated in positive ion mode. The gas of sheath was auxiliary gas and nitrogen, and the flow rate was 10 units and 45 units. The scanning range of mass spectrometry was 100 ~ 1000 m/z, the stray voltage was set to 4.2KV, and the temperature of the ion transport tube was 350 °C. The data were obtained by high-resolution Fourier transformation mode, the MS1 resolution was 60,000, and the MS2 was 15,000. The secondary adopted data dependent analysis mode, the dynamic elimination time was 15 s, and the fragmentation method was high energy collision dissociation (HCD). The relevant parameters were set as follows: isolation width: 3 m/z; collision energy: 20%, 40% and 60% depending on the different lipids. And the activation time was 30ms.
Data statistical analysis
The raw data obtained by UPLC-LTQ orbitrap is then processed using software Progenesis QI (Version 2.0, Nonlinear Dynamics, Waters, UK). The process includes peak identification, peak alignment, peak correction and matrix output. Variables with the coefficient of variation (CV)less than 30% were screened for subsequent multivariate statistical analysis. First, the variable matrix is imported into SIMCA-P software 14.0 (UmetricsAB, Umea, Sweden) for principle component analysis (PCA). Student T-test was used to calculate the significance of variables. Variables with adjusted p-value < 0.05 are considered to be differential features. Lipids subclass enrichment was performed using MS peaks intensity tables by MetaboAnalyst 6.0 (
https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/
). Potential biomarker selection was performed based on AUC value (AUC > 0.75), and the mean decrease accuracy value of random forest classification model. Prediction model was optimized using random forests algorithm, and visualized by Medcalc software. Differential lipids annotation was further determined from the exact mass composition, from the goodness of the isotopic fit for the predicted molecular formula and from MS/MS fragmentation matching with databases (HMDB, LIPID MAPS, and METLIN), using Progenesis QI 2.2 (Waters, Milford, MA, USA).
Results
A total of 214 subjects were enrolled, comprising 106 NMIBC patients (NMIBC group) and 108 healthy adults recruited from the physical examination center (control group). The discovery cohort included 72 patients from the NMIBC group and 72 healthy controls. The validation cohort consisted of 34 NMIBC patients and 36 healthy controls. Among the NMIBC patients, 47 were diagnosed with low-grade disease and 59 with high-grade disease. (Table

Data quality control
To avoid bias during analysis, random sampling was performed and quality control samples were prepared as a pooled mixture of aliquots from plasma samples in each group. The QC sample was injected once every twenty samples throughout the analytical run to monitor instrument stability. Pearson correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the method stability, which showed high correlation with the medium of correlation coefficient of 0.99 (Fig S2A). PCA score plot show that the principal component deviation of QC samples is within 2SD along with the progress of analysis (Fig S2B), indicating good repeatability (The first four injections are QC injections used to balance the system before formal sample injection).
NMIBC group vs. control group
Unsupervised principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to compare the NMIBC and control groups (Fig.
1
A). The PCA score plot revealed clear separation between the groups. Differentially expressed lipids (adjusted p-value < 0.05) were then subjected to pathway enrichment analysis using the Mummichog algorithm. The results indicated significant enrichment of hydroxy fatty acids, O-TAG, O-LPGs, and hydrocarbons in the NMIBC group compared to the control group (Fig.
1
B).

Further annotation of the differential features determined by MS/MS evaluation identified 31 differential lipids (Fig.
1
C, and Table S1a). ROC analysis showed that 18 out of 31 lipids have a good diagnostic value for NMIBC and controls distinction with the AUC values above 0.75 (Table S1b). Further random forests were used to perform feature selection and model parameter optimization. Finally, a lipid panel consisting of PE (14:1/20:0), PE (18:2/16:0) and 19-methyl-heneicosanoic acid (Fig.
1
D) was used to establish a robust model for distinguishing between NMIBC and control samples. Metabolite of 19-methyl-heneicosanoic acid contributes the most to this model, followed by PE (14:1/20:0) and PE (18:2/16:0) (FigS2). The AUC was 0.88 for the discovery set. The sensitivity and specificity were 0.77 and 0.85, respectively (Fig.
1
E). This model was further used to predict NMIBC in the validation set (24 NMIBC patients, and 32 healthy adults). The achieved AUC value was 0.82, with the sensitivity and specificity of 0.88 and 0.72 (Fig.
1
F).

Discussion
Currently, cystoscopy remains the gold standard for bladder cancer diagnosis despite being highly invasive and costly. Whereas, NMP22 or FISH [
14
] offer alternatives, they lack the sensitivity and specificity to replace cystoscopy entirely. The value of mass spectrometry-based biomarkers stems from their potential to overcome current diagnostic limitations.
Our study revealed significant alterations in diacylglycerol (DG) and phospholipid (PE, PC) levels in bladder cancer, suggesting their potential biological function. As a lipid second messenger, diacylglycerol modulates cell proliferation pathways, offering promising therapeutic targets for tumor growth inhibition [
15
].
Emerging evidence underscores DG’s pivotal role in tumor cell metabolism [
16
] and renal clear cell carcinoma [
17
]. Diacylglycerol has been limited reported in bladder cancer, T24 has shown high motility in bladder cancer cells, and the intracellular signal transduction process of interleukin-8-induced T24 motility involves the involvement of diacylglycerol in cellular regulatory signal transduction [
18
,
19
]. Piyarathna et al. [
20
] pioneered the identification of lipidomic signatures associated with urothelial carcinoma progression. Their comparative analysis of 126 urothelial carcinoma cases versus 39 normal tissues revealed progression decreases in phosphatidylserine, phosphatidylethanolamine and phosphocholine levels, while elevated DG concentrations correlated with advancing tumor stage. These findings align with our screening results, collectively suggesting DG’s diagnostic specificity and sensitivity for bladder cancer. Further investigation is warranted to fully elucidate DG’s potential as a biomarker and therapeutic target in bladder cancer management.
Phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) is a major phospholipid component of cellular membranes, localized in both the cytoplasmic membrane and mitochondria. Beyond its structural role, PE participates in critical biological processes, including cell division, apoptosis, and anticoagulation [
20
]. Karthik et al. [
21
] conducted a racially balanced cohort study comparing African Americans and European populations, identifying significant dysregulation in 86lipid species. Notably, the most prominent alterations were observed in phosphatidylcholine (PC), phosphatidylethanolamine, and diglycerides-making the first reported race-specific lipidomic changes in bladder cancer. Further reinforcing these findings, Piyarathna et al. [
22
] demonstrated that hemolysis (> 0.4% of samples) significantly influences PE lipid concentrations, potentially confounding analytical results [
23
]. This underscores the important of sample integrity in lipidomic studies.
Our study demonstrates that carnitines (CAR) can effectively discriminate between low-grade and high-grade NMIBC patients, achieving an AUC value of 0.815. This highlights CAR’s potential as a diagnostic biomarker for tumor stratification. Acylcarnitines, key regulators of cellular energy metabolism, exhibit significant dysregulation in various cancers, including bladder cancer. Supporting this, Wu et al. [
24
] observed elevated fatty acylcarnitine levels in preoperative bladder cancer patients, followed by a significant postoperative decline in cytokine levels and microbiome restructuring (
p
< 0.05), further implicating CAR in bladder cancer pathogenesis. Additionally, Won et al. [
25
] identified profound disruptions in the carnitine-acylcarnitine metabolic pathway (e.g., CPT1A, CPT1B, CPT1C, CPT2, SLC25A20, and CRAT) in bladder cancer patients versus healthy controls. Intriguingly, NMIBC patients exhibited downregulation of CPT1B, CPT1C, SLC25A20, and CRAT, while muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) cases showed reduced expression of CPT1B, CPT1C, and CRT. Collectively, these findings position CAR as a critical mediator of metabolic reprogramming and disease progression in bladder cancer.
The candidate biomarkers identified in this study will undergo further validation and clinical translation using targeted mass spectrometry-based approaches. The estimated cost for the entire analytical process, from sample preparation to detection, ranges between $50 and $100 per test. These mass spectrometry-derived biomarker panels show promise as a non-invasive preliminary screening tool for bladder cancer, potentially augmenting existing diagnostic modalities such as urine biochemical tests and cystoscopy.
While our findings are promising, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center investigation with a relatively limited sample size. To strengthen the validity of our results, future multicenter studies with larger cohorts will be essential to confirm the diagnostic reliability and clinical utility of these lipid metabolites. Additionally, further research is needed to optimize the biomarker panel and establish standardized protocols for clinical implementation. Thirdly, we are not collecting samples from patients with other urological tumors at present. In the future, we plan to include cases involving only other urological tumors, as well as benign urological conditions, to serve as specific control groups. This will be crucial for validating the specificity of bladder cancer biomarkers.
Conclusion
Our study reveals significant perturbations in the plasma lipidome of NMIBC patients, with particularly pronounced alterations observed in low-grade disease compared to healthy controls. The identified lipid biomarkers demonstrated robust diagnostic performance for NMIBC detection, highlighting their potential clinical utility. Notably, we found that distinct plasma lipid signatures could partially stratify tumor grade, suggesting their possible role in disease classification and management. These findings contribute to the growing body of evidence supporting lipidomics as a valuable approach for bladder cancer characterization and may inform future diagnostic strategies.
