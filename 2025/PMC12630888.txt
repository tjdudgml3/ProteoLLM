Background
Cardiomyocytes constitute the contractile units of the heart. They are mostly terminally differentiated mature cells with low turnover. Thus, the heart regeneration capacity is inadequate to compensate for the vast myocardial loss after injury
1
. Currently, heart transplantation is the only curative option for patients affected by end stage heart failure. However, recent preclinical studies and clinical trials showed that transplantation of cells or cell products as next-generation therapies can provide promising approaches for regenerating injured myocardium
2
. Pluripotent stem cells (PSCs) appear to be suitable cell sources for cardiac regeneration due to their capacity for infinite proliferation and ability to differentiate into three cardiac lineages, i.e., cardiomyocytes, smooth muscle, and endothelial cells
3
,
4
. Furthermore, in vitro PSC-based models provide an unprecedented opportunity for mechanistic studies of cardiomyogenesis, cardiac disease modeling, drug discovery, and cardiotoxicity testing
5
,
6
.
Tremendous efforts have been made to establish efficient and chemically defined protocols for large-scale culture of human PSCs (hPSCs) and their differentiation into cardiac progenitor cells (CPCs) and cardiomyocytes
7
,
8
. However, to find their path into pharmaceutical research and clinical-oriented applications, they should be subjected to in-depth cellular, subcellular, and molecular characterization of their pluripotent, differentiated, and mature states
9
. These characterizations will pave the way for their safety and efficacy evaluations. Proteomics provides valuable information on the protein map of each specific developmental stage during cardiomyocyte differentiation. Consequently, these high throughput data can be employed to enrich the differentiation-related signaling pathways and identify the lineage-specific markers. This will result in producing a more pure population of target cells and eliminating undesired cell types for the aforementioned applications
10
.
Recent proteomic studies have mainly focused on identifying the molecular processes involved in the late stages of cardiac differentiation, i.e., CPCs and differentiated cardiomyocytes
11
,
12
. A single cell proteomic analysis reported a glycolytic metabolic profile for human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs)-derived cardiomyocytes in contrast to fatty acid metabolism, a pathway enriched in adult cardiomyocytes
13
. However, the proteome profiles of differentiating cells at mesendoderm (ME) and cardiac mesoderm (CME) stages, as two fundamental steps for CPC production, have been underrepresented. To fill this gap, an in-depth quantitative proteomic analysis was performed during human embryonic stem cell (hESC) differentiation into cardiomyocytes, in which temporal proteome dynamics were monitored to unravel the underlying mechanisms involved in cardiac development. We identified specific markers of hESC, ME, CME, CPC, and cardiomyocyte by taking advantage of tandem mass tag (TMT)-based quantitative proteomics and bioinformatics tools.
This PSCs-based model of cardiac development in a dish provides valuable information for purifying stage-specific populations and accelerates the process toward efficient cardiac research and regenerative medicine.
Results and discussion
Proteome profiling identified twenty-three protein classes during cardiomyocyte differentiation
To systematically profile the proteome of differentiating cells during in vitro directed differentiation, the hESC line Royan H6[RH6] was expanded and differentiated into cardiomyocytes in a dynamic suspension culture using a well-established stepwise protocol (Fig.
1
). In brief, 5-day-old hESCs were induced to ME using a WNT activator for one day. The differentiating cells were subsequently subjected to CME induction using a cocktail of the small molecules (SMs), including a WNT antagonist, a sonic hedgehog agonist, and an inhibitor of transforming growth factor-b superfamily type I activin receptor-like kinase receptors (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Cardiospheres (Supplementary Fig. 1B) started beating at day 7, as presented in Supplementary Movie 1. Samples were collected at particular differentiation stages (hESC (d0), ME (d1), CME (d3), CPC (d6), and cardiomyocyte (d12)) to perform an in-depth quantitative proteomic analysis (Fig.
1
). To confirm the efficacy of the differentiation process, expression and intracellular localization of the well-known pluripotency and cardiac differentiation markers were assessed by qRT-PCR and immunostaining at particular differentiation time points (Fig.
2
). The expression of pluripotency marker NANOG was substantially decreased upon cardiac induction and remained low during differentiation, while cardiogenic markers TBX5, MYH6, and TNNT2 were gradually upregulated by differentiation initiation (Fig.
2
A). Brachyury (T), an ME marker, showed an elevated expression pattern in the early phase (day 1) and switched back to the original levels at late cardiogenesis. Differentiating cells showed enhanced expression of MESP1 at the CME stage (Fig.
2
A). Likewise, these developmental markers’ protein expression and intracellular localization confirmed hESC differentiation into cardiomyocytes (Fig.
2
B). The expression pattern in differentiating cells was supported by previous findings of heart development
14
,
15
. Developmental studies in Xenopus revealed that tbx5 expression increases in two lateral stripes, corresponding to the cardiac primordia, on either side of the embryo and continue to be expressed in the migrating precardiac mesoderm
16
,
17
.

Overall, 52,693 peptides and 44,402 unique peptides from 6279 proteins were identified with a false discovery rate (FDR) of 1%. Furthermore, 5182 and 5530 proteins were quantified in each TMT experiment, among which 4433 unique proteins were found to be in common in all biological replicates across all time points (Fig.

Based on the PANTHER classification system, the identified proteins were classified into 23 protein classes, contributing to 19 biological processes (BPs). According to pi-chart, the highest number belonged to regulatory proteins, such as 292 protein modifying enzymes, 376 nucleic acid metabolism proteins, and 484 metabolite interconversion enzymes, as well as 115 proteins involved in trafficking processes (Fig.

Cardiomyocyte differentiation is mediated by proteome reorganization through some important biological processes
Unsupervised clustering of 4417 proteins with substantial differential expression during the time course of differentiation, segregated the proteome profile into six main clusters (Fig.
4
A and Additional Data File 3). Enriched BPs in each cluster identified sophisticated functional events related to cardiac lineage differentiation. Differentiation transition was very prominent in cluster 4, where the 467 proteins showed a steep linear pattern of upregulation from pluripotency to cardiomyocyte stage. Intriguingly, most of the enriched BPs in this cluster were associated with protein packaging, post-translational modifications, and trafficking as indicated by endoplasmic reticulum to Golgi vesicle-mediated transport, protein modification process, COPII vesicle coating and budding, vesicle targeting to cis-Golgi, phosphorylation, and post-Golgi vesicle-mediated transport. Furthermore, there was a relevant contribution of BPs related to autophagy, cytoskeletal organization, and cell migration in this cluster, e.g., actin filament organization, actomyosin structure organization, and cell–cell junction organization.

In contrast, cluster 2, containing 487 proteins with a linear decreasing trend over the differentiation course, was associated with enriched BPs mostly involved in DNA replication and cell proliferation. In this regard, some of these enriched BPs were related to mitotic DNA replication, DNA repair, DNA strand elongation, mitotic sister chromatid segregation, DNA metabolic process, and positive regulation of the DNA biosynthesis process. Additionally, the downregulation of proteins involved in regulating telomerase RNA localization and protein localization to the Cajal body is another indication of pluripotency exit. These findings indicated that the differentiation transition is accompanied by a subcellular switch from proliferation to functional protein specification and localization, as well as cytoskeletal reorganization and morphological adaptation to push the cell into a more committed fate.
Interestingly, the special pattern of cluster 5, including a steady-state expression of transcription-related processes throughout pluripotency, ME, and CME stages, followed by their drop over CPC and cardiomyocyte stages, was observed. In contrast, the temporal profile observed in cluster 6 was very well matched with the fading of stemness and emerging of cardiac lineage commitments’ BPs, such as aerobic electron transport chain, mitochondrial ATP synthesis coupled electron transport, fatty acid beta-oxidation, muscle contraction, and myofibril assembly.
In cluster 1, an overrepresentation of BPs that may govern the development of CME and CPC was observed. Transmembrane receptor protein tyrosine kinase signaling ranked the top BP and has been shown to play a determinative role in cardiac development
18
. A study showed that inhibition of receptor tyrosine kinase influences the proliferation and differentiation capacity of human CPCs
19
. Furthermore, BPs related to chromatin remodeling and epigenetic modification were enriched in this cluster, reflecting the pluripotency exit and overexpression of specification-related genes (Fig.
4
B).
Potential biomarkers were identified for each step of hESC differentiation into cardiomyocytes
Identifying stage-specific proteins may help elucidate the molecular mechanisms of cardiogenesis and introduce efficient biomarkers for detecting, sorting, tracking, and monitoring particular cell types at specific cardiac developmental stages in research and clinical settings. Thus far, a collection of markers has been introduced for cardiac lineage cells. However, they are not adequately cell- or stage-specific. Therefore, the demand remains to specifically enrich the in vitro-derived target cell population. To nominate these biomarkers, a pairwise comparison between each time point and four other stages of in vitro cardiomyocyte differentiation was conducted using the lmFit, and empirical Bayes (eBayes) functions within the ‘limma’ package. The intersection of differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) (fold change > 1.5 and adjusted P value > 0.05) obtained from all four comparisons was considered stage-specific identifiers. We found 37, 11, 9, 4, and 212 DEPs that were specifically related to ESC, ME, CME, CPC, and cardiomyocyte stages, respectively (Fig.
5
and Additional Data File 4).

,

Emerging evidence suggests their potential function in preserving the self-renewal and pluripotency properties of ESCs. For instance, GLDC, an upregulated protein in hESC, is a key enzyme in glycine cleavage with a recently defined role as a metabolic regulator in hESC for pluripotency state preservation
23

,
26

27
. In addition to the above-mentioned biomarkers, we detected some ESC-specific nucleic acid binding proteins (SPATS2L-i3, SAMHD1, and LARP1B) and chaperone molecules (HSPA2 and CLU) involved in the cell cycle and protection of the proteome from stress, whose specific function in ESC stage needs to be experimentally clarified.
Our findings represent 11 DEPs at the ME stage (Fig.
5
and Additional Data File 4), among which WNT signaling associated genes such as eomesodermin (EOMES), Dickkopf WNT Signaling Pathway Inhibitor 4 (DKK4), and mix paired-like homeobox 1 (MIXL1), are known key transcription factors and biomolecules for mesoderm development

29

Enrichr transcription factor analysis on ME-specific markers showed that IFITM3, SLC7A5, MIXL1, and EPHA2 of 11 DEP could also be target genes of the EOMES transcription factor (Additional Data File 5). IFITM3 is a cell surface protein whose effect on the migration and regionalization of Primordial Germ Cells (PGC) during gastrulation has been reported. IFITM3-expressing cells are accumulated in the posterior and proximal epiblast. Subsequently, IFITM3 activity results in the cell movement toward the endoderm
30
. EPHA2 belongs to the Eph receptor tyrosine kinases family that binds ephrins on adjacent cells and activates cell-contact-dependent signaling that regulates the focal adhesion and mesodermal gene expression program
31

32

33
. SLC7A5 is a plasma membrane transporter, whose expression depends on Wnt/ß-catenin signaling, elevates the uptake of large neutral amino acids in differentiating cells. Slc7a5 mRNA expression has also been demonstrated in epiblast, primitive streak, and emerging ME during embryogenesis
34
. Furthermore, we found some ME-specific proteins contributing to ion storage and transportation (e.g., NQO2, a chloride ion binding protein, and VAMP8, a chloride channel regulator). However, their specific roles in ME development have not been well documented.
Our findings also signify nine DEPs at the CME stage (Fig.
5
and Additional Data File 4), among which PDGFRA, ANPEP (CD13), and ITGA5 are membrane proteins which have previously displayed a transient expression surge at early CME stage and been introduced as specific CME surface markers

36

According to the literature, the most important molecular function of ITGA5 in this context could be vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2 (KDR) binding activity, because KDR is a plasma membrane protein on PSC-derived ME progenitors with differentiation capacity into cardiovascular lineages
37

The other identified CME-specific proteins, like POTEF, CADM1, CDC42EP, HVCN1, PRRX2, and SC5D, are involved in BPs associated with cell shape control, recognition, proliferation, differentiation, ion homeostasis, and cholesterol biosynthesis of the nervous system. However, their specific functions in the CME stage have not been well studied. Among them, CDC42EP and PRRX2 gene promoters have a binding site for MEIS1, an important transcription factor that activates certain enhancers for cardiac lineage commitment
38
. These findings show the remarkable potential of these identified DEPs as cardiomyocyte differentiation inducers and potential biomarkers for diagnostic and prognostic purposes. Furthermore, we uncovered CME stage-specific membrane proteins like CADMA1 and HVCN1 that can be used as biomarkers for selecting a homogeneous population of CME cells (Fig.

As shown in Fig.
5

The two most significant differentially expressed proteins, SHISA2 and SCD5, are the inhibitors of the WNT signaling pathway, essential for specifying the CME cells towards cardiac progenitors. SHISA2 is an endoplasmic reticulum (ER) protein that traps FZD in the ER and prevents its glycosylation and transfer into the cell membrane

40

SCD5 plays a role in the unsaturated fatty acid biosynthetic process, a prerequisite for metabolic transition from embryonic to adult cardiomyocytes. Another upregulated protein in the CPC stage was PANK2, which belongs to the pantothenate kinase family and is the only family member expressed in the mitochondria. Pantothenate kinase is a key factor in the biosynthesis of coenzyme A (CoA) by catalyzing the phosphorylation of pantothenate, resulting in the generation of 4’-phosphopantothenate in the first step of coenzyme A (CoA) synthesis as its rate-determining step
41
. PANK2 overexpression results in elevated CoA levels, which indeed supports fatty acid and ketone body oxidation. Thus, its function makes it an important enzyme in various physiological and pathological cardiac conditions, such as cardiac development and remodeling, as
PANK1
deletion in cardiomyocytes resulted in heart failure

Transitional-specific proteins govern the Stepwise differentiation of hESCs into cardiomyocytes
In order to gain a deeper insight into the development of cardiac lineage cells and to explore transitional–specific proteins (TSPs), pairwise comparisons were performed between two consecutive differentiation time points. Our results showed that TSPs actively participated in the differentiation promotion from hESCs to cardiomyocytes. CME showed a considerable change with differential expression of 325 TSPs from d1(M) to d3 (CME). The transition from hESC to the ME stage resulted in 75 TSPs (d1), while differentiation to CPC (d6) and cardiomyocyte (d12) involved 284 TSPs and 361 TSPs, respectively (Fig.
6
A and Additional Data File 6).
In vitro induction of hESCs towards ME involved differential expression of 75 TSPs, of which 33 were downregulated and 42 TSPs were upregulated. As previously discussed, 37 TSPs were identified as pluripotency biomarkers and 11 TSPs as mesoderm biomarkers. Ingenuity Pathways Analysis (IPA) was employed to construct a network of interactions that drives cellular differentiation (Fig.
6
B). The results highlighted the downregulation of three well-known pluripotency markers (FGF2, DNNT3B, and DPPA4) during the transition to the ME stage (Fig.
6
B). ANXA1, which has been previously shown to be enriched in the colonies of hPSCs, specifically the apical side of the central cells and the basolateral side of the peripheral cells
47
, was downregulated during ME differentiation probably by the inhibitory effect of EOMES mesodermal-specific transcription factor. Some Akt signaling-related proteins, such as FSTL1, CGNL1, and LMCD1, were downregulated during pluripotency exit and ME transition (Fig.
6
B). It has been shown that Akt signaling is involved in maintaining pluripotency
48
. FSTL1 regulates Akt signaling and cell proliferation
49
, whose downregulation might promote differentiation into the ME stage. Furthermore, Fstl1, or follistatin, an activin-binding protein, supports the stable self-renewal of mouse embryonic stem cells through activation of Zscan4 and inhibition of telomere shortening
50
. LMCD1 downregulation can also promote differentiation by releasing GATA6 inhibition and suppressing Akt signaling and proliferation
51
. Moreover, the network analysis of the D0 to D1 transition showed the co-expression of known ME-specific markers: EOMES, DKK4, and MIXL1 (Fig.
6
B). Additionally, the network involves some enriched proteins with unknown functions in ME specification, including ZNHIT2, VANGL1, and PPP2R3A, whose role should be further elucidated. The RNA exosome nuclease complex (EXOSC3) regulates hESC pluripotency through differentiation inhibition and suppression of ME-enriched proteins, including ZNHIT2, MIXl1, and EOMES
52
. VANGL1 is a member of the tetraspanin family, which contributes to planar cell polarity signaling
53
, while its role in the ME specification has not been identified. Another upregulated protein with uncharacterized function in ME is PPP2R3A, a Ser/Thr phosphatase counteracting Ser/Thr kinases in signal transduction.

Ferroptosis is likely activated during cardiac mesoderm differentiation
Pathway enrichment of DEPs during ME (d1) to CME (d3) differentiation revealed the involvement of ferroptosis in this process (Fig.

In the physiological state, there should be a balance between ROS production and antioxidant system activation

In contrast, some types of oxidative stress can help cells adapt to a change in cellular state to preserve cell survival or promote differentiation under stressful conditions

Sirtuin signaling is activated during the conversion of CPC to cardiomyocytes
The normal heart function requires huge amounts of energy production and consumption, mainly provided by the mitochondria [114]. The normal function of mitochondria as energy-producing engines is the basis of the heart’s normal function. As an eminent guardian of mitochondrial homeostasis, SIRT3 also plays an irreplaceable role in heart disease.
The cardiomyocytes, as functional units of the heart, consume a large amount of energy, which accounts the highest among all human organs. This supplies the energy-demanding contractile function of the myocardium, owing to the 30% mitochondrial density of cardiomyocytes and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS)
107
. Thus, the mitochondrial proteins should be tightly regulated as the core of all mitochondrial functions. Sirtuin 3, a member of class III NAD
+
-dependent HDACs, functions mainly in mitochondrial metabolic activities through regulating proteins of tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, fatty acid oxidation, electron transport chain, and ATP production, ROS detoxification, and mitochondrial biogenesis

,
109

110
. SIRT3 was upregulated during CPC differentiation to cardiomyocytes (at D12), which coincides well with the metabolic switch of these cells to OXPHOS. Therefore, SIRT3 might be a master regulator of cardiomyogenic transition (Fig.
8
). Adult Sirt3-deficient mice developed cardiac hypertrophy and interstitial fibrosis, whereas the overexpression inhibited stimuli-induced hypertrophy. In vitro experiments also showed an important role for Sirt3 in inhibiting hypertrophy induction by regulating ROS detoxification proteins, including Foxo3a, MnSOD, and catalase
111
. MnSOD was enriched in D12 differentiating cardiomyocytes (Fig.
8
A, B), which might contribute to ROS scavenging of metabolically active cardiac myocytes.

Conclusion
Proteomics is a major provider of information regarding organ development, however, it has not been applied for in-depth analysis of cardiomyocyte differentiation stages. In the current study, a stepwise proteome profiling of in vitro cardiomyocyte differentiation suggested novel aspects of molecular processes involved in heart development, such as ferroptosis as potentially a major determinant of CME specification and Sirtuin signaling as a potential driver of cardiomyocyte development. However, functional in vitro and in vivo studies are required to validate these molecular processes. Furthermore, a series of TSPs were assigned to each in vitro cardiomyogenesis stage, which might further specify the major protein drivers and related signaling pathways that govern a differentiation step. Introducing potential stage-specific biomarkers will also support the study of each developmental step towards heart form and function. Furthermore, these biomarkers might have an inherent potential for obtaining a homogeneous population of cardiac lineage cells towards clinical application. Altogether, heart physiology and pathology will face an information revolution in the near future by focusing on high throughput studies such as transcriptomic and proteomics.

Protein digestion and TMT labeling
Interfering detergents and contaminants were removed using the methanol-chloroform precipitation method. The protein pellet was resuspended in 200 µL urea (8 M) in 50 mM Tris (pH 8.8). Protein quantification was carried out by a Bicinchoninic Acid (BCA) Assay Kit (Pierce, USA). 150 µg protein sample was subjected to dual digestion with Lys-C (Wako, Japan) and trypsin (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) at a 1:100 enzyme: protein ratio. Trifluoroacetic acid at a final concentration of 1% was used to stop the reactions. Digested peptides were desalted by SDB-RPS (3 M-Empore) Stage Tips, followed by vacuum centrifugation to dryness. Peptides were solubilized in 200 mM HEPES. Then, the peptide concentration was determined using the Micro BCATM Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific, USA).
We designed two interdependent TMT runs to accommodate four biological replicates from five differentiation time points (0, 1, 3, 6, and 12 days). Each TMT experiment included two biological replicates per time point, as shown in Fig.

Digested peptides were labeled using TMT 10-plex reagent (Thermo Scientific, USA) for 1 h at RT with constant agitation. Labeling was quenched by adding 8 µL of 5% hydroxylamine (Sigma-Aldrich) for 15 min. All 10 labeled samples were combined and dried by vacuum centrifugation.

Data processing and protein identification
Raw data files were generated by Xcalibur software (Thermo Scientific) and processed with Proteome Discoverer V1.3 (Thermo Scientific, USA). A local MASCOT server V2.3 (Matrix Science, UK) was used for Peptide identification. Peak lists were searched against a UniProt Homo sapiens database (August 2018, 20,352 sequences). Search parameters and adjustments included precursor mass tolerance of ± 10 ppm, fragment mass tolerance of 0.1 Da, and only one missed cleavage for trypsin. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine was set as a fixed modification, and TMT 10-plex modification of peptide N-termini plus lysine residues, deamidation of asparagine or glutamine, and oxidation of methionine were set as variable modifications. Only peptides that scored > 15 and below the Mascot significance threshold filter of
P
= 0.05 were deconvoluted for high-resolution MS2 spectra and used in the search results. Furthermore, a threshold ≥ Mascot identity was employed to incorporate single peptide identifications into the search results. If a set of peptides in one protein was completely matched to another, they were included in one protein group. Proteins identified with at least two unique peptides were included as confident identifications in search results. Finally, proteins with q-values (FDR) of < 1% and master proteins, which were assigned through the protein-grouping algorithm, were kept.
For each TMT experiment, the relative quantitation of proteins was obtained by pairwise comparison of TMT reporter ion signal to noise (S/N) ratios for each differentiation time point vs. the labels of the internal control (labeled with TMT 126 reagent) of the corresponding run (Additional Data File 1).
Data analysis
Two TMT experiments were used to extract and combine relative quantification of all protein abundances at each differentiation time point compared to the reference (labeled with TMT 126 reagent) (Additional Data File 1).
To gain an overall understanding of proteome dynamics, the data was first checked by the analysis of variance (ANOVA) with adjusted P-value ≤ 0.05 (Additional Data File 2). The custom-made programs written in the R statistical computing environment were used for subsequent data analysis and visualization. The principal component analysis (PCA) was plotted by the ggplot2 package, and the Pearson correlations coefficients among different time points were calculated through the “cor” function from the stats package on log2 of ratio and visualized by the “pheatmap” function from the pheatmap package. The depth of protein coverage (identified proteins) in 2 TMT mass spectrometry experiments was compared using the “ggvenn” function from the ggVennDiagram package. Protein classification was accomplished using a c-means unsupervised fuzzy clustering algorithm by the “cmeans” function from the package e1071. The degree of fuzzification was set to 2 while a maximum of 100 iterations were considered. To find the temporal dynamic of the proteome during differentiation, pairwise comparisons were performed for DEPs between two consecutive differentiation time points. To extract stage-specific markers, a pairwise comparison was performed between every stage and the four other differentiation stages using the lmFit, makeContrasts, contrasts.fit, and ebayes functions within the ‘limma’ package. The intersection of DEPs (fold change > 1.5 and adjusted P values > 0.05), which was obtained from all four comparisons, is considered a stage-specific identifier. The DEPs related to each analysis were demonstrated by the “pheatmap” function from the pheatmap package. Furthermore, the up- and downregulated proteins related to dynamic analysis were illustrated by volcano plots. Subsequently, the protein members of each cluster and DEPs in each differentiation stage were subjected to Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) for pathway enrichment and network analysis. Additionally, the Gene Ontology (GO) analysis of the stage-specific and stage-transition markers was performed using Enrichr with the adjusted P value cut-off of < 0.05.
Western blot analysis
Western blot analysis was performed as previously described
139
. Harvested cells were lysed with RIPA buffer. The lysates were centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C. Protein concentration was determined by the Bradford Protein Quantification kit (DNAbioTech, Iran). Proteins were resolved using SDS-PAGE and subsequently transferred to a 0.2 μm polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane (Bio-Rad, USA). The membranes were then blocked with 5% BSA (Sigma Aldrich, USA) in 0.1% Tween 20 for 1 h. Then, the membranes were incubated with Anti-SOD2 (0.2 µg/mL, Abcam), Anti-SIRT3 (1/500, Abcam), Anti-SLC3A2 (1/1000, Abcam), Anti-PODXL (1/1000, Abcam), Anti-ANPEP (Invitrogen 1; 500), and anti-β actin (1/2500, Abcam) antibodies for 1 h at room temperature (RT). Subsequently, membranes were washed with TBST and incubated with goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (HRP) (1/10,000, Abcam) secondary antibody. The membranes were then incubated with enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) for 1–2 min. Protein expression was normalized to β-actin.
