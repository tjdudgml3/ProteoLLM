1
Introduction
Extracellular vesicles (EVs) show promise as early detection, disease, and therapeutic monitoring biomarkers in several conditions, such as cancer and autoimmune diseases (Spitzberg et al.
2023
; Zhu et al.
2023
; Hoshino et al.
2020
; Skog et al.
2008
). EVs are a heterogeneous population of lipid‐bilayer vesicles in the size range of 30 nm to 1 µm and are released by all cells, including dysfunctional cells, into the extracellular space (Hendrix et al.
2023
; van Niel et al.
2022
; Mathieu et al.
2021
). There are two distinct biogenesis pathways, either encapsulating cargo by outward budding of the plasma membrane or through an endosomal pathway, generating a multivesicular body that can fuse with the plasma membrane (van Niel et al.
2022
; Mathieu et al.
2021
). Through these, EVs are equipped with common features, such as proteins, but also contain donor cell‐dependent cargo, like proteins, nucleic acids, and metabolites, reflecting the cellular physiological state (Zhu et al.
2023
; Jang et al.
2019
; Kumar et al.
2024
). Therefore, EVs can be a source of disease‐specific markers originating from many cells. Besides their composition, EVs are present in high concentrations in biofluids, the second most abundant particle in the blood after erythrocytes in terms of membrane area (Jahnke and Staufer
2024
). Due to their high blood concentration and derivation from an easily accessible biofluid, blood plasma‐derived EVs are particularly interesting for biomarker discovery (Hendrix et al.
2023
; Palviainen et al.
2020
). However, their isolation and characterization are challenging due to the complexity of plasma and the abundance of soluble proteins. Lipoproteins have a similar density and size range (e.g., high‐density lipoprotein (HDL) 7–13 nm, low‐density lipoprotein (LDL) 18–23 nm, intermediate‐density lipoprotein (IDL) 23–27 nm, very low‐density lipoprotein (VLDL) 30–80 nm; chylomicrons 75–1200 nm) to EVs, while being 10
7
to 10
9
‐fold more abundant in plasma than EVs (Simonsen
2017
; Veerman et al.
2021
). Numerous efforts have been made to design an EV isolation method allowing for the separation of EVs from lipoproteins; however, so far, no technology has yielded pure EVs (Théry et al.
2018
). Certain proteins that were previously assumed to be contaminants may also be part of the so‐called biomolecular corona of EVs, such as complement factor 3 (Palviainen et al.
2020
; Dietz et al.
2023
; Tóth et al.
2021
). Their presence on the surface impacts the functionality of EVs and aids cellular uptake (Dietz et al.
2023
). Especially in the context of biomarker discovery, the differentiation between contaminants and the protein corona poses a problem (Palviainen et al.
2020
).
Platelets are also a common contaminant of plasma EV isolation. Normal human platelets are between 1.5 and 3 µm in diameter (Handtke and Thiele
2020
), overlapping with the size range of large EVs. Separation of platelets and EVs is challenging, as commonly used protocols for generating “platelet‐free plasma” may still contain a considerable number of platelets (Boilard et al.
2025
), which may fragment or become activated through harsh centrifugation conditions or freeze‐thaw cycles. Platelet activation can lead to an increased production of EVs, which does not reflect the state in vivo and affects downstream analysis (Bettin et al.
2022
; Bracht et al.
2023
; Małys et al.
2023
). Besides plasma, serum is also commonly used as a source for EVs; however, Palviainen et al. (
2020
) have shown that coagulation causes an increase of platelet‐derived EVs. This is crucial in the context of biomarker discovery, as these artifactually generated EVs have been shown to influence miRNA populations (Palviainen et al.
2020
; Mitchell et al.
2016
).
The most common methods to isolate EVs with little co‐isolated contaminants and high yield include density gradient ultracentrifugation (DGUC) and size‐exclusion chromatography (SEC) using commercial or self‐made columns. However, no isolation method fits all requirements, and the technique must be carefully chosen for each source material and study aim (Hendrix et al.
2023
; Welsh et al.
2024
). SEC offers several advantages over other techniques, making it a method easily applied in clinical settings (Thietart and Rautou
2020
). Compared to surface marker‐based isolation, commercially available columns are designed for unbiased EV isolation. SEC requires minor laboratory instruments, in contrast to DGUC, and recovers EVs with few contaminations, unlike precipitation kits. It has been shown that the combination of multiple techniques that combine both size‐ and density‐based isolation, like DGUC and SEC, yields a purer EV isolation, with minimal contamination from plasma proteins. However, a combination leads to loss of material and an increase in hands‐on time and therefore the opportunity for human error (Veerman et al.
2021
; Karimi et al.
2018
; Böing et al.
2014
).
Protocols published about EV isolation using SEC columns are not necessarily unified in their reporting. For example, the commercial SEC columns from IZON offer isolation protocols with varying buffer volumes and fraction numbering depending on the desired yield and purity. The omission of critical information, like buffer volume and fraction numbering, in published work may hamper reproducibility and cause variability in reported results (Veerman et al.
2021
). Additionally, a common unanswered question for junior researchers in the field is how much material is required to perform adequate EV characterization. In this study, we aim to give a detailed description of EV isolation using SEC columns of two different pore sizes (35 and 70 nm) and two starting volumes (0.5 and 10 mL), describe differences in the quality of isolated EVs, and estimate the number of feasible experiments with varying amounts of input material. The in‐depth analysis of individual fractions for the 10 mL columns allows us to give a detailed description of each fraction and detect differences in the elution pattern. We characterize EVs using LC‐MS/MS, nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), Western blot, and flow cytometry, including with a multiplexed bead‐based EV phenotyping technology known as MACSPlex.
2
Methods
2.1
Blood Collection and Plasma Processing
The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Medical Faculty of Heidelberg University, and all study participants gave written informed consent. After overnight fasting, peripheral blood from healthy volunteers (one male and two females, age 30–40) was drawn into serum tubes for the first 3 mL and discarded to circumvent the increased EV production from activated platelets through venipuncture. Then, 80 mL was collected in S‐Monovette Citrat 9NC 0.106 mol/l 3.2% tubes (Sarstedt AG & Co. KG) and centrifuged at 1000 ×
g
for 10 min at RT with no brake to separate cells from platelet‐rich plasma (PRP). PRP was additionally centrifuged at 2500 ×
g
for 10 to generate platelet‐poor plasma (PPP).
2.2
EV Enrichment by Differential Ultracentrifugation (UC)
For the comparison of EV enrichment by differential UC and SEC, 20 mL of PPP was centrifuged at 18,000 ×
g
at 4°C for 30 min. The obtained 18k pellet was washed with PBS, centrifuged again and resuspended in 200 µL of PBS. The PPP supernatant was split so that 10 mL was subjected to UC at 103,000 ×
g
at 4°C for 2 h (24,000 rpm, JS‐24.38 Swinging‐Bucket Rotor, Avanti J‐30I, Beckman Coulter, Inc.) on a 20% sucrose cushion. In detail, 6 mL of freshly prepared 20% filtered sucrose solution was added to the bottom of the tube, overlaid with 10 mL of plasma diluted with 20 mL of PBS to fill up the tubes. The obtained 100k pellet was washed with PBS, subjected to another round of UC under the same conditions, and resuspended in 100 µL of PBS. The other 10 mL of the PPP supernatant was fractionated by SEC, as described below.
2.3
EV Enrichment by SEC
Prior to EV isolation by SEC, PPP was filtered through a 1200 nm filter. To enrich EVs from 0.5 mL of plasma, we used the 35 and 70 nm qEVoriginal size exclusion columns (Izon Science Limited), referred to as small 35 (S35) and small 70 (S70) columns. The columns were used according to the manufacturer's instructions. In brief, after the equilibration of the columns at room temperature, 500 µL of plasma was loaded and topped up with 2 mL PBS, to reach the buffer volume of 2.5 mL.
For EV enrichment from 10 mL of plasma we used the 35 and 70 nm Izon qEV10 columns, referred to as large 35 (L35) and large 70 (L70) columns. According to the manufacturer's protocol, after equilibration at room temperature 10 mL of plasma was loaded to the columns and topped up with 13.2 mL (L35) and 12.9 mL (L70) PBS, to reach buffer volumes of 23.2 mL and 22.9 mL, respectively.
After the buffer volume had been displaced, 10 fractions of 0.5 mL (for S35 and S70) or 5 mL (for L35 and L70) were collected and labelled as EV‐rich (fractions 1–5) and protein‐rich (fractions 6–10) according to manufacturer's instructions. The fractions were either kept separately or pooled as specified in each experiment and concentrated using Amicon Ultra‐4 100 kDa centrifugal filters (Merck KGaA) at 4000 ×
g
. For the collection of the EV‐rich pool in the small columns, fractions 1–5 were pooled and concentrated to 500 µL. Similarly, for the protein‐rich pool, fractions 6–10 were collected and concentrated. For the large columns, 1 mL of unconcentrated sample was reserved, and the remaining 4 mL of all fractions were concentrated for 10 min. All fractions were aliquoted and stored at –80°C.
2.4
Flow Cytometry Measurement of Platelets
To assess platelet contamination, an aliquot of PRP, PPP, and PPP with 1200 nm filtration was analysed by flow cytometry. Five‐hundred  microlitre of each sample was washed with PBS and centrifuged at 2000 ×
g
for 5 min at RT. Pellets were stained with anti‐CD42b‐PE (1:50, clone REA185, Miltenyi) and anti‐CD9‐AF488 (1:50, clone 209306, R&D Systems) for 15 min at RT protected from light. The samples were then washed with PBS and centrifuged at 2000 ×
g
for 5 min at RT. Pellets were resuspended in 200 µL PBS and analysed on the Sony ID 7000 Spectral Cell Analyzer (Sony Biotechnology Inc.). PBS and antibody staining controls were run simultaneously. Samples were diluted to 6000 events per second, measured at flow rate 1 for 30 s, and analysed by FlowJo software.
2.5
NTA
To measure particle size and concentration, the samples were diluted in PBS (Gibco) to a range of 1 × 10
8
–1.5 × 10
9
particles/mL and measured on the ZetaView Quatt (Particle Metrix GmbH) running on the ZetaView Software (version 8.05.14 SP7). All instrument settings were kept the same during the measurement (camera shutter 100, camera gain 28.8), and one measurement per sample was performed, with 11 cell positions and 1 frame per position. The output text file was analysed in R version 4.3.3 for Windows (R Core Team, R Foundation for Statistical Computing), and the code has been published (Potakowskyj
2025
). PBS background showed no particle contamination.
2.6
TEM
For electron microscopy (EM), EVs from purified preparations were adsorbed onto glow‐discharged carbon‐coated copper‐grids (300mesh, Science Services), washed in water (injectable‐quality, Braun) and negatively stained with 1% aqueous uranyl acetate (Serva). Micrographs were taken with a Zeiss EM 910 at 80 kV (Carl Zeiss) using a slow scan CCD camera (TRS).
2.7
Single‐EV Flow Cytometry
Flow cytometry of EVs was performed in accordance with MIFlowCyt‐EV. For flow cytometry, 10 µg of concentrated EVs isolated by the L35 or L70 column were adjusted to 100 µL with PBS and stained with 1 µL of anti‐CD81‐APC antibody (clone REA513, Miltenyi) or anti‐CD9‐Alexa Fluor 488 (clone 209306, R&D Systems) for 30 min at 37°C. Prior to staining, the antibody was diluted in PBS and centrifuged at 18,000 ×
g
for 5 min to remove aggregates. Buffer‐only and buffer plus antibody controls were prepared and measured in parallel to samples with the same instrument settings. After sample measurement, all samples were treated with detergent to ensure lipid‐bilayer particles. All samples were measured on the Sony ID 7000 Spectral Cell Analyzer. The instrument was cleaned using FACS cleaning solution and MilliQ‐water, and the cleanliness was checked by keeping the events/second and abort rate of PBS (< 500 events/s). Monitoring beads (Megamix‐Plus SSC and FSC beads (Cosmo Bio co., Ltd.)) were measured to ensure the best resolution of small particles. Samples were measured at serial dilutions at flow rate 1 for 30 s, triggering on SSC with a threshold of 2.2. The serial dilutions were used to determine the optimal range for single‐EV flow cytometry of each sample, by ensuring lowering of events/second with consistent fluorescence intensity.
We performed light scatter calibration according to de Rond et al. (
2018
) to convert arbitrary into standard sizing units. In short, Megamix‐Plus SSC and FSC beads were run on the Sony ID 7000 Spectral Cell Analyzer using our established EV settings. The median scattering intensity was extracted from FlowJo and plotted against the mean bead size in R. Subsequently, a theoretical scattering model for polystyrene beads was generated according to the Mie theory to correlate scattering cross section (
σs
) to the size and refractive index of the particles. A scaling factor
F
was determined to account for optical parameters. Then, a model to predict EV light scattering was generated and adjusted using the calculated
F
factor. EVs were modelled as core‐shell spherical particles with a membrane thickness of 5 nm and a refractive index of 1.48 and 1.38 for the membrane and the lumen, respectively. The EV Flow Reporting Framework has been submitted in the
Supporting Information
.
2.8
Multiplex EV Surface Marker Analysis
The MACSPlex EV Kit IO, human (130‐108‐813, Miltenyi) was used for surface marker detection of EVs isolated from both large and small columns according to the manufacturer's instructions. In brief, 50 µL of EV preparation from L35 and L70, and 20 µL of EV preparation from S35 and S70 were adjusted to 120 µL using the supplied buffer and incubated with 15 µL EV Capture Beads (containing 39 different antibody‐coated bead subsets) on an orbital shaker (450 rpm) overnight at RT, protected from light. The samples were subsequently washed with 500 µL MACSPlex buffer and centrifuged at 3000 ×
g
for 5 min at RT. The pelleted beads were incubated with 15 µL of MACSPlex EV Detection Reagent cocktail (CD9, CD63, and CD81 conjugated to APC) on an orbital shaker (450 rpm) for 1 h at RT, protected from light. After washing the beads twice with the supplied buffer, the samples were measured on the Sony ID 7000 Spectral Cell Analyzer, and 10,000 events were acquired. A blank control containing no EV sample was processed in parallel. The beads were gated in FlowJo according to their PE and FITC content. Median APC fluorescence intensity (MFI) was used to quantify the signal after subtracting the value of the blank control, resulting in background‐corrected MFI. To normalize experiments that were performed on different days, background‐corrected MFI values were normalized to the mean signal of the MFI from CD9, CD63, and CD81 within each sample.
2.9
Western Blotting
For samples isolated with the L35 and L70, 30 µL of EVs were lysed using 10X RIPA lysis buffer (20‐188, Sigma‐Aldrich Chemie GmbH) and protease inhibitor (4693132001, Sigma‐Aldrich Chemie GmbH), both diluted to 1X working concentration for 10 min on ice. The samples were mixed with 4X Laemmli Buffer (Bio‐Rad Laboratories GmbH) and 10% TCEP (646547, Sigma‐Aldrich Chemie GmbH), boiled for 5 min at 95°C, and loaded on a 4%–20% Mini‐PROTEAN TGX Stain‐Free Protein Gel (4568094, BioRad). Electrophoresis was performed at 70 V for 10 min, followed by 150 V for 35 min. Proteins were then transferred onto an Immobilon‐PSQ PVDF Membrane (ISEQ00010, Merck KGaA) using NuPAGE Transfer Buffer (NP0006‐1, Thermo Fisher Scientific GmbH) for 1.5 h at 200 mA in the cold room. Following the transfer, the membrane was blocked for 1 h in 5% milk in Tris‐buffered saline with 0.1% Tween 20 (TBS‐T). After three washes with TBS‐T, the membrane was immunostained for 1 h at RT with the following antibodies: anti‐CD63 (ab134045, Abcam), anti‐CD9 (sc‐20048, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), anti‐CD81 (52892, Cell Signaling Technology), anti‐ApoA1 (ab52945, Abcam) diluted at 1:1000; anti‐HSC70 (sc‐7298, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) diluted at 1:2000 or anti‐albumin (ab10241, Abcam) diluted at 1:5000. As a secondary antibody, either anti‐rabbit or anti‐mouse IgG HRP‐linked antibodies (7074S or 7076S, Cell Signaling Technology) were used in a 1:5000 dilution for 1 h at RT. Membranes were imaged using SuperSignal West Pico PLUS chemiluminescent substrate (34580, Life Technologies GmbH) on the Fusion FX7 (Vilber). For EVs isolated from S35 and S70, 3 µg of protein was loaded and processed as described above.
2.10
LC MS/MS
Isolated EVs from small and large columns were analysed using label‐free quantitative proteomics. For small columns, data‐independent acquisition mode was used, while large columns were acquired with data‐dependent acquisition mode. EVs equivalent to 5 µg of protein were lysed in RIPA buffer. Then samples were digested with trypsin either on an AssayMAP Bravo liquid handling system (Agilent Technologies) following the autoSP3 protocol (Müller et al.
2020
), or on a KingFisher Apex system using the SP3 protocol (Hughes et al.
2014
). Peptides were separated using an UltiMate 3000 UPLC (Thermo Fisher Scientific) connected to an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer for small columns or using an UltiMate 3000 RSLC nano LC system (Dionex) coupled to an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos Tribrid Mass Spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for the large columns. Raw files of the small columns were analysed using Spectronaut (Biognosys, version 18.6.231227.55695) in directDIA+ (deep) library‐free mode, searching against the human proteome from Uniprot (20,597 unique entries) and the MaxQuant contaminants FASTA. For the large columns, raw files were processed using MSFragger v4.0 within Fragpipe v21.1, searching against the Swissprot Homo sapiens database. The label‐free proteomics dataset was deposited on the Proteomics Identifications Database (PRIDE) and is available with identifier PXD068074.
2.11
Proteomic Analysis
Downstream analysis was performed in R (version 4.3.3). Only proteins quantified with two unique peptide matches were used for analysis, and their raw intensities were log2 transformed. Identified proteins were compared to a corona protein list, curated from recent publications (Palviainen et al.
2020
; Dietz et al.
2023
; Tóth et al.
2021
). Not‐assigned proteins were then matched with a plasma protein list provided by Veerman et al. (
2021
). And then remaining proteins were assigned to the Top 100 EV proteins from the Vesiclepedia database (
www.microvesicles.org
, downloaded on 12.01.2024). For principal component analysis (PCA) raw data was used and the first two principal components were plotted. For hierarchical clustering analysis (HCA), relative abundances were calculated by dividing each protein's intensity by the sum of its intensities across all conditions. Clustering was performed using Ward's minimum variance as a linkage method, and the heatmap was plotted using the pheatmap package in R. For each cluster, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed using “GO_Cellular_Component_2018” (Carbon et al.
2019
) in “enrichR” (Kuleshov et al.
2016
). To identify donor cell markers on isolated EVs, a cell marker database was curated. First, the PanglaoDB database (downloaded on 25.10.2024) (Franzén et al.
2019
) was used, and human immune and platelet cell markers were retrieved. Cell types were summarized into larger categories, and duplicates were removed. Only cell markers that were specific for a cell type were kept. Then, the database was completed using the markers used in the MACSPlex EV Kit and compared to the unique markers from the immune and endothelial cells of the CellMarker 2.0 resource database (Hu et al.
2023
).
2.12
Statistics
Statistical analysis and graphs were done in R version 4.3.3 for Windows and GraphPad Prism 10.2.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).
2.13
EV‐TRACK
We have submitted all relevant data of our experiments to the EV‐TRACK knowledgebase (EV‐TRACK ID:
EV250067
) (Van Deun et al.
2017
).
3
Results
3.1
Comparison of Differential Centrifugation and SEC for Plasma EV Enrichment
Initially, we compared EV yield and purity obtained by differential centrifugation versus SEC. Plasma was first centrifuged at 18,000 ×
g
to remove platelets and large particles (18k pellet), and the supernatant was then processed either by UC at 100,000 ×
g
(100k pellet) or by SEC. The 18k pellet contained the highest levels of EV markers (CD9, CD81, HSC70), but also substantial plasma protein contamination, consistent with co‐pelleting of protein aggregates and lipoproteins. This suggested that while 18k centrifugation efficiently removes platelets, it could lead to a loss of certain EV subpopulations. Particle counts and protein concentrations were comparable between 18k and 100k pellets and the first three SEC fractions. However, unlike UC, which yields a single sample, pooling several SEC fractions provided a higher total EV recovery (Figure
S1
). Additionally, SEC physically separates EVs from protein‐rich fractions, enabling the recovery of EV‐enriched fractions with reduced contaminants. Based on this data, we focused on SEC for the remainder of the study, systematically comparing different column types to identify EV‐rich fractions.
We compared plasma EV enrichment efficiency and contamination with non‐EV co‐isolates using commercially available 35 and 70 nm SEC columns (IZON Science Limited) starting from either 500 µL (qEV original, “small column”) or 10 mL (qEV10, “large column”) of blood plasma (Figure
1
and Figure
S2
). The obtained SEC fractions were characterized using LC‐MS/MS, NTA, Western blot, TEM, and flow cytometry. To minimize platelet contamination while avoiding potential EV loss, we introduced 1.2 µm filtration as a defined size cut‐off (Figure
S3
).

After ultrafiltration (UF), the samples were stored in aliquots at –80°C and subsequently analysed by nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA), electron microscopy, flow cytometry, MACSPlex, Western blot and LC‐MS/MS.
3.2

2A
), suggesting increased non‐EV proteins in later fractions. To map the distribution of EV‐associated proteins and contaminants, we analysed each fraction by label‐free LC‐MS/MS.

Next, the detected proteins were analysed using HCA and grouped into 10 clusters according to their intensity and distribution in different fractions. The heatmap showed a similar pattern as the PCA, confirming that fraction 1 clusters separately from other fractions, as it was the only fraction highly enriched in proteins from cluster VII (Figure

The EV‐associated term “secretory granule lumen” was also present in clusters V, I, and IV, although these proteins were enriched in later fractions. 3.3
Comparison of 35 and 70 nm SEC Columns Reveals Improved EV Recovery With 70 nm
To test whether EV purity could be improved, we compared 35 and 70 nm SEC columns of different sizes. For the small columns, protein yield was too low to analyse individual fractions (Table
S2
); thus, we pooled fractions 1–5 (“EV‐rich”) and 6–10 (“protein‐rich”), based on the manufacturer's guidance and our data (Figure
2
). Proteomics confirmed that fractions 1–5 contained more EV‐associated proteins (Vesiclepedia top 100) than fractions 6–10 (Figure
3A
), although plasma proteins were distributed equally across pools. The 70 nm column yielded about twice as many EV proteins and more unique proteins than the 35 nm column. Next, we performed HCA with consecutive GO enrichment analysis (Figure
3B
). There was a clear separation of proteins enriched in fractions 1–5 (e.g., clusters V, X, IX, IV, II, VIII, and III) and proteins enriched in fractions 6–10 (e.g., clusters VI, XI, VII, I). EV‐associated GO terms (“secretory granule lumen” and “endocytic vesicle lumen”) were found in clusters V, IV, II, VIII, and III, enriched in fractions 1–5. However, the composition of enriched EVs appeared distinct depending on the column pore size. For instance, proteins from cluster V (including CD81) were detected only with the 70 nm column, while proteins from cluster IV were almost exclusively found with the 35 nm column. Additionally, clusters II and VII, containing GO terms associated with lipoproteins, were detected both in fractions 1–5 and 6–10, with a similar distribution for columns of the two pore sizes. Altogether, our data indicated that the pooling of fractions 1–5 yielded EVs containing many non‐EV plasma proteins.

In an attempt to reduce the plasma protein contamination and pinpoint the EV‐rich fractions, we next analysed fractions 1–5 separately using large 35 and 70 nm columns. Unsupervised clustering revealed that the fractions from both columns were similar and clustered together, albeit with a consistent shift of one fraction (i.e., 35 nm fraction 1 clustered with 70 nm fraction 2, etc.) (Figure
3C
). EV proteins were detected mostly in the first two fractions of the 35 nm column, with a peak in fraction 1, while with the 70 nm column, EV proteins were found in the first three fractions, with a peak in fraction 2 (Figure
3D
). In parallel, plasma protein counts were lower in the EV‐rich fractions of the 70 nm column than in the 35 nm column. Together, these data suggested that EVs may be eluting earlier in the 35 nm column than in the 70 nm column, resulting in loss of EV content. The 70 nm column resulted in more efficient EV isolation in fractions 1–3, with higher EV protein counts and lower plasma protein contamination compared to the 35 nm column.
Next, we performed HCA followed by GO enrichment analysis, which showed that both 35 and 70 nm columns enriched for EV‐related proteins, indicated by GO terms like “specific granule membrane,” “ficolin‐1‐rich granule,” and “tertiary granule membrane.” The heatmap showed a clear separation of “non‐EV” from EV clusters. The “non‐EV” clusters (II, IV and I) were driven mainly by plasma proteins, like lipoproteins, complement proteins, and immunoglobulins, and were considered as a measure of co‐isolated proteins. The “non‐EV” proteins were highly enriched in fractions 3–5 from the 35 nm column, while in the 70 nm column, they were detected to a lesser extent in fractions 4 and 5. In contrast, clusters containing EV‐related proteins (clusters VI, VIII, V, III, and VII) were highly enriched in fractions 1–3 from the 70 nm column compared to fractions 1 and 2 from the 35 nm column (Figure
3E
). A proportion of EV‐related proteins, for example, from clusters VI, VIII, and XI, were found predominantly in one column type, confirming our findings that the two columns may isolate EVs with a different proteomic profile. Nonetheless, EVs containing tetraspanins CD9 (cluster III) and CD81 (cluster VII), were isolated by both columns.
Taken together, our data showed that the 70 nm column isolates a higher number of EV proteins with less abundant non‐EV contaminant proteins compared to the 35 nm column. Additionally, we identified the first three fractions as “EV‐rich,” while fractions 4 and 5 contained mostly non‐EV proteins.
3.4
Particle Count, Size Distribution, and Protein Amount Obtained With Different 35 and 70 nm Columns
We performed NTA to compare particle recovery between different columns and found distinct elution patterns. With the small columns, particle counts peaked in fractions 1–3, whereas with large columns, higher counts appeared in fractions 4 and 5 (Figure
4A
). However, total protein measurements (Table
S2
) indicated greater protein contamination in fractions 4 and 5, likely inflating particle counts since NTA may not reliably distinguish between EVs, protein aggregates, and lipoproteins (Witwer et al.
2013
). Indeed, normalization to protein concentration revealed a drop in particle counts after fraction 3 (Figure
4B
). The large 70 nm column showed lower levels of protein contamination and relatively higher normalized particle counts compared to the 35 nm column, indicating improved EV enrichment. Consistent with this, the 70 nm column displayed a wider spread of values across donors (Figure
4C
), which may reflect increased sensitivity to biological heterogeneity. Using TEM, we confirmed the presence of cup‐shaped EVs in both column types, along with non‐EV particles, likely lipoproteins (Figure
4D
). Together, our findings support that the 70 nm column enhances EV enrichment and may increase sensitivity to donor‐specific differences.

To further verify the presence of EV‐associated markers and co‐isolates in different fractions, we next performed Western blot analysis. In the small columns, we detected CD81 in pooled fractions 1–5, with stronger signal intensity in the 70 m column, consistent with more efficient EV recovery (Figure

Of note, we did not detect CD81 in fraction 1 of the 35 nm column, which suggested that this column may retain or lose a portion of EVs during elution and could explain the higher overall CD81 recovery with the 70 nm column. 3.5
EV Surface Marker Characterization by Flow Cytometry
To analyse qualitative differences of the isolated EVs with pore sizes 35 and 70 nm, we performed surface staining for CD81 and analysed EVs by Sony ID 7000 Spectral Cell Analyzer according to the MIFlowCyt‐EV reporting framework (Welsh et al.
2020
). Samples from both columns showed CD81‐positive events that were sensitive to detergent treatment, confirming their vesicular nature (Figure
5A
). When implementing Mie Theory to calibrate the side scatter axis (Franzén et al.
2019
), we determined that our analysis was limited to particles larger than 550 nm in diameter, due to our instrument's limit of detection (Figure
S4
).
We next used the MACSPlex bead‐based array to analyse 37 surface markers simultaneously on all isolated particles, including those that are smaller than 550 nm. As expected, the strongest signal was detected for platelet markers, as most blood EVs originate from platelets (Li et al.
2020
). To exclude the possibility that the residual platelets in PPP obtained after the centrifugation at 2500 ×
g
released EVs ex vivo during the sample cryopreservation (Małys et al.
2023
), we filtered all PPP samples with a 1.2 µm filter prior to storage. Indeed, the filtration step removed the residual platelets from PPP (Figure
S3
). Additionally, EV release can occur due to platelet activation during blood sample handling (Małys et al.
2023
); however, this was likely not the case since the levels of platelet activation markers CD62P and CD31 (Małys et al.
2023
) were low (Figure
5B
).
MACSPlex confirmed the presence of canonical EV markers such as CD9, CD63, and CD81, which were enriched mostly in the first 3 fractions, and the detected signal appeared stronger following separation with the 70 nm column compared to the 35 nm column (Figure
5B
). Nonetheless, MACSPlex showed limited sensitivity for CD81 compared to Western blotting (Figure
4
), likely due to methodological differences, as MACSPlex relies on antibody binding to intact epitopes on the vesicle surface, while Western blotting detects denatured proteins. Accordingly, we did not detect significant differences in EV or platelet markers between the two columns (Figure
5C,D
). Instead, MACSPlex was particularly informative for immune‐cell–derived markers, which were more abundant in the first 3 fractions and significantly higher in 70 nm compared to the 35 nm column (Figure
5E
).
To confirm the enhanced isolation of immune cell‐derived EVs using the 70 nm column, we compiled a list of specific cell type markers sourced from the published PanglaoDB (Franzén et al.
2019
) and CellMarker 2.0 (Hu et al.
2023
) databases and compared it to our LC‐MS/MS data. Additionally, we included all proteins found in the MACSPlex panel. The final database contained markers for immune cells, endothelial cells, stem cells, platelets, and a category for general adhesion molecules (Table
S3
). HCA revealed that nine proteins were exclusively identified in EVs isolated with the 70 nm column, while only one protein was uniquely detected in EVs from the 35 nm column (Figure
6A
). Cell type‐specific markers were detected in the first two fractions from the 35 nm column, and in fractions 1–3 from the 70 nm column.

Next, all identified cell types were grouped, and the relative protein abundances were summarized as mean protein abundance (Figure

). While this visualization highlighted the enrichment of immune cell markers in EVs isolated using the 70 nm column, the overall distribution among different cell types was comparable. These results indicate that, compared to the 35 nm column, the 70 nm column not only captures a larger quantity of EVs, but also presents a distinct composition, with a greater enrichment of EVs derived from the immune system.
4
Discussion
Due to its reproducibility and relative ease of use, SEC utilizing Izon columns is widely used for the enrichment of EVs from blood plasma. These columns are available with matrices of different pore sizes and are optimized for various volumes, allowing for accessibility and scalability (Sidhom et al.
2020
; Mol et al.
2017
). Usually, comparisons between different columns are carried out on pooled fractions, limiting any conclusion to the exact pooling protocol (Stranska et al.
2018
). In this study, we performed a thorough comparison of EVs isolated with the 35 and 70 nm columns starting from 500 µL (“small” columns) and 10 mL (“large” columns) of plasma. We performed the basic characterization and an in‐depth proteomic analysis of each fraction separately, and we conclude that: (1) the 70 nm columns show more EV enrichment and less non‐EV contaminants compared to the 35 nm columns; (2) fractions 1–3 of the 70 nm column contain the most enriched EVs, including leukocyte‐derived EVs; (3) EV‐rich fractions obtained with small columns must be pooled to obtain sufficient material for MS‐LC/LC analysis and basic EV characterization.
Many studies used affinity capture (Muraoka et al.
2022
) or a combination of methods (SEC + density cushion) (Karimi et al.
2018
) for the enrichment of plasma EVs to minimize contamination with lipoproteins and plasma proteins for subsequent proteomics analysis. This is sometimes not feasible, as it requires special equipment, a more laborious procedure, and a larger volume of starting material to account for EV loss with each handling step. SEC columns have been previously shown to yield EVs co‐enriched with lipoprotein contaminants, including ApoA1 (Holcar et al.
2020
). This can be circumvented with the use of the 70 nm column and precise determination of EV‐rich fractions. Our data showed that EVs are enriched in fractions 1–3, and ApoA1 was not detectable before fraction 5. In accordance with our results, György et al. (
2024
) also showed that ApoA1 appears in the later fractions, albeit with a slightly different pooling scheme of EV‐rich fractions. Our data from 500 µL columns showed that suboptimal pooling may increase the presence of non‐EV contaminants, including lipoproteins and plasma proteins, in the EV‐rich fractions. Indeed, with 10 mL columns, the GO terms assigned to highly abundant clusters in fractions 4 and 5 were mainly lipoproteins. This emphasizes the requirement of analysing separate fractions in depth before pooling. Of note, several reports showed the presence of EVs in later fractions (Veerman et al.
2021
). We detected EV‐associated proteins in all analysed fractions, although they were clearly enriched in fractions 1 and 2 of the 35 nm column and fractions 1, 2, and 3 of the 70 nm column compared to other fractions. As a limitation of our study, we could not obtain sufficient material from 500 µL columns to analyse each fraction separately and confirm that the EV‐rich fractions follow the same scheme as in 10 mL columns. One possibility to increase the input material is to repeatedly load the plasma sample onto the 500 µL column (Veerman et al.
2021
). However, this requires more hands‐on time, as it equals multiple isolations, and several washing steps must be carried out between the loadings. Alternatively, concentration of a larger sample volume by ultrafiltration is usually performed when isolating EVs from cell culture supernatant; however, this is not recommended for plasma or serum samples due to the high density of the biofluid.
NTA showed that the 35 nm columns isolated more particles compared to the 70 nm independent of the starting volume. It has been proposed that a higher particle‐to‐protein ratio would suggest a purer EV preparation (Webber and Clayton
2013
). Indeed, Ter‐Ovanesyan et al. (
2021
) concluded that the 35 nm column isolates EVs with higher purity than the 70 nm column, based on the ratio of EVs to albumin. However, due to their similarity in size, EVs and lipoproteins cannot always be distinguished by NTA, and high lipoprotein contamination can contribute to high particle counts (Zhang et al.
2022
). To further understand EV enrichment versus lipoprotein contamination between the two pore sizes, we performed orthogonal methods, including mass spectrometry and flow cytometry. Together, our data showed that more EVs and fewer plasma proteins are enriched with the 70 nm pore size. Additionally, we found that the 70 nm columns show higher donor variability, which is in line with previous findings (Veerman et al.
2021
).
Flow cytometry of EVs is a promising technique, allowing for measurement of concentration and quantification of subpopulations expressing certain surface markers, but it comes with many challenges. While cells are big enough to exceed the background noise, the signal from EVs partially overlaps. Only EVs above the limit of detection of the instrument can be characterized (Welsh et al.
2023
). By calibrating the side scatter axis according to de Rond et al. (
2018
), we found that the limit of detection of the Sony ID7000 is 550 nm (diameter). Considering that the typical size distribution finds most EVs to be smaller than 200 nm, and 95% smaller than 500 nm, which is in line with our NTA data, we conclude that the detergent‐sensitive CD81+ particles detected make up only a fraction of the total EVs isolated with SEC (Arraud et al.
2014
). However, Van Der Pol et al. (
2018
) suggested that there is still utility for flow cytometers that can detect EVs in the size range of 600 to 1200 nm. An alternative approach for the analysis of small EVs is the commercially available semi‐quantitative MACSPlex kit for EVs, which consists of 37 surface markers. Despite losing the ability of single EV detection, which is an advantage of flow cytometry, this panel offers a thorough characterization with little input material (Welsh et al.
2022
). This allowed us to determine the surface marker profile of EVs enriched with different columns. Previous studies have shown that plasma EVs carry immune cell‐derived markers, making them a rich source of biomarkers for immune‐mediated diseases (Vallejo et al.
2023
; Holcar et al.
2025
). We identified cell‐specific proteins originating from platelets, B cells, T cells, monocytes, stem cells, and endothelial cells, and we showed that these proteins were more abundant in EVs enriched with the 70 nm column. Moreover, both MACSplex and LC‐MS/MS data showed that the fractions 1–3 obtained with the 70 nm column were particularly enriched in cell‐specific markers, which is of relevance for biomarker discovery. While we cannot entirely explain the distinct composition of EVs isolated with the columns of different pore sizes, we speculate that pore size variations may differentially retain or elute EV subpopulations—perhaps favouring immune‐derived EVs in the 70 nm format—though further investigation is warranted.
Platelet activation during freeze‐thawing can lead to increased production of EVs and/or induce platelet fragmentation, with the fragments being indistinguishable from EVs (Bracht et al.
2023
; Zhang et al.
2022
; Coumans et al.
2017
). Indeed, it was recently shown that EVs isolated from frozen plasma have a higher contamination of ex vivo‐generated EVs (Małys et al.
2023
). Often, haematology analysers are used to assess platelet contamination in plasma prior to storage or EV isolation; however, their limit of detection is between 10
6
and 10
7
platelets/mL (Bracht et al.
2023
). Therefore, we used the more sensitive flow cytometry approach to assess the efficiency of platelet removal before EV isolation. The commonly used protocol for the generation of platelet‐free plasma (Coumans et al.
2017
), consisting of two‐step centrifugation at 2.500 ×
g
, does not ensure the complete removal of platelets (Bettin et al.
2022
). Therefore, to limit the contribution of platelets to the isolated EV pool, we included a filtration step to completely remove platelets and performed the EV isolation directly on fresh platelet‐free plasma.
Using MACSPlex analysis, the EV and platelet‐associated markers CD9, CD41b, and CD42a could be detected with higher abundance in the first fractions independent of pore size. This pattern is expected due to the high abundance of platelet‐derived EVs in the blood circulation (Zhang et al.
2022
; Ferreira et al.
2020
; Li et al.
2020
). CD62P, which is uniquely expressed on activated platelets, was less abundant, indicating that our isolation protocol does not induce platelet activation (Małys et al.
2023
). Additionally, CD31, as suggested by Małys et al. (
2023
) to be expressed by ex vivo generated EVs from platelets, was also low. This is in line with the data from CD62P, suggesting that the levels of platelet activation induced by our isolation protocol were negligible.
In conclusion, 35 and 70 nm Izon columns enrich for plasma EVs with different yield, purity, and composition. Our results highlight the importance of examining individual SEC fractions prior to pooling the EV‐rich fractions. The pooling of fractions 1–3 from the 70 nm column results in the enrichment of EVs with cell‐specific markers, suitable for biomarker studies in immune‐mediated diseases.
