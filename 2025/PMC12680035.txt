INTRODUCTION
Osteoarthritis (OA) is the most common chronic degenerative joint disease among elderly populations, and the global prevalence is continuously increasing due to aging demographics and other contributing factors. To date, more than 250 million people worldwide are estimated to suffer from OA (
1
). This condition is characterized by the continuous degeneration of cartilage and severe pain, with a high risk of disability. Since the articular cartilage is avascular and aneural, its self-repairing and regenerating ability is extremely limited. Although symptoms can be managed, it is impossible to cease or reverse the disease process with current approaches for OA. Therefore, OA is a widely acknowledged therapeutic obstacle in orthopedics and sports medicine.
Lipids are widely recognized as critical regulators of cartilage structure and function. They serve as fundamental constituents of cellular and organelle membranes, where they modulate membrane fluidity, facilitate signal transduction, and support adaptive responses to cellular stress. Beyond their metabolic roles, lipids act as precursors for a range of bioactive mediators, including arachidonic acid (AA) derivatives and specialized proresolving mediators (SPMs), which play pivotal roles in modulating inflammation (
2
,
3
). Furthermore, recent studies by Ramos
et al
(
4
). have identified a distinct “lipocartilage” phenotype characterized by lipochondrocytes enriched with stable intracellular lipid vacuoles (
4
,
5
). These lipid reserves are exceptionally stable, remaining largely insulated from systemic metabolic fluctuations, and have been highlighted as essential for maintaining cartilage biomechanical integrity. Presently, abnormalities in lipid metabolic disorders are observed to exert a considerable influence on the initiation and development of OA (
6
–
8
). On the one hand, abnormalities in lipid metabolism interconnectedly interact with systemic factors that highly contribute to OA, such as obesity (
9
). On the other hand, increasing research underlined the crucial role of lipids both in the physiology of cartilage and in OA pathology (
10
–
12
). The aberrant accumulation of lipids in chondrocytes promotes the progression of OA through the induction of cellular dysfunction and inflammation (
13
). Therefore, modulating lipid metabolism might offer a promising therapeutic strategy for OA. Notably, recent findings suggest that intra-articular adipose tissue and adipocytes in OA joints could facilitate the degeneration of cartilage by influencing lipid metabolism in chondrocytes (
14
,
15
), which indicates a solution of direct intra-articular injection of specific lipid metabolites or regulatory factors to modulate chondrocyte lipid metabolism effectively.
However, the delivery of lipids or lipid pharmaceuticals to chondrocytes within the aqueous environment of synovial fluid is highly impeded. Lipids in the form of small molecules, when injected locally, are rapidly cleared by the high fluid turnover in the joint cavity. Because of the nanoscale size, excellent biocompatibility, and low immunogenicity, exosomes have received considerable attention as drug delivery vehicles. The phospholipid bilayer of exosomes is regarded as an optimal loading site for lipid pharmaceuticals. More recently, an attractive idea has been introduced: Cells release lipid-rich exosomes as a source of lipids for the acceptor cells, which are termed “AdExos” (
16
). This phenomenon suggests that the exosomes serve as an intrinsic pathway in local lipids release in vivo, which further supports the development of exosome-based delivery for lipid pharmaceuticals. For OA, exosomes derived from bone marrow–derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) have been extensively validated for their therapeutic efficacy by modulating intercellular communication to promote cartilage repair, suppress synovitis, and regulate subchondral bone remodeling (
17
,
18
).
Despite the favorable prospects of exosomes as in vivo vectors for lipid pharmaceuticals (
19
–
23
), the distinguished features of cartilage tissue limit the bioavailability and therapeutic efficacy of exosomes. First, in contrast to trauma-induced focal defects of the cartilage, the early OA lesions are not visible to the naked eye; hence, it is difficult to exclusively localize exosomes to the appropriate site, causing absorption indiscriminately by all intra-articular cells and decreasing delivery to OA chondrocytes. Another challenge is the physical barrier of the cartilage extracellular matrix (ECM): a dense three-dimensional network of collagen fibers. In this network, the glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) monomers of proteoglycans form densely packed, brush-like structures that limit the transport of exosomes. How to endow the exosomes with lesion-targeting and ECM penetration capability is one of the most urgent challenges. Previous studies in tumor drug delivery have demonstrated the feasibility of micelle-exosome assembly (Mic-Exo) (
24
–
26
). This inspired us to construct a responsive delivery of lipid pharmaceuticals for OA.
Micelles are stable nanostructures formed by the spontaneous self-assembly of amphiphilic monomers in solution once the critical micelle concentration is reached, driven by hydrophobic interactions and entropy-mediated free energy minimization (
27
,
28
). These structures, with a hydrophobic core and a hydrophilic shell or vice versa, have been widely used as therapeutic carriers. Compared with other types of nanocarriers, micelles have advantages in good tissue-penetrating ability, low toxicity, and controllable drug release (
29
). Modification of the exosomes with micelle materials has further advantages: (i) The hydrophilic shell will protect the exosomes from hydrolysis, opsonization, and degradation by enzymes and mask the surface markers to avoid recognition and endocytosis by normal chondrocytes. (ii) The alterations in exosome particle size following micelle modification are negligible, resulting in only a minor increase in resistance to penetration of ECM. (iii) Mic-Exo can be constructed by self-assembly, without disruption to the exosomal membrane (
24
). (iv) Micelles can be readily modified in various ways, including charge tuning by incorporating cationic lipids. Among them, 1, 2-dioleoyl-3-trimethylammonium-propane (DOTAP) is a well-studied cationic lipid commonly used in drug delivery applications and even as a transfection reagent in cell-based experiments (
30
,
31
)
This study intends to identify specific protective lipids or lipid pharmaceuticals for OA by lipidomics profiling of human cartilage and develop a charge-modified, responsive-penetrative Mic-Exo for targeted delivery in OA. The micelles are made up of monomers of an amphiphilic block copolymer linked through a peptide, GPLGVRG, which hydrolyzes responsively, thus yielding block separation and loss in amphiphilicity. Matrix metalloproteinase-13 (MMP13) at the lesion site promotes micelle disassembly away from the exosome surface, resulting in increased endocytosis of chondrocytes. The use of DOTAP confers positive charge modification to the Mic-Exo, which allows the exosome to penetrate the cartilage matrix, changing it from a barrier to a drug reservoir (
Fig. 1
).
Fig. 1.
Schematic illustration of the preparation and therapeutic mechanism of Mic-Exo.
The figure shows the chemical synthesis of PEG-GPLGVRG-DOPE. Mic-Exo comprises PEG-GPLGVRG-DOPE micelle and exosome, loads DHA, and forms a stable assembly. The Mic-Exo assembly is designed to effectively penetrate cartilage tissue via electrostatic interactions, ensuring efficient drug delivery. In a normal cartilage environment, the Mic-Exo assembly bypasses chondrocytes; however, in osteoarthritic (OA) cartilage, the Mic-Exo assembly undergoes responsive degradation, allowing exosomes and the internal docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) to be internalized by chondrocytes. DHA alleviates inflammation through the SPMs pathway while concurrently regulating lipolysis via GPR120-mediated signaling.

DHA as a lipid mediator in OA cartilage
Kaplan-Meier survival analysis showed that higher dietary intake of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) significantly delayed progression to arthroplasty (
P
< 0.05), while monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs) and saturated fatty acids (SFAs) had no significant effect (
Fig. 3A
). The hazard ratio for higher PUFA intake was 0.76, suggesting a protective effect in OA progression. On the other hand, no significant effect was observed in OA incidence (fig. S1). TAG breakdown is mediated by lipases such as adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL), hormone-sensitive lipase (HSL), and monoacylglycerol lipase (MGL), sequentially hydrolyzing TAG into glycerol and free fatty acids (
Fig. 3B
). Considering the dynamic interplay between TAG synthesis and FFA release in lipid metabolism, we aimed to investigate FFA, small molecules that hold promise as optimal lipid pharmaceuticals. Among FFA, FFA 22:6 [docosahexaenoic acid (DHA)] was markedly decreased in lesional cartilage compared to intact cartilage (average 12%,
P
< 0.05) (
Fig. 3C
), suggesting a potential protective role in OA pathology

DOTAP was incorporated into the PEG-GPLGVRG-DOPE system at varying molar ratios to enhance penetration, resulting in a progressive reversal of the zeta potential (from −37.6 to +20.1) and a slight increase in particle size (
Fig. 4E
). TEM revealed the morphology of exosomes, micelles, Mic-Exo, and DHA-loaded Mic-Exo (DHA/Mic-Exo), all exhibiting uniform spherical structures (
Fig. 4F
).
Modification of exosomes with micelles reversed surface charge and slightly increased particle size (about 40 nm) (
Fig. 4, G and I
). The incorporation of DHA into Mic-Exo had minimal impact on size and zeta potential. Fluorescence imaging confirmed the successful colocalization of micelles, exosomes, and DHA in DHA/Mic-Exo assembly (
Fig. 4H
). Electrophoresis experiments showed uniform migration, confirming the successful integration of multiple components (
Fig. 4K
). WB analysis verified the presence of exosomal markers in the Mic-Exo assembly (
Fig. 4J
).
Live/Dead cell staining of chondrocytes treated with DHA/Mic-Exo showed minimal cytotoxicity and high proportions of live cells over 72 hours (
Fig. 4L
). Hemolysis assays confirmed excellent biocompatibility (hemolysis rate <3% at the therapeutic concentration) (
Fig. 4M
and fig. S7). The stability of Mic-Exo particles was maintained for up to 96 hours when the micelle lipid-to-exosome ratio exceeded 10:1 (
Fig. 4N
). With an initial DHA concentration of 25 μM at 0 hours, Mic-Exo exhibited a more controlled, sustained cumulative release (39.7% at 6 hours; 88.4% at 72 hours) than exosomes alone (11.8% at 6 hours; 77.3% at 72 hours) (
Fig. 4O
).
Evaluation of chondrocyte responses to DHA/Mic-Exo treatment
Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) analysis showed that DHA/Mic-Exo downregulated catabolic markers (
MMP13
and
ADAMTS5
) and up-regulated anabolic markers (
SOX9
,
COL2
, and
ACAN
) compared to the interleukin-1β (IL-1β)–induced OA group (fig. S8A). Wound healing assays demonstrated enhanced chondrocyte migration with DHA/Mic-Exo treatment (fig. S8B), with a wound closure rate of 88.7% at 48 hours compared to 62.2% in the IL-1β group (fig. S8F). Flow cytometry revealed a lower apoptotic rate in the DHA/Mic-Exo group (15.0%) compared to the IL-1β group (28.1%) (fig. S8, C and D). Cell Counting Kit-8 assays showed robust chondrocyte proliferation with DHA/Mic-Exo treatment (fig. S8E).
Evaluation of the responsive capabilities of Mic-Exo
Histological analysis showed increased MMP13 expression in lesional cartilage compared to intact cartilage (
Fig. 5A
). Consistently, MMP13 enzyme activity, measured via Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) peptides (fig. S9), was higher in lesional cartilage (
Fig. 5B
). Dual fluorescent labeling was used to effectively track the colocalization and transport of both Mic-Exo and exosomes. Exosomes were first labeled with the red fluorescent dye Dil. Separately, micelles were labeled by incorporating distearoylphosphatidylethanolamine–fluorescein isothiocyanate (DSPE-FITC), a green fluorescent probe. The Mic-Exo was then formed by combining the labeled micelles with Dil-labeled exosomes, which resulted in dual-labeled Mic-Exo exhibiting both red and green fluorescence (merged as yellow) (
Fig. 5C
). To validate the activity of MMP13 in an in vitro OA model, we added a FRET peptide containing the GPLGVRG sequence into IL-1β–stimulated chondrocytes. The FRET peptide emitted a strong fluorescence signal, confirming enzymatic cleavage in response to inflammatory conditions. Moreover, this fluorescence was significantly suppressed upon the addition of an MMP inhibitor, demonstrating that the sequence undergoes specific enzymatic cleavage in the presence of MMPs activated by IL-1β stimulation (
Fig. 5D
). Fluorescence microscopy images demonstrated the responsive uptake capability of Mic-Exo (
Fig. 5, E to G
). Specifically, under normal conditions, Mic-Exo exhibited significantly reduced uptake compared to native Exo. However, in inflammatory conditions induced by IL-1β stimulation, the uptake of Mic-Exo returned to levels comparable to that of native Exo. To minimize the impact of individual cellular variability, both Mic-Exo and native exosomes were introduced simultaneously into chondrocytes. Under normal conditions, Mic-Exo showed lower internalization efficiency compared to native exosomes (11.0 versus 70.3 in dot count,
P
< 0.001). However, in an inflammatory environment, the uptake efficiency of Mic-Exo was similar to that of native exosomes (83.3 versus 77.7 in dot count,
P
> 0.05) (
Fig. 5, H to J
). These findings confirm that in an inflammatory setting, Mic-Exo achieves uptake efficiency comparable to native exosomes, demonstrating that the acquisition of responsiveness does not compromise its overall uptake potential. Results from the additional OA + MMP inhibitor group (fig. S10) demonstrated that the MMP inhibitor (marimastat) effectively suppressed Mic-Exo uptake by OA chondrocytes, further confirming that the observed selectivity was mediated by MMPs. Furthermore, fluorescence-labeled Mic-Exo or unmodified exosomes were injected into the knee joints of normal and OA model rats, and cryosection analysis revealed that Mic-Exo, penetrated into cartilage and preferentially accumulated in the medial tibial plateau of OA rats, demonstrating potential in vivo lesion selectivity (fig. S11).

The therapeutic effects of DHA/Mic-Exo in an OA rat model
The study used a stepwise analysis workflow to evaluate the effects of DHA/Mic-Exo on OA rats (fig. S18). One rat died during anesthesia, and one rat developed a grossly visible incisional infection 2 weeks postoperatively after suture-biting and resuturing. Both animals were excluded from further experiments according to our predefined criteria. Serum biochemical analyses showed no significant changes in liver and kidney function markers between DHA-treated and vehicle-treated rats, indicating no adverse effects on organ function from the treatments (fig. S19). Histopathological evaluation of rat visceral organs also showed no significant abnormalities after all treatments, indicating that both treatments were well-tolerated with no detectable organ toxicity (fig. S20).
The levels of rat serum triglycerides (TRIGLs), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol showed no significant differences among all experimental groups, indicating that localized DHA injections exert minimal effects on systemic lipid metabolism. Meanwhile, functional and behavioral assessments showed a trend toward improved knee function in the DHA/Mic-Exo group compared to the control groups, although the sample size was limited (fig. S21 and movie S1).
As shown in Safranin O staining (
Fig. 7A
and fig. S22), DHA/Mic-Exo treatment effectively preserved the structural integrity of the cartilage, with a noticeable reduction in cartilage degradation compared to the control group. Quantitative assessments (
Fig. 7B
) revealed a significant improvement compared with the control group at 3 months, including the Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) score (4.3 versus 20.7,
P
< 0.001), cartilage thickness (244.4 μm versus 12.0 μm,
P
< 0.001), and chondrocyte number (174.7 versus 73.7,
P
< 0.001).

Micro–computed tomography (Micro-CT) illustrate dynamic changes in subchondral bone architecture over time. Early-stage trabecular bone loss of OA is evident by the reduced density and sparsity of trabeculae. Micro-CT imaging revealed that the DHA/Mic-Exo treatment group exhibited more numerous and denser trabeculae, suggesting an alleviation of subchondral bone loss at 1 month (

These findings demonstrate the protective role of DHA/Mic-Exo in maintaining subchondral trabecular bone integrity, which is typically disrupted in OA due to pathological bone resorption and microarchitectural deterioration. Notably, some results from the rat cohort may be underpowered, as this part was primarily intended to provide methodological and data support for the subsequent in vivo beagle studies and was animal-sparing.

DISCUSSION
Although the repair and reconstruction of bone (
35
–
37
) and cartilage (
38
–
40
) have been extensively studied and have achieved substantial progress, the treatment OA remains highly challenging. This research highlights the potential role of DHA in OA therapy via human cartilage lipidomics analysis. To deliver the lipid pharmaceuticals in an efficient and target way, we have first developed OA-responsive drug release system with Mic-Exo, which also has enhanced cartilage matrix penetration ability through positive-charge modification. We preliminarily demonstrated that DHA promoted inflammation resolution via SPMs pathway and activated lipolysis via GPR120-mediated PKC/PLIN5 pathway.
Accumulating evidence from previous studies has highlighted the beneficial role of DHA in the treatment of OA. Randomized controlled trials have demonstrated that fish oil significantly alleviates OA-specific pain and burden compared with untreated controls (
41
). A meta-analysis further indicated that supplementation with omega-3 PUFAs exerts beneficial effects on pain relief and joint function in patients with OA (
42
). Specifically, regarding DHA, oral administration in anterior cruciate ligament transection (ACLT) and destabilization of the medial meniscus (DMM)–induced OA rat models was shown to promote chondrocyte proliferation and reduce apoptosis (
43
). Moreover, studies in canines revealed that daily DHA supplementation significantly improved clinical symptoms of OA, including discomfort, lameness, and joint severity (
44
). Given the acidic pH of the stomach and the complexity of systemic metabolism, orally administered DHA is unlikely to effectively accumulate in OA-affected joints, potentially failing to reach therapeutic concentrations. In contrast, intra-articular injection of DHA offers potential advantages, including enhanced local targeting, direct action at the lesion site, reduced systemic side effects, and avoidance of first-pass metabolism.
Up to now, most of the previous works have investigated MMP-responsive micelles or micelle-modified liposomes/exosomes for rheumatoid arthritis (
45
–
48
) and cancer (
49
–
51
) treatment, while relatively few attempts were taken when applying MMP-responsive micelles to OA drug carriers, except for theranostic probes (
52
,
53
). Lan
et al.
(
52
) constructed a specific responsive platform, MRC-PPL@PSO, which was cleaved in the presence of MMP13 to release Cy5.5 with fluorescence emission, revealing arthritis conditions. Chen
et al.
(
53
) designed a ferritin nanocage modified with FRET-MSP, capable of emitting fluorescence related to MMP13 activity. In the above two systems, however, drug release was independent of or minimally affected by probe responsiveness. Our study showed that there was a significant difference in the activity of the MMP13 enzyme between lesional and intact cartilage in OA, and targeted drug delivery to lesional OA cartilage could be achieved with the MMP-responsive Mic-Exo. Existing cartilage-targeting strategies—such as electrostatic interactions, chondrocyte-affinity peptides, and type II collagen-binding peptides—lack this discriminatory capability. Conceptually, Purcell
et al.
(
54
) demonstrated an injectable hydrogel containing MMP-cleavable peptide crosslinkers that selectively degraded in postinfarction myocardium, which inspires our Mic-Exo design.
Strategies to enhance penetration include cartilage-targeting peptide modification (
55
,
56
), the use of neutrophil-derived exosomes (
57
), and surface modification with positive charges (
58
,
59
). The drug delivery within positive exosomes is justified because negatively charged GAGs are highly dense within the cartilage matrix. It has been shown that the uptake of cationic drugs or carriers within cartilage is 100 to 400 higher than neutral drugs (
60
). For exosomes, positively charged modifications can enhance delivery efficiency by several folds (
58
,
59
,
61
,
62
). Vegadhaghavami
et al.
(
60
) prepared cationic carriers of various net charges for the delivery of therapeutic agents and studied their transport by electrophoresis in healthy and arthritic cartilage. As illustrated by their results, the uptake increased with increasing net charge up to +14, but further positive charge reduced transport due to the fact that while strong binding enhanced retention, it reduced movement. In our study, charge reversal after the incorporation of DOTAP into the Mic-Exo improved the penetration efficiency. As structural and morphological differences are great between small animal and human cartilage, we confirmed the penetration in both rat and human cartilage. The positively charged modification resulted in about a threefold increase in exosome penetration efficiency in rat cartilage and a twofold increase in human cartilage at 72 hours. Unmodified exosomes typically infiltrate cartilage explants to depths of only 30 to 40 μm (
63
). With cationic modification, our Mic-Exo achieved millimeter-scale penetration, comparable to reported phosphatidylethanolamine-modified extracellular vesicles by Feng
et al.
(
58
). For another comparison, avidin—despite having a small size of only a few nanometers—penetrates only about 1 mm into cartilage (
64
,
65
). Thus, the penetration depth achieved in our study can be considered particularly encouraging.
We hypothesize that DHA stimulates the PKC/PPAR pathway through GPR120, which increases the expression of PLIN5 and hence promotes lipolysis. GPR120 is a major receptor for DHA and exerts significant anti-inflammatory effects in various conditions, such as neuroinflammation (
66
), respiratory inflammation (
67
), and liver inflammation (
68
). Previous research has also identified GPR120 as a critical therapeutic target in OA. Xu
et al.
(
69
) established that agonists of GPR120 protect chondrocytes from inflammatory-elicited destruction propagated by agents such as IL-1β by inhibiting the breakdown of the components of ECM, type II collagen and aggrecan. Traumatic OA was further accelerated in studies in
GPR120
knockout mice, further evidence for the protective role GPR 120 plays in OA (
70
). The current study identified a marked reduction of GPR120 expression within the cartilage of both patients with OA and OA rat models. In addition, DHA application was confirmed to markedly promote GPR120 expression in chondrocyte and animal models. PLIN5 is a lipid droplet–associated protein that has recently garnered considerable attention for its dual role in lipid storage and mobilization. It facilitates the physical and metabolic coupling between lipid droplets and mitochondria, thereby promoting the efficient channeling of liberated fatty acids into mitochondrial β-oxidation and preventing lipotoxic accumulation (
71
). Upon phosphorylation, PLIN5 enhances the recruitment of adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL) and its coactivator comparative gene identification-58 (CGI-58) to the lipid droplet surface, thereby accelerating triglyceride hydrolysis and initiating lipolysis (
72
).
SPMs exert their effects not only through classical anti-inflammatory mechanisms, such as suppressing the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and tumor necrosis factor–α, but also by reprogramming immune cell function. Specifically, they promote M2 macrophage polarization, enhance the clearance of apoptotic cells (efferocytosis), and limit the spread of inflammation, thereby facilitating the restoration of tissue homeostasis (
73
). Wang
et al.
(
74
) reported that RvD1 alleviated cartilage degeneration and osteophyte formation in the OA model of Sprague-Dawley rats. Another potential mechanism by which DHA exerts its anti-inflammatory effects is through competition for shared enzymes such as LOXs with the AA metabolic pathway (
75
,
76
), which is one of the most well-characterized proinflammatory cascades involved in OA pathogenesis (
77
–
79
). This competition for LOX isoforms, including 5-LOX and 12/15-LOX, may shift lipid mediator production from proinflammatory eicosanoids, such as leukotrienes and prostaglandins.
This study has several limitations. First, rats were euthanized at three different time points to assess potential temporal effects, resulting in only a few animals per group per time point. This limited sample size at each time point reduces the statistical power and may increase the risk of type II errors, particularly for behavioral outcomes, which are inherently more variable. Further studies with larger cohorts and more comprehensive functional assessments are warranted to validate these preliminary findings. Second, vascular remodeling is an important factor to consider in tissue repair (
80
). Our study did not fully account for the continuous lipid diffusion or exchange mediated by subchondral bone vasculature, which may limit the regulation of cartilage lipid metabolism. Third, the MMP-sensitive peptide GPLGVRG may be cleaved by multiple MMP family members due to the inherent cross-reactivity among MMPs. Although structural analyses suggest that the variable S1′ pocket of each MMP could be exploited to develop more selective substrates (
81
,
82
), achieving absolute specificity for a single MMP remains unfeasible at present and warrants further investigation. Fourth, although this study was designed and interpreted on the premise that DHA exerts therapeutic effects mainly on chondrocytes, other effects of the therapy may also exist, particularly on bone metabolism. For example, CT scans in beagles revealed aberrant femoral condylar phenotypes, which could be attributable to DHA’s actions on osteoclasts (
83
,
84
) and osteoblasts (
85
).
In the present study, an effective treatment against OA was developed from the aspect of lipid metabolism by constructing an exosome-based delivery system for lipid pharmaceuticals. The employment of positively charged responsive micelles could enable deeper penetration into the cartilage matrix and specific delivery to the diseased cells. The self-assembly procedure of this system is relatively straightforward and can be readily adapted for various lipophilic small molecules, suggesting promising potential for broader applications in intracartilage drug delivery in the future.
MATERIALS AND METHODS
Lipid metabolomics analysis
Cartilage was obtained from the tibial plateau of patients with OA receiving total knee arthroplasty (TKA). This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University, with ethical registration numbers 2024 (2598). Informed written consent from all participants was obtained before the research. After the pretreatment and preparation of the samples, separation was performed on the liquid chromatography system to optimize the isolation of various lipid molecules (HPLC System - Shimadzu LC-30 AD; Column: Phenomenex Kinetex C18 2.6 μm, 2.1 mm by 100 mm. The separated samples were subsequently measured by a Q-Trap 5500 mass spectrometer (AB Sciex, Framingham, USA) in the positive or negative ion mode by collecting multiple reaction monitoring data for highly sensitive quantification of the particular lipid molecules. Data were processed and quantitatively analyzed using MultiQuant 3.0.3. For detailed protocol and parameters and donor information, see the Supplementary Materials and table S1.
Survival analysis
This analysis uses knee replacement data provided by the OAI database at
www.niams.nih.gov/grants-funding/funded-research/osteoarthritis-initiative
to compare survival time curves of knee replacement between different levels of intake of fatty acid. OAI database is one of the largest and most comprehensive publicly available longitudinal cohorts dedicated to knee OA, comprising more than 4700 participants across multiple centers. In the analysis, participants were divided into high and low-intake groups according to their intake level. The Kaplan-Meier method has been applied to plot the survival curves, while the log-rank test has been used for testing differences among groups.
Chondrocyte culture
Immediately after the TKA surgeries, the cartilage tissues were immersed in sterile cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and stored at 4°C to be transported within an hour to the laboratory. Under sterile conditions, the particular cartilage tissues were minced into small fragments (1 to 2 mm
3
) and treated with a solution of 0.25% trypsin at 37°C for 30 min to remove superficial impurities. The tissue was then transferred into high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (pH 7.4), containing 0.2% type II collagenase, and was digested further in a 5% CO
2
incubator at 37°C for 12 to 16 hours until it was fully dissolved into a single-cell suspension. The suspension was filtered through a 70-μm cell strainer into a collection tube to remove the undigested tissue debris and then centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min to collect the cells. The cells were resuspended in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin and seeded in 5 ml per 25 cm
2
culture flask. Cultures were maintained at 37°C in a 5% CO
2
atmosphere. The medium was exchanged after 24 hours to discard nonadherent cells, and further medium replacement was done every 2 days. When the cell confluence reached 80 to 90%, a detachment was done using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA for 2 to 3 min, with light tapping on the flask side to detach the cells. The enzymatic activity was neutralized by adding 10 ml of DMEM with FBS to it, and centrifugation at 1000 rpm for 5 min ensued. The obtained cell pellets were then lentivirally expanded at a 1:3 passage and expanded up to the second or third passage to assure their proliferative capacity and phenotype stability for experimentation (
86
).
Isolation and culture of rat BMSCs
The bone marrow was harvested from the femurs and tibias of 4-week-old Sprague-Dawley rats under sterile conditions. After removing the epiphyses, the marrow cavity was flushed with α-minimum essential medium (αMEM; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing 1% penicillin-streptomycin. The cell suspension was filtered through a 70-μm cell strainer and centrifuged at 300
g
for 5 min. The cell pellet was resuspended and seeded in αMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and cultured at 37°C in a 5% CO
2
incubator. Nonadherent cells were removed after 24 hours, and the medium was refreshed every 2 to 3 days. Cells were passaged upon reaching 80 to 90% confluence.
Characterization of BMSCs
For trilineage differentiation, cells were induced using osteogenic, adipogenic, and chondrogenic differentiation media (Cyagen Biosciences, Suzhou, China) following the manufacturer’s protocols. Osteogenic and adipogenic induction were performed in monolayer cultures for 21 and 14 days, respectively, while chondrogenic differentiation was conducted in pellet culture (2 × 10
5
cells per pellet) for 21 days. Medium were refreshed every 3 days. Surface markers were assessed by flow cytometry: BMSCs were stained with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies against CD90, CD105, CD73, CD34, and CD45 and analyzed with a flow cytometer after gating at least 10,000 events per sample.
Isolation and characterization of BMSC-derived exosomes
BMSCs at 80 to 90% confluence were cultured in αMEM with 10% exosome-depleted FBS for 48 hours. The conditioned medium was collected and sequentially centrifuged at 300
g
(10 min), 2000
g
(20 min), and 10,000
g
(30 min) to remove cells and debris, followed by ultracentrifugation at 100,000
g
(70 min, 4°C) twice. The exosome pellet was resuspended in PBS and stored at −80°C. TEM was performed to observe morphology after uranyl acetate negative staining. Nanoparticle tracking analysis was conducted using the ZetaView PMX110 system (Particle Metrix, Germany) to assess size and concentration under standard conditions. WB was performed to detect exosomal markers CD9, CD63, and TSG101, with glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) as a control.
DHA ELISA assay
DHA levels were assessed by the DHA ELISA Kit (ELK Biotechnology, Wuhan, China), and 50 μl of sample and biotin conjugate were to each well, incubated at 37°C for 1 hour, then discarded, washed, and repeated with streptavidin–horseradish peroxidase. Following a second incubation, sample was washed, and 90 μl of TMB substrate was added to develop color at 37°C for 15 to 20 min. The reaction was stopped with 50 μl of stop solution, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm within 5 min.
Peptide synthesis
GPLGVRG and (Mca)-GPLGVRGC-(Dnp) peptides were prepared by the Fmoc solid-phase synthesis method. Detailed procedures are available in the Supplementary Materials. The synthesis of standard peptides, FRET peptides, and pegylated peptides was made with the assistance of QyaoBio (Shanghai, China).
Synthesis of PEG-GPLGVRG-DOPE
PEG-GPLGVRG-DOPE was synthesized as follows: PEG-2000–GPLGVRG was activated by an excess of DCC/HOBt (Adamas, Shanghai) in the presence of trace triethylamine. This activated compound was then made to react with DOPE (Adamas, Shanghai) in chloroform at a molar ratio of ~1:1.2, allowing the reaction to proceed overnight at room temperature. The product was subsequently diafiltered through a 2000-kDa molecular weight cutoff membrane Repligen, Waltham, to remove unreacted reagents and small-molecule by-products following the reaction. The resulting PEG-GPLGVRG-DOPE was freeze-dried to a white powder.
Assembly of Mic-Exo
PEG-GPLGVRG-DOPE and DOTAP were dissolved in PBS at a lipid mass ratio of 1:1 and sonicated at room temperature until fully dissolved and micelle formation was achieved. The micelles were then mixed with purified exosomes at a mass ratio of 10:1 (micelle:exosome protein) and incubated at 37°C for 2 hours (in the presence of abundant DHA, for DHA loading) (
24
,
26
). After incubation, unbound micelles were removed by ultrafiltration using a 30–kDa molecular weight cutoff membrane (Millipore) (
87
).
FTIR spectroscopy
The synthesis of PEG-GPLGVRG-DOPE was confirmed by Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy within the wave number region of 4000 to 400 cm
−1
. Individual samples of DOPE, PEG-GPLGVRG, and PEG-GPLGVRG-DOPE were prepared to investigate the shifting of characteristic peaks. Measurements were carried out in the solid state by the KBr pellet method. Spectra were recorded from the FTIR; baseline correction and peak analysis were performed using software to confirm successful conjugation and a chemical linkage between the components.
Nuclear magnetic resonance
Samples were dissolved with the help of appropriate solvents, D
2
O or CDCl
3
, and then transferred into NMR tubes. The chemical shifts of hydrogen in the target molecule were identified by making measurements of
1
H NMR at 400 MHz. Each measurement was run for at least 16 scans to enhance the signal-to-noise ratio. From the comparison of spectra of PEG, GPLGVRG, DOPE, and product PEG-GPLGVRG-DOPE, the confirmation of successful coupling maintaining the structural integrity for each of its components was established.
Transmission electron microscopy
TEM was used to characterize the morphology of exosomes, PEG-GPLGVRG-DOPE micelles, and their assembly. Each sample was prepared as a suspension at an appropriate concentration and dropped onto a carbon-coated copper grid. The sample was left standing at room temperature for 1 to 2 min, after which the excess liquid was removed with filter paper. The samples were then negatively stained with either 2% phosphotungstic acid (pH 7.0) or 1% uranyl oxalate for 30 s to 1 min. The excess stain was blotted, and the samples were air-dried. Images were captured by TEM at an accelerating voltage of 80 to 120 kV.
Particle size and zeta potential measurement
The size and charge of the particles were determined using Nicomp 380 ZLS DLS (Billerica, USA). Measurement samples were diluted in deionized water, mixed, and then loaded into disposable cuvettes. All measurements were carried out at 25°C with a 90° scattering angle, and the data obtained were calculated on the basis of the Stokes-Einstein equation. The zeta potential was measured in an electrophoretic light scattering (phase analysis light scattering) mode.
Fluorescence colocalization
To visualize the colocalization between DHA, PEG-GPLGVRG-DOPE micelles, and exosomes, multichannel imaging was performed. Exosomes were stained with DiI (red fluorescence, Yeasen, Shanghai), while micelles were labeled with DSPE–PEG-2000–FITC (green fluorescence, Ruixi, Xi’an). DHA was stained with DiB (blue fluorescence, Biotium, CA). After staining, the samples were assembled and placed on slides, and the signals of the red (exosomes), green (micelles), and blue (DHA) channels were separately collected using a fluorescence microscope (
87
). For electrophoresis, separated and assembled samples were loaded into different lanes on a polyacrylamide gel and run at 120 V for 30 min. Fluorescent signals in each channel were captured using a chemiluminescent imaging system (
87
).
Western blot
Proteins separated according to molecular weight by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis were transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane. Membranes were blocked after transfer in a blocking solution composed of 5% nonfat milk to avoid nonspecific binding. Specific primary antibodies against target proteins were added, followed by washing and incubation with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies. Target proteins were detected by chemiluminescent, and images were quantitatively analyzed by ImageJ (for antibody information, see table S2).
Live/Dead cell staining assay
The cytocompatibility of Mic-Exo was determined by a Live/Dead cell staining assay on chondrocytes. Chondrocytes were seeded in culture plates and thereafter treated with a Mic-Exo. After different time points, the cells were stained with green fluorescent calcein-AM, which marked living cells, and propidium iodide (PI), a red fluorescent indicator of dead cells. Images were taken by means of a fluorescence microscope.
Hemolysis assay
To evaluate the blood compatibility of the Mic-Exo, a hemolysis assay was performed. A Mic-Exo was previously prepared at different concentrations. It was mixed with a red blood cell suspension and subsequently incubated at 37°C for 1 hour. At the end of the incubation, the samples were centrifuged at 12,000
g
for 5 min to collect the supernatant. Then, the supernatant was read for its absorbance at 540 nm. PBS was used as a negative control, while distilled water acted as a positive control. Hemolysis rates were calculated to acquire the blood compatibility of the Mic-Exo.
DHA release profile
To investigate the release characteristics and rate of DHA, DHA/Mic-Exo was dispersed in PBS buffer with gentle agitation at 37°C. At designed time points, samples were collected and the particles were removed by ultrafiltration. Then, adding an equal volume of PBS into the system was conducted after each sampling to keep the volume consistent. The concentrations of DHA at each point were measured by using ELISA. The cumulative release curve was plotted with the data on concentration versus time.
DHA dosage
In the in vitro experiments, chondrocytes were seeded into culture dishes and cultured to 50% confluence. The negative control (NC) group received no treatment, while the OA control group was stimulated with IL-1β (10 ng/ml). The DHA, DHA/exo, and DHA/Mic-Exo groups were treated with the corresponding interventions on OA chondrocytes, with the DHA concentration uniformly maintained at 25 μM. In the in vivo experiments, a 50-μl dose containing 250 μM DHA was administered into the rat knee joint and a 1-ml dose containing 250 μM DHA was for beagle knee joint.
Quantitative PCR
Total RNA was extracted from the treated chondrocytes. The total RNA was then reversed into cDNA using a reverse transcription kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Vazyme, R323-01). Last, the expression of target genes was analyzed by real-time qPCR. The qPCR reaction mixture contained cDNA templates, specific primers, and SYBR Green dye (Vazyme, Q711-02/03). Amplification was completed in a real-time PCR system, and each sample was performed in triplicate to ensure the accuracy of the results. Data were analyzed using the 2
−ΔΔCt
method to calculate relative levels of gene expression among different groups (for primer information, see table S3).
Scratch assay
The cells were seeded in a six-well plate and cultured until a confluent monolayer was achieved. A straight-line scratch wound was created in the center of the cell layer using a sterile pipette tip. The detached cells were washed with PBS very gently, followed by the addition of serum-free medium and specific treatments based on grouping. The cells were incubated at 37°C under a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO
2
. Images of the scratch area were captured at selected time points such as 0, 24, and 48 hours. Scratch closure rates were measured using image analysis software.
Apoptosis detection
The annexin V–FITC/PI double staining was used for the evaluation of chondrocyte apoptosis. Collected cells from treatments were washed twice by PBS and resuspended in a binding buffer. Annexin V-FITC/PI dyes were added to the cell suspension and incubated in the dark for 15 min. After staining, the fluorescent signal from FITC and PI was detected by a flow cytometer to separate the early apoptotic, late apoptotic, and necrotic populations by different fluorescence intensities. The obtained data were analyzed with CytExpert software for the calculation of apoptosis rates.
Immunohistochemistry
For decalcification, the fixed cartilage tissues were transferred into a 10% EDTA decalcification solution and kept gently rocking at room temperature. The decalcification solution was changed every 2 weeks until the tissues turned soft. Following this, the decalcified samples underwent dehydration, embedding in paraffin, and sectioning at 5 μm in thickness. Sections were further deparaffinized and then processed for antigen retrieval, blocking of endogenous enzymes, and blocking by using an appropriate blocking agent followed by incubation with primary antibodies. The secondary antibody staining was followed by 3,3′-Diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining for chromogenic detection, section counterstaining with hematoxylin, and microscopic evaluation.
Safranin O/fast green staining
The cartilage tissue sections were deparaffinized to water. From here, the sections were treated with safranin O solution to visualize acidic GAGs in the cartilage matrix. Subsequently, fast green staining highlighted the collagen fibers and other structures within the tissue. Following the staining steps, a series of graded ethanol dehydrated the sections. After that, the sections were cleared in xylene. Last, the sections were mounted with a coverslip for microscopic examination.
MMP13 enzyme activity assay
The activity of the MMP13 enzyme was determined using a FRET peptide substrate: Mca- GPLGVRGC-DAPDnp, wherein the detailed steps of synthesis can be seen in the Supplementary Materials. Different experimental groups were established, such as normal cartilage, diseased cartilage, positive and blank control, and corresponding cartilage tissue homogenate supernatant or other relevant samples will be added to each group, followed by the addition of FRET peptide substrate. All the samples were incubated at 37°C in the dark, while emission fluorescence signals were recorded at 390 nm upon excitation at 330 nm using a fluorescent plate reader. At specific time points thereafter, the MMP inhibitor marimastat was added.
Uptake efficiency of Mic-Exo in normal/OA chondrocytes
Chondrocytes seeded into culture dishes were grown to 50% confluence, and the IL-1β–treated group was stimulated by IL-1β (10 ng/ml) for the onset of the inflammatory environment, with the control group receiving no treatment. Subsequently, each of the two groups was further divided into subgroups, and red fluorescently labeled exosomes and yellow (fused with green fluorescent micelles and the red fluorescent exosomes) fluorescently labeled Mic-Exo were added to the respective groups. After that, cells were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO
2

Uptake was assessed by microscopy and further quantified by ImageJ software based on the number and area of fluorescent signals.
To further eliminate interference from individual chondrocytes difference, red fluorescently labeled exosomes and yellow fluorescently labeled Mic-Exo in equivalent amounts were simultaneously applied to chondrocytes. Then, the uptake efficiency was quantified while following the procedures described above, thus allowing the comparison of uptake between exosome and Mic-Exo in identical chondrocytes.

Cryosection
Immediately after collection, tissue samples were directly embedded in the optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound so that the tissues were fully covered by the embedding medium. Then, the samples were quickly frozen in liquid nitrogen. Frozen tissues were cut into 5-μm-thick sections using a cryostat at −20°C and gently placed onto glass slides. OA rat model
This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University, with ethical registration numbers 20221019006 (rats) and 20240510003 (beagles). Male Sprague-Dawley rats, 12 weeks old and weighing 300 to 350 g, were housed under specific pathogen–free conditions with a 1-week acclimatization period at 22° ± 2°C, a 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle, and free access to food and water. A knee OA model was established by ACLT combined with DMM. The procedure is as follows: Rats were anesthetized under general anesthesia, and a median incision was made on the right knee joint under sterile conditions to expose the internal structures of the knee. The anterior cruciate ligament was first transected using micro-scissors to induce joint instability, followed by the transection of the meniscotibial ligament to further destabilize the joint. After surgery, the incision was sutured. Rats were randomly assigned to five groups: Sham, OA + PBS, OA + DHA, OA + DHA/Exo, and OA + DHA/Mic-Exo. For intra-articular injection, a 50-μl dose containing 250 μM DHA was administered into the knee joint for treatment groups.
Animals that died intraoperatively or developed infection were excluded and documented. All surgeries were performed under 2% isoflurane anesthesia. Treatment groups received intra-articular injections 15 days after surgery. Behavioral tests (von Frey, rotarod, gait analysis, and open field test) and morphological assessments (Micro-CT, safranin O/fast green staining, and immunohistochemistry) were performed at designated time points (
n
= 3 per group per time point; fig. S18) to evaluate pain, joint function, and cartilage structure. Cartilage and subchondral bone within the central load-bearing region of the medial tibial plateau were selected for morphological assessments. Considering that small-animal cartilage differs substantially from human cartilage, particularly in thickness, the small rat cohort was intentionally used for preliminary exploration, primarily to inform and support the subsequent beagle studies.
OA canine model
Male and female beagles, 12 to 15 months old and weighing 8 to 12 kg (OA) model, were created by ACLT. Beagles were randomly assigned to four groups: Sham, OA + PBS, OA + DHA/Exo, and OA + DHA/Mic-Exo, with five animals per group. Intra-articular injections of PBS or a 1 ml of solution containing 250 μM DHA was administered at 30, 60, and 90 days postsurgery as indicated (
Fig. 8A
). Behavioral (gait and pain), morphological (radiography, MRI, and histology), and biochemical (ELISA) evaluation were performed at 120 days to assess pain, joint function, and cartilage integrity. Cartilage and subchondral bone within the central load-bearing region of the medial tibial plateau were selected for morphological assessments.
In vivo imaging
The rats were then anesthetized by inhalation of isoflurane. This was followed by the intra-articular administration of equivalent CY7-labeled lipids, alone or within different vectors, using a sterile syringe. In vivo imaging after injection: Anesthetized rats were placed onto an imaging stage of the in vivo imaging system. Anesthesia was maintained throughout all procedures. The imaging system was set at an excitation wavelength of 750 nm with an emission filter of 775 nm. Images were then imported into Aniview software after data acquisition for further analysis, calculating the average fluorescence intensity of region of interest (ROI) to quantify the distribution of the drug within the joint.
von Frey testing
To evaluate mechanical allodynia, von Frey testing was performed. Rats were placed in wire mesh cages for a 5-min acclimatization period, and the plantar surface of the operated limb was stimulated with a series of ascending von Frey filaments. The force threshold (g) eliciting a paw withdrawal response was recorded. Each animal was tested three times, and the mean value was used for analysis.
Open field test
Rats were placed in an open square arena of 100 cm by 100 cm, and the floor was divided with equally spaced grids and kept in quiet uniformly lit conditions. To minimize the stress responses, the rats were brought to the laboratory for acclimatization 30 min before testing. At the beginning of the test, the rat was placed in the center of the open field and considered free for 10 min from every outer disturbance. During this session, the movement patterns of rats were continuously recorded by a video recording system, including time spent in the center area, number of grid crossings, and wall-climbing behavior. The walking speed was analyzed by EthoVision XT 17. After each trial, the arena was cleaned with 75% ethanol to eliminate scent cues that could affect successive rats.
Gait analysis
The rats were then put on the gait analysis system in a quiet environment with controlled lighting to reduce distractions. Before actual testing, rats were allowed to become acclimatized to the walkway for a few minutes to reduce stress and allow them to adopt a natural gait. During the test, rats walked along the walkway, while the camera systems recorded the footprints in real time, with stride length, step width, contact area, walking speed, step frequency, and paw pressure calculated. After each session, the runway was wiped with 75% ethanol to remove lingering odors that could influence the behavior of the subsequent rats.
Histopathological evaluation
Histopathological evaluation was primarily conducted using the OARSI scoring system, which encompasses assessments of cartilage matrix loss, cartilage degeneration, osteophytes, calcified cartilage, subchondral bone damage, synovium, and related items (

Ex vivo Micro-CT
Ex vivo Micro-CT was performed on harvested knee joints. After reconstruction, a cylindrical ROI (1 mm in height, 1 mm in diameter for rats, 2 mm in height, 4 mm in diameter for beagles) was selected beneath the subchondral bone plate at the central load-bearing area of the medial tibial plateau. Trabecular bone mineral density was calculated by converting computed tomography (CT) values using a calibration phantom. Bone morphometric parameters—including BV/TV, Tb.Th, trabecular number, and trabecular separation—were quantified using the built-in morphometric analysis module of the micro-CT software.
Statistical analysis
Quantitative data were presented as means ± SD and analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.4.0. Comparisons between two groups were performed using the unpaired Student’s
t

U

When analyzing multiple groups across multiple time points, a two-way ANOVA was performed. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and group differences were evaluated with the log-rank test. Statistical significance was defined as
