INTRODUCTION
Alphavirus-induced neuropathies pose a significant global threat to pediatric, geriatric, and immunocompromised populations. Among the alphaviruses, eastern equine encephalitis virus (EEEV) and chikungunya virus (CHIKV) stand out as the deadliest and most widespread, respectively, both capable of causing severe neurological disorders in humans. While EEEV cases are rare, up to 50% of infections result in fatal encephalitis.
1
CHIKV is primarily studied for its arthritogenic effects, but it can also result in seizures and encephalitis.
2
,
3
Notably, CHIKV RNA has been detected in the cerebrospinal fluid of all fatal cases in one study, underscoring the role of neuroinvasion.
4
To establish infection in the central nervous system (CNS), alphaviruses rely on hematogenous dissemination and must bypass the blood-brain barrier (BBB).
5
Consequently, understanding the molecular alphavirus-BBB interactions is essential for developing countermeasures.
The BBB is the primary obstacle preventing alphaviruses from accessing the CNS.
6
–
8
It is composed of brain microvascular endothelial cells (BMECs) structurally supported by pericytes and astrocytes.
9
Crossing the BBB can occur via transcellular transport through BMECs, paracellular migration, or transport by immune cells.
10
,
11
However, alphaviruses appear to cross without relying on barrier disruption or immune cell-mediated carriage.
12
–
14
Indeed, BBB disruption increases neuroinvasion; however, it has been suggested to play a larger role in pathogenesis.
15
,
16
Other studies on Venezuelan equine encephalitis virus (VEEV) suggest that it exploits vesicle trafficking machinery in BMECs to achieve neuroinvasion.
12
Additionally, VEEV and western equine encephalitis virus (WEEV) can infect BMECs, pericytes, and astrocytes, though the contribution of each to neuroinvasion remains unclear.
Recent studies show that human pluripotent stem cell (hPSC)-derived brain microvascular endothelial-like cells (hPSC-BMELCs) accurately recapitulate the
in vivo
functionality of the BBB.
17
Additionally, hPSC-BMELCs share the distinct gene expression profiles of primary human BMECs
18
and can be further driven toward endothelial maturation with the introduction of specific transcription factors,
19
,
20
making the former a physiologically relevant model for investigating molecular interactions at the BBB. This model has been employed to investigate host BBB-pathogen interactions for malaria, group B strep, and severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2).
21
–
23
This model also recapitulates the
in vivo
neuroinvasive phenotypes of arthropod-borne viruses (arboviruses), and was used to identify the interferon (IFN)-stimulated gene (ISG) product IFITM1 as a BBB-specific restriction factor against flaviviruses.
24
Thus, such models are ideal for identifying host factors that affect neurotropism.
Historically, research into alphavirus neuroinvasion has concentrated on New World alphaviruses. However, Old World alphaviruses such as Sindbis virus (SINV), Semliki Forest virus (SFV), and CHIKV also infect the CNS.
2
,
25
,
26
CHIKV remains particularly understudied in this context due to the lack of
in vivo
models that faithfully replicate its neurological complications, with macaques being a notable exception.
27
SINV is widely used as a model because of the robust mouse models and relatively low biosafety level. This accessibility has led to valuable tools and strains for investigating SINV neuroinvasion. Serial passage of an SINV isolate in the brains of young mice resulted in a neurovirulent strain, named SVN, that was not neuroinvasive. SVN was further passaged to produce SVNI, which was both neurovirulent and neuroinvasive.
6
Subsequent work identified the genetic determinants of fatal SINV neuroinvasion in mice, yielding the recombinant strain, R47.
7
R47 has three key differences from SVN as follows: a mutation in the 5′ untranslated region (UTR) that is homologous to the nucleotide changes in VEEV that confer resistance to the antiviral ISG product, IFIT1,
28
and two amino acid changes in the E2 glycoprotein. While these mutations drive neuroinvasion, their functional consequences are unclear.
The alphavirus E2 glycoprotein mediates attachment, receptor engagement, and assembly.
5
,
29
,
30
Critical E2 residues determine neuroinvasion and pathogenicity
in vivo
for SINV and CHIKV.
7
,
31
Alphaviruses infect various cell types, driving interactions with several different host attachment factors and receptors, collectively called entry factors.
5
,
32
These include glycosaminoglycans, such as heparan sulfate, chondroitin sulfate, and various cell surface protein receptors in a lineage-specific manner.
32
Most Old World alphaviruses engage matrix remodeling-associated protein 8 (MXRA8) in a species-specific manner, while New World alphaviruses rely on several low-density lipoprotein receptors, including very low-density lipoprotein receptor (VLDLR), low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR), low-density lipoprotein receptor class A domain containing 3 (LDLRAD3), and LDLR-related protein 8 (LRP8, also named ApoER2).
32
–
37
Other receptors, including protocadherin 10 (PCDH10) and natural resistance-associated macrophage protein 2 (NRAMP2, also named SLC11A2), have also been identified.
38
,
39
Despite these advances in receptor identification, the cell type-specific relevance of these factors to alphavirus pathogenesis remains largely unexplored.
Here, we show that alphavirus neuroinvasion correlates with the ability to infect all BBB cell types. Specifically, neuroinvasive SINV residues enhance viral attachment, internalization, and fusion to BMECs, driving neuroinvasion. Further investigation revealed that R47 primarily relies on PCDH10, while SVN relies on NRAMP2, MXRA8, and VLDLR in addition to PCDH10. Moreover, we overlaid transcriptomic and proteomic datasets to identify potential receptor candidates exploited by R47 by searching for proteins uniquely abundant on brain endothelial cells compared to peripheral endothelial cells. Instead, we found LDLR-related protein 1 (LRP1) as a receptor candidate uniquely engaged by SVN. Overall, this establishes a mechanism where loss of receptor usage results in a more virulent strain. Finally, we demonstrate that other Old World alphaviruses, including CHIKV, infect BBB cells consistent with their neuroinvasive capacity. These findings illuminate critical alphavirus-BBB interactions and lay the groundwork for neuroinvasion-targeted countermeasures.
RESULTS
Neuroinvasive SINV efficiently infects cells of the BBB
The BBB is primarily composed of BMECs that are structurally supported by pericytes and astrocyte endfeet.
9
Infection of these cell types is well documented for some alphaviruses and flaviviruses,
12
,
40
,
41
yet how the infection of these cells contributes to neuroinvasion is less understood. Previous work demonstrated that the ability to infect hPSC-BMELCs is dependent on the neuroinvasive capacity of SINV.
24
To confirm that this is specific to endothelial cells in the brain, we investigated this differential infection phenotype across various endothelial cell models. We first generated hPSC-BMELCs, which express the proper endothelial cell markers such as platelet endothelial cell adhesion molecule 1 (PECAM-1, also named CD31), active transporters such as glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1), and tight junction proteins such as claudin 5 (CLDN-5)
18
,
42
–
44
(

–
S1C
). To ensure that the differential infection phenotype between SVN and R47 is not an artifact of differing virus input, we infected hPSC-BMELCs with various MOIs of SVN and R47. Significant differential infection was observed for all MOIs tested; however, the difference is lower at the highest MOI of 10, likely due to saturation (
Figure S2A
).
We next tested multiple MOIs in primary human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), primary human BMECs (HBMECs), and immortalized human BMECs (HCMEC/D3). As expected, HUVECs were efficiently infected by both strains at every MOI, suggesting that the ability to infect peripheral endothelial cells is conserved for both neuroinvasive and non-neuroinvasive SINV strains (
Figure S2B
). HBMECs were differentially infected at all MOIs tested, but only statistically significant at MOIs of 1 and 10, and at a lower maximum infection rate compared to hPSC-BMELCs (
Figure S2C
). Surprisingly, HCMEC/D3 cells were not infected at the lower MOIs and only reached a maximum infection rate of 11% at the highest MOI, although R47 infection at an MOI of 10 is trending higher than SVN infection (
Figure S2D
). Together, these data suggest that HCMEC/D3 cells are poorly infected by both SVN and R47. This is likely due to general differences between hPSC-derived cells and immortalized cells. Due to the conserved differential infection phenotype between hPSC-BMELCs and HBMECs, we proceeded with hPSC-BMELCs to thoroughly investigate the mechanism behind efficient infection of the BBB with R47.
To better understand BBB infection as a possible route of neuroinvasion for SINV, we compared the ability of these strains to infect all cells of the neurovascular unit (
Figure 1A

Figure 1B

Figure 1C
). Astrocyte infection revealed that both SVN and R47 replicate to high levels already by 18 h post-infection, even though SVN has a slight advantage over R47, which diminishes by 24 h post-infection (
Figure 1D
). We next decided to investigate virion production in each of these cell types. For both hPSC-BMELCs and pericytes, there is greater R47 virion production as early as 12 h post-infection; however, the major difference between SVN and R47 does not occur until 24 h post-infection (
Figures 1E
and

Figure 1B
) to almost 1000-fold in virion production (
Figure 1E
). Strikingly, we also observed a 100-fold increase in R47 virion production compared to SVN in astrocytes, despite no advantage in viral replication (
Figure 1G
). Together, these data demonstrate that neuroinvasive SINV has gained the ability to replicate more efficiently in BMECs and pericytes and produce virus particles more efficiently in all cell types of the BBB.
The three minimal mutations that confer a neuroinvasive phenotype lie in the 5′UTR and the E2 glycoprotein in the SINV genome.
7
A guanine at nucleotide position 8 of the SINV 5′UTR, which is homologous to position 3 in VEEV, is critical for evasion of the antiviral ISG product, IFIT1.
28
Moreover, studies have shown that the E2 glycoprotein, in addition to mediating viral entry and egress, has antagonistic activities against other innate immune factors.
45
Therefore, we chose to further understand the contribution of each mutation in the efficient infection of pericytes and astrocytes, as was done previously in hPSC-BMELCs.
24
Specifically, we infected pericytes and astrocytes with SVN, SVN with each individual mutation from R47, SVN with both E2 mutations, and R47. Previous work demonstrated that the E2 glycoprotein mutations were critical for hPSC-BMELC infection, while the 5′ UTR mutation was negligible.
24
In contrast, we demonstrate that both E2 mutations are insufficient for completely recapitulating the levels of infection in pericytes observed for R47 (
Figure S2E
). Strikingly, the 5′ UTR mutation alone provides a boost in infection efficiency in pericytes, suggesting that innate immunity plays a large role in pericyte infection (
Figure S2E
). Additionally, for both pericytes and astrocytes, either E2 mutation alone is disadvantageous, cutting infection efficiency down by 30%–50% relative to SVN (
Figures S2E
and
S2F
). Together, these data suggest that infection efficiency in each BBB cell type is likely the result of several distinct mechanisms, including differences in entry, innate immune modulation, and egress.
SINV neuroinvasion precedes barrier breakdown
in vitro
In vivo
work suggests that BBB disruption is a consequence of alphavirus neuroinvasion.
13
We chose to follow up on this work
in vitro
using the versatility of the hPSC-BMELC model. hPSC-BMELCs exhibit physiologically relevant barrier properties and thus, provide an excellent model for observing changes in the barrier function
in vitro.
17
,
24
We cultured hPSC-BMELCs in a transwell system, where a transwell insert separates a larger well into two distinct compartments. The inner compartment, referred to as the apical chamber, represents the inside of the blood vessels, while the outer compartment, referred to as the basolateral chamber, represents the brain parenchyma outside of the blood vessels. We started with a simple model using only a monolayer of hPSC-BMELCs in the apical chamber of a transwell system. Specifically, we measured transendothelial electrical resistance (TEER) over the course of infection with SVN or R47 and measured virus crossing by determining the amount of infectious virus production in the basolateral chamber at each time point via plaque assay. TEER values for hPSC-BMELCs began at around 1500 Ωcm
2
, which is close to the minimum expected value of human BMECs
in vivo.
17
Barrier integrity then decreases by half for both SVN- and R47-infected hPSC-BMELCs at 18 h post-infection; however, the TEER for SVN-infected hPSC-BMELCs plateaus at about 600 Ωcm
2
while the TEER for R47-infected hPSC-BMELCs reaches almost 0 Ωcm
2
by 24 h post-infection (
Figure S3A
). Importantly, 100-fold more R47 is detected in the basolateral chamber compared to SVN as early as 12 h post-infection (
Figure S3B
), suggesting SINV neuroinvasion is not a consequence of barrier breakdown. However, the fact that SVN particles are detected in the basolateral chamber suggests an incomplete model.
Therefore, we generated a more complete model of the BBB
in vitro
using a tri-culture of hPSC-BMELCs, pericytes, and astrocytes. Barrier integrity is further bolstered when cultured with the other BBB cell types, mimicking intact human BBB properties.
24
For our tri-culture, hPSC-BMELCs were seeded on the inside of the apical chamber, primary pericytes were seeded on the underside of the transwell insert, facing the basolateral chamber, and primary astrocytes were seeded in the well below the insert, in the basolateral chamber (
Figure 2A
). We used this system to more accurately determine the order of events leading to neuroinvasion, further defining the roles of BBB infection and leakage. Similar to the monoculture, we infected hPSC-BMELCs in the apical chamber and measured TEER over time. However, we now measured astrocyte GFP-positivity as a proxy for virus crossing since astrocytes should be highly infected by both viruses (
Figure 1D
). Over the course of infection, the integrity of the barrier remained strong and robust, reflected by the TEER values exceeding 2000 Ωcm
2
(
Figure 2B
). While TEER values fluctuated between 2000 and 8000 Ωcm
2
across time points, there was no significant trend in either direction that was suggestive of barrier compromise. Despite the high barrier integrity, R47 was able to make it across and infect astrocytes while SVN could not. R47 started with a modest increase of about 2%–10% infection by 18 h post-infection, which later increased up to 40% of astrocytes infected by 24 h (
Figure 2C
). This is in direct contrast to SVN, which resulted in no astrocyte infection, despite the ability to infect astrocytes to the same levels as R47. Overall, these data suggest that initial SINV neuroinvasion is dependent on barrier infection.
Innate immune profiling reveals modest differential modulation by neuroinvasive and non-neuroinvasive SINV
In hPSC-BMELCs, the K190M mutation alone in E2 is slightly advantageous, while the K260E mutation is only advantageous in the background of K190M.
7
,
24
This could suggest a role for innate immune evasion for one residue, while the other plays a role in other processes. Therefore, we sought to investigate potential differences in immune modulation within hPSC-BMELCs between SVN and R47. We first tested expression of three key ISGs—
ISG15
,
IFIT1
, and

Figure 3A
). Next, we decided to investigate whether the two viruses differed in their resistance to IFN pretreatment. Therefore, we titrated IFN-β on hPSC-BMELCs prior to infection with SVN or R47 and found that R47 is indeed modestly more resistant to IFN-β pretreatment but only at the concentration of 0.1 ng/mL (
Figure 3B
).
Finally, we also screened a panel of 71 different cytokines in media taken from uninfected hPSC-BMELCs and those infected with SVN or R47. Of the 71 different cytokines, only 47 had values within the detectable range for all samples (
Table S1

,

, and

The first pattern is where the cytokine is not secreted at all in uninfected cells, whereas infected cells have increased levels of secreted cytokines that align with the expected infection levels (i.e., there is more infection with R47, resulting in more cytokine production). This is observed for interleukin (IL)-28A (also called IFN-λ) and IFN gamma-induced protein 10 (IP-10, also called CXCL10), where infection with R47 results in 3-fold and 1.5-fold higher levels of secretion for each cytokine, respectively (
Figures 3C

3D

The second pattern is where the cytokine is already being secreted at a relatively high basal level in uninfected cells, and infection with SVN or R47 either modestly induces or reduces these levels. This can be observed with monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF), platelet-dervied growth factor (PDGF) isoforms PDGF-AA and PDGF-AB/BB, and interleukin 20 (IL-20) (

Neuroinvasive SINV is more efficient at hPSC-BMELC entry
Since BMEC infection is a critical correlate of neuroinvasive ability, and we demonstrated that innate immune modulation is a modest contributor, we next investigated which step in the viral life cycle is enhanced by R47-specific mutations. Previous work demonstrated E2 glycoprotein mutations as critical for hPSC-BMELC infection.
24
Therefore, we wanted to investigate the role of E2. We used single-cycle pseudotyped vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV) particles bearing SINV structural proteins to investigate entry, similar to prior SARS-CoV-2 studies.
46
These particles rely on SINV structural proteins for attachment and fusion but use the VSV core for decapsidation and replication of a luciferase reporter, without producing new virions. Thus, differences in infectivity result from different E2-host interactions at the entry steps. We cloned SINV structural genes lacking capsid and included an N-terminal myc tag on E2 to generate VSV particles with SVN or R47 glycoproteins, and confirmed equivalent myc-E2 incorporation (
Figure S5A
). Consistent with the role of E2 mutations in modulating alphavirus entry, R47-VSV particles infected hPSC-BMELCs ~2-fold better than SVN, supporting a role for BMEC entry in neuroinvasion (
Figure 4A
).
Alphavirus entry requires cell surface attachment, internalization, and endosomal fusion. We hypothesize that R47 has a selective advantage over SVN during this viral entry process, which we assessed using an entry assay.
47
,
48
Briefly, we treated hPSC-BMELCs with a fusion inhibitor, bafilomycin A
1
, before, during, or after virus addition to compare entry kinetics between SVN and R47 relative to the effects of bafilomycin A
1
. While early inhibitor addition blocked both viruses, R47 RNA was significantly more detected than SVN RNA when the drug was added simultaneously with the virus (
Figure 4B
). This indicates faster or more efficient entry by R47. This difference persisted as bafilomycin A
1
was added at later time points, suggesting that R47 is more efficient in at least one of the entry steps leading up to and including fusion.
To pinpoint which E2 mutations drive this advantage, we performed a similar assay with SVN variants harboring individual or combined R47 substitutions. SVN with the 5’ UTR mutation from R47 (referred to as SVN-U8G) behaved similarly to SVN, suggesting that this mutation does not affect entry (
Figure 4C
). Interestingly, SVN-E260K also matched SVN, indicating residue 260 alone is insufficient (
Figure 4C
). By contrast, SVN-K190M showed significantly improved entry at the 30 and 60 min time points, identifying K190M as a key driver of BMEC entry; however, this advantage is not to the same degree as R47 (
Figure 4C
). Interestingly, the E260K mutation confers an additional advantage in the background of the K190M mutation, and together, they match the entry efficiency of R47 (
Figure 4C
). These data are consistent with
in vivo
experiments where K190M drastically decreases the intraperitoneal LD50 compared to SVN, while K190M + E260K in the U8G background fully restores fatal neuroinvasion.
7
Together, this work suggests that the 190 position is critical for BMEC entry and neuroinvasion, while the 260 position increases the replicative fitness.
We next dissected the contributions of attachment and internalization to this phenotype. For attachment, we allowed varying concentrations of virus particles to bind to hPSC-BMELCs for 1 h and measured viral RNA immediately afterward. R47 bound more efficiently starting at MOI 0.01, reaching a 10-fold advantage over SVN at MOI 10 (
Figure 4D
). For internalization, we allowed virus uptake in the presence of bafilomycin A
1
to prevent fusion, then removed surface virions with proteinase. Similar to the binding assay, we measured viral RNA as a proxy for internalized virions. We found that SVN and R47 internalize similarly at low MOIs but begin to significantly differ at MOIs of 1 and 10 (
Figure 4E
). These data demonstrate that both cell attachment and internalization are critical for the efficient infection of BMECs.
Since attachment showed the largest difference, we decided to investigate a well-known attachment factor: heparan sulfate. hPSC-BMELCs were treated with heparanase to remove heparan sulfate before infection. Surprisingly, removal of heparan sulfate did not eliminate the >2-fold difference between the strains (
Figure S5B
). These data suggest that other host entry factors, such as receptors, underlie efficient BMEC infection.
Neuroinvasive SINV relies primarily on PCDH10 for efficient BMEC infection and neuroinvasion
We have demonstrated that neuroinvasive SINV must efficiently attach and enter BMECs for high levels of BMEC infection; however, the host factors exploited during this mechanism remain unknown. We have also shown that the differential infection phenotype is not dependent on heparan sulfate; therefore, we investigated the role of cell surface protein receptors. To date, there are seven known cellular receptors previously identified to interact with alphaviruses, including NRAMP2 (gene name
SLC11A2
), PCDH10, MXRA8, LDLR, VLDLR, LDLRAD3, and ApoER2 (gene name
LRP8
)
32
–
39
; however, none of these have been studied in the context of neuroinvasion, and specifically, infection of the BBB. We therefore chose to investigate whether SVN and R47 are dependent on these seven receptors for hPSC-BMELC infection. We did so by using previously cited antibodies to pretreat and block the receptors on hPSC-BMELCs prior to infection at varying MOIs. At an MOI of 0.1, R47 is almost completely inhibited by the blockade of PCDH10 and partially inhibited by the blockade of MXRA8, while blockade of other receptors has almost no effect (
Figure 5A
). In contrast, SVN was significantly inhibited by blockade of PCDH10 (10-fold), MXRA8 (5-fold), and NRAMP2 (2-fold) at a low MOI of 0.1 (
Figure 5A
). At a higher MOI of 1, R47 loses any dependence on MXRA8 and is now only reliant on PCDH10 (
Figure 5B
). In contrast, SVN is primarily dependent on PCDH10, but also partially relies on other entry factors, including NRAMP2 and VLDLR (
Figure 5B
). At the highest MOI of 10, however, only blockade of PCDH10 results in a strong inhibition of both SVN and R47 (
Figure 5C
). These data suggest that, while there are multiple entry factors that can be used to infect BMECs, PCDH10 is the primary receptor that must be engaged for BMEC infection.
We next wanted to investigate whether there were any differences in PCDH10 usage efficiency between SVN and R47 by titrating anti-PCDH10 antibody onto hPSC-BMELCs prior to infection. Both viruses seem to use PCDH10 similarly, as there are no differences in the slope of the inhibition curve. However, it is interesting to note that there are different levels of plateaued inhibition. Specifically, at the two highest concentrations of antibody, inhibition of SVN has already plateaued at 20% of the maximum infection (
Figure 5D
). In contrast, R47 is still being inhibited further, down to less than 10% the maximum infection (
Figure 5D
). We also wanted to determine the importance of PCDH10 in BMEC-specific infection; therefore, we also performed receptor blockade experiments in a different cell line, namely HEK293T cells. Here, we confirm that SVN is more reliant on several entry factors, including NRAMP2, PCDH10, MXRA8, LDLR, and LDLRAD3, though to varying degrees (
Figure S5C
). In contrast, R47 is only reliant on NRAMP2, PCDH10, and LDLR (
Figure S4C
). In HEK293T cells, the primary receptor used is NRAMP2, followed by LDLR, PCDH10, MXRA8, and LDLRAD3. This is in stark contrast to hPSC-BMELCs, where the primary receptor used is PCDH10. Taken all together, these data demonstrate that heavy PCDH10 reliance is most critical for efficient BMEC infection.
Omics approaches reveal LRP1 as a novel SINV entry factor uniquely engaged by SVN
While we demonstrated that SVN and R47 use a different range of entry factors for BMEC infection, we next asked whether R47 uniquely exploits BMEC-specific factors. We performed RNA sequencing with differential analysis on hPSC-BMELCs, HCMEC/D3, and HUVECs. One HUVEC replicate deviated slightly (
Figure S6A
) but clustered with its group and passed sample quality checks, so it was retained to preserve statistical power. Importantly, the efficient infection of HUVECs by SVN and R47 implies that any entry factors differentiating SVN and R47 would likely be low or absent in HUVECs. HCMEC/D3 cells, though poorly infected, showed a 6-fold difference in attachment between SVN and R47 (
Figure S6B
). This suggested that while replication in HCMEC/D3 cells was inefficient, the entry process was similar to hPSC-BMELCs. We therefore searched for cell-surface proteins enriched in brain-derived endothelial cells (hPSC-BMELCs and HCMEC/D3) but low in HUVECs.
In line with this reasoning, we performed variance stabilizing transformations on our dataset to accurately compare gene expression distribution. We observed that most variance stabilized the counts to range between 7 and 10 for all three cell types, representing genes that are regularly expressed (
Figure S6C
). Genes with counts >11 in brain-derived cells and <10 in HUVECs were selected, then filtered against the published cell surfaceome, yielding 35 candidates (
Figure 6A
).
To corroborate this at the protein level, we performed cell surface proteomics by biotin labeling the surface of hPSC-BMELCs, HUVECs, and HCMEC/D3 cells, followed by neutravidin enrichment and mass spectrometry. Principal component analysis (PCA) showed that hPSC-BMELCs clustered with HCMEC/D3 and apart from HUVECs along PC1 (
Figure S6D
), confirming similarity in tissue origin. Biotinylated samples separated clearly from non-biotinylated controls (
Figure S6E
) and showed 3–5× higher unique protein/peptide counts (
Figures S6F
and
S6G
), validating enrichment. While the proteins pulled down were not only from the cell surface, several enriched proteins indeed had cell surface gene ontology terms, indicating successful biotinylation and cell surface protein enrichment. We then filtered for proteins with transmembrane domains and generated a list of 30 candidates that are abundant in hPSC-BMELCs and HCMEC/D3 cells relative to HUVECs (
Figure 6B
).
Finally, we overlapped transcriptomic and proteomic datasets, moving forward with shared hits. Surprisingly, only one candidate, LRP1, was found to overlap across both datasets (
Figure 6C
). LRP1 is related to the LDLR class of receptors, which contains the most alphavirus receptors. We first confirmed LRP1 as the most abundant in hPSC-BMELCs and HCMEC/D3 and absent in HUVECs (
Figure S6H
). We then used an antibody to block cluster II of LRP1, as it is the most closely related to other LDLR proteins. Strikingly, blocking cluster II selectively reduced SVN infection by 50% without affecting R47 infection (
Figure 6D
). This supports our model that SVN is a generalist that engages multiple receptors, while R47 is a PCDH10-specific specialist. Here, we establish a critical role for PCDH10 in neuroinvasion and demonstrate how receptor promiscuity is detrimental to neuroinvasion.
BMEC infection correlates with neuroinvasive ability of diverse Old World alphaviruses
We sought to determine whether the viral determinants of neuroinvasion are shared among other Old-World alphaviruses. We first infected hPSC-BMELCs with several Old World alphaviruses, including CHIKV, ONNV, and RRV in addition to SVN and R47. We found that none of the additional Old World alphaviruses infect hPSC-BMELCs beyond 1% (
Figure 7A
). However, among these alphaviruses, only CHIKV is known to cause severe complications of the CNS via potential direct neuroinvasion, as evidenced by the CHIKV RNA found in the cerebrospinal fluid of fatal human cases. Furthermore,
in vivo
mouse studies have demonstrated that the CHIKV 181/clone 25 vaccine strain is non-neuroinvasive while the virulent La Reunion (LR) strain is neuroinvasive. Therefore, we investigated whether there would be a differential infection phenotype of CHIKV strains in the cells of the BBB, similar to SVN and R47. Indeed, we found that the virulent CHIKV LR strain efficiently infects hPSC-BMELCs 6-fold more than the attenuated 181/clone 25 vaccine strain. Moreover, the CHIKV LR strain significantly infects hPSC-BMELCs (40%) to similar levels as R47 (55%) (
Figures 7A
and
7B
). Additionally, pericytes are infected 2-fold more by the LR strain than the 181/clone 25 vaccine strain. Importantly, astrocytes are efficiently infected by both CHIKV strains, suggesting the previous phenotypes are not a result of broad attenuation (
Figure 7B
). Together, these data support that neuroinvasive Old World alphaviruses efficiently infect all cell types of the BBB.
Lastly, we wanted to investigate whether BMEC attachment is the critical interaction necessary for CHIKV neuroinvasion, as it is for SINV. We performed the same binding assay as before, but with the CHIKV 181/clone 25 vaccine strain and LR strain. Surprisingly, the CHIKV LR strain binds to hPSC-BMELCs almost 10 times less than the CHIKV 181/clone 25 vaccine strain (
Figure 7C
). We also investigated CHIKV internalization and again found that the LR strain internalizes about 10 times less than the 181/clone 25 vaccine strain (
Figure 7D
). Finally, we investigated the differences in overall entry efficiency as we previously measured for SINV. Despite the major differences in attachment and internalization, there were no significant differences when comparing virus entry during bafilomycin A
1
treatment, suggesting that CHIKV LR fusion may compensate for differences in attachment and internalization, resulting in similar entry levels as the 181/clone 25 vaccine strain (
Figure 7E
). Overall, these data suggest that, while neuroinvasive Old World alphaviruses have gained the ability to infect cells of the BBB for neuroinvasion, they have evolved distinct mechanisms for infection. Specifically, CHIKV mechanisms of BMEC infection are likely post-entry. Importantly, we identify BBB infection as critical for subsequent neuroinvasion for SINV and the more clinically relevant CHIKV. This can potentially provide a broad-spectrum target for blocking Old World alphavirus entry into and infection of the CNS.
DISCUSSION
Our study is the first to establish a correlation between BBB infection, neuroinvasion, and alphavirus pathogenesis. Using hPSC-BMELCs, we show that neuroinvasive E2 residues enhance BMEC and pericyte infection. Specifically, neuroinvasive E2 critically enhances BMEC attachment and entry. We propose a model where specialized usage of PCDH10 promotes BMEC infection and neuroinvasion, while broader receptor engagement is detrimental. Enhanced BBB infection also correlates with CHIKV neuroinvasive ability, although CHIKV likely relies on post-entry mechanisms, underscoring distinct mechanisms to achieve efficient BBB infection. Overall, BBB infection emerges as a central determinant of neuropathogenesis.
Stem cell-derived models enable viral pathogenesis studies in a physiologically relevant context.
49
–
52
For instance, hPSC-BMELCs have been pivotal in investigating flavivirus neuroinvasion,
24
while overcoming major limitations of traditional models: primary BMECs are difficult to isolate and culture beyond a few passages, and immortalized cell lines lack the robust barrier integrity required for transwell systems. By contrast, hPSC-BMELCs are scalable, genetically tractable, and offer robust barrier properties. Despite some differentiation protocols yielding BMEC-like cells that also express some non-endothelial markers,
20
ongoing advancements—such as the incorporation of retinoic acid to enhance barrier integrity, shear force to better mimic vascular conditions, and the introduction of specific transcription factors—continue to refine these models.
18

,
53
–
55
This adaptability underscores the unique advantage of stem cell-derived systems, which evolve to address experimental needs. To complement our work, future models incorporating vascularization and flow will clarify how circulating virions attach and disseminate from the BBB.
Our model also revealed a neuroinvasion mechanism that had not yet been observed
in vivo
. Previous work on VEEV and WEEV neuroinvasion suggests that cells of the murine BBB are not infected
in vivo.
12
Instead, VEEV transverses BMECs via caveolin-mediated endocytosis, implying that direct BBB infection is not a conserved neuroinvasion strategy among encephalitic alphaviruses. While these studies showed that all murine BBB cell types and human BMECs are susceptible and permissive to VEEV and WEEV infection
in vitro
, no evidence of BMEC or pericyte infection was found
in vivo
in the presence of type I IFN signaling. This discrepancy could be a result of the time points chosen or the age of the mice. Alternatively, New World alphaviruses may have evolved distinct mechanisms for neuroinvasion, underscoring the diversity of alphavirus pathogenesis. Systematic testing of neuroinvasive and attenuated strains across alphavirus lineages will be critical to resolve these mechanisms.
Mechanisms of SINV and CHIKV neuroinvasion have remained elusive, despite their critical importance in the context of CHIKV-associated fatal infections.
4
Zebrafish studies have demonstrated that neither virus relies on macrophage carriage, while murine models reveal SINV BBB crossing precedes barrier disruption.
13
,
14
Human cohort studies suggest that CHIKV crosses the BBB through leakage and immune cell carriage, findings that contrast with those from zebrafish models and our
in vitro
model
4

56
–
58
Together, these findings highlight the value of integrating stem cell-derived systems with animal models to capture early infection events.
We have demonstrated how alphaviruses engage host entry factors in specific human cell types critical for pathogenesis. Several LDLR proteins are essential receptors for VEEV, WEEV, EEEV, and SFV,
34
–
36
highlighting a conserved role for LDLR proteins in promoting alphavirus encephalitis
in vivo
. For SINV, the LDLR-related proteins LRP8 and VLDLR have been implicated as receptors in mouse neuroblastoma N2A cells, although this is context-dependent. While SINV structural proteins can bind LRP8 and VLDLR, overexpression of these receptors in non-susceptible cell lines only modestly increases infection. Furthermore, blocking these receptors on susceptible cells does not reduce SINV infection, in contrast to SFV. Here, we identify LRP1 as a BMEC-specific receptor candidate for SINV. Interestingly, LRP1 engagement may hinder neuroinvasion, raising important questions about its role in disease progression. Future studies must investigate how LRP1 interactions prevent efficient and streamlined infection in BMECs and other cell types.
Our data suggest receptor usage is dynamically reshaped by cell type, modulating viral tropism and pathogenesis. For example, other groups have shown that several Old World alphaviruses, including CHIKV, o’nyong’nyong virus (ONNV), and Ross River virus (RRV), use MXRA8 as a receptor on mouse embryonic fibroblasts and 3T3 cells, which is critical for mouse pathogenesis.
33
In contrast, SINV does not rely on mammalian MXRA8, but avian MXRA8 found in the wild bird reservoir for SINV
37
,
59
; however, our results demonstrate that certain SINV strains can engage mammalian MXRA8 in a concentration- and cell-specific manner. Another example is NRAMP2, which was previously identified as a receptor for SINV in mosquito cells and primary murine fibroblasts
39
; yet, it is unclear how cell type-specific expression contributes to pathogenesis. Our study demonstrates that the role of NRAMP2 engagement varies with cell type. For instance, HEK293T cell infection relies primarily on NRAMP2, and blocking these interactions completely abrogates infection. In contrast, NRAMP2 blockade in BMECs results in moderate inhibition of only non-neuroinvasive SINV, suggesting that interacting with NRAMP2 is beneficial for HEK293T infection, but detrimental to BMEC infection. Most strikingly, a similar phenotype is observed for the PCDH10 receptor, where PCDH10 interactions are beneficial to BMEC infection and less important for HEK293T infection. Future studies should validate how receptor hierarchies across tissues dictate clinical outcomes.
Our study demonstrates that SINV can use PCDH10 as a receptor, and PCDH10 is essential for BMEC infection. While PCDH10 has been identified as a neuronal receptor for WEEV, no studies have examined its role in WEEV infection of BMECs. Our findings raise the important possibility that PCDH10-mediated BMEC infection likely contributes to WEEV neuroinvasion. Notably, we show that neuroinvasive SINV relies exclusively on PCDH10, in contrast to non-neuroinvasive SINV, which engages multiple receptors, including LRP1. This specialist-versus-generalist entry strategy underlies differences in BBB infection and neuropathogenesis. Our findings suggest that therapeutic targeting of PCDH10 via antibodies or soluble decoys may prevent SINV and potentially WEEV neuroinvasion.
BMEC tropism heavily correlates with neuroinvasive capacity of alphaviruses and may serve as a key predictor of disease severity. This is underscored by previous findings showing CHIKV RNA in the cerebrospinal fluid of all fatal CHIKV cases, suggesting neuroinvasion as a key determinant of lethality.
4
Our data support the model that alphavirus BBB tropism is a central determinant of neuroinvasion and lethality. Moreover, these findings open new avenues for vaccine development. The determinants of attenuation for vaccine strains of CHIKV and VEEV were previously mapped to mutations in the E2 glycoprotein and believed to attenuate the virus through increased affinity for heparin sulfate.
31
,
60
However, the functional impact of these mutations on tropism are not well understood. We reveal that attenuating mutations in CHIKV result in a loss of BMEC and pericyte tropism, directly linking neuroinvasion and pathogenesis, though future work must also investigate whether this is a lineage-specific phenomenon. Ultimately, this work demonstrates that future attenuated vaccines for other alphaviruses may be designed to specifically disrupt barrier tropism, thereby reducing virulence.
In summary, BBB infection correlates with neuroinvasion and virulence across alphaviruses. Our results establish receptor specialization as a key determinant of alphavirus neuropathogenesis and open new avenues for targeted antivirals and rational vaccine design.
Limitations of the study
We acknowledge three primary limitations of our study with regard to our hPSC-derived model, applications
in vivo
, and the systematic investigation of CHIKV neuroinvasion. First, the hPSC-BMELCs used in this study are derived using recent and advanced differentiation protocols; however, these endothelial-like cells are also known to express some epithelial cell markers.
20
Future experiments should increase the model’s accuracy by including vascularized 3D cultures. Second is the lack of
in vivo
work that further corroborates the importance of BMEC infection in SINV and CHIKV neuropathogenesis. We have shown that infection of the BBB strongly correlates with neuroinvasive capacity, but future work
in vivo
should determine whether the murine BBB is efficiently infected via immunostaining. Additionally, future work should investigate the role of LRP1 and PCDH10 on R47 neuroinvasion in mice. Finally, we acknowledge the limitations of our system in investigating CHIKV neuroinvasion. In this study, we used two strains of CHIKV with opposite
in vivo
neuroinvasiveness; however, using just two strains limits our ability to pinpoint residues critical for infection of the BBB. Instead, future work should systematically identify residues critical for CHIKV neuroinvasion. These limitations present exciting opportunities for future research that will facilitate the next step in the development of novel therapeutic strategies to mitigate alphavirus-induced neuropathies.

METHOD DETAILS
Generation of hPSC-BMELCs from hPSCs
Stem cells were differentiated into hPSC-BMELCs according to previous publications (Lippmann et al., 2012; Lippmann et al., 2014; Neal et al., 2019). Briefly, stem cells were thawed on Matrigel coated flasks and grown for a minimum of 4 days in mTeSR1. After the fourth day, mTeSR1 was replaced by growth media (20% KnockOut Serum Replacer, 1mM L-glutamine, 1X NEAA, 0.1mM β-mercaptoethanol, and 100ng/mL basic fibroblast growth factor in 1:1 DMEM/F-12) to initiate differentiation. After 5–7 days, the growth media was changed to endothelial cell (EC) media (Gibco, 11111044) supplemented with 1X B-27 supplement, 20 ng/mL human basic fibroblast growth factor and 10μM of retinoic acid (RA). After 2 days, the cells were dissociated with StemPro Accutase (Gibco, A1110501) for 30–45 min at 37°C, followed by cell scraping. Cells were seeded on collagen IV (400 μg/mL) and fibronectin (100 μg/mL) coated plates and cultured in EC media supplemented with 1X B27 and 10μM ROCK Inhibitor.
Immunofluorescence staining
hPSC-BMELCs were seeded onto each slide of the Thermo Scientific Nunc Lab-Tek II Chamber Slide System (Thermofisher, 12–565-7). The cells were allowed to grow for 24 h and were subsequently fixed with ice-cold methanol for 15 min. The cells were then incubated with blocking buffer (10% Fetal Bovine Serum and 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS) for 30 min at room temperature. Primary antibodies were diluted in blocking buffer and incubated at 4°C overnight. After two washes with PBS, the cells were incubated with secondary antibody diluted in blocking buffer for 1 h at room temperature. The chambers were washed twice with PBS and were stained with 1X DAPI for 3 min and mounted with coverslips using Fluoromount-G Mounting Medium (Thermofisher, 00–4958-02). All slides were imaged using the Zeiss Axio-Observer 1 microscope and processed using ImageJ software.
Flow cytometry
Cell media was aspirated, and cells were immediately lifted with either 200μL of StemPro Accutase (for hPSC-BMELCs) or 200μL of Trypsin (all other cells). After 5–10 min of incubation at 37°C, the lifted cells were moved into a 96 well U-bottom plate and centrifuged at 1500rpm to pellet the cells. The cells were then resuspended in 200μL fixation buffer (2% Paraformaldehyde and 1% FBS in PBS) and fixed at 4°C for a minimum of 30 min. Samples were measured using the Miltenyi Biotec MACSQuant Analyzer 10 Flow Cytometer.
Plaque forming assay
All virus titers quantified as plaque-forming units (PFU) in this study were done using BHK-21 cells cultured in MEM with 7.5% FBS. BHK-21 cells were seeded at high confluency (3.5×10
5
cells/mL) in 6 well plates and were infected with 200μL virus inoculum at 24 h post seeding. Virus inoculum was made by performing 1:10 serial dilutions of virus-containing sample in virus binding buffer (1%FBS in PBS). After virus addition, cells were gently agitated every 15 min for 1 h for adsorption. The cells were then overlayed with 2.25% Avicel, 10% FBS, 1X Penicillin/Streptomycin, and 1X MEM NEAA in water and incubated at 37°C for 24 h. The plates were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for a minimum of 30 min at room temperature and then stained with crystal violet for a minimum of 30 min at room temperature. Finally, the plates were washed with water and left to dry overnight before plaques were counted.

Proteomics sample processing
The Pierce Cell Surface Biotinylation and Isolation Kit (Thermofisher, A44390) was appended with the EasyPep MS Sample Prep Kit (Thermofisher, A40006) to generate the cell surface proteomics dataset according to manufacturer’s protocols. Mass spectrometry proteomics acquisition
Dried peptides were resuspended in 0.1% (v/v) Formic acid (FA, LC/MS grade, Sigma Aldrich) in water (LC/MS grade, Fisher Scientific) and analyzed on a timsTOF HT mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics), paired with a Vanquish
Neo
ultra-high pressure liquid chromatography system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Mobile phase A consisted of 0.1% (v/v) FA in water (LC/MS grade, Fisher Scientific), and mobile phase B consisted of 0.1% (v/v) FA in 100% Acetonitrile (LC/MS grade, Fisher Scientific). The LC was operated in trap-and-elute mode, where the peptides were first trapped onto a PepMap
Neo
Trap column (5 mm, 100 Å pore size, 5 μm particle size) and then reversed-phase separated using gradient mentioned below on a Bruker PepSep C18 reverse phase column (15 cm, 150 μm I.D., 100 Å pore size, 1.5 μm particle size), kept at 50°C using a column oven for Bruker CaptiveSpray source (Sonation Lab Solutions). The capillary voltage was set to 1700 V. The peptides were separated at 500 nL/min flow rate, on a gradient of %B as follows: 5%–50% B over 40.5 min, then to 60% in the next 1 min, followed by a column wash step where %B was increased to 95% B in 0.5 min, and stayed same for next 3 min at 1.5 μL/min flow rate. The raw data was acquired in data-independent acquisition coupled with parallel accumulation–serial fragmentation (dia-PASEF) mode. Using equal-size windows of 25 Da with an overlap of 1 Da in the m/z vs. ion mobility plane, the precursor ion coverage was maximized for further MS/MS. For dia-PASEF, in the ion mobility (1/K0) range 0.64–1.40 Vs. cm-2, the collision energy was linearly decreased from 59 eV at 1/K0 = 1.40 Vs. cm-2 to 20 eV at 1/K0 = 0.64 Vs. cm-2 to collect the MS/MS spectra in the mass range 254.2–1287.2 Da. The estimated mean cycle time was 1.59s. The ion accumulation time and ramp times in the dual TIMS analyzer were set to 100 ms each.
Mass spectrometry proteomics data analysis
The raw files were processed with DIA-NN ver. 1.9.2 in two steps. The first step generated an
in-silico
spectral library using reviewed protein entries from the UniProt Human database (downloaded Nov. 11, 2024) and the DIA-NN default common contaminants list. For precursor ion generation, ‘FASTA digest for library free search/library generation’ and ‘Deep learning-based spectra, RTs, and IMs prediction’ options were selected. Trypsin/P was selected as protease, with a maximum of two missed cleavage and three variable modifications allowed. Carbamidomethylation at Cysteine was kept as a fixed modification. The variable modifications used were: N-term M excision, Oxidation (M), Acetylation (N-term), and CAMthiopropanoyl (K, N-term, Unimod Accession # 293). Other parameters used for the library generation are-Peptide length range: 7 to 30, Precursor charge range: 1–4, Precursor m/z range: 250–1800, and Fragment ion m/z range: 200–1800. The generated spectral library was used to search the raw files. For the DIA-NN search, both MS1 and MS2 mass accuracy was set to automatic inference, with the match-between-run option selected. Other search parameters were set to default settings.
Identified peptides were next evaluated for quality and replicate consistency. First, an abundance correlation matrix of peptide intensities and identities was generated to evaluate the correlation between replicates. Samples C+.2 and iB+.2 were excluded due to poor correlation between replicates. Next, based on the number of unique peptides and proteins detected, sample C−.1 was excluded because it contained a substantially higher number of peptides and proteins compared to its replicates, and sample U−.2 was excluded due to an insufficient number of peptide and protein identifications. After this quality control step, the DIA-NN report.tsv output file was imported into MSstats for cross-run normalization, protein summarization, and statistical comparative analysis between conditions. MSstats performs normalization by median equalization, imputation of missing values and median smoothing to combine intensities for multiple peptide ions or fragments into a single intensity for their protein group, and statistical tests of differences in intensity between infected and control time points. Default settings for MSstats were used for adjusted
p
values. By default, MSstats uses the Student’s t-test for
p
value calculation and the Benjamini–Hochberg method of FDR estimation to adjust
p
values. To identify the background proteins (nonspecific binders), we compared proteins detected in the + biotin and − biotin samples for each cell line. Proteins with an adjusted

Western blotting
Samples were lysed in homemade RIPA Buffer (150mM NaCl, 1% Nonidet P-40 Subtitute, 0.5% Sodium Deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS, 50mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4) with Roche cOmplete Mini, EDTA-free Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Millipore Sigma, 11836170001). Lysates were clarified at >16,000rpm for 5 min, and protein concentration was measured using Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (ThermoFisher, 23225) according to manufacturer’s protocols. Samples were denatured by mixing 20μg of protein with 4x Laemmli buffer and boiled at 100°C for 10 min. The samples were separated by 12%–15% sodium dodecyl sulphate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto methanol-activated 0.2 μm PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% milk in PBS-T (0.1% Tween 20 in PBS) for 30 min at room temperature. Primary antibodies were diluted in 5% milk in PBS-T and incubated with the membranes overnight at 4°C. After three washes with PBS-T, the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies for at least 1 h at room temperature. Finally, a chemiluminescent HRP substrate was applied to the membrane for detection of proteins. Protein detection was done using a ChemiDoc Imager (Bio-Rad), and images were processed using ImageLab (Bio-Rad).
