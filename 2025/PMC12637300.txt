INTRODUCTION
The small intestine’s vital functions—nutrient absorption, barrier protection, and immune regulation (
1
)—arise from its distinct microenvironmental complexity (
2
,
3
), including fluid flow, extracellular matrix, and so on (
Fig. 1A
). Among these, flow-induced shear stress exerts critical physiological influence (
4
,
5
) and varies spatially because of the intestine’s macroscopic structures, notably circular folds (
6
). Replicating this intricacy in vitro is critical for advancing regenerative medicine (
7
,
8
), drug development, and disease modeling (
9
). However, most models simulate microenvironmental cues while neglecting the role of large-scale architecture in shaping spatial shear patterns (
10
,
11
). Integrating biomimetic structural features with controllable fluid dynamics is necessary for physiologically relevant modeling (
12
,
13
), which is often lacking in current systems. For example, as typical biomedical technologies, organ-on-chip platforms replicate homogeneous flow dynamics and shear stress in two-dimensional (2D) or 2.5D environments (
14
,
15
), while 3D intestinal models with villi or circular folds, which are constructed by tissue engineering, are typically cultured statically and lack mechanical stimulation (
16
,
17
). As a result, replicating the functional regulatory role of human intestinal spatial shear stress in tissue models remains a challenge (
18
), limiting their fidelity in mimicking native intestinal functions.

To address this limitation, we developed a 3D bioprinted small intestine model that combines biomimetic macroarchitecture with physiologically relevant dynamic flow (
Fig. 1B
). Using an embedded extrusion–based strategy, we fabricated high-fidelity thin-walled tubular structures with precise filament resolution, preserving internal lumen geometry essential for controlling flow fields. Anatomically scaled circular folds were computationally designed to mimic native architectural features and generate shear gradients, enabling region-specific (RS) microenvironments under perfusion. This biomimetic platform revealed shear-dependent transcriptomic patterns, including zonated regulation of tight junctions, secretory capacity, and transporter expression, resulting in epithelial specialization toward either barrier or absorptive phenotypes. High-shear regions enhanced proliferation and barrier integrity, while low-shear zones retained absorptive function. These findings were validated at transcript and protein levels across time points, establishing long-term functional stability.
The model’s physiological relevance was further supported by the host-microbe interaction studies. Coculture with
Lactobacillus plantarum
led to spatially distinct colonization patterns, activating localized immune responses and epithelial remodeling, recapitulating known in vivo dynamics. In addition, nutrient and drug absorption assays revealed distinct transcellular and paracellular uptake patterns, shaped by flow-regulated permeability and transporter activity—features often absent in conventional static models. Quantitative assessment of drug absorption demonstrated strong in vitro–in vivo correlation, reinforcing the model’s potential for pharmacokinetic applications and translational relevance.
By unifying anatomical precision, mechanical zonation, and biological validation, this platform bridges a critical gap in intestinal modeling. It enables mechanistic studies of shear-regulated epithelial function, RS microbial interactions, and absorption behavior, offering a scalable and physiologically relevant system for applications in drug testing, microbiome research, and personalized medicine.
RESULTS
Shear stress–induced regulation of epithelial cell function
Replicating intestinal function in vitro requires the dynamic microenvironment due to intestinal physiological responses to mechanical cues, such as fluid shear stress. Shear stress arises naturally from fluid flow through the intestine and varies spatially because of the influence of intestinal anatomical structure, performing the complex regulation to intestinal function (
19
,
20
). To investigate the effect of shear stress, we developed a dynamic culture system driven by a peristaltic pump, which allowed us to control shear stress levels while maintaining a noncontact setup, thus avoiding direct mechanical interference with the cells. This system enabled homogeneous flow generation within a hollow straight-tube model, resulting in uniform shear stress applied along the entire inner wall surface (fig. S1). This configuration, referred to as the homogeneous flow (HF) models, was used under both static (HF
static
) and dynamic (HF
dynamic
) conditions (
Fig. 2A
). In HF
static
models, no flow was applied, while HF
dynamic
models subjected the adherent epithelial cells to physiologically relevant shear. This distinction allowed us to assess shear-induced effects independent of architectural features, providing a baseline for comparison with the more complex models.

Transcriptome analysis revealed substantial changes in cellular function, with 1550 genes up-regulated and 2061 genes down-regulated under dynamic culture (
Fig. 2B
). Pathway enrichment analysis based on Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes‌ (KEGG) highlighted the activation of pathways related to adherens junction, DNA replication, regulation of actin cytoskeleton, and protein processing, which are essential for maintaining epithelial integrity. In contrast, pathways related to fructose metabolism, drug metabolism, and fat absorption were suppressed in HF
dynamic
models (
Fig. 2C
and table S1). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis supported these findings, highlighting biological processes related to functional differentiation under shear stress (fig. S2A and table S2).
Shear stress induced cellular changes that contributed to barrier function, including the enhanced tight junctions, cell morphology, proliferation, and secretory capacity. Notably, key markers for barrier integrity, including lysozyme (
LYZ
), mucin 2 (
MUC2
), and genes related to tight junctions were up-regulated under shear stress. This result indicated an activation of barrier functions associated with Paneth and Goblet cells (
Fig. 2D
and fig. S2C) (
21
). While different flow rates (1 × 10
−3
to 4 × 10
−3
Pa) induced comparable levels of shear stress within physiological ranges, no statistically significant differences were observed between flow groups. This suggests that epithelial cells exhibit a threshold response to physiological shear, where barrier-enhancing effects are activated once a minimal level of mechanical stimulation is met. These findings support the physiological relevance of applying controlled shear stress, laying the groundwork for introducing more spatially varied mechanical cues in later models to explore RS epithelial specialization.
At protein level, we found that F-actin protein showed expanded cytoskeleton in HF
dynamic
models, suggesting activated tight junction and barrier function (
Fig. 2E
). Furthermore, immunostaining results showed elevated levels of tight junction protein zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1), confirming improved barrier integrity. The increased levels of mucin (MUC2) and mucus [wheat germ agglutinin (WGA)] further supported the enhanced mucus production and barrier function under shear stress (
Fig. 2, F and G
). The staining results also show an increased expression of villin proteins (VILLIN), indicating increased microvilli density. Since microvilli support mucus secretion, this also reflects enhanced epithelial barrier function. These findings indicated that shear stress promotes barrier integrity by modulating epithelial morphology, proliferation, and secretory activity. The up-regulation of VILLIN does not contradict the down-regulation of absorption-related genes, as VILLIN is a structural component of the apical brush border rather than a transporter itself.
Shear stress also suppressed absorption-related pathways, indicating a functional shift from absorption toward barrier protection. The down-regulation of aldolase B (
ALDOB
), a marker for absorptive cells, along with reduced expression of carbohydrate transporters such as solute carrier family 2 member 5 (
SLC2A5
), suggests diminished absorptive capacity (
Fig. 2D
and fig. S2B). Meanwhile, the up-regulated expression of drug efflux transport gene such as adenosine 5′-triphosphate–binding cassette subfamily C member 2 (
ABCC2
) also indicated the suppressed absorptive function (
22
). In addition, the up-regulation of transcription factors such as
MYC
, cyclin D1 (
CCND1
), and sex-determining region Y-box transcription factor 9 (
SOX9
) (
23
,
24
), suggesting differentiation toward secretory cell types, such as Goblet and Paneth cells, aligning with the observed suppression of absorptive function. To explore the lower boundary of flow sensitivity, we further reduced the flow rate to 80 μl/min, yielding shear stress levels that closely approximate the physiological threshold. Transcriptomic analysis revealed 636 differentially expressed genes between conditions of 80 and 240 μl/min, with expression profiles under 80 μl/min deviated from other dynamic conditions (fig. S2, D to F). Notably, the expression of key markers such as
MUC2
and
SLC2A5
shifted under low shear toward levels observed in static cultures, confirming a reduction in mechanosensitive functional responses.
These findings demonstrate that shear stress induces a functional trade-off in epithelial cells, prioritizing barrier integrity over absorption activities, reflecting a functional adaptation to mechanical stimulation. This adaptation provides critical insights into epithelial cell behavior under mechanical force and establishes a foundation for developing more accurate in vitro models of the human intestine.
Structure design and manufacturing of biomimetic small intestine models
Given the critical role of shear stress in regulating intestinal function, we designed our model to replicate the macroscopic circular folds found in the native intestine. These structures induce zonated flow patterns essential for diverse cellular behaviors (
25
). Replicating these macroscopic features in vitro is essential for providing a biomimetic environment conducive to cellular development and overcoming the limitations of homogeneous models, such as the straight-tube system.
The small intestine typically ranges from 15 to 35 mm in diameter, with circular folds measuring 1 to 4 mm in thickness and spaced 2 to 31 mm apart. These correspond to fold thicknesses of ~3 to 25% and spacings of ~6 to 90% relative to the intestinal diameter (
Fig. 3A
) (
26
). Guided by these anatomical features, we optimized the geometry to reproduce zonated shear stress patterns characteristic of the native intestine (
Fig. 3B
) (
27
). To evaluate how these structural parameters modulate local flow dynamics, we used computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations to iteratively adjust fold thickness and spacing, balancing shear gradients with nutrient exchange and fluid transport efficiency (
Fig. 3, C to F
). Excessive fold thickness dampened flow and diffusion, while insufficient thickness reduced the zonation of mechanical stimuli. Likewise, tightly spaced folds hindered transport, while excessive spacing diminished local microenvironmental heterogeneity. On the basis of this anatomical range, we proportionally designed a model with a 5.6-mm inner diameter, 0.8-mm fold thickness (15%), and 2.8-mm interfold spacing (50%), all falling within the native physiological range. CFD simulations confirmed that our design could effectively recapitulate the native intestinal shear stress range, which spans ~2 × 10
−4
to 8 × 10
−3
Pa (
28
). In our optimized model geometry, the targeted shear stress values reached ~1 × 10
−3
Pa at the circular folds and 1 × 10
−4
Pa within the lumen, corresponding to the lower end of the physiological range relevant for epithelial function (fig. S3).

To bridge the structural gap between filament-based printing and computer-aided design-generated solid models, we developed a mass conservation–based extrusion control strategy that ensures both design fidelity and continuous wall structure. In conventional slicing software, filaments were assumed to have a perfectly circular cross section and were arranged tangentially to construct the walls. However, this tangential arrangement resulted in voids between adjacent filaments because the total extruded volume was insufficient to fill the design space completely. Although increasing the extrusion rate could theoretically eliminate these voids, it would compromise the fidelity of the inner profile, which was critical for regulating flow fields and maintaining structural precision. To address this limitation, we developed a precise extrusion control strategy to optimize filament morphology, effectively filling voids without sacrificing design accuracy (
Fig. 3I
). By applying mass conservation principles, we recalculated the mass flow rate to ensure that the total extruded volume precisely matched the design requirements

In the fabrication process, we selected 5% (w/v) gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA)/3% (w/v) poly(ethylene glycol) diacrylate (PEGDA) as bioink and 10% (w/v) Pluronic F-127 (PF-127)/2.5% (w/v) hydrophobically modified hydroxypropylmethyl cellulose (H-HPMC) as embedding medium, with the suitable temperature determined through rheological testing (fig. S5, A to D). The filament diameter was controlled within a range of 200 to 800 μm, maintaining circular cross-sectional shapes by regulating printing speed and extrusion pressure (
Fig. 3H
and fig. S5, E to G). This revealed how filament deposition behavior directly determined the final architecture. Three representative structural outcomes were identified—discrete, fitted, and excessive—depending on whether filament diameter under- or overshot the path-planned dimensions (
Fig. 3J
and fig. S5H). The discrete structure resulted in visible voids and liquid leakage due to undersized filaments (fig. S5I). The excessive structure caused deformation due to overpacking. The fitted structure minimized voids, maintained geometry, and preserved zonated shear gradients.
To assess the influence of architectural fidelity on macromolecular permeability, we conducted dynamic perfusion of fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)–dextran (4 kDa) through all three models. Fluorescence imaging showed rapid leakage in the discrete model, while the fitted structure maintained gradient-driven diffusion and barrier control (fig. S6). Uniaxial mechanical testing showed that the discrete model was weakest, especially under longitudinal tension (~1 kPa), while fitted and excessive types reached 10 to 20 kPa (fig. S7). While these values remain lower than native intestinal tissue (~1 MPa), they fall within the functional range for hydrogel-based in vitro models.
On the basis of its structural fidelity, mechanical integrity, and ability to recapitulate native shear stress zonation, the fitted model was selected for downstream functional validations. The resulting intestine model successfully replicated the native topological of circular folds, establishing a biomimetic microenvironment conducive to cellular development and RS flow dynamics.
Impact of regional shear stress on cellular functionality
To explore how shear stress influences epithelial behavior, we extended the homogeneous model by introducing an RS model incorporating biomimetic circular folds. This design allows for the capture of heterogeneous flow fields under dynamic culture, mimicking the natural intestinal environment. In this model, the lumens (RS
lumen
) experience relatively static conditions, while the folds (RS
fold
) are exposed to dynamic flow. On the basis of our extraction methodology, RS
fold
models comprise the circular folds and the transitional region between the circular folds and the lumens (fig. S8). While RS
lumen
and RS
fold
models share similarities with HF
static
and HF
dynamic
models, respectively, it is complex to replicate the functional diversity of the intestine by integrating these regions into a single structure.
Following dynamic culture, RS gene expression profiling revealed differences in cellular responses between RS
lumen
and RS
fold
models (
Fig. 4A
). The dynamic flow over the circular folds created a mechanically heterogeneous microenvironment, while the lumens remained comparatively stable. To explore the impact of these mechanical differences, we performed principal components analysis (PCA) of gene expression data across the RS and HF models. PCA revealed that RS
fold
models clustered closely with HF
dynamic
models along the first principal component, while RS
lumen
models resembled HF
static
models, reflecting shared responses to shear or static conditions, respectively (
Fig. 4B
). However, RS
fold
and HF
dynamic
models were distinctly separated along the second principal component, indicating that RS
fold
models has distinct gene expression features not explained by homogeneous shear stress alone. This divergence likely reflects contributions from the flow gradient within the transitional zones, particularly at vertical walls and curved corners, where shear magnitude and direction vary rapidly. Heatmap clustering of differential expressed genes further highlighted the distinctiveness of the RS
fold
and HF
dynamic
models transcriptome, supporting the distinctiveness of the transitional region in the RS model (fig. S9A). Correlation analysis showed high similarity between RS
fold
and HF
dynamic
models due to dynamic conditions, while RS
lumen
and HF
static
models aligned with static or low-shear environments, emphasizing the influence of zonated shear stress on cellular transcriptomic profiles (fig. S9B).

By combining static and dynamic microenvironments within a single biomimetic architecture, the RS model successfully emulates the functional complexity of native intestine. This platform highlights the critical role of regional mechanical cues in epithelial specialization, offering a physiologically relevant tool for studying intestinal physiology, microbial interactions, and drug response.
Spatially defined host-microbe interactions
The intestinal microbiome plays a critical role in intestine function, with host-microbe interactions shaped by localized physiological conditions, such as mucus secretion. Building on our RS model, which mimics the intestine’s mechanical heterogeneity, we next examined how flow-driven microenvironmental differences influenced microbial colonization and subsequent immune responses, barrier function, and absorptive capacity. Commensal bacterium
L. plantarum
, which plays a regulatory role in immune function and absorption within the homeostasis of the small intestine, is well suited as a model microbe for small intestine studies. To achieve this, we introduced
L. plantarum
into the microbial RS (MRS) model, analyzing spatial variations in microbial colonization and their effects on immune and epithelial activity (
Fig. 5A
).

Collectively, our results demonstrate that flow-driven heterogeneity in the RS model governs microbial colonization density, which, in turn, modulates RS immune responses, barrier function, and absorptive function. By recapitulating the spatial complexity of host-microbe interactions, this platform provides a physiologically relevant system to investigate microbial ecology, immune regulation, and therapeutic interventions.
Shear-dependent differential absorption behaviors in small intestine models
To validate the functional heterogeneity observed in transcriptomic analyses, we assessed nutrient and drug absorption across three models: HF
static
, HF
dynamic
, and the RS model. HF
static
model lacked flow-induced stimulation, similar to the conventional Transwell model, while HF
dynamic
model provided homogeneous flow stimulation resembling organ-on-chip systems. To ensure a consistent experimental environment and to minimize systematic error, we evaluated models exhibiting different structural features within the same testing apparatus. Glucose and dextran were selected as representative nutrients to distinguish transcellular and paracellular absorption pathways. Absorption efficiency was quantified by measuring concentration changes in the external fluid over time. Substances transported via the transcellular pathway showed the highest absorption efficiency in the HF

Conversely, dextran absorption followed a different pattern due to its reliance on paracellular transport through intercellular gaps, which depended on the leaky pathway rather than the pore pathway (

These results highlight how flow dynamics regulate absorptive function. Shear stress enhanced paracellular transport via increased junctional permeability (

This shear-induced permeability adjustment provided valuable insights into the macromolecule absorption and other paracellularly transported compounds. For transcellular absorption, shear stress reduced influx transporter activity while enhancing efflux transporter activity, leading to lower absorption rates in RS and HF
dynamic
models compared to HF
static

To further validate the physiological relevance of the RS model, we evaluated the absorption characteristics of six widely used drugs: propranolol, omeprazole, ranitidine, diclofenac, erythromycin, and furosemide. These compounds exhibited distinct apparent permeability coefficients (

By integrating RS shear stress and epithelial heterogeneity, our model recapitulates complex absorption behaviors observed in vivo. The correlation between transporter expression, junctional permeability, and absorption efficiency underscores its utility for predicting nutrient and drug uptake. This platform advances in vitro intestinal modeling by resolving limitations of conventional systems, offering a robust tool for pharmaceutical development and mechanistic studies of intestinal transport.
To further validate the robustness of our model across different biological contexts, we compared the performance of discrete, fitted, and excessive model features under physiological, drug-exposed, and microbial coculture conditions. Immunostaining for epithelial function markers (E-cadherin, VILLIN, MUC2, LYZ, and ALDOB) revealed that only the fitted model maintained RS functional distributions across folds and lumens under all tested conditions (figs. S14 to S17). In contrast, the excessive model exhibited convergent expression due to flow disturbance, and the discrete model suffered from barrier compromise due to structural discontinuities and fluid leakage. During microbial coculture, the discrete model allowed basolateral microbial invasion and epithelial disruption. These findings underscore the importance of precise structural fidelity in maintaining RS functions and barrier protection, further supporting the fitted model’s utility in pharmacological and host-microbe interaction studies.
DISCUSSION
This study introduces a 3D bioprinted human-scale small intestine model that replicates the macrostructure, microenvironmental, and functional properties of native intestinal tissue. By integrating anatomical fidelity, dynamic flow conditions, microbial interactions, and absorption behaviors, the model serves as a physiologically relevant in vitro platform for simulating human intestinal functions.
A primary focus of this work was the design and fabrication of circular folds, a hallmark of the small intestine’s architecture, to mimic the flow dynamics and zonated shear stress observed in vivo. These structural features regulated epithelial barrier integrity and absorption, demonstrating the critical role of coupling morphology with fluid dynamics to ensure physiologically relevant flow dynamics. CFD analyses guided the optimization of fold thickness and distance to balance shear stress gradients with efficient nutrient transport, thereby enabling flow regimes within the physiological shear stress range. Unlike prior models with either simplified straight channels or uncontrolled geometry-driven shear variation, our model achieves stable RS flow patterns that reflect native anatomical zonation (
51
,
52
).
Compared with conventional tissue engineering approaches such as molding, our embedded bioprinting strategy enables high-resolution, support-free fabrication of thin-walled and folded geometries in a single step. This addresses major challenges in constructing hollow, biomimetic structures with spatial precision. A key innovation was the integration of filament-level mass flow recalibration, which eliminated structural voids caused by software-based tangency assumptions (
32
,
53
), ensuring consistent inner wall morphology and preserving shear field fidelity. This improvement enables reproducible fabrication of structures that maintain both flow stability and mechanical integrity, which are critical for long-term culture and functional analysis.
Dynamic flow conditions revealed shear stress as a dual regulator of epithelial function. Cells exposed to controlled fluid flow exhibited enhanced barrier function, including cellular proliferation, mucus secretion, and tight junction formation. However, shear stress also reduced absorptive functions, highlighting its dual role in epithelial modulation. Transcriptomic analysis confirmed RS gene expression changes and uncovered distinct functional profiles between circular folds and lumens, shaped by local mechanical cues. While RS
fold
and HF
dynamic
models shared similarities, PCA and heatmap analysis demonstrated transcriptional divergence, likely reflecting transitional shear gradients. These findings emphasize the importance of capturing microenvironmental heterogeneity, beyond binary static versus flow conditions, to accurately model epithelial physiology.
The model’s ability to support host-microbe interactions was validated through coculture with
L. plantarum
, revealing spatially restricted colonization and localized immune activation. This response reflects in vivo dynamics where barrier properties and flow patterns shape microbial attachment and signaling (
39
,
54
). While demonstrated with a single species, this platform is readily extensible to polymicrobial ecosystems for studying microbial consortia, competition, and host modulation in a controlled setting.
Substance absorption studies confirmed the platform’s relevance for pharmacokinetic applications. By distinguishing paracellular and transcellular pathways using nutrient and drug probes, we demonstrated RS transport characteristics shaped by local flow conditions. The RS model provided a better match to in vivo human drug absorption data than dynamic or static homogeneous controls. By enabling in vitro–in vivo correlation through a power-law model linking
P
app
to
F
intestine
, we provide a quantitative benchmark for physiological predictability, which is often lacking in existing systems (
55
,
56
).
While the model replicates key macroarchitectural and functional features, it currently lacks microstructures such as villi and crypts. These elements, along with systemic signals such as hormonal and neural inputs, represent important next steps for enhancing model complexity. At present, the formation of a fully confluent epithelial layer on the platform is hindered by the 3D morphology of the model surface. Although ~90% epithelial coverage with low variance represents a robust outcome for a 3D printed construct, optimizing the material properties of the embedded medium and bioinks to achieve smoother interfacial transitions and minimize disturbances during printing may facilitate complete epithelial confluency. Future efforts incorporating spatial transcriptomics and real-time imaging could further delineate RS functional responses and host-microbe interactions.
In summary, this zonated small intestine model contributes a platform that bridges structural precision, RS mechanical environments, and functional validation across barrier, microbial, and pharmacological axes. While not replacing all existing models, it expands the toolkit for studying spatial intestinal physiology with greater control and fidelity. This work demonstrates how engineering principles—geometry, flow dynamics, and fabrication precision—can be integrated to create robust intestinal models for disease modeling, therapeutic development, and personalized medicine.
MATERIALS AND METHODS
CFD simulations
CFD simulations were performed using Ansys Fluent (2022) to explore the impact of a biomimetic 3D intestinal structure on fluid flow and shear stress. The k-ε model was used, and a Newtonian fluid model with properties analogous to the culture medium was used for the simulation. In the simulation process, boundary conditions of velocity inlet, free outflow, and zero-slip wall were applied. The simulation results of the fluid flow were presented through the velocity field and wall shear stress, and the velocity values near the wall and the wall shear stress were extracted for scatter plot visualization. The impact of flow rate on shear stress over the circular folds was evaluated by systematically increasing the flow rate from 240 to 3800 μl/min. Therefore, a flow rate of 240 μl/min was selected for subsequent simulations, as it produced a maximum shear stress of ~1 × 10
−3
Pa, which fell within the physiological range observed in the human intestine. 3D intestinal models with different structural characteristics were established using SolidWorks. Flow simulations were conducted on models with various structural features under the flow rate of 240 μl/min, and the results were used as the basis for model design.
Preparation of the embedding medium, the bioink, and the removal bath
PF-127 (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and the H-HPMC (Daido Chemical Co., Japan) were prepared for the embedding medium. The PF-127 powder was dissolved in 1× phosphate-buffered saline (1× PBS) and stored overnight at 4°C. The PF-127 solution was placed on a magnetic stirrer and stirred at 600 rpm with the temperature heated to 60°. The H-HPMC powder was gradually added to the solution, while the stirrer continued to rotate. After the powder was completely dissolved, heating was stopped, and stirring was maintained at a low speed until the solution temperature dropped to 40°. The solution was then transferred to a transparent container and allowed to cool naturally to room temperature.
For the preparation of the bioink, 5% (w/v) GelMA (EFL-GM-60, China), 3% (w/v) PEGDA (Macklin, China; molecular weight, 5000), and 0.5% (w/v) lithium phenyl-2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (EFL, China) were weighed separately, added to the 1× PBS solution, and kept at 37° for 2 hours until completely dissolved.
For the preparation of the removal bath, 5% (w/v) β-cyclodextrin (Macklin, China) was mixed into the 1× PBS to form a homogeneous suspension. The removal bath was used for removing embedding medium.
Printing process
3D models of the intestinal geometry, including circular folds, were designed using SolidWorks 2020 and sliced using Cura 4.11.0, with both layer height and line width set to 400 μm. The prepared bioink and embedded medium were respectively loaded into a syringe and a container, respectively. The integrated heating system of the printer maintained the temperature of both the bioink and the embedded medium at 27°C to ensure thermal stability. A 25-gauge needle was attached to the syringe, and pneumatic extrusion was used for deposition, with the printing speed controlled by the motion system of the printer. Both the printing speed and extrusion pressure were selected on the basis of experimental requirements. For the fitted RS model, a printing speed of 5 mm/s and an extrusion pressure of 2 bar were applied. This ultimately resulted in a printed filament with an approximate width of 450 μm, meeting the filament width specifications required by the optimized printing strategy. Constructs were built layer by layer, producing a final outer diameter of 7.2 mm and a continuous printed length of 25.4 mm per fabrication cycle. A video demonstrating the printing process is provided (movie S1).
Particle image velocimetry
A flow system was established using a peristaltic pump, with tracer particles (10 μm, 4 mg/ml) suspended in the perfusate to visualize fluid motion. A continuous-wave laser (2 W) was used to illuminate the flow field, and high-speed imaging was captured using a high-speed camera at a sampling rate of 1000 frames/s. The recorded image sequences were processed using the PIVlab toolbox in MATLAB to generate velocity contour maps. Quantitative profiles of flow velocity and shear rate in both the circular folds and the lumen were extracted from the resulting data.
Cell culture and cell seeding
Human intestinal epithelial cells Caco-2 (iCell, China) were used for subsequent biological experiments. The cells were grown in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Life Technologies, USA) supplemented with 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Life Technologies, USA), 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) and 1% nonessential amino acids (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cell passaging was conducted every 2 days, with epithelial cells from passages 5 to 20 used for cell seeding and subsequent experiments. Once the epithelial cells formed a tightly connected monolayer, they were washed with PBS, followed by treatment with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Gibco). After centrifugation, the cell pellet was reconstituted in culture medium to form the cell suspension with the cell density of 10
7
/ml and injected into the lumen of model through pipette. The tube was rotated by 90° every 30 min to allow the cells to settle on all surfaces of the internal tube through gravity. To promote even cell attachment across the inner surface, the tube was rotated by 90° every 30 min for 4 hours (seven total rotations). This stepwise static rotation approach, commonly used in hydrogel-based scaffolds, allows gravitational settling while minimizing disruption to early adhesion events. Although complete luminal coverage is not achieved immediately, subsequent static culture allows cells to proliferate and migrate, resulting in a confluent epithelial layer. Quantitative assessment of the fluorescence-stained cytoskeletal images is conducted to calculate the coverage rate of the epithelial layer. Following 7 days of static culture, an epithelial layer achieving ~90% surface coverage was established (fig. S18).
Microbial culture
L. plantarum
was used for subsequent biological experiments.
L. plantarum
was a facultative anaerobe and was cultured in an environment with 5% carbon dioxide at 37°C. MRS broth (Solarbio, China) was dissolved in distilled water and sterilized at high temperature to serve as the culture medium. The microbial culture was maintained in 1-ml Eppendorf tubes, and the microbial density was regularly monitored using a spectrophotometer by measuring the optical density at 600 nm (OD
600
). When the OD value reached 1.0, the microbial culture medium was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min to pellet the microbe at the bottom of the Eppendorf tube. The supernatant was removed, and fresh culture medium was added to resuspend the microbe. The resuspended liquid was divided into five portions and transferred to Eppendorf tubes, with the medium being added to a total volume of 1 ml.
L. plantarum
was further incubated until the OD value again reached 1.0, at which point the above steps were repeated before using the microbial for experiments.
Dynamic culture of intestine model
The intestinal model was placed in a perfusion chamber, which served as the container and the site for dynamic culture. The culture medium for dynamic cultivation was placed in a reservoir and continuously circulated by a peristaltic pump, flowing via rubber tubing into the model. The tubing was connected to the intestine model by a barbed connector, ensuring a tight and leak-proof seal. The culture medium in the perfusion chamber remained static. The entire system ensured the isolation of the internal environment of the model tube from the external environment. Once the epithelial medium was introduced into the perfusion chamber, rotation of the peristaltic pump initiated medium circulation through the loop. The pump’s rotation speed was set to achieve an average flow rate of 240 μl/min. The entire perfusion system was then placed in an incubator for dynamic culture over a period of 48 hours. After the dynamic culture, the intestinal model was extracted for subsequent analysis, and the remaining equipment was cleaned and disinfected with 75% medical-grade alcohol.
Coculture of intestinal model with microbe
To prepare for host-microbe interaction studies, printed intestinal models were first maintained under static conditions for 7 days (days 1 to 7) to support epithelial proliferation and confluence. This was followed by 48 hours of dynamic culture under sterile conditions (days 8 to 9) at a flow rate of 240 μl/min and intended to induce functional maturation of the epithelium. The approximate cell number of the matured model was inferred from preliminary Cell Counting Kit-8 assays and benchmarked against confluent 48-well plates with known surface area and seeding density (1 × 10
5
cells/cm
2
). The average absorbance from the model was about twice that of the plate standard, corresponding to an estimated epithelial coverage of ~2 × 10
5
cells (fig. S19).
On day 10, the circulating medium was replaced with DMEM containing a quantified suspension of
L. plantarum
(~1 × 10
8
colony-forming units/ml, OD
600
-calibrated) and was introduced into the perfusion system at a calculated volume yielding a microbe-to-cell ratio of approximately 0.2. While exact multiplicity of infection could not be precisely controlled, this indirect method ensured consistency across experimental batches. The coculture was then continued for an additional 48 hours (days 10 to 11) under dynamic conditions, and samples were collected on day 12 for downstream analysis.
Protein level evaluation
To evaluate the functional performance of cells after dynamic culture, MUC2, VILLIN, ZO-1, β-catenin, and E-cadherin proteins were stained and semiquantitatively analyzed. Samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, followed by three washes with PBS. Cell was permeabilized using 0.1% PBS-Triton X-100 and then washed again with PBS. Samples were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin blocking buffer at room temperature for 1 hour. Subsequently, different samples were covered with primary antibodies [anti-MUC2 rabbit polyclonal antibody (pAb), anti-VILLIN rabbit pAb, anti–ZO-1 rabbit pAb, anti–β-catenin rabbit pAb, and anti–E-cadherin rabbit pAb; ServiceBio, China] at a 1:100 concentration and incubated overnight at 4°C. The next day, after washing with PBS, secondary antibodies (DyLight 594 goat anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G, Abbkine, China) at a 1:100 dilution was added, incubated for 2 hours in the dark, and followed by two PBS washes. Last, 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (0.5 μg/ml; Solarbio, China) was added for staining the nuclei and incubated for 15 min, followed by three washes with PBS. Fluorescence was observed and images were captured using a confocal fluorescence microscope (Leica TCS SP8, Germany). Images of the same protein were acquired using identical microscope settings. Fluorescence intensity in the images was subsequently analyzed using ImageJ software.
For the fluorescence staining of the F-actin, the samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with 0.1% PBS-Triton X-100 as above. Then, the samples were stained with tetramethyl rhodamine isothiocyanate–phalloidin solution (80 nM; Fushen, China) for 30 min. The cytoskeletal structures within the images were identified using an open-source generalist algorithm “Cellpose” (
57
). Subsequent postprocessing was conducted in ImageJ to quantitatively analyze morphological parameters, including the skeletonized area and aspect ratio.
For the fluorescence staining of the mucus layer, the samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with 0.1% PBS-Triton X-100. Subsequently, the samples were covered with iFluor 488–WGA (Solarbio, China) and incubated for 30 min, followed by washing with PBS. DAPI was then added and incubated for 15 min, and the samples were washed. Last, fluorescence images were observed and captured using a confocal microscope.
Proteins were extracted from the samples for subsequent quantitative analysis. Distinct regions of the intestinal model were classified on the basis of their structural characteristics and sampled accordingly (fig. S8). Radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer was precooled on ice, and phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) was added to a final concentration of 1 mM. RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) was precooled on ice, and PMSF (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) was added to a final concentration of 1 mM. For each group of samples, homogenization was performed at a ratio of 100 μl of lysis buffer per 20 mg of tissue. Homogenization was carried out using a glass homogenizer to ensure thorough cell lysis. Following complete grinding, the homogenate was centrifuged at 10,000
g
for 5 min at 4°C, and the supernatants were collected.
The expression levels of MUC2, VILLIN, and ANPEP were quantitatively determined using human MUC2 enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit, human VILLIN ELISA kit, human aminopeptidase N (APN) ELISA kit (Jianglai Biotechnology, China), respectively. The microelisa stripplate was prepared with designated wells for standard samples, blank controls, and test samples. For the standard wells, 50 μl of standard solutions with varying concentrations were added in triplicate to establish a calibration curve. For the experimental samples, 50 μl of previously collected sample was added to each designated test well. Except for the blank wells, 100 μl of horseradish peroxidase–conjugated detection antibody was added to each well. The plate was incubated at 37°C for 60 min. After incubation, the liquid in each well was aspirated, and the wells were washed five times with washing buffer, allowing 30 s of soaking for each wash cycle. Subsequently, 50 μl of chromogenic reagent A and 50 μl of chromogenic reagent B were added to each well. The plate was then incubated in the dark at 37°C for 15 min. The reaction was terminated by adding 50 μl of stop solution to each well. Last, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader. Protein concentrations in the test samples were calculated by generating a standard curve based on the absorbance values of the standard samples. The expression level of ALP was quantitatively measured using the ALP Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). The supernatant of the sample was incubated with the chromogenic substrate at 37°C for 10 min. Subsequently, the reaction was terminated by adding the stop solution, and the absorbance at 405 nm was measured. The ALP concentration was determined using standard samples for calibration.
RNA sequencing and analysis
Regional differentiation between the circular folds and the lumen in the RS model was achieved by assessing structural thickness features (fig. S8). After retrieving the bioprinted constructs post–dynamic culture, the models were longitudinally bisected with a sterile scalpel to expose internal structures. Subsequently, the model was further manually refined to isolate the annular folds and the luminal region. Each region was rinsed with sterile nuclease-free water, snap frozen in liquid nitrogen for 15 min, and stored at −80°C for downstream RNA extraction. The total RNA was extracted with the TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen Life Technologies), followed by assessment of its concentration, quality, and integrity using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For RNA sample preparation, 3 μg of RNA were used. The process of generating sequencing libraries began with the isolation of mRNA from total RNA using magnetic beads attached to poly-T oligos. Fragmentation was induced with divalent cations at high temperatures in a proprietary Illumina buffer. cDNA synthesis was carried out. To select cDNA fragments of the desired size range [400 to 500 bp (base pairs)], the AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter, Beverly, CA, USA) was used for purification. The fragments, now with adapter sequences on both ends, were enriched through a 15-cycle polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using the Illumina PCR Primer Cocktail. The resulting products were cleaned (AMPure XP system) and quantified with the Agilent high-sensitivity DNA assay on a Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent). Last, the sequencing library was run on the NovaSeq 6000 platform.
The samples were sequenced, generating image files, which were converted into FASTQ raw data by the software provided with the sequencing platform. The sequencing data were further filtered, and HISAT2 (v2.1.0) was used to build the reference genome index and perform alignments. HTSeq (v0.9.1) was used to count the number of reads aligned to each gene, representing the raw expression level, and FPKM (fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads) was used to normalize the expression levels. Differential expression analysis between comparison groups was performed using DESeq2 (v1.42.1). The difference expression of genes was analyzed using Benjamini-Hochberg method, with the criteria for differentially expressed genes set at |log
2
foldchange| > 1 and a significance threshold of the adjusted
P
value (
P
.adjust) < 0.05.
Permeability assays
We developed a custom-designed perfusion chamber incorporating two internal partitions, which divided the chamber into three isolated compartments. Each model was housed in an isolated chamber with static culture medium outside the model, simulating a unidirectional absorption setup (fig. S3A). To quantify absorption kinetics, samples were periodically collected from the static medium to assess the concentration of the transferred substances. For carbohydrate absorption studies, glucose (G8270, Thermo Fisher, Germany) was added to the circulating solution at a concentration of 10 mg/ml. Both the static and perfused solutions consisted of 1× PBS to eliminate interference from additional carbohydrates present in culture medium. During the absorption assay, samples from the static compartment were collected every 20 min for glucose concentration analysis, performed using the Glucose Content Assay Kit (Solarbio, China). The three separate compartments served as biological replicates. To investigate macromolecular transport, FITC-dextran (4 kDa; 46944, Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) was added to the circulating phase at a concentration of 10 mg/ml. Because of the large molecular size of dextran, a longer sampling interval was adopted, with samples from the static phase collected every 1 hour. The concentration was measured using a microplate reader at 490 nm. Metoprolol (51384-51-1, Aladdin) and colchicine (64-86-8, Aladdin) were introduced into the circulating solution at concentrations of 0.5 and 2 mg/ml, respectively. Samples from the static medium were collected at 20-min intervals and analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography. During the test, A C18 chromatographic column (Agilent) was used, with a mobile phase of acetate buffer and acetonitrile (4:1). The column temperature was maintained at 30°C, with the flow rate of 1 ml/min and the detection wavelength of 274 nm. In addition, propranolol (R126019, RHAWN), omeprazole (R002048, RHAWN), ranitidine (R304317, RHAWN), diclofenac (R038361, RHAWN), erythromycin (E6036, RHAWN), and furosemide (HY-B0135, MedChemExpress) were added to the circulating solution at a concentration of 0.1 mg/ml. Samples were also collected every 20 min from the static phase and analyzed using liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry system.
