Background
Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a complex neurodegenerative disorder that is the primary cause of dementia. Currently, it is estimated that 55 million individuals worldwide are affected by dementia, including AD, and this number is expected to increase over time [
1
]. The disease is marked by the accumulation of amyloid plaques and tau neurofibrillary tangles in the brain. As a result, biomarkers related to amyloid and tau pathology are integral to the biological definition of AD [
2
–
5
].
Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI), regarded as the prodromal phase of AD, is characterised by mild yet measurable cognitive changes in the elderly and represents a high-risk condition for progressing to AD dementia [
6
]. However, MCI presents variability in clinical presentation, pathology and molecular characteristics, and does not always lead to AD dementia. Detailed MCI subtyping reduces disease heterogeneity by stratifying individuals into distinct subgroups based on their clinical, molecular, and imaging profiles. This approach minimises the variability within subgroups, allowing for a more precise understanding of the diverse molecular mechanisms underlying MCI and AD. Enhanced subtyping also increases the accuracy of predictions regarding which individuals with MCI are most likely to progress to AD, and additionally offers better targeting in clinical trials. Thus, early identification of high-risk MCI subtypes is essential, as it can facilitate more effective preventive interventions, enhancing the chance of delaying or potentially preventing the progression to AD. Furthermore, the detection of particular MCI subtypes is critical for applying precision medicine strategies and, ultimately, for developing effective, subtype-specific treatment and prevention strategies for AD.
Significant advancements in AD treatment have emerged in recent years, primarily focused on disease-modifying therapies (DMTs) and symptom-targeting medications. Eight FDA (Food and Drug Administration)-approved drugs currently target AD [
7
,
8
], targeting either amyloid pathology or cognitive, behavioural, or sleep-related symptoms. An overview of drugs in AD, grouped by category and target, is provided in Table

.

Although there are existing drugs for individuals with MCI, none currently addresses the substantial heterogeneity within this population. This limitation may reduce treatment efficacy, as factors such as variations in molecular mechanisms, cognitive function and disease progression patterns across MCI subtypes remain unconsidered. Petersen and Morris conducted extensive research on the heterogeneity of MCI in its early stages and proposed a classification system that divides MCI into four clinical subtypes according to memory impairment [
19
]. This system differentiates between amnestic and non-amnestic MCI, with each type further classified into single-domain MCI, which involves impairment in just one cognitive domain, and multiple-domain MCI, where impairments occur across various cognitive domains. Amnestic MCI, irrespective of whether it is single- or multiple-domain MCI, has been reported to have a comparatively high conversion rate to dementia, primarily AD dementia, ranging from 10 to 15% per year [
20
]. Additionally, individuals with MCI are categorised into early (EMCI) or late (LMCI) MCI based on the extent of cognitive decline and performance on cognitive screening tools [
21
–
23
].
Numerous studies have been conducted to provide further insights into MCI, beyond the aforementioned categorisations, and AD subjects. Many of these studies have used specific cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers (Abeta, total tau, phosphorylated tau proteins), neuropsychological scores, CSF proteomics, and brain imaging data to identify other MCI and AD subtype classification systems [
24
–
28
]. However, these rely on a single data type. Other studies have incorporated multiple imaging data types, such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and positron emission tomography (PET), however, their analyses often report results separately for each data type or compare findings after normalising the data within each modality, rather than fully integrating and analysing the data types simultaneously within an integrated framework [
29
]. Furthermore, some studies have applied an association-based approach, where subtypes are initially defined using one data modality (such as proteomics) and subsequently linked to other modalities through post hoc analysis. While these approaches provide valuable insights, they do not fully exploit the potential of multi-modal integration, where different data types are analysed simultaneously within a comprehensive computational framework [
30
,
31
].
Subtyping using the integration of multi-modal data is expected to capture simultaneously a wider spectrum of the patient’s profile characteristics, which can predict conversion to AD more accurately or define more accurately distinct pathological processes and molecular functions for different subgroups. Indeed, various studies have employed multi-modal data by integrating either different imaging data types (such as MRI and PET), or different types of omics data (such as genomics, transcriptomics, and epigenomics), or selected established markers from both imaging and molecular modalities. These studies have used both unsupervised [
32
–
37
] and supervised [
38
,
39
] approaches to define subgroups among subjects with MCI, normal cognition and AD. Those subgroups can help to provide valuable insights into the heterogeneity of MCI, enabling a deeper understanding of variations in diagnosis, progression and underlying pathology.
To identify subtypes within MCI and related cognitive conditions without relying on predefined labels, various unsupervised methods have been applied. Feng et al
.
[
32
] used a Deep Generalised Canonical Correlation Analysis (DGCCA) framework, to identify two imaging-driven MCI subtypes based on multi-modal data, including structural MRI images, amyloid-b PET, and fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-PET scans from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI). Each subtype exhibited unique characteristics in cognitive decline, brain volume changes, and survival patterns. These subtypes not only demonstrate differences in disease severity but also align with specific genetic markers linked to AD, illustrating the potential of DGCCA in providing clearer disease subtypes beyond early and late-stage MCI. Another study [
33
] investigated the heterogeneity within MCI by employing the Similarity Network Fusion (SNF) algorithm to classify MCI patients based on single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and gene expression profiles, using data exclusively from MCI patients in the ADNI cohort. SNF is a network-based method specifically developed to integrate several multi-omic data modalities simultaneously [
40
]. The two subtypes detected differed in AD conversion time, cognitive scales, brain atrophy, and enriched molecular pathways. The findings of this study highlight the potential of genetic and molecular profiling in predicting AD progression among MCI patients and provide a foundation for more personalised therapeutic approaches. Iturria-Medina et al
.
[
34
] introduced an innovative approach to understanding AD by creating a comprehensive molecular taxonomy that addresses both the underlying heterogeneity and progression of the disease. By integrating multiple molecular data layers, including epigenomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics, using the SNF algorithm, the authors identified three distinct molecular subtypes of AD, each reflecting unique biological profiles correlated with different disease pathways, cognitive effects, and brain pathology characteristics. This molecular classification captures the heterogeneity of AD by distinguishing different disease subtypes, while also mapping their progression trajectories, from no cognitive impairment to advanced AD. An additional study [
35
] used a multi-layer network framework within a cohort of participants with MCI, AD, and cognitively normal (CN) classification to capture the heterogeneity and progression of AD. By integrating data from structural MRI, amyloid-b PET, CSF biomarkers, cognitive assessments, and genetic information, the researchers applied a community detection method to find two groups of individuals with shared similarities across modalities.
In contrast, supervised methods enable the classification of MCI subtypes and enhance the capacity to predict subtype-specific outcomes and disease progression trajectories. For instance, a study by Li et al
.
[
38
] focused on predicting the MCI-to-AD progression using a subtyping-based classification approach. Initially, MCI subtypes were determined using SNF based solely on molecular data, including SNPs and gene expression profiles, as established in their previous study [
33
]. Subsequently, they selected relevant features from SNPs, gene expression, and structural MRI data, which were then used to build separate classifiers for each subtype. These classifiers employed the variational Bayes approximation with a probabilistic multiple kernel learning algorithm to distinguish between progressive MCI (P-MCI) and stable MCI (S-MCI) within each identified subtype.
Another study [
39
] used a heterogeneous mixture learning (HML) method to simultaneously subtype MCI individuals and predict their conversion to AD. Using multi-modal data – including CSF biomarkers, five MRI volumes, Apolipoprotein E (APOE) genotype data, and age at examination – they built a decision tree model that identified five MCI distinct subtypes with varying levels of CSF biomarker abnormalities, brain atrophy, and cognitive decline. These were further grouped into three categories: one subtype with low conversion rates, resembling cognitively normal individuals; three subtypes with moderate conversion rates; and one subtype with a high conversion rate, closely aligning with characteristics of AD patients.
While extensive research has been conducted on MCI subtyping, the majority of studies rely predominantly on established CSF biomarkers, preselected features, or exclusively molecular or imaging data. Consequently, there is still space for exploration regarding the integration of molecular and imaging data, independently of traditional biomarkers, to gain a more comprehensive and unbiased understanding of the underlying mechanisms in MCI.
Motivated by the lack of an approach that capitalises simultaneously on molecular and imaging modalities to stratify individuals with MCI into meaningful subgroups, we implemented a clustering-based approach in this study, by integrating multi-modal data using SNF. The SNF method has been used in different studies to subtype MCI subjects [
33
] and patients with AD [
34
], as well as in other diseases such as Parkinson’s disease [
41
]. The advantage of the SNF approach is that it integrates sample-similarity networks built from each distinct data type and constructs a final fused network, which contains patient relationships combining information from all data sources. This integrated network is then used to classify patients into distinct subgroups using clustering. Using this framework, we integrated imaging and omics data to stratify MCI subjects into distinct subgroups based on two data modalities: (i) structural imaging features from brain MRIs (regional cortical thickness, cortical and subcortical volumes and surface area measurements) and (ii) CSF peptides/proteins abundances from ADNI [
42
]. We then performed post hoc analyses to characterise MCI subgroups in terms of clinical and biological characteristics known to be associated with AD i.e., established CSF markers, APOE4 status, and neuropsychological scores. Additionally, we interpreted MCI subgroups profiles by examining biological/molecular processes through enrichment analyses, and by identifying imaging patterns. We further explored subgroup-specific disease progression patterns towards AD and longitudinal changes in cognitive function. We used the Data Integration Analysis for Biomarker discovery using Latent components (DIABLO) method [
43
] to explore signatures that characterise each subgroup separately and to define associations of imaging features and peptides/proteins. Finally, we applied a computational drug repurposing approach to identify potential candidate repurposed drugs for the recognised subgroups of the MCI cohort.
Methods
Overview
The overall methodology of this study is depicted in the workflow Fig.
1
and is divided into three main parts: (i) Collection and Preprocessing of Data, which includes multi-modal data selection, feature extraction and preprocessing. The features of the two modalities (imaging features and peptide/protein abundances) were retrieved from the ADNI dataset and preprocessed. Clinical indices were also extracted to use in downstream analysis; (ii) Multi-modal Data Integration and Clustering, where the SNF algorithm is employed to fuse the data, followed by the use of the Spectral clustering algorithm to cluster the subjects; and (iii) Downstream Analysis, where we performed clustering comparisons using individuals from MCI subgroup 1 and MCI subgroup 2 as well as healthy controls and AD patients. Molecular and imaging signatures were identified through DIABLO analysis, using as input the clustering result (MCI subgroup 1 and MCI subgroup 2 separately). Additionally, correlation analyses between each modality and clinical variables or neuropsychological scores were conducted within each subgroup, using the individuals from each subgroup and healthy controls as a whole cohort. Moreover, MCI individuals were labelled within subgroups according to their future conversion stage. We further examined longitudinal differences in cognitive function among subjects in each subgroup, healthy controls, and AD patients. Finally, an in silico drug repurposing approach is applied, in which proteins differentially expressed between each subgroup and healthy controls and strongly associated with imaging features, were selected and subsequently associated with potential drug candidates for each subgroup.

Collection and preprocessing of data
This study used data from ADNI, an initiative established in 2003. ADNI is a public–private partnership under the guidance of Principal Investigator Michael W. Weiner, MD, with the key goal being the evaluation of whether the combination of serial MRI, PET data, various biological markers, clinical and neuropsychological assessments, can help in characterising the progression of MCI and early AD. The ADNI dataset includes data from a large significant number of CN, MCI, and AD dementia patients, collected from over 50 centres across the US and Canada, with follow-up evaluations conducted every six months. The study procedures were approved by institutional review boards, regarding all participating institutions, and written informed consent was secured from all participants whose data contributed to the ADNI dataset [
42
,
44
]. For up-to-date information, see
www.adni-info.org
.
Our study includes data from individuals that have MRI as the imaging modality and CSF proteomics at baseline visit. Specifically, this presupposition resulted in the inclusion of 119 Late MCI subjects from the ADNI I project that was used in the integration scheme, and comprises of 80 CN subjects, and 64 AD dementia patients, that were used for the comparisons with the clustering output (downstream comparisons – not in the fused scheme). The data were obtained from the ‘
ADNIMERGE’
R package v0.0.1 [
45
].
All subjects’ (MCI, CN, AD patients) records were retrieved, including CSF biomarker data, structural MRI data, CSF multiplex MRM panel (proteomics), APOE genotype data, neuropsychological tests and other established CSF markers of neuronal injury and the inflammatory response. The diagnosis for AD dementia patients and MCI subjects primarily relied on neuropsychological assessments, including the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), Clinical Dementia Rating–Sum of Boxes (CDRSB), and Wechsler Memory Scale Logical Memory II. The AD dementia patients evaluated in this study were diagnosed with Alzheimer’s dementia or syndrome based on clinical evaluations and neuropsychological test results, without measurements of pathological markers like amyloid-beta (Ab) or tau protein.

Structural MRI
Structural MRI data were retrieved from the ADNI dataset using the ‘ADNIMERGE’ package, which included results from 1.5T MRI scans in the ADNI I phase. Cortical reconstruction and volumetric segmentation were conducted by the University of California San Francisco through the FreeSurfer image analysis suite (version 4.3 for 1.5T MRI scans). The scan data were processed in a cross-sectional manner utilising the 2010 Desikan-Killany atlas [
46
]. After removing regions with missing values (NAs), we concluded to a total of 111 region-of-interest (ROI) volumes, including cortical and subcortical volumes, 68 ROIs of cortical thickness (based on average measurements per region) and 70 ROIs of total surface area.
We normalised ROI volumes and surface area features to correct for the different head sizes of individuals. We used a common normalisation method which uses a linear regression model between ROI volumes and surface area features and intracranial volume (ICV) values to predict ROI-adjusted volume and surface area [
47
]. Adjusted volume is obtained as follows:

CSF proteomic data
The CSF proteomic dataset used in this study was obtained from the ADNI targeted mass spectrometry (multiple reaction monitoring, MRM) analyses [
48
]. For our analyses, the final normalised peptide intensity data were used. Detailed technical information regarding mass spectrometry platform data acquisition, quality control metrics, and validation protocols is available in the ADNI data primer titled ‘Use of Targeted Mass Spectrometry Proteomics Strategies to Identify CSF-Based Biomarkers in AD’. The ADNI MRM panel included 567 peptides found in the CSF, corresponding to 222 unique proteins, with the selection made based on their relevance to AD. For each peptide, two mass transitions were monitored. The results are reported in arbitrary signal intensity units on a natural logarithm scale. Given that alternative splicing or post-translational modifications can lead to biologically significant differences in the levels of two peptides from the same protein, we analysed the ‘log (Peptide Intensities)’ for validated transitions (640 detectable transitions from 320 peptides across 142 unique proteins).
CSF biomarkers
The CSF biomarkers comprised the following measurements: Ab(1–42), Ab(1–40), and Ab(1–38) peptide levels; total tau (t-tau) and phosphorylated tau (p-tau) protein levels; and ratios such as tTau/Ab(1–42), pTau/Ab(1–42), Ab(1–42)/Ab(1–40), and Ab(1–38)/Ab(1–40). Levels of Ab(1–42) (IMN), t-tau, and p-tau were acquired using Roche Elecsys® immunoassays, from which we calculated the tTau/Ab(1–42)(IMN) and pTau/Ab(1–42)(IMN) ratios. These CSF biomarkers were reported as quantitative variables. However, the levels were often represented with inequality signs when the values reached the upper limit or were below the detection limit of the immunoassays. In the cases of Ab(1–42)(IMN), we considered ‘ > 1700’ as equivalent to 1,700 pg/mL. Concentrations of Ab(1–42)(MS), Ab(1–40)(MS), and Ab(1–38)(MS) were measured using 2D-UPLC tandem mass spectrometry, and we calculated the Ab(1–42)/Ab(1–40)(MS) and Ab(1–38)/Ab(1–40)(MS) ratios based on these measurements. CSF Beta secretase-1 (BACE1) activity assessed using ELISA was also obtained from ADNI.
APOE genotype
APOE genotyping data were derived from blood DNA samples collected from each individual using an APOE genotyping kit. Our analysis primarily concentrated on the number of e4 alleles (APOE4), since the e4 allele is widely recognised as a significant risk factor for AD.
Neuropsychological scores
We obtained scores that reflect domain-specific measures of memory, executive functioning, language, visuo-spatial skills and daily independence from the ADNI dataset. These include the scores: Alzheimer’s Disease Assessment Scale-Cognitive Subscale (ADAS-Cog: ADAS11, ADAS13, ADASQ4), the Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test (RAVLT – immediate, learning, forgetting, perc.forgetting), Logical memory-delayed recall (LDELTOTAL), Logical Memory-immediate recall (LIMMTOTAL), Clock Drawing (CLOCKSCOR), Clock Copying (COPYSCOR), Category Fluency (Animals—CATANIMSC), Category Fluency (Vegetables—CATVEGESC), Boston Naming test (BNTTOTAL), Digit Symbol Substitution (DIGITSCOR), Trail Making Test (part A and B), Functional Activities Questionnaire (FAQ), Mini Mental State Examination (MMSE) and Clinical Dementia rating-Sum of Boxes (CDRSB). We also derived the composite scores of the four cognitive domains: memory (ADNI_MEM), executive function (ADNI_EF), language (ADNI_LAN), and visuo-spatial function (ADNI_VS).
CSF markers of neuronal injury and the inflammatory response
We retrieved data on the CSF levels of the following markers from the ADNI dataset: the neuronal injury markers Visinin-like protein-1 (VILIP-1), and Neurofilament light chain (NFL) protein; the synaptic dysfunction markers Synaptosomal-associated protein, 25 kDa (SNAP-25), and Neurogranin (NGRN); and the inflammation marker YKL-40. The levels of VILIP-1, SNAP-25 and NGRN were obtained using the Erenna® immunoassay system. The levels of NFL were measured using ELISA method. The levels of YKL-40 were obtained using a MicroVue YKL-40 ELISA. Data for the markers VILIP-1, SNAP-25, NGRN, and YKL were not available for all individuals and were limited to approximately 50 CN, 60 MCI subjects and 15 AD patients.
Data integration and clustering
Subtype identification with similarity network fusion (SNF)
Integration of the four types of data was achieved using the SNF algorithm through the R package
SNFtool
v2.3.1 [
49
]. Overall, we considered two modalities: structural imaging features and peptides/protein abundances. Despite cortical thickness measurements, cortical and subcortical volumes and surface area measurements being derived from the same data modality (imaging modality), they are calculated with different algorithms, allowing us to treat them as distinct sources. The SNF algorithm facilitates the combination of different measurement types within a population. It begins by forming a sample similarity network for each of the four data types and then iteratively integrates these networks into a fused subject-to-subject similarity network using message-passing theory method [
50
]. In this network, the nodes represent individuals, and the weighted edges are equivalent to pairwise individual similarities. This algorithm is robust against variations in data scales, collection biases, and noise across different measurement types and is advantageous since it does not require prior feature selection. In this context, the non-linear data fusion algorithm enables the utilisation of common and complementary information from the various data types.
Similarity matrices for each of the four data types were computed using Euclidean distance with the nearest neighbours parameter of K = 12 (K is the number of neighbours used to construct the similarity matrices, adhering to the suggested value of sample size/10 from the SNF developers), a normalisation parameter of alpha/sigma = 0.8 (where alpha/sigma is a hyper-parameter that adjusts edge weight scaling between 0.3 and 0.8), and a total number of algorithm iterations set to T = 10 (ranging from 10 to 50). Spectral clustering is the default clustering method for the
SNFtool
package, which is an unsupervised soft clustering method rooted in graph theory [
51
]. We applied the SNF
spectralClustering
function to identify groups based on the complete fused affinity matrix, employing 1000 iterations that resampled 85% of participants. To determine the optimal number of clusters (ranging from 2 to 10), we used the rotation cost [
52
] and eigen-gap [
51
] methods. We selected a cluster number of 2 based on SNF’s
estimateNumberOfClustersGivenGraph
function, as this reflected the minimum optimal number of clusters. This decision guaranteed an adequate distribution of the 119 MCI subjects across each cluster for further analyses.
Normalised mutual information (NMI) was used to interpret the contribution of each data type to the fused clustering and each feature to the fused similarity network. NMI detects alignment between two input matrices, helping in identifying which data types or features strongly influence the fused similarity network estimated by SNF. The NMI values range from 0 to 1 and there is no established threshold to determine the significance of this value. To do so, we used NMI-based functions from the
SNFtool
package. Specifically, the
concordanceNetworkNMI()
function was used to compare the similarity network of each individual data type with the final fused clustering to assess data-type-level contribution. Additionally, the
rankFeaturesByNMI()
function was used to compute NMI values between the similarity profile of each feature and the fused network derived from all model features. This allows us to rank individual features based on their alignment with the final integrated representation captured by SNF. Features with higher NMI scores indicate a stronger contribution to participant similarity. [
40
]
Comparative analysis of data-driven subgroups
Multiple pairwise comparisons were performed to assess the differences between the baseline imaging features, peptides/proteins intensities, age, sex, education, APOE4 status, neuropsychological scores, CSF biomarkers (e.g., Abeta, t-tau, p-tau) and other established markers of neuronal injury and the inflammatory response, across clustering solutions, and compared to CN individuals and AD patients.
Differential expression analysis of peptides/proteins
The
limma
R package, available from the Bioconductor, was used for the differential abundance analysis [
53
]. This uses a linear model with an empirical Bayes method. Age, sex and APOE4 status were included as covariates in all comparisons. The False Discovery Rate (FDR < 0.05) method was applied for multiple testing corrections and Fold Change (FC) cutoff (abs(logFC) > 0.10). The analysis was conducted using peptide intensities as input to the models.
Differential analysis of imaging features
Analysis of imaging features (cortical thickness measurements, cortical and subcortical volumes and surface area measurements) was performed using a linear regression model implemented via the
lm
and
glm
functions from the
stats
R package to assess associations across all pairwise comparisons. Specifically, each imaging feature was modelled as the dependent variable, with group membership as the independent variable. Age, sex, APOE4 status and education were included as covariates in all comparisons. While adjusting for site-related variability is generally suggested in multi-site studies, all MRI data used in our study, have undergone harmonisation to reduce scanner- and site-related effects [
54
]. Given this level of standardisation, and considering the small and uneven distribution of participants across sites in our dataset, we chose not to include the site variable as a covariate in our models. Significant features were identified using FDR-adjusted
p
-value < 0.05.
Differential analysis of additional ADNI measurements
To detect differences in demographic characteristics, neuropsychological scores, and CSF markers, we first assessed normality across all groups using the Shapiro–Wilk test. As the data did not meet normality assumption, the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied across groups. For pairwise comparisons, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to examine differences between individuals in subgroup 1 and subgroup 2, as well as between the CN group and each MCI subgroup independently, and between AD patients and each MCI subgroup independently. Significant features were identified using an FDR-adjusted
p
-value threshold of < 0.05. For categorical variables, group differences were assessed using the Chi-square test of independence. Additionally, Fisher’s exact test was performed to compare the overall distribution of individuals between the two subgroups within the AT(N) framework, as some AT(N) patterns contained low sample counts. The test assessed whether certain AT(N) patterns were overrepresented beyond what would be expected by chance.
Enrichment analysis
Over-representation pathway enrichment analysis was conducted for proteins. For peptide intensities, if at least one peptide of a specific protein was identified as significant, the name of the targeted protein was used as input for the enrichment analysis. Enrichment analyses were performed on the proteins using the R packages
clusterProfiler
v4.12.0 [
55
] available in Bioconductor and
enrichR
v3.2 [
56
]. These packages provide a complete set of gene functional annotation tools to detect the enriched Gene Ontology (GO) terms. More specifically,
enrichGO
(clusterProfiler) and
enrichr
(enrichR) functions were applied, using as input the differentially abundant proteins from the
limma
output. The BP (Biological Process), CC (Cellular Component) and MF (Molecular Function) terms with a significant corrected
p
-value (Benjamini-Hochberg < 0.05) were selected.
Identification of imaging and molecular signatures for the two subgroups
To further investigate the heterogeneity within MCI subgroups, we utilised the clustering output and transformed the unsupervised pipeline into a supervised approach. This method aims to identify distinctive imaging and molecular patterns for each subgroup. Defining unique signatures that characterise each subgroup is crucial, as it provides a more precise understanding of MCI subgroups.
Integrative biomarker discovery using DIABLO
Imaging measurements and peptide/protein data for each subgroup were integrated using DIABLO, implemented in the
mixOmics
R package v6.28.0 [
57
]. This approach aims to distinguish between the CN and MCI subgroups and to identify the optimal markers from these modalities. DIABLO is a supervised multivariate method that extends sparse Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA) for classification purposes. CCA evaluates the correlation between block variables, specifically the imaging and peptidomics/proteomics blocks used in this study. The sparse version of CCA performs variable selection, enabling the exclusion of noisy variables and redundancies within and between datasets; only the predictive variables concerning diagnosis status are retained in the final model. DIABLO operates in two phases: the first involves determining the optimal number of latent components, while the second fixes the optimal number of variables to select from each dataset. DIABLO’s objective is to leverage combined imaging features and peptidomics/proteomics data to distinguish between CN and MCI subgroups, ultimately identifying a minimal set of markers that reflects heterogeneity status. The separation between sample groups was assessed based on the correlation of the top 40 imaging and peptide/protein features derived from the first component of partial least squares least discriminant analysis (PLS-DA). We implemented DIABLO for MCI subgroup 1 versus CN, and MCI subgroup 2 versus CN as separate analyses. The
network
function illustrates the relevance associations network. This function circumvents the computational intensity of Pearson correlation matrices on large datasets by directly calculating a pairwise similarity matrix from the latent components of the integrative approach (block.plsda method). The similarity value between variable pairs is derived from the sum of correlations between the original variables and each latent component of the model. Consequently, the values in the similarity matrix serve as a robust approximation of Pearson correlation [
58
–
60
].
We extracted the edge lists from the two network schemes described above for subsequent analysis and visualisation. Using these outputs, we generated an association map in the form of a heatmap to highlight the top features responsible for subgroup separation, specifically between CN and subgroup 1, and between CN and subgroup 2. We further filtered the associations to include only those with the highest weights (top 75th percentile), focusing on peptides/proteins that were differentially expressed and their corresponding imaging features.
Diagnostic capability of potential markers
We used the union of highly weighted associations (between imaging features and proteins from specific peptides) identified by DIABLO analysis for comparisons between MCI subgroup 1 versus CN and MCI subgroup 2 versus CN to assess the diagnostic potential of candidate markers for MCI subgroup 1 and MCI subgroup 2. Receiver-operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed in R using a generalised linear model (
glm
function) with a binomial fit applied to each imaging and peptide/protein association for the binary diagnosis subset (subgroup 1 versus subgroup 2). The
pROC
package was used to generate ROC curve plots and to calculate the area under the curve (AUC), along with the DeLong 95% confidence interval for the AUC [
61
]. For each pair of associations, we constructed four models: the first included only the imaging feature, the second included only the peptide/protein, the third combined both the imaging feature and peptide/protein, and the fourth included an interaction term between the imaging feature and peptide/protein. We selected models where the
p
-value for the single variable or pair of associations was < 0.05. A scatterplot was generated to illustrate the AUC values across models, highlighting candidate markers that best distinguish MCI subjects in subgroup 1 and subgroup 2.
Correlation analysis between imaging features, peptidomic/proteomic markers, and clinical indices
To identify regional and molecular patterns associated with clinical measurements and neuropsychological scores, partial Spearman’s rank correlations were conducted. Specifically, partial correlations were calculated separately for each subgroup between imaging measurements and clinical indices (clinical measurements and neuropsychological tests), as well as between proteomic/peptide measurements and clinical indices, using the
pcor.test
function from the R package
ppcor
[
62
]. Analyses involving imaging features were adjusted for age, sex, APOE4 status, and education, while those involving proteomic/peptide features were adjusted for age, sex, and APOE4 status. Heatmaps illustrating the relationships between imaging features, proteomics/peptides, and clinical indices were generated using the
ComplexHeatmap
R package [
63
]. Hierarchical clustering was applied simultaneously to the rows and columns of each matrix.
Labelling of MCI subjects according to their reported future conversion to AD
We labelled individuals according to their future conversion to AD, as recorded in the ADNI dataset, to further characterise the clustering output and assess the relationship between subgroup membership and disease progression. Motivated by previous studies that classified MCI subjects based on their conversion timing to AD using similar categorisation approaches [
38
,
39
], we implemented a refined classification scheme to enhance the resolution of the MCI cohort. Specifically, among the 119 MCI subjects, we were able to determine conversion status for 108 subjects (with the remaining lacking clear conversion data). We categorised the 108 MCI subjects as follows: those who progressed to AD between 6 and 12 months from baseline were labelled as 'Fast converters_6-12', those who converted between 18 and 30 months as 'Fast converters_18-30', those who converted at 36 months or later as 'Slow converters_36-last follow-up', and those who did not convert to AD by the last recorded visit as 'Stable'. This classification builds upon existing methodologies by incorporating multiple time windows to capture more granular conversion patterns within the MCI population. Additionally, a Chi-square test was conducted to assess whether the distribution of individuals across the predefined conversion categories differed significantly between the two subgroups.
Longitudinal cognitive assessment
To define how the subgroups of MCIs progressed over time based on the cognitive assessment we used the CDRSB and MMSE scores, as well as the four composite scores (memory—ADNI_MEM, executive functioning—ADNI_EF, language—ADNI_LAN and visuo-spatial skills—ADNI_VS) from each subject from baseline to 36 months (3 years from baseline). Pairwise comparison using Wilcoxon test (FDR < 0.05) for each visit was conducted to define differences between the MCI subgroups and the CN individuals, as well as AD patients.
Computational subgroup-specific drug repurposing analysis
An in silico drug repurposing approach was employed to identify potential candidate drugs for each subgroup separately. This subgroup-specific strategy is essential since distinct molecular profiles require tailored therapeutic interventions.
To perform computational drug repurposing using peptide/protein data, it was necessary to confirm that the direction of protein expression between MCI subjects and CN was consistent at the transcriptomic level. To achieve this, we first reviewed the literature for evidence of CSF gene expression patterns and explored publicly available CSF gene expression (CSF transcriptomics – microarray and RNA-Seq) data that aligned with our markers of interest, specifically the differentially expressed markers identified in comparisons between MCI subgroup 1 and CN, and MCI subgroup 2 and CN (converted from UniProt IDs to corresponding gene symbols). However, as such CSF datasets were not available, we sought to identify the corresponding genes that were differentially expressed, in brain tissue datasets. We gathered this information from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database [
64
] and performed analyses on publicly available brain tissue datasets using GEO2R (
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/geo2r/
) [
65
]. The corresponding GSE numbers are presented in Fig.
1
in the Supplementary Material. Genes showing consistent differential expression and strong association with imaging features from the DIABLO analysis were subsequently used as input for drug repurposing via the Drug Gene Budger (DGB) tool (
https://maayanlab.cloud/DGB/
) [
66
].
DGB is a web-based application that ranks drugs and small molecules based on their ability to up- or down-regulate a target gene, using as input gene symbols from transcriptomic data. For each small molecule, DGB offers important statistical metrics, to quantify the degree of gene expression alteration and assess the significance of differential expression, computed by the limma method. The experimental data used in DGB is sourced from the LINCS L1000 dataset [
67
], the original Connectivity Map (CMap) dataset [
68
], and the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) [
64
].
Following the approach outlined by Bourdakou et al
.
[
69
], we downloaded the lists from the DGB tool (accessed August 2024) and retained significant small molecules with a
q
-value < 0.05 and an absolute log2FC ≥ 1 that were found to reverse the expression of the selected genes in L1000 data. Using a majority voting approach, we prioritised candidate drugs based on the number of differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) they affected, selecting those that reversed the expression of at least 50% of the DEPs. To further explore the selected drugs, we searched for the corresponding gene targets of each drug in CLUE – The Drug Repurposing Hub (
https://repo-hub.broadinstitute.org/repurposing
) [
70
].
Pathway analysis of the drug target genes was performed through the
enrichR
R package (using the
enrichr
function with KEGG 2021 Human version, Reactome 2022, and GO BP, MF, and CC 2023 databases) and the
clusterProfiler
R package (using the
enrichGO
,
enrichKEGG
, and
enrichPathway
functions). Analyses were conducted using both gene symbols and Entrez IDs. Due to the limited number of targets for some candidate drugs, no threshold was applied for pathway selection; instead, a membership-based analysis was performed. Pathways targeted by each drug gene target were identified, and to refine the selection of candidate drugs, we determined common pathways based on the union of all pathway-related databases from drug gene target pathways and enrichment analyses from the clustering output. The final proposed drug list for each subgroup was based on drugs represented in both
enrichR
(gene symbols) and
clusterProfiler
(using Entrez IDs and gene symbols).
Results
We analysed data from 119 individuals classified as Late MCI from the ADNI I dataset, each with available multi-modal data. These include imaging features of MRI (cortical thickness measurements, cortical and subcortical volumes, and surface area measurements) and peptide/protein abundances. Hence, these four data types were integrated to identify MCI subgroups within the Late MCI group.
To systematically explore the heterogeneity of MCI and identify distinct subgroups, we conducted a series of interrelated analyses, generating a comprehensive set of results:
(1) We first generated an integrated multi-modal network. (2) Clustering was then performed on this network to identify MCI subgroups, which were subsequently characterised based on (3) their associations with clinical and neuropsychological scores, established AD biomarkers, (4) distinct biological and (5) imaging profiles, and (6) integrated imaging and peptide/proteomic signatures. (7) Further correlation analyses were conducted linking imaging features and peptide/protein measurements with clinical indices. (8) Additionally, we characterised MCI subgroups based on future disease conversion, providing insights into disease progression. Moreover, (9) changes in cognitive function over three years were analysed, defining differential cognitive trajectories among MCI subgroups. (10) Lastly, the identification of subgroup-specific therapeutic candidates using an in silico drug repurposing approach was carried out.

As part of an extended characterisation of the MCI subgroups, we applied the recently proposed AT(N) system to further explore the heterogeneity of AD. This framework categorises the disease condition based on b-amyloid pathology (A: amyloid PET or CSF Ab(1–42)), tau pathology (T: CSF phosphorylated tau or tau PET), and neurodegeneration or neuronal injury (N: FDG-PET, structural MRI, or CSF total tau) [

These distributions align with our previous findings of lower t-tau and p-tau levels in subgroup 2 and the higher levels of t-tau and p-tau in subgroup 1, and abnormal Ab(1–42) levels in both subgroups. Furthermore, the presence of A + T + N + individuals in both subgroups highlights the pathological heterogeneity within the MCI population, suggesting that similar amyloid and tau profiles along with neurodegeneration can correspond to distinct molecular levels and imaging patterns. Lastly, the equal distribution of individuals with an A-T-N- profile suggests that the integrative profiles of molecular and imaging patterns extend beyond the established biomarkers, further emphasising the complexity of MCI heterogeneity.

Overall, the analysis in this section shows that subgroup 1 and subgroup 2 exhibited differentiated clinical, neuropsychological, and biological profiles, highlighting the heterogeneity within the MCI population. Subgroup 1 was characterised by a younger age, higher amyloid metabolism markers (e.g., Ab(1–40) and Ab(1–38)), elevated tau pathology (t-tau and p-tau), and greater BACE1 activity than subgroup 2 and controls. Neuropsychological assessments revealed better cognitive performance in subgroup 1 compared to subgroup 2 and AD patients, particularly in executive function scores and CDRSB score for dementia severity. Conversely, while subgroup 2 exhibited similarities to AD patients in terms of Ab(1–42), Ab(1–40) and Ab(1–38) levels, it differed by having significantly lower levels of neuronal injury and synaptic dysfunction markers (e.g., VILIP-1, SNAP-25, and NGRN), and better neuropsychological scores compared to AD. Subgroup 2 also exhibited lower tau pathology (t-tau and p-tau) than subgroup 1, aligning more closely with controls for these markers. The AT(N) classification further emphasised the heterogeneity, with subgroup 1 dominated by A + T + N + profiles, while subgroup 2 showed a mixture of A + T-N- and A + T + N + profiles, reflecting the pathological and molecular diversity within the MCI population.
Multi-modal integration-derived MCI subgroups are associated with divergent functional and imaging profiles
Divergent biological mechanisms characterising multi-modal MCI subgroups
To investigate the biological mechanisms underlying the heterogeneity of the two subgroups, we performed an over-representation pathway analysis using the
enrichR
and
clusterProfiler
R packages. DEPs were initially identified using the
limma
R package, adjusting for age, sex, and APOE4 status. Given the limited number of DEPs, these complementary pathway analyses were employed to ensure a comprehensive exploration of enriched BPs, MFs, and CCs. The detailed results of the individual enrichment analyses from
enrichR
and

MCI Subgroup 1 versus controls
Individuals in subgroup 1 exhibited significantly higher levels of all identified peptides/proteins compared to controls, as indicated by logFC, with 20 distinct DEPs. Enrichment analyses revealed shared pathways across the aforementioned databases, suggesting that subgroup 1 is predominantly characterised by neuronal hyperplasticity. In particular, proteins with elevated levels in this subgroup were associated with neuronal plasticity processes, including synapse assembly, synapse organisation, neuron projection guidance, axon guidance, and axonogenesis.
MCI Subgroup 2 versus controls
Subgroup 2 predominantly exhibited decreased peptide/protein levels compared to controls, as indicated by logFC. Among the identified proteins, five distinct proteins showed decreased levels, while two proteins exhibited increased levels relative to controls. The results of the enrichment analyses suggest that subgroup 2 is associated with dysfunctions across various aspects of AD-related pathways. These include metabolism-related processes (e.g., lipid transport, fatty acid oxidation, phospholipid biosynthetic process), signal transduction pathways (e.g., platelet-derived growth factor receptor signaling, glutamatergic synapse), ion transport mechanisms (e.g., calcium ion transport) and inflammatory responses (e.g., negative regulation of cytokine production, monocyte chemotactic protein-1 production).
MCI Subgroup 1 versus MCI subgroup 2
Additionally, a comparison between individuals in subgroup 1 and subgroup 2 identified 127 differentially abundant peptides. Enrichment analyses highlighted that the differences between subgroup 1 and subgroup 2 were characterised by distinct alterations across multiple biological mechanisms. These included pathways related to oxidative stress responses (e.g., detoxification mechanisms and antioxidant activity), neuronal and synaptic processes (e.g., neuronal development, synaptic plasticity, learning, memory, neurotransmission), metabolic pathways (lipid metabolism, phospholipid catabolism, fatty acid homeostasis), immune and inflammatory responses (e.g. cytokine regulation and complement activation), cell adhesion and extracellular matrix interactions (e.g. collagen-containing extracellular matrix), and protease activities (e.g., peptidase and endopeptidase regulation).
Subgroup comparisons with AD patients
Individuals in subgroup 1 had significantly altered CSF peptide levels compared to AD patients, with 85 peptides exhibiting increased levels and 34 showing decreased levels. The common pathways identified by both
enrichR
and
clusterProfiler
suggest significant alterations in several biological mechanisms. Neuronal development and plasticity were profoundly impacted, as evidenced by changes in axon guidance, synaptic assembly, and synaptic transmission. Defence mechanisms against oxidative stress and homeostasis were also prominently affected, highlighting processes such as detoxification and adaptation to oxidative damage. Protease activities and regulation were significantly altered, with enrichment in endopeptidase and peptidase inhibitor activities, suggesting disruptions in proteolytic processing. Additionally, immune function and inflammatory responses were also dysregulated, as reflected by alterations in phagocytosis, complement activation, humoral immune responses, and apoptotic cell clearance. Moreover, structural and extracellular matrix remodelling processes highlight potential deficits in neuronal structural integrity and connectivity. Intracellular trafficking mechanisms also showed significant alternations, including disruptions in endocytosis and vesicle-associated processes.
Overall, the differences highlighted by the enrichment analyses included pathways involved in neuronal plasticity, response to oxidative stress, immune regulation, protease activity, metabolic processes, and extracellular matrix remodelling. The unique profile of increased synaptic activity and neuronal hyperplasticity observed in subgroup 1 distinguished it from subgroup 2, which was characterised by metabolic and inflammatory dysfunctions. Subgroup 1 differed from both controls and AD patients, with enrichment in pathways such as synaptic integrity, oxidative defence, and protease regulation. In contrast to subgroup 1, no significant differences in peptide/protein intensities were observed between subgroup 2 and AD patients. This lack of statistical difference suggests that the CSF proteomic profile of subgroup 2 closely resembles that of AD patients. The enrichment results imply molecular heterogeneity within the cohorts studied.
Divergent imaging patterns characterising multi-modal MCI subgroups
To investigate whether the identified subgroups exhibited distinct spatial patterns across the three imaging data types, we analysed each data type separately using linear regression models and adjusting for age, sex, APOE4 status and education.
MCI subgroup 1 versus controls
For cortical and subcortical volumes, subgroup 1 differed from the controls in three regions: the left amygdala and the right and left hippocampus, both located within the medial temporal lobe. Concerning cortical thickness features, significant differences were observed in three regions: the right precuneus gyrus, the right superior parietal lobule, and the left entorhinal cortex. Regarding surface area measurements, subgroup 1 differed from controls in only one region, the right postcentral gyrus within the parietal lobe. Overall, these findings indicate that subgroup 1 exhibited differences in a limited number of brain regions, predominantly within the parietal and temporal lobes.
MCI subgroup 2 versus controls
When comparing subgroup 2 to controls, significant differences were observed in 57 cortical and subcortical volumes, including the right and left entorhinal cortex, left and right hippocampus, left and right parahippocampal gyri, right precentral gyrus, right superior parietal gyrus, lateral and medial orbitofrontal gyri, amygdala, third ventricle, left and right lateral ventricles and white matter hypointensities, among others. Notably, subgroup 2 exhibited low hippocampal and entorhinal cortex volumes, consistent with ventricular enlargement. For cortical thickness features, subgroup 2 differed from controls in 57 regions, including the right paracentral gyrus, right and left precuneus, right and left temporal poles, right and left rostral middle frontal gyri, right and left insula, right and left lateral occipital gyri, right and left inferior parietal lobules, and the left and right banks of the superior temporal sulcus (bankssts), among others. No significant differences in surface area features between subjects in subgroup 2 and controls were observed after correction for multiple comparisons.
MCI subgroup 1 versus MCI subgroup 2
Comparisons between subgroup 1 and subgroup 2 revealed significant differences in 74 cortical and subcortical regions, 42 regions in cortical thickness and 18 regions in surface area measurements. These findings suggest that, relative to controls, subgroup 2 exhibited more pronounced atrophy and cortical thinning than subgroup 1 across multiple brain regions and lobes.
Subgroup comparisons with AD patients
We conducted a comparative analysis of the clustering output with AD patients. In subgroup 1, comparisons with AD patients revealed significant differences in 66 cortical and subcortical volumes, including regions such as the right and left hippocampus, right and left lateral ventricles, and the right and left fusiform gyri. For cortical thickness measurements, subgroup 1 showed differences in 57 regions, including the right and left insula, left and right pars opercularis, and the right and left superior temporal gyri.
In contrast, subgroup 2, when compared to AD patients, exhibited differences in cortical thickness in only three regions: the right inferior parietal lobule, right inferior temporal gyrus, and right middle temporal gyrus. No significant differences in volumetric measurements were observed. Additionally, neither subgroup 1 nor subgroup 2 showed differences from AD patients in surface area features. These findings, along with the similarity in CSF proteomics profiles between subgroup 2 and AD patients, suggest that individuals in subgroup 2 have multi-modal profiles closely resembling those of AD patients. The limited differences in cortical thickness measurements further support this alignment.

Divergent imaging and peptidomic/proteomic signatures for subgroup 1 and subgroup 2 identified using a supervised multivariate multi-modal integration approach

In this section, we expanded the analysis by transforming the unsupervised clustering approach into a supervised framework to identify variables that can effectively distinguish between the two MCI subgroups. The resulting associations between imaging features and peptides/proteins define the unique signatures of each subgroup. These signatures capture the distinctive molecular and structural characteristics of each subgroup, offering a basis for further exploration of the heterogeneity within the MCI population. Discussion
AD is a neurodegenerative disease characterised by heterogeneity and multifactorial pathogenesis, making it challenging to capture its complexity using a single modality. Capturing the heterogeneity that exists across the MCI spectrum, a prodromal stage of AD, is crucial for effective subject stratification and therapeutic development. In this study, we propose a multi-modal integration methodology combining imaging and peptidomics/proteomics data to cluster MCI subjects into distinct subgroups/subtypes to better understand MCI heterogeneity. We identified two distinct subgroups associated with a divergent signature across biological mechanisms, imaging patterns, clinical outcomes, and potential therapeutic implications.
Multi-modal network highlights volumetric and peptidomics/proteomics as top-contributing data types
We employed the SNF method to integrate multi-modal features. Specifically, structural MRI (cortical thickness, volumes and surface area measurements) and CSF peptidomic/proteomic features were used as input to the algorithm. This revealed that volumetric data was the data type contributing most to the fused clustering by NMI, followed by peptidomic/proteomic data, cortical thickness, and surface area measurements. The significant contribution of volumetric and proteomic data types aligns with existing studies that emphasise the use of cortical and subcortical volumes to explore AD pathology and define disease subtypes [
39
,
78
–
80
]. Additionally, proteomic data derived from CSF offers insights into pathophysiological processes within the brain, providing a deeper understanding of the biological mechanisms underlying AD [
30
,
31
,
81
,
82
].
Identification and characterisation of multi-modal MCI subgroups: integrative clinical, molecular, imaging and therapeutic insights
Following the SNF integration, spectral clustering was applied to the fused network, resulting in the identification of two distinct multi-modal MCI subgroups. Due to their unique molecular and structural characteristics, we refer here to these subgroups as MCI subgroup 1: Resilient Neuronal Hyperplasticity subtype and MCI subgroup 2: Vulnerable Neurodegenerative subtype, explaining in the following sections the rationale behind these characterisations.
MCI subgroup 1: resilient neuronal hyperplasticity subtype
The first subgroup consists of individuals that are characterised by neuronal hyperplasticity, with minimal atrophy and cortical thinning. Individuals with MCI in this hyperplasticity subtype exhibited elevated levels of most proteins compared to controls, with enriched pathways involving synaptic structure, axonal development, and neuron projection guidance – processes associated with synaptic function and remodelling. A study on Autism Spectrum Disorder suggests that hyperplasticity, although often associated with early-life cognitive and behavioral symptoms, may provide a neuroprotective effect later in life. This phenomenon could enhance resilience against cognitive decline due to aging and AD [
83
]. While the underlying mechanisms may vary, individuals with MCI in this subgroup, who showed hyperplastic characteristics and minimal cortical atrophy, might represent a similar phenotype. In such cases, greater synaptic adaptability may lead to a delay in neurodegeneration. This subgroup also showed high levels of t-tau, p-tau, Ab(1–40), Ab(1–38), and BACE1. Previous studies that clustered individuals based on CSF levels of amyloid, t-tau, p-tau, and/or ubiquitin identified three to five AD subtypes distinguished by low, intermediate, and high tau levels [
84
–
86
]. Consistently, our multi-modal scheme identified two subgroups differing in tau levels (e.g. synaptic hyperactivity), providing further insights into the biological processes underlying interindividual variability in tau. This hyperplasticity subgroup exhibited higher levels of markers associated with neuronal injury, including t-tau, p-tau, NGRN, VILIP-1, and SNAP-25. Although typically associated with early synaptic and neuronal damage [
87
,
88
], the elevated markers in cognitively preserved individuals may reflect compensatory synaptic activity rather than neurodegeneration [
89
,
90
]. Additionally, this elevation may represent a stage where these markers reach their peak before plateauing, consistent with the dynamic trajectories proposed by a previous study [
91
]. Hyperactive neurons have been observed in AD, which are associated with increased levels of tau and amyloid [
92
,
93
]. This hyperactivity may stem from amyloid oligomers, which interrupt the excitatory and inhibitory balance within neuronal circuits [
93
]. Earlier research indicated that CSF levels of VILIP-1 associate with CSF Ab and p-tau levels, implying VILIP-1 serves as a marker for neuronal degeneration linked to Ab and tau pathologies [
91
,
94
]. Furthermore, studies comparing CSF VILIP-1 levels between CN individuals, those with MCI, and AD dementia patients revealed that VILIP-1 levels increased annually only in MCI subjects, while no significant increases were found in CN or AD patients [
95
]. This observation implies that CSF VILIP-1 levels might rise during inflammation and neurodegeneration stages associated with Ab and tau, yet, decline following significant neuronal death and brain atrophy. Based on the ATN framework, we also observe that subgroup 1 showing high prevalence of A + T + N + individuals, aligns with the abnormal Ab(1–42) levels, elevated t-tau and p-tau levels observed in subgroup 1.
MRI analysis revealed that subgroup 1 exhibited less atrophy and cortical thinning than subgroup 2, which corresponded to better performance in specific neuropsychological tests. While subgroup 1 showed worse scores across various cognitive domains compared to controls, its performance was better than that of subgroup 2 and AD patients. Comparisons between subgroup 1 and AD revealed a substantial number of significant differences in both peptidomics/proteomics and imaging data types, with these differences being more pronounced than those observed between subgroup 2 and AD.
Based on future conversion stage labels, the majority of individuals in this subgroup were classified as stable in their MCI condition. Over a three-year period, cognitive performance in this subgroup, as measured by CDRSB and MMSE scores, declined compared to controls but was less steep than that of the subgroup 2 and AD patients.
MCI subgroup 2: vulnerable neurodegenerative subtype
The second subgroup is characterised by functional impairments associated with AD, widespread atrophy and cortical thinning. Individuals with MCI in this subgroup exhibited lower levels of most proteins compared to controls, with enriched pathways indicating disruptions in metabolism-related processes, signal transduction, ion transport, and inflammatory responses. These individuals showed abnormal Ab(1–42) levels and severe atrophy, particularly in regions typically affected in AD, such as the medial temporal lobe. Interestingly, subgroup 2 exhibited lower t-tau and p-tau CSF levels on average compared to subgroup 1. This indicates that CSF tau levels could reflect a complex interplay of tau pathology and other neurodegenerative processes, as lower levels do not necessarily rule out the presence of underlying tau pathology. Conversely, they could imply less neuronal injury; however, this seems improbable due to the significant atrophy seen in this subgroup and the increased levels of axonal damage markers like CSF NFL. This second subgroup also exhibited lower p-tau levels compared to AD, consistent with some longitudinal studies [
91
,
96
]. These studies indicate that p-tau tends to decline in the later stages of the disease, possibly reflecting neuronal loss and decreased tau metabolism. Subgroup 2 showed lower levels of VILIP-1, SNAP-25, and NRGN compared to AD patients, which may reflect a plateau or even a declining phase in marker expression. Such patterns have been observed in later disease stages [
91
,
96
]. Collectively, these findings suggest that subgroup 2 could indicate a biologically more advanced stage of MCI, even with lower levels of markers. This highlights the variability within MCI and underscores the importance of a multi-modal approach. Additionally, enriched pathways to lipid oxidation and the negative regulation of platelet-derived growth factor receptor signalling suggest a potential link to blood–brain barrier dysfunction [
97
]. Dysfunction of the blood–brain barrier interrupts glucose metabolism, potentially hindering neuronal activity and processes related to plasticity [
98
,
99
]; given the relationship between tau levels in this subgroup may suggest impaired synaptic remodelling or neuronal activity. This may account for the reduction of peptides/proteins linked to synaptic function observed in subgroup 2 (
O95502
-NPTXR,
P47972
-NPTX2,
O15240

P05090
(APOD) and
P05413
(FABPH), were increased in subgroup 2 compared to controls. Furthermore, individuals in this subgroup showed high levels of complement proteins
P01024
(CO3) and
P0C0L4

98
,
99

Comparison between subgroup 2 and the AD group revealed no statistically significant differences in overall peptide/protein expression, suggesting that the molecular profile in this subgroup is more similar to AD than expected. Similarly, imaging analyses showed no volumetric differences and only three regions differing in cortical thickness. In addition, neuropsychological test scores for subgroup 2 were generally lower than those for subgroup 1, indicating worse cognitive performance, but better than those for AD patients. Notably, the executive domain was more severely affected in this subgroup compared to subgroup 1. Regarding the ATN framework, subgroup 2 was predominantly classified as A + T-N- (26 individuals), followed by A + T + N + (21 individuals), indicating a biomarker profile with lower tau-related pathology. The lower levels of t-tau and p-tau in this group, combined with greater similarities to AD in terms of molecular and imaging characteristics, suggest that alternative mechanisms such as metabolic and inflammatory dysregulation may contribute more prominently to disease progression in this subgroup, although tau pathology may still be involved in different ways. The presence of A + T + N + individuals in both subgroups highlights the heterogeneity of MCI, demonstrating that similar biomarker profiles can correspond to distinct molecular and structural profiles and suggesting that traditional AD biomarkers alone do not fully capture the complexity of disease subgroups. Additionally, the near equal presence of individuals with A-T-N-, A-T-N + , A-T + N + , and A + T + N- profiles suggest that our multi-modal approach captures molecular and imaging alterations that extend beyond conventional biomarkers, underscoring the need for more refined classification strategies in MCI research.
Based on future conversion stage labels, the majority of MCI individuals in subgroup 2 were classified as fast converters. This aligns with the observed cognitive decline over three years, as reflected in worsening CDRSB and MMSE scores.
Linking imaging features, peptidomics/proteomics data, and clinical indices
Our comparative modality analysis’ findings across MCI subgroups and CN/AD support the notion that the two identified subgroups represent distinct molecular and pathological profiles within the MCI spectrum. To further explore these differences, we conducted two complementary analyses (1) association analysis to examine the relationships between imaging and peptidomic/proteomic features, identifying biological connections/relationships across modalities, and, (2) correlation analysis, assessing how these imaging and molecular markers relate to clinical indices, providing insights into their relevance to cognitive function and disease progression.
The association map revealed distinct molecular and structural signatures for both subgroups, highlighting that different protein markers contribute to different brain regions. Notably, the cortical thickness data type showed a similar impact on both subgroups, with consistent reductions in cortical thickness observed. This reduction was associated with different peptide/protein markers, reflecting unique molecular underpinnings for each subgroup. Volumetric measurements presented contrasting patterns: in subgroup 1, a positive correlation was observed between volumetric features and DEPs, potentially reflecting neuronal hyperplasticity. Although we do not infer a direct increase in brain volume due to plasticity, similar observations have been described in the literature, where region-specific or temporary volumetric changes have been linked to compensatory mechanisms. These mechanisms include synaptic enlargement, dendritic spine formation, and functional network reorganisation in the early or preclinical stages of AD. These changes are seen as signs of compensatory synaptic plasticity or hyperplastic responses, likely intended to preserve cognitive function despite existing pathology [
100
–
103
]. In contrast, subgroup 2 exhibited decreased volumes across various brain regions, consistent with widespread atrophy. Surface area measurements also revealed differential associations. In subgroup 1, surface area showed positive correlations with multiple markers, possibly reflecting the heterogeneity within MCI. In subgroup 2, however, surface area demonstrated negative associations, which may reflect the diffuse brain atrophy. It is worth noting that surface area as a data type has been less extensively studied, underscoring the need for further research to understand its role in neurodegenerative conditions.
Our analysis is also consistent with previous findings at the molecular/omics and imaging levels. Some of the protein-brain region pairs identified in our analysis were also represented in NeuroPro (an exhaustive review of 38 published AD proteomic studies, resulting in a comprehensive map of protein changes in human brain tissue. This map encompasses thirteen brain regions across three stages of the disease—preclinical AD, MCI, and advanced AD-, highlighting proteins linked to amyloid plaques, neurofibrillary tangles, and cerebral amyloid angiopathy), reflecting potential region-specific dysregulation [

These findings demonstrate alignment between proteins highly associated with imaging features in our cohort and the regional distribution of CSF proteins in the brain, as reported in NeuroPro. The left lateral orbitofrontal cortex, a region involved in decision-making and emotional regulation, has been consistently implicated in AD and MCI. Studies have reported cortical thinning in this region as a hallmark of neurodegeneration associated with disease progression, particularly in individuals transitioning from MCI to AD [

In an effort to gain a better understanding of the significance of the different types of data, we examined associations for each data type used in the fused scheme separately against the available clinical data. Previous studies have utilised neuropsychological tests and CSF biomarkers to characterise individuals with MCI, AD, or both. Based on our correlation analysis, subgroup 1 demonstrated a smaller number of significant correlations with clinical indices compared to subgroup 2. For imaging features, cortical thickness and volumetric measurements showed significant correlations, particularly with scores critical in AD diagnosis and progression, such as CDRSB, ADAS, and RAVLT scores. The significance of the aforementioned analyses is further emphasised by the observation that without multi-modal subtyping, key differences at the molecular and imaging levels remain largely hidden.
Importantly, the lack of subtyping within the MCI cohort indicates that it is challenging to identify differences at the molecular level. Targeted subgroup-specific therapeutic candidates using an in silico drug repurposing approach
The multi-modal subgroups identified in this study may hold significant implications for treatment strategies. By applying a computational drug repurposing approach, we identified candidate repurposed drugs tailored to the distinct characteristics of the two subgroups. Interestingly, while the drugs exhibit specificity for each subgroup, some commonalities were also observed, suggesting shared underlying mechanisms across the MCI cohort.
Geldanamycin, a unique candidate drug for subgroup 1, is an Hsp90 inhibitor initially developed as an antifungal, and has gained interest for its potential to reduce the pathogenic protein aggregation characteristic of neurodegenerative diseases like AD. Interestingly, a geldanamycin analogue, 17-AAG, has shown promise in reducing aggregated tau with reduced toxicity compared to geldanamycin itself, marking a significant step toward safer therapeutic options. The protective roles of Hsp90 inhibitors highlight them as potential agents for halting or reversing protein toxicity in AD models [
107
,
108
]. Additionally, another unique candidate for this subgroup is tubastatin-a, a histone deacetylase 6 (HDAC6 inhibitor). In [
109
], they assessed the efficacy of tubastatin-a, in AD in transgenic mice. The treatment had a positive impact on behavioural outcomes, Ab load and tau phosphorylation. Subgroup 1, characterised by hyperplasticity and elevated levels of tau and p-tau, may benefit from this treatment targeting synaptic plasticity and tau-associated pathways.
In contrast, subgroup 2, which exhibited more extensive molecular disruptions and AD-like pathology, resulted in a broader spectrum of candidate drugs, reflecting its more complex disease profile and highlighting the need for multi-targeted therapeutic strategies. Curcumin, derived from turmeric (Curcuma longa), exhibits a wide array of therapeutic properties relevant to neurological health, including anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective effects. These have positioned it as a candidate for AD treatment. Although clinical application of curcumin is limited, advances in nanotechnology—such as the development of liposome and nanoparticle-based delivery systems—are being explored to improve its delivery and efficacy, something that may unlock its full potential as an adjunct treatment for AD. [
110
] Another candidate drug for subgroup 2 is thiothixene, an antipsychotic initially used to treat schizophrenia, has sparked interest as a repurposed drug for neurodegenerative disorders due to its modulation of neurotransmitter pathways implicated in both psychotic and neurodegenerative conditions. By targeting dopaminergic and serotonergic systems, thiothixene may offer symptomatic relief in AD, especially where behavioural symptoms overlap with psychotic features. While its application in AD is still exploratory, the drug’s known neurochemical effects warrant further investigation into its potential for stabilising mood and cognition in dementia-related settings [
111
].
Panobinostat (both subgroups), mocetinostat (subgroup 2), entinostat (subgroup 2), belinostat (subgroup 2), vorinostat (both subgroups), and trichostatin-a (both subgroups), all HDAC inhibitors, present a promising approach for addressing the epigenetic dysregulation observed in AD. HDAC inhibition has shown beneficial effects in preclinical AD models, notably in reducing Ab plaque accumulation, tau phosphorylation, and neuroinflammation. HDAC inhibitors like the aforementioned ones contribute to a less toxic neuronal environment by stabilising microtubules and supporting cell survival mechanisms. These findings position HDAC inhibition as an innovative therapeutic avenue that may complement existing strategies by preserving neuronal integrity and mitigating progressive cognitive decline in AD [
112
–
115
].
Triptolide (subgroup 2), a compound known for its anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects, has shown benefits in AD-like mouse models. Studies indicate that triptolide supports cognitive functions by preserving synaptic proteins, such as gephyrin and collybistin, and maintaining levels of Gamma-Aminobutyric Acid Type A Receptor Subunit Alpha1 (GABRA1), which stabilises inhibitory neurotransmission. Additionally, triptolide has been associated with reduced Ab accumulation and decreased levels of inflammatory cytokines in the brain. This dual action (reducing oxidative damage and inflammatory markers) suggests that triptolide may be beneficial for reducing AD-related cognitive decline, supporting its potential as a therapeutic agent in neurodegenerative conditions. [
116
,
117
]
Additionally, fms-like tyrosine kinase 3 (FLT3) inhibitors such as tozasertib (subgroup 2), have demonstrated efficacy in models where oxidative stress contributes to pathology, suggesting they could reduce oxidative damage and associated neurotoxicity in AD. Given that oxidative stress is a known factor in AD progression, FLT3 inhibition could offer neuroprotective benefits, although further studies are required to evaluate its impact on cognitive outcomes in AD [
118
]. However, tozasertib is also an aurora kinase inhibitor. Recently, a study by Afghah et al
.
[
119
] showed that there is reduced aurora kinase A activity in AD, which may play a key role in Ab accumulation and formation of extracellular amyloid plaques. Further investigation of this drug is necessary due to conflicting outcomes.
Pf-562271 (both subgroups) is a potent ATP competitive inhibitor of focal adhesion kinase (FAK), known to be overactivated in AD pathology, and of proline-rich tyrosine kinase 2 (Pyk2), an activator of downstream signalling related to the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative diseases. Due to its ability to inhibit these two players, pf-562271 may potentially help in neurodegeneration, something that needs to be further tested. [
120
,
121
]
Moreover, cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs) are important regulators of the cell cycle and neuronal functions in AD. Abnormality of CDK activity, for instance, CDK5, has been linked to pathological events including tau protein phosphorylation and neuronal death. At-7519 (subgroup 2) and pha-793887 (subgroup 2), both CDK inhibitors, could be potentially used as therapeutic approaches in AD. Indeed, studies have demonstrated that CDK inhibitors can rescue neuronal cells from death stimulated by factors such as nerve growth factor deprivation and oligomeric beta-amyloid. [
122
]
Prior studies have emphasised the importance of the inhibitory kappa B kinase beta (IKKβ) in AD. Its absence in neurons showed that it can lead to reduced neuroinflammation, tau hyperphosphorylation, and the buildup of Ab in APP- and tau-transgenic models. Of the candidate drugs for repurposing proposed through our analysis, bms-345541 (subgroup 2) was found as an IKKβ inhibitor. Given that IKKβ is a key player in both neuroinflammatory and cell survival pathways, its reduction could potentially deal with numerous dimensions of AD pathology. [
123
,
124
]
Evidence has emerged showing that spleen tyrosine kinase (Syk) affects AD pathology, especially concerning modifications to the tau protein [
125
]. Results from this study suggest that Syk inhibitors are effective in reducing tau phosphorylation at various sites within human neuroblastoma cells expressing wild-type tau. Additionally, treatment of wild-type mice with R406 Syk inhibitor reduced the levels of phosphorylated tau. Another candidate drug for repurposing proposed through our analysis, er-27319 (subgroup 2), is a Syk inhibitor, which could be potentially tested as an AD treatment.
Many of these drugs may benefit subgroup 2, as they target various aspects of the condition, including neuroinflammation, which plays a critical role in the disease mechanisms associated with this subgroup.
We also assessed the above repurposed drugs that were identified in both subgroups regarding their current status in clinical trials. Among the identified drugs,
curcumin
and
vorinostat
have been tested in clinical trials for AD. Curcumin is currently under investigation in ongoing clinical trials for AD, while the remaining candidate drugs have not been evaluated for AD or MCI in clinicaltrials.gov.
Overall, this approach of performing subgroup-specific drug repurposing provides a framework for subgroup-specific therapeutic strategies, highlighting the importance of tailoring treatments to the distinct pathological profiles of MCI subgroups.
Limitations and future work
Despite these contributions, our study has limitations. Although statistically sufficient, the moderate sample size may limit generalisability of our findings across broader populations. Second, the reliance on cross-sectional data constrains our ability to infer causal relationships between biomarkers and cognitive trajectories. Longitudinal studies are essential to validate our findings and explore the temporal dynamics of subgroup-specific changes. Third, while our focus on multi-modal data integration enhances the comprehensiveness of our analysis, the exclusion of PET imaging and transcriptomic data may have omitted additional insights into amyloid and tau dynamics. We selected the most commonly used imaging methods to ensure interpretability and maximisation of cohort coverage. Additionally, multi-drug combinations may be more effective in targeting the complex pathology of AD and Late MCI but were out of the scope of this study.
Another noteworthy consideration in our study is the observed age difference between the identified MCI subgroups, particularly between MCI subgroup 1 and subgroup 2. While the age difference was statistically significant (FDR-adjusted
p
-value = 0.018), it was small in magnitude (~ 3.5 years median difference between the two subgroups), and it was not the sole driver of subgroup separation, but rather one of several features characterising the two subgroups. Age is a well-established biological risk factor for AD and exhibits a complex, bidirectional relationship with disease processes [
126
]. Specifically, ageing contributes to the development of AD pathology, while AD-related mechanisms are also known to accelerate biological ageing [
127
,
128
]. Future studies could explore this further to evaluate the specifics of this interplay and its contribution to subtype formation [
129
].
The next steps should include validation of our findings in larger, independent and more diverse cohorts, and longitudinal and multi-modal data should also be incorporated in order to enhance our understanding of MCI heterogeneity and its progression to AD. Moreover, by adding cutting-edge imaging techniques (e.g., tau PET and functional MRI) to the multi-modal integration pipeline, could further allow early detection of neurodegenerative changes, enhance the detection of network disruptions in MCI with greater sensitivity and provide a more comprehensive assessment of the subgroup-specific pathological pathways. For instance, PET scans can measure the accumulation of Ab and tau, as well as glucose metabolism in the brain. These molecular and metabolic changes often occur before any structural degeneration [
130
–
133
]. Resting-state fMRI provides information on functional brain dynamics and connectivity changes, especially within major networks like the default mode and salience networks, which are known to be disrupted early in MCI and are closely linked to cognitive function [
134
–
137
]. Studies that integrate different types of imaging have shown that higher levels of amyloid and tau are linked to disrupted connectivity in these networks, even in early stages of the disease. This suggests that molecular pathology might play a direct role in disrupting brain function early on [
138
]. Combining PET and fMRI with structural MRI could potentially help map how the disease spreads through specific functional circuits. This approach can reveal which networks are more vulnerable and help identify early phenotypic differences. Including these modalities along with omics (such as proteomics) in future studies could improve our ability to detect disease early and stratify individuals more accurately, and tailor treatments to individual needs.
Additionally, by integrating different omics modalities (e.g., transcriptomics, epigenomics and metabolomics), key regulatory mechanisms involved in the different MCI subgroups could be revealed.
Artificial intelligence (AI)-based approaches (network-based machine learning and deep learning) can also be used to improve MCI stratification. Actually, our study produces AI-ready multi-modal subject profiles that integrate imaging and molecular signatures. These profiles can be considered as an initial stepping stone for AI-driven predictive modelling, automated subtyping, and treatment recommendations. These profiles can be incorporated into machine learning systems to refine early diagnostic models, improve the accuracy of disease trajectory predictions, and support personalised intervention strategies tailored to each subgroup. The use of the latter could lead to precision medicine approaches that target specific pathological processes in different MCI subgroups. Finally, the integration of AI-ready profiles into clinical practice can improve early detection processes, aiding physicians in making data-driven treatment decisions based on molecular and imaging profiles.
Conclusions
Our study highlights the importance of multi-modal data integration for detecting distinct subgroups of individuals and capturing MCI complexity. The identification of two subgroups differing at molecular, structural, and clinical levels enhances our understanding of MCI heterogeneity and its implications in AD progression. MCI subgroup 1, the Resilient Neuronal Hyperplasticity subtype, was characterised by elevated markers of neuronal plasticity, minimal brain atrophy and cortical thinning, primarily affecting the temporal and parietal lobes, and better clinical outcomes. In contrast, MCI subgroup 2, the Vulnerable Neurodegenerative subtype, exhibited processes commonly deregulated in AD, including dysfunctions in metabolism-related pathways, signal transduction, ion transport, and inflammatory responses, accompanied by diffuse atrophy and thinning across multiple brain regions, executive dysfunction, and faster progression.
Our findings underscore the potential of multi-modal data integration to elucidate disrupted biological processes and at the same time which brain regions are vulnerable in MCI subjects. The differentiation between the two subgroups implies that disease pathways are likely to be driven by different biological factors, thus, emphasising the need for stratification and precision medicine approaches to MCI research. Our study notably generates AI-ready multi-modal subject profiles, offering structured datasets that can be used in AI-driven predictive modelling, automated subtyping methods, and recommendations for treatment. Moving forward, the clinical relevance and the validation of the robustness of these findings will rely on the expansion of this work to larger, longitudinal, and multi-modal datasets. In summary, refining MCI subtyping through multi-modal integration can lead to improving the characterisation of the disease at an earlier stage, refining risk prediction, and informing the design of stratified intervention measures in AD research and clinical practice.
