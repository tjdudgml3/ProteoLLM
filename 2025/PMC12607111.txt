1
Introduction
Sporotrichosis is a subcutaneous mycosis with global distribution, caused by thermodimorphic fungi of the genus
Sporothrix
. Among the pathogenic species,
S. brasiliensis
and
S. schenckii
are the most prevalent etiological agents (
Rodrigues et al., 2022
).
S. brasiliensis
is highly virulent in both animal and human hosts, has been associated with more severe clinical manifestations, and is responsible for outbreaks in South America (
Fernandes et al., 2013
;
Gómez-Gaviria et al., 2023
;
Poester et al., 2024
;
Rossow et al., 2020
). This species has emerged as the main agent of cat-transmitted sporotrichosis in Brazil (
Almeida-Paes et al., 2014
;
de Oliveira Bento et al., 2021
;
Gómez-Gaviria et al., 2023
;
Rodrigues et al., 2022
). Transmission occurs through sapronotic or zoonotic routes, typically via traumatic inoculation of fungal propagules into the skin, primarily affecting domestic cats and humans (
Orofino-Costa et al., 2017
;
Ramírez-Soto et al., 2018
). The
S. schenckii
-host interaction is influenced by surface adhesins, biofilm formation, melanin production, proteinases, and extracellular vesicles (EVs) (
García-Carnero and Martínez-Álvarez, 2022
). However, studies on the virulence factors of
S. brasiliensis
remain limited (
Gómez-Gaviria et al., 2023
).
Fungal EVs were first described in
Cryptococcus neoformans
(
Rodrigues et al., 2007
). EVs have been reported in
S. brasiliensis
(
Ikeda et al., 2018
) and
S. schenckii
(
Albuquerque et al., 2008
) grown in liquid cultures.
S. brasiliensis
EVs have been associated with fungal physiology, virulence-related responses and host–pathogen interactions (
Campos et al., 2021
;
da Silva et al., 2025
;
Ikeda et al., 2018
), whereas EVs production by
S. schenckii
was explored in less detail (
Albuquerque et al., 2008
). EVs are membranous structures composed of a lipid bilayer that transport a variety of molecules, including proteins, lipids, polysaccharides, glycans, pigments, and nucleic acids (
Joffe et al., 2016
;
Liu and Hu, 2023
). Determination of small molecule composition of fungal EVs (structures with molecular masses less than 900 Da) is in its infancy, and the biological functions of low molecular weight structures associated with EVs is poorly known.
In this study, we explored the proteomic and low-molecular-mass compound diversity of
S. brasiliensis
and
S. schenckii
EVs isolated from solid media. The EV proteomes confirmed and expanded our knowledge on the diversity in the protein composition of
S. brasiliensis
and
S. schenckii
EVs. In addition, we identified a previously unknown small molecule composition of the
Sporothrix
EVs. Notably, the tripeptide isoleucine-proline-isoleucine (IPI) was identified as a vesicular molecule common to EVs from both species. This compound reduced the adhesion of
S. brasiliensis
and
S. schenckii
to type I collagen, a key component of the host extracellular matrix. Our findings highlight the potential of EV-associated molecules for the investigation of novel biological functions and cellular responses in these pathogenic fungi.
2
Methods
2.1
Strains and culture conditions
The strains
S. brasiliensis
5110 (ATCC MYA-4823) and
S. schenckii
1099–18 (ATCC MYA-4821) were used in all experiments.
S. brasiliensis
5110 (ATCC MYA-4823) was originally isolated from a feline skin lesion in Rio de Janeiro, Brazil (
Castro et al., 2013
;
Teixeira et al., 2014
).
S. schenckii
1099–18 (ATCC MYA-4821) was isolated from a human case of sporotrichosis at the Mycology Section, Department of Dermatology, Columbia University, New York (
Fernandes et al., 2000
;
Lopes-Alves et al., 1992
;
Teixeira et al., 2014
). Yeast-phase cells were cultured in brain heart infusion (BHI) broth (Difco™-BD), pH 7.8, at 37 °C for 7 days under continuous agitation.
2.2
Isolation and characterization of EVs
EVs from the
Sporothrix
strains were isolated from solid medium cultures (
Reis et al., 2019
). After growth in BHI broth, a suspension containing 3.5 × 10
7
yeasts/mL, in 300 μL, was plated onto BHI medium solidified with 2 % agar and incubated for 7 days at 37 °C. Dry weight determination was employed to characterize growth rates, yielding comparable values for each strain (data not shown). Additionally, we assessed the cultures' viability using propidium iodide staining, which labels the nuclei of dead cells with red fluorescence. Fungal cells were scraped from the medium with a Drigalski loop and suspended in PBS at a concentration of 10
7
cells/mL. Propidium iodide was added to a final concentration of 15 μg/mL, and the suspension was incubated at 37 °C in the dark. Dead cell controls (fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde) were prepared in a similar manner. Finally, the cells were observed under a fluorescence microscope to quantify the stained cells. Although nearly all the fixed cells were stained, the observation of stained cells after collection from the culture medium was scarce and was estimated to correspond to less than 1 % of the total cells observed (data not shown).
For EV isolation, the cells were scraped with an inoculation loop and homogenized in a centrifuge tube containing 30 mL of sterile PBS. Vesicle isolation was performed according to differential centrifugation at 15,000 x
g
at 4 °C for two times to remove cells and debris. The supernatant was then filtered through 0.22 μm membranes following to ultracentrifugation at 100,000 x
g
for 1 h at 4 °C. The EVs pelleted were resuspended in 300 μL of PBS and stored at 4 °C. For proteomic and metabolomic analysis, 30 plates of each strain were required to obtain the ideal protein concentration (10 μg total) and the material abundance for experimentation.
EV characterization and quantification were performed using Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA) with an LM10 analysis system coupled with a 488 nm laser, camera, and flow pump (Malvern Panalytical, Malvern, United Kingdom). Particles tracking was conducted with NTA 3.0 software (Malvern Panalytical). Samples were diluted to reach the recommended concentration range (1 × 10
8
–1 × 10
9
particles/mL) and injected using 1 mL syringes connected to the flow system. For each sample, five 60-s videos were recorded using camera level of 16, gain set at 1, and a detection threshold of 5. For transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis, the particles were adhered to formvar-coated grids for 1 h at room temperature, followed by negative staining using Karnovsky's fixative. In this approach, EVs are observed without the need for ultrathin sections. Instead, they are surrounded by electron-dense heavy metal salts, which create a bright outline of the specimen against a dark background, revealing its fine details. EVs were visualized using a JEOL 1400Plus microscope (JEOL, USA) operating at 100 kV.
2.3
Proteomics
EVs were stored at −80 °C and lyophilized at −50 °C under 0.04 mBar for 18 h. Vesicles were lysed with 0.1 mm zirconium beads in five cycles of 1-min of vortexing followed by 1 min on ice. Subsequently, 100 μL of 8 M urea was added to the pellet, followed by five additional cycles of vortex/ice cycles. The samples were centrifuged at 18,000 x
g
for 15 min, and the supernatants were collected. Protein concentration was determined using the determined using the Qubit™ fluorometric assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and adjusted to 100 μg. Protein were reduced using dithiothreitol (DTT) at a final concentration of 10 mM at 56 °C for 30 min, then cooled to room temperature. Alkylation was performed using 15 mM iodoacetamide for 25 min at room temperature in the dark. Protein digestion was carried out with trypsin at a 1:50 enzyme-to-substrate ratio, overnight. After digestion, peptides were desalted and quantified with the Qubit™ system, and concentrated using stage tips as previously described (
de Oliveira et al., 2024
).
Following mass spectrometry analysis, desalted peptides were loaded, in technical duplicates, onto reverse-phase columns manually packed with ReprosilPur C18-AQ stationary phase (3 μm particle size, 120 Å pore size, Dr. Maisch). Columns measured 15 cm in length and 75 μm in internal diameter. Peptide loading was performed on an UltiMate 3000 nanoLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.). Peptides were eluted from the column using a 90-min chromatographic gradient ranging from 5 % to 40 % of mobile phase B (95 % acetonitrile in 0.1 % formic acid). The eluate was directly introduced into an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) located at the Proteomics and Mass Spectrometry Platform, ICC, Fiocruz-PR.
Mass spectra were acquired in data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode, automatically switching between full MS scans and MS/MS, with a dynamic exclusion of 60 s. Full MS scan were acquired at a resolution of 60,000 at
m/z
200. The most intense precursor ions were selected every 2 s, excluding singly charged species, and fragmented using higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) with a normalized collision energy of 30. MS/MS spectra were acquired at resolution of 15,000 at
m/z
200. Additional instrument settings included a spray voltage of 2.6 kV, a capillary temperature of 250 °C, predictive automatic gain control (AGC) enabled, and an S-lens RF level set to 70 %. Chromatographic gradients and data acquisition were controlled using Xcalibur 4 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.).
The data analysis was performed with the PatternLab for Proteomics V (PLV) software that is freely available at
https://www.patternlabforproteomics.org
(
Santos et al., 2022
).
Sporothrix brasiliensis
and
Sporothrix schenckii
protein sequences were downloaded from FungiDB on 31 January 2025, and a target-decoy database was generated to include a reversed version of each sequence, along with 127 common mass spectrometry contaminants. The data were preprocessed with the Y.A.D.A. 3.0 deconvolution algorithm (
Clasen et al., 2022
) to enable multiplexed spectra identification (
Clasen et al., 2023
). The Comet 2021 search engine (
Eng et al., 2015
), which is embedded into PLV, was used for mass spectra identification. The search parameters included fully and semi-tryptic peptide candidates with masses between 500 and 6000 Da, up to two missed cleavages, 35 ppm for precursor mass, and fragment binning at 0.02
m/z
for MS/MS. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine and oxidation of methionine as fixed and variable modification, respectively.
The validity of the PSMs was assessed using Search Engine Processor (SEPro) (
Carvalho et al., 2012a
). Identifications were grouped by charge state (2 + and ≥ 3 +), and then by tryptic status, resulting in four distinct subgroups. For each group, the XCorr, DeltaCN, DeltaPPM, and Peak Matches values were used to generate a Bayesian discriminator. The identifications were sorted in nondecreasing order according to the discriminator score. A cutoff score accepted a false-discovery rate (FDR) of 2 % at the peptide level based on the number of decoys (
Barboza et al., 2011
). This procedure was independently performed on each data subset, ensuring FDR control independent of charge state or tryptic status. Additionally, a minimum sequence length of five amino-acid residues and a protein score greater than 2 were imposed. Finally, identifications with precursor mass deviations greater than 10 ppm from the theoretical value were discarded. These final filters ensured that the protein-level FDR remained below 1 % across all search results.
Relative protein quantification was based on extracted ion chromatograms (XICs) (
Neilson et al., 2011
) and analyzed using the TFold module in PatternLab V for comparisons between strains (
Carvalho et al., 2012b
). Only proteins identified with two or more unique peptides and a q-value ≤0.05 were considered. Data are available via ProteomeXchange with identifier
PXD066550
. Subsequently, species-specific proteins identified from Venn diagrams were subjected to enrichment analysis using the Gene Ontology Annotation (GOA) database available at FungiDB Fungal & Oomycete Informatics Resource (
https://fungidb.org/fungidb/app/workspace/strategies
). The five most enriched terms in each of the three GOA categories—molecular function, biological process, and cellular component—were annotated. Notably, the approach employed in this study differs from those utilized in previous studies that employed proteomics for analyses of
Sporothrix
EVs (
Campos et al., 2021
;
da Silva et al., 2025
;
Ikeda et al., 2018
). Consequently, compositional differences are anticipated.
2.4
Metabolomics
EVs samples from the two
Sporothrix
strains were weighed (1.0 mg), solubilized in 1.0 mL of methanol (HPLC-grade), and filtered through 0.22 μm PTFE (polytetrafluoroethylene) filter (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). LC-HRMS data were acquired using a Thermo Fisher U3000 RSLC nano system coupled to a QExactive Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) equipped with a heated electrospray ionization (H-ESI) source. Chromatographic separation was performed using an Intensity Solo C18 column (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany) with the following specifications: 100 mm × 2.1 mm, 2.2 mm particle size. The mobile phase flow rate was set to 250 μL/min at 40 °C, with an injection volume of 5 μL. The mobile phase consisted of ultrapure water (solvent A) and acetonitrile (solvent B), both acidified with 0.1 % formic acid. The gradient elution method was as follows: 5 % B (0–5 min), 5–40 % B (5–10 min), 40–45 % B (10–12 min), 45–98 % B (12–18 min), 98 % B (18–20 min).
The samples were analyzed in Full-scan mode and data-dependent acquisition (DDA), covering an
m/z
range of 100–1.500 Da. Ionization was performed in positive mode, with the following parameters: auxiliary gas 10 (arbitrary unit), sheath gas pressure 35 (psi) and temperature 300 °C; spray voltage +3.5 kV, capillary temperature of 300 °C. For MS1, a resolution of 70,000 (at
m/z
200) was used, with an AGC target of 1 × 10
6
, and a injection time of 100 ms. For MS2, a resolution of 35,000 was used, with an AGC target of 5 × 10
5
and an injection time of 100 ms. Data were acquired by selecting six precursors per cycle, and normalized collision energy (NCE) was applied stepwise (20, 35, and 45 eV). The execution conditions for MS2 were a minimun AGC target of 8 × 10
3
, excluding isotopes, without repeating the same
m/z
for 10 s. The operation of the Q Exactive Orbitrap MS was controlled using Xcalibur 4.5 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).
2.5
LC-HRMS data processing and Feature-based molecular networking
The LC-HRMS data initially obtained in RAW format (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) were converted to mzXML format using MSConvert 3.0.2 (ProteoWizard, Palo Alto, CA, EUA) (
Adusumilli and Mallick, 2017
). Then, the .mzXML files were processed using MZMine3 (version 4.5.37) (
Heuckeroth et al., 2024
) for feature detection, chromatogram building, chromatogram deconvolution, isotope grouping, alignment, and filtering. The parameters used for data processing are provided in the supplementary material (Supplementary Table 1).
After processing, the following files were generated: a quantification table of the detected features (.csv), MS/MS data associated with precursor ions (.mgf), and a metadata table. These files were submitted to the Global Natural Products Social Molecular Networking (GNPS) platform (
https://gnps2.org
) (
Wang et al., 2016
) for metabolite annotation and construction of molecular networks based on the similarity of fragmentation patterns. The MS feature outputs were processed using the Feature-Based Molecular Networking (FBMN) workflow (
Nothias et al., 2020
). The parameters used are present in the supplementary material (Supplementary Table 2). The FBMN job in GNPS2 for EVs from
S. brasiliensis
5110 and
Sporothrix schenckii
1099–18 can be accessed through the following link:
https://gnps2.org/status?task=34590a39d04941019f0d101dad4fdbb0
. The metabolomics data are publicly available at
https://massive.ucsd.edu/
(accesses July 23, 2025) under the unique identifier MSV000098560.
Compound annotation was carried out following the classification levels proposed by the Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) (
Fiehn et al., 2007
;
Sumner et al., 2007
). The annotation was based on GNPS spectral library and was refined through manual curation, based on comparison of accurate mass values within a 5 ppm tolerance for MS1 and characteristic fragment ions from MS/MS data.
2.6
Enzymatic activity of Sporothrix
To determine the dipeptidyl peptidase IV (DPP4) activity of the isolates (1 × 10
7
cells/mL), the yeast was co-incubated at 1:1 ratio with a fluorogenic substrate H-Gly-Pro-MCA (200 μM), provided in the “DPP4 Activity” kit (Sigma). The 96-well black Greiner μClear plates were used.
The plate was incubated at 37 °C for 15 min prior to fluorescence measurement. In this assay, DPP4 cleaves the non-fluorescent substrate H-Gly-Pro-AMC, releasing the fluorescent product 7-amido-4-methylcoumarin (AMC) (λₑₓ = 380 nm / λₑₘ = 440 nm). Fluorescence was monitored over 35 min using a Synergy H1M2F microplate reader (BioTek), at 37 °C.
The isolates were tested in the presence of the DPP4 inhibitor IPI (Geneone; 1 mg/mL) and in its absence (control condition). As positive control for enzymatic inhibition, assays were also performed in the presence of sitagliptin (TOCRIS Bioscience, Bristol), a commercially available DPP4 inhibitor that is structurally unrelated to IPI. To serve as a negative control for enzyme inhibition, biotinylated IPI (biotin-IPI; Peptide 2.0) was employed. Biotinylation at the N-terminal of IPI renders it incapable of inhibiting DPP4 (data not shown).
2.7
Adhesion of Sporothrix to type I collagen
To assess the adhesion of
S. brasiliensis
and
S. schenckii
to type I collagen from rat tail (Sigma), 96-well polystyrene microtiter plates were coated with collagen (200 μg/mL) for 15 min. The solution was then removed, and the plates were air-dried, under laminar flow to ensure sterility. Subsequently, 200 μL of RPMI 1640 medium buffered with 3-(N-morpholino) propanesulfonic acid (MOPS), pH 7.0, and supplemented with 2 % glucose (Sigma-Aldrich), containing 5 × 10
7
yeasts/mL, were added to each well. Experimental conditions included the addition of IPI at 1, 10, and 100 μg/mL (Geneone), and sitagliptin phosphate (TOCRIS Bioscience, Bristol) at 1.5, 15, and 153 μg/mL. The inhibitory activities of the highest concentrations of IPI and sitagliptin were compared with the ability of biotin-IPI (100 μg/ml), which is incapable of inhibiting DPP4, to block adhesion. An anti-DPP4 antibody (CD26 Recombinant Rabbit Monoclonal Antibody, JM11–42, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was also tested at a concentration of 2 μg/ml as an adhesion inhibitor. As a control system, an isotype-matched irrelevant antibody (rabbit IgG, PP64, Meck) was used at the same concentration. The control condition consisted of RPMI medium containing only yeast cells.
The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 15 min under agitation. After incubation, wells were washed three times with PBS to remove non-adherent cells. Adhesion was quantified by measuring total metabolic activity using the XTT [2,3-bis(2-methoxy-4-nitro-5-sulfophenyl)-5-[(phenylamino)carbonyl]-2H-tetrazolium hydroxide] reduction assay (
de Souza et al., 2025
). In addition, adhesion was evaluated by confocal microscopy. For this purpose, sterile 13-mm round coverslips (Perfecta, Brazil) were coated with collagen and subjected to the yeast adhesion assay under the same conditions described above. Adherent yeast cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde and stained with calcofluor white (1.25 μM) (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 30 min. Representative images were acquired using a confocal microscope (STELLARIS 8, Leica Microsystems, Germany) operated with Las X software.
2.8
Effects of IPI on the morphology of
Sporothrix
IPI failed to influence the growth of both strains used in this study (data not shown), but the possibility of other effects could not be ruled out. To determine if IPI affects the morphology of
S. brasiliensis
5110 and
S. schenckii
1099–18, the cells were treated for 15 min under the conditions used for the adhesion experiments and then washed three times with PBS. The cells were then fixed overnight in 4 % paraformaldehyde in PBS at room temperature. After fixation, the cells were washed three times with PBS. The cell wall chitin was stained with 15 μM calcofluor white (Sigma) for 30 min at 37 °C. The cells were washed three times with PBS and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C with 5 μg/mL of succinate-labeled wheat germ agglutinin coupled to tetramethylrhodamine isothiocyanate (WGA-TRITC) for staining surface chitooligomers. After this incubation, the cells were again washed three times with PBS and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C with 15 μg/mL concanavalin A coupled to fluorescein isothiocyanate (ConA-FITC) for staining surface mannoproteins. The cells were washed again three times with PBS and suspended in PBS. The images were recorded using the Confocal Stellaris 8 (Leica) at the Microscopy Facility at the Carlos Chagas Institute, Fiocruz-PR.
2.9
Statistical analysis
All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 8.0.1 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test was used to assess statistical differences among groups. Results were considered statistically significant when
p
values were less than or equal to 0.05.

Results
3.1
Isolation of EVs produced by
S. brasiliensis
and
S. schenckii
from solid media
Previous studies characterized
S. brasiliensis
and
S. schenckii
produce EVs at the functional and compositional levels (
Campos et al., 2021
;
da Silva et al., 2025
;
Ikeda et al., 2018
). In these previous studies, EVs were isolated from liquid cultures. However, a recently described method for isolating vesicles from solid media proved to be faster and more efficient (
Reis et al., 2019
). So far,
Sporothrix
EVs produced in solid media were not observed, and a comprehensive analysis under these conditions has never been done. We then applied in the current study the recently described method for isolating EVs from fungi using a solid culture medium matrix (
Reis et al., 2019
) to EVs produced by
S. brasiliensis
and
S. schenckii
cultured for 7 days on solid BHI.
The samples obtained from both species were initially observed by TEM after negative staining, confirming the presence of fungal particles with morphology and structure typical of fungal EVs (
Fig. 1
A-B). To determine the size and concentration of the EV particles, the samples were analyzed by NTA. The vesicle populations ranged from 100 to 400 nm in size with a concentration in the range of 1.17 to 2.9 × 10
12
particles/mL for
S. brasiliensis
and 1.09 to 2.6 × 10
12
particles/mL for
S. schenckii
(
Fig. 1
C and D). All EV samples were prepared as independent triplicates, and the results shown in
Fig. 1
are illustrative of this approach. At this point, we concluded that both
S. brasiliensis
and
S. schenckii
produced similar EVs in solid medium, as described for other fungi (
Reis et al., 2019
).

3.2
Vesicular proteome in
S. brasiliensis
and
S. schenckii
Following the initial identification of EVs in samples obtained from solid media, we proceeded to comparatively analyze the protein composition of EVs produced by
S. brasiliensis
and
S. schenckii.
The EV proteomes of both species exhibited considerable diversity, comprising 822 proteins in
S. brasiliensis
and 955 proteins in
S. schenckii
. A relatively small subset of 223 proteins was shared between both species (Supplementary Table 3), whereas 599 proteins were unique to
S. brasiliensis
(Supplementary Table 4) and 732 were exclusive to
S. schenckii
(Supplementary Table 5), as illustrated in the Venn diagram (
Fig. 2
A). Notably, the number of proteins identified in EVs from solid cultures was approximately 20-fold higher than those reported in earlier studies using liquid media (
Ikeda et al., 2018
). As in previous studies (
Ikeda et al., 2018
), heat shock proteins (HSP) were present in both species. HSP70 was abundantly detected in our study in
S. schenckii
and
S. brasiliensis
, in addition to a minor detection of HSP101 in both species (Supplementary Tables 3–6).

Small molecule characterization of
S. brasiliensis

The small molecule composition of EVs in the
Sporothrix
genus is unknown. We then performed a metabolomic analysis of EVs produced by
S. brasiliensis
and
S. schenckii
. The samples were analyzed by ultra-high performance liquid chromatography coupled with high-resolution mass spectrometry (LC-HRMS). The resulting data were submitted to molecular networking analysis using GNPS platform, an interactive online resource for analyzing and annotating mass spectrometry data focused on small molecules (
Krug and Müller, 2014
;
Nguyen et al., 2013
;
Purves et al., 2016
;
Wang et al., 2016
;
Watrous et al., 2012
).
The molecular networking obtained from the FBMN workflow in positive ionization mode provided insights into the chemical composition of EVs of
S. brasiliensis
and
S. schenckii
. A total of 35 spectral family clusters and 19 self-loop nodes were identified (Supplementary Material, Fig. S1). The comparison of MS/MS spectra with the GNPS library resulted in 44 spectral matches leading to a level 2 confidence annotation (
Schymanski et al., 2014
). The annotated metabolites are listed in
Table 1
, and their respective spectral matches and MS/MS from raw data are available in the Supplementary Material (Fig. S2-S45).

Functional characterization of a small molecule component of
Sporothrix
EVs
Previous studies conducted by our group analyzed the composition of small molecules in EVs from
Cryptococcus gattii
also identified the presence of IPI (
Reis et al., 2021
), a component of
Sporothrix
EVs identified in the present study. In
Cryptococcus
, treatment with IPI prolonged the survival of lethally infected
G. mellonella
(
Reis et al., 2021
). Therefore, we initially tested whether IPI could protect
G. mellonella
against
S. brasiliensis
and
S. schenckii
, but the results were not conclusive due to the lack of reproducibility (Supplementary Material, Fig. S46). Importantly, the physiological concentration of IPI in fungal EVs remains unknown. Consequently, any model testing its biological functions will constitute proof-of-concept investigations with uncertain physiological relevance.
IPI is a well-known inhibitor of DPP4 (
Liu et al., 2024
;
Nongonierma and Fitzgerald, 2013
). This enzyme has been implicated in the pathogenesis of
Blastomyces dermatitidis
(
Lorenzini et al., 2017
) and
Aspergillus fumigatus
(
Beauvais et al., 1997
;
Landis et al., 2008
). Therefore, we asked whether the IPI-DPP4 interaction would affect pathogenic elements in
Sporothrix
. We initially tested if both species would show DPP4 activity through the hydrolysis of the fluorogenic substrate Gly-Pro-7-amido-4-methylcoumarin by intact fungal cells. DPP4 activity was observed in both species, and the enzyme was inhibited by IPI (
Fig. 5
). Of note,
S. schenckii
DPP4 was more susceptible to IPI inhibition than the
S. brasiliensis
enzyme.

As a subcutaneous pathogen, the ability of
Sporothrix
to adhere to extracellular matrix protein is essential for the establishment of fungal lesions (
Figueiredo et al., 2007
;
García-Carnero et al., 2021
;
Gómez-Gaviria et al., 2025
). On the basis of the previously connections between DPP4 and fungal adhesion to collagen (
Beauvais et al., 1997
), we asked whether IPI could affect the ability of
S. schenckii
and
S. brasiliensis
to adhere to this extracellular matrix protein. We therefore investigated the adhesion of both pathogens to type I collagen in the presence or in the absence of IPI. We also included in this analysis sitagliptin, a well-characterized inhibitor of DPP4, at the same molar concentration as IPI. Both IPI and sitagliptin (
Fig. 6
A) significantly reduced (
p
<
0.05
) the adhesion of
Sporothrix
spp. to type I collagen in a concentration-dependent manner. The reduction in
Sporothrix
adhesion to collagen in the presence of IPI and sitagliptin is illustrated microscopically in
Fig. 6
B. To confirm that the reduction in adhesion was indeed due to a putative role of DPP4, we first compared the inhibitory activities of IPI and sitagliptin to that caused by biotin-IPI, which has no DPP4 inhibitory capacity. As shown in
Fig. 6
C, unlike IPI and sitagliptin, biotin-IPI had no effect on the adhesion of
Sporothrix
to collagen. To further support the connections between DPP4 and adhesion, we used an anti-DPP4 antibody as an alternative tool to block the enzyme, compared to an irrelevant, isotype-matched antibody. Treatment with the DPP4 antibody significantly reduced the adhesion of both
Sporothrix
species to collagen, while the irrelevant antibody had no inhibitory effect (
Fig. 6
D).

Discussion
Isolation of
Sporothrix
EVs from solid media has not been investigated so far, although this approach has been reported for other pathogens such as
C. neoformans, C. gattii
,
Histoplasma capsulatum
,
Saccharomyces cerevisiae
,
Candida albicans
(
Piffer et al., 2024
;
Reis et al., 2019
) and
Aspergillus fumigatus
(
Freitas et al., 2023
). The use of solid medium-based protocol for EV isolation proved effective for
Sporothrix
, yielding a relatively concentrated population of vesicles with reproducible profiles across all experiments, as confirmed by NTA and TEM analysis. These conditions consisted of the experimental basis for our further characterization of
Sporothrix
EVs.
As consistently described in the current literature, the mechanisms of biogenesis of fungal EVs are still poorly known. In this context, compositional studies are highly strategic for the understanding of how fungal EVs are formed (
Rodrigues et al., 2025
). The current results were exciting in this sense. Initial proteomic studies identified 40 proteins in
S. schenckii
and 63 proteins in
S. brasiliensis
, with only four proteins shared between the two species (
Ikeda et al., 2018
). Under the conditions used in this study, the numbers of proteins detected in each species were much higher than those observed before (
Ikeda et al., 2018
), which could be attributed to a more efficient isolation of EVs using the solid medium protocol. However, we cannot rule out the impact of equipment sensitivity, which can explain the larger numbers observed in the present study, which identified 223 shared proteins. Collectively, our results and the previous literature suggest that
S. schenckii
and
S. brasiliensis
EVs share a minor protein population, suggesting that the mechanisms used for protein cargo and possibly biogenesis of EVs are divergent in these two species.
Regarding the similarities in the EVs of
S. schenckii
and
S. brasiliensis
, the most abundant EV proteins were related to DNA replication, metabolic pathways and synthetic processes. The presence of heat shock proteins (HSPs) among the differentially abundant proteins in
S. schenckii
, as well as in the total EV proteomes of both species, underscores the relevance of these structures as carriers of important pathogenic determinants in
Sporothrix
. The presence of HSPs in EVs and their role in promoting sporotrichosis in a murine model have already been demonstrated for
S. brasiliensis
(
Ikeda et al., 2018
). EV proteins from
S. schenckii
also showed high abundance proteins corresponding to subunits of the 20S proteasome. EVs from
S. brasiliensis
also contained 20S proteasome subunits, but they were not differentially abundant. In eukaryotic cells, the 20S proteasome constitutes the catalytic core of the 26S proteasome complex, typically localized in the cytoplasm and nucleus (
Fabre et al., 2014
;
Staszczak, 2021
). Recent studies have shown that the 20S proteasome can be encapsulated within EVs, rather than being membrane-associated, enhancing its structural stability (
Kim et al., 2025
;
Martínez-López et al., 2022
). The transport of 20S proteasomes via fungal EVs has been described in
Candida albicans
, where they are secreted exclusively by EVs derived from hyphal cells (
Martínez-López et al., 2022
). Interestingly, in
Candida
, the 20S proteasome acts as a repressor of morphogenesis; its depletion induces filamentation and leads to abnormal nuclear content and altered molecular signaling (
Hossain et al., 2020
). In
Sporothrix
, the cellular responses associated with this phenomenon remain to be elucidated.
Fungal EVs transport various biomolecules, and we are still far from comprehending their biological functions. Among these molecules, small molecules transported in fungal EVs are particularly noteworthy as crucial players in both fungal physiology and pathogenesis. In
H. capsulatum
, the plasticity of EV loading in response to nutritional differences revealed that nutrition played an important role in the small molecule composition of EVs, potentially translating into differences in the biological activities of the fungus in various fluids and tissues (
Cleare et al., 2020
). In
Penicillium digitatum
, compositional analysis of EVs produced during infection revealed a complex cargo of low mass molecules that included tryptoquialanines and the mycotoxin fungisporin, which were associated to phytotoxic activity in vitro and caused damage to citrus seed tissues (
Costa et al., 2021
). The
Sporothrix
EVs contained a wide range of chemical classes of small molecules, including dipeptides, tripeptides, glycopeptides, phospholipids, glycerophospholipids, lysophospholipids, phosphatidylcholines, polyamines, nucleosides, ethers, sterols, alcohols, flavin derivatives, oxidized steroids, phenolic compounds, fatty acid esters, acylated amino acids, and additional polyamines. Their functions remain widely unknown.
In
C. deuterogattii
, analysis of the small molecule composition of EVs produced by wild-type cells and mutants with defects in sugar transport revealed an altered small molecule composition that was associated with cryptococcal virulence (
Castelli et al., 2024
). In the same model, IPI was identified as an EV component that controlled cryptococcal infection in
G. mellonella
(
Reis et al., 2021
). This tripeptide was a common component found in this study in
S. schenckii
and
S. brasiliensis
EVs. Whether IPI exhibited a similar protective potential against
S. brasiliensis
and
S. schenckii
remains unknown, since our results were not consistently reproducible, and consequently unconclusive.
DPP4 is a membrane-bound serine protease that cleaves dipeptides from the N-terminus of polypeptides when the penultimate residue is proline or alanine. This enzyme is highly conserved across bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and mammals (
Alkin et al., 2021
). In humans, the best-characterized DPP4 is CD26, a plasma membrane glycoprotein involved in immune regulation. CD26 exhibits both
endo
- and exopeptidase activity, capable of degrading collagen types I, II, III, and V, thereby contributing to extracellular matrix remodeling (
Bermpohl et al., 1998
). DPP4 also regulates metabolic processes through degradation of incretin hormones such as glucagon-like peptide (GLP-1) and glucose-dependent insulinotropic peptide (GIP), which are essential for insulin secretion. Accordingly, DPP4 inhibitors, such as sitagliptin, vildagliptin, saxagliptin, and linagliptin, are widely used in the treatment of type 2 diabetes (Gallego; Aristoy; Toldrá, 2014). DPP4 homologs have been described in several fungal pathogens, including
A. fumigatus
(
Beauvais et al., 1997
;
Landis et al., 2008
),
H. capsulatum
(
Cooper and Woods, 2009
),
Trichophyton rubrum
(
Monod et al., 2005
) and
Microsporum canis
(
Vermout et al., 2008
).
Sporotrichosis primarily targets skin and subcutaneous tissues, largely due to the pathogen's affinity for extracellular matrix components such as collagen, fibronectin, fibrinogen, and laminin (
López-Ramírez et al., 2024
). In
A. fumigatus,
DPP4 - the physiological target of IPI – acted on collagen-derived dipeptides to promote colonization of the pulmonary surface (
Beauvais et al., 1997
). In this context, we conducted functional analyses to assess whether IPI could influence the adhesion of
Sporothrix
to type I collagen, which was indeed observed. DPP4 activity was detected in intact
Sporothrix
cells and was at least partially responsible for mediating this adhesive mechanism, as sitagliptin (a DPP4 inhibitor structurally unrelated to IPI) produced a similar inhibitory effect. This effect was also observed when fungal cells were treated with an anti-DPP4 antibody. These results position DPP4 as a potential contributor to the pathogenesis of sporotrichosis and suggest that DPP4 inhibitors are promising candidates for investigation in animal models of the disease. In this context, the export of IPI via EVs may represent an efficient mechanism by which the fungus removes this inhibitory molecule from the cellular environment, thereby allowing fungal DPP4 to remain active. Our unpublished results suggested that IPI-containing EVs exhibited a slight inhibitory effect on the adhesion of
S. schenckii
to collagen, but not
S. brasiliensis
(data not shown). The underlying reasons for this observation may be multifaceted, encompassing variations in IPI concentrations among the species and, most likely, the inhibitory activity of unrelated compounds present in
S. schenckii
but absent in
S. brasiliensis
. In this sense, our ongoing studies focus on quantifying IPI in the EVs, in conjunction with the identification of additional biologically relevant compounds. Notably, our findings only constitute a proof-of-concept model demonstrating that EV components could influence the interaction between
Sporothrix
and host components. More comprehensive models, encompassing in vivo infection models and organoids, would be more effective in demonstrating whether IPI indeed affects the interaction between
Sporothrix
and host tissues.
Our study consistently demonstrated that EVs from the main etiological agents of sporotrichosis transport a wide range of biomolecules and proteins potentially involved in key aspects of the pathogenesis and biology of these pathogens. These findings underscore the potential of EVs as valuable tools for studying sporotrichosis, particularly in the search for novel therapeutic targets, antivirulence strategies, and biomarkers.
