Introduction
C19orf12 is an orphan gene with elusive function that is highly conserved in metazoa. Genetic mutations in C19orf12 are associated with neurodegeneration with brain iron accumulation-4 (NBIA4), hereditary spastic paraplegia 43 (SPG43), and sporadic Parkinson’s diseases [
8
,
16
]. C19orf12 protein is found in mitochondria, the endoplasmic reticulum, and the cytosol in cultured cells, where it may regulate lipid metabolism, mitochondrial homeostasis, autophagy, and ferroptosis [
23
,
27
,
30
,
34
]. Due to its close association with the mitochondrial membrane, NBIA4 is also named as mitochondrial membrane protein-associated neurodegeneration (MPAN), one of a group of related diseases characterized by loss of motor function, iron accumulation, and often with onset in childhood and early adulthood. Human MPAN presents predominantly autosomal recessive and less frequent dominant inheritance, indicating a potential loss of physiological function of C19orf12 in MPAN pathogenesis [
2
,
5
,
21
]. Animal models, including drosophila and zebra fish lacking C19orf12 exhibited neurodegenerative features [
12
,
18
,
19
]. To investigate neuropathogenesis in a more relevant mammal model, we employed a rodent model to assess the neurological impact of the absence of C19orf12 mouse homolog gene 1600014C10Rik in this study.
Neuroaxonal dystrophy is a prominent feature of ageing and neurodegenerative diseases particular in NBIA [
14
,
28
,
33
]. Characteristic eosinophilic spheroids or swollen axons were well documented in brain autopsies of NBIA cases carrying mutations either in PKNA2, PLA2G6, C19orf12, or WDR45. Immunohistochemistry examination of spheroids in MPAN autopsy revealed immunoreactivity to ubiquitin, tau and α-synuclein [
8
,
10
], but the mechanism underlying the formation of spheroids has not been well investigated in a suitable model of MPAN. Here, we found that 1600014C10Rik knockout mice presented numerous axonal spheroids in brain and spinal cord, alongside increased redox iron levels, neuroinflammation and α-synuclein pathology. Importantly, our study found that such spheroids consisted of abnormal expansion of smooth ER membranes in brains particularly in axonal terminals of dopaminergic neuron, which may contribute to the motor deficits of C19orf12 knockout mice in an age-cohort of animals. Altogether, C19orf12 knockout mice showed strikingly similar neuropathology to human MPAN, suggesting that this MPAN mouse model is a valuable tool for related research.
Result
1600014C10Rik knockout mouse recapitulates neuropathological hallmarks of MPAN
The human C19orf12 gene encodes a 152-amino-acid (aa) isoform, a 141-aa isoform, and other shorter isoforms. While the 141-aa isoform is conserved in all animals, the long 151-aa isoform is unique in primates (Fig.
1
A). The remarkable preservation of the C19orf12 protein structure between mice and humans indicates that a mouse line lacking C19orf12 would be an ideal model for studying the pathogenesis of human MPAN (Fig.
1
B). We retrieved single-cell RNA sequencing data from the Tabula-Muris database [
1
], revealing that the mouse homologue, 1600014C10Rik, is expressed in neurons, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, and other cell types in the mouse brain (Fig.
1
C). However, all these cells displayed low abundance of 1600014C10Rik RNA transcripts. To investigate the neuropathological implications of mouse 1600014C10Rik, we investigated a mouse line with a knockout of the exon 3 using CRISPR-Cas9 genomic editing, resulting in the removal of approximately 60% of its protein domain (Fig.
1
D). Real-time PCR analysis demonstrated that the brains of homozygous knockout animals exhibited almost complete absence of 1600014C10Rik RNA transcripts, while heterozygous animals retained half the levels observed in wildtype animals (Fig.
1
E).

Given the loss of function of C19orf12 in human MPAN, homozygous knockout mice were investigated with age and gender matched wild-type control mice in this study. There was no difference in birth rate and development between knockout and wild-type animals. However, at the age of 18 months, the brains of all knockout animals showed numerous spheroid bodies with hematoxylin and eosin staining (Fig.
2
A). These spheroid bodies were eosinophilic, varying in size and morphology, and were found in the cerebellum nucleus, thalamus, pons, spinal cord and cuneate, which were absent in age-matched wild-type mice (not shown). High-power images revealed that these spheroid bodies contained lucent clefts (Fig.
2
A, lower panel). Additionally, increased aggregation of reactive microglia was present in these brain regions only in the knockout mice (Fig.
2
B). Notably, increased levels of redox iron were observed in the affected brain region of knockout animals compared to WT mice at 18 months of age (Fig.
2
C). Consistently, real-time PCR analysis of iron metabolism genes indicated the upregulation of Fpn1, Mfrn1, Mfrn2, Fth1, Ftl1, Abcb7, and Trfc2 in the brain of knockout animals, suggesting disturbed iron metabolism, a defining feature of human NBIA/MPAN disorders. (Fig.
2
D). These findings revealed that the knockout mice faithfully replicated the neurological hallmarks of human MPAN disease.

Tubular ER abnormality and damaged mitochondria in axonal spheroid
Given the numerous axonal spheroids found in knockout mice, we examined electron micrographs that revealed characteristic swollen axons in multiple brain regions (Fig.
3
A). There were tubulovesicular structures, long condensed membranes, and electron-lucent clefts in swollen axons in the striatum. In the cuneate, abnormal mitochondria and ER were arranged with tubulovesicular structures in swollen axon. In the cerebellum, spiral layers of smooth ER membranes contained autophagic vesicles in the center, along with nearby damaged mitochondria. Similar structures were not seen in the WT mouse EM images. To explore the underling mechanism of axonal spheroid formation, we performed cerebral proteomics analysis of brains tissues of 12 months old mice by LC-MS/MS assay. Indeed, the abundance of proteins involving tubular ER network organization such as ARL6IP1, ATL1 and REEP5 were significantly increased in knockout mice brains (Fig.
3
B), and other tubular ER proteins including RTN3 also mildly skewed toward higher expression in knockout mice brain, suggesting a disturbed tubular ER homeostasis (Fig.
3
C). Previous studies had shown that RTN3 protein served as a reliable indicator of tubular ER homeostasis in the mouse brain and cultured cells [
7
,
24
]. Therefore, we employed a well-characterized anti-RNT3 antibody to investigate the potential alterations in tubular ER observed in swollen axons. Indeed, 3D reconstruction of confocal images with anti-RTN3 immunostaining, showed a tubular ER membrane pattern, and decorated swollen axons wrapped by a partial myelin sheath (Fig.
3
D). In addition, double immunofluorescence staining with anti-RTN3 and anti-PDHE1 for tubular ER and mitochondria respectively, showed enlarged abnormal mitochondria around anti-RTN3 lucent clefts of axonal spheroids (Fig.
3
E). Importantly, the anti-RTN3 immunostaining decorated frequent fluorescence-lucent clefts as identified in EM graphs. According to RTN3 immunostaining in 3D graphs, the estimated maximal diameter of swollen axons was about 15 μm, and the maximal length about 20 μm, suggesting a confined expansion of ER membranes in swollen axons. These findings suggested abnormal tubular ER and mitochondria were the major component of swollen axons which likely expand longitudinally to no more than 20 μm in knockout animals.

Early axonal accumulation of tubular ER protein RTN3 in KO mice
We then examined the onset and distribution of RTN3-positive swollen spheroids in the brains of knockout mice. The brainstem in 4-month old knockout animals showed abnormally high immunoreactivity of the anti-RTN3 antibody, while there was only weak staining of neuronal soma in wildtype mice (Fig.
4
A). Importantly, abnormal anti-RTN3 immunoreactivity became more prominent in additional regions including hippocampus, substantia nigra (SN), and striatum of 18-month knockout mice with negligible immunostaining in the 18-month WT mice (Fig.
4
B). Anti-RTN3 antibody decorated three types of spheroids based on their size: large spheroid aggregate (type I), medium-sized spheroid with lucent cleft (type II), and small spheroid associated with the regular size of an axon (type III) (Fig.
4
C). Notably, these small type III spheroids could be identified by common ER markers, such as anti-IP3R and anti-Calnexin antibodies, but not for large type I and type II (Fig.
4
D). Co-immunostaining of RTN3 with other tubular ER proteins such as REEP2 and REEP5 revealed their co-localization in those large type I and type II swollen axons of aged knockout mice (Fig.
4
E). Further analysis of anti-RTN3 showed its co-localization with the axonal marker β-III-tubulin (Fig.
4
F). Importantly, reactive microglia were closely associated with these abnormal large RTN3 immunoreactive aggregates (Fig.
4
G).

Axonal swellings in dopaminergic neurons containing pathological protein inclusions
Given the prominent development of axonal spheroids and abnormal RTN3-positive structures in the SN and striatum of knockout animals, we asked whether dopaminergic neurons were affected in these regions. There was no difference of overall immunoreactivity of anti-tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) among wild-type and knockout animals in these two regions. While there was no apparent loss of axonal terminals in the striatum in the knockout mice even at 18 months of age, we observed many abnormal, large, and swollen axonal terminals stained with the anti-TH antibody (Fig.
5
A). These terminals appeared as large aggregates or pearls on a string, alongside numerous fine, normal dopaminergic axons that were also stained with the anti-TH antibody (Fig.
5
B). Interestingly, immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence staining revealed that anti-α-synuclein antibody also decorated inclusion-like structures in the same brain region (Fig.
5
C) which were often found in close association with microglia (Fig.
5
D), along with anti-ubiquitin immunoreactivity (Fig.
5
E). Notably, these swollen dopaminergic axonal terminals co-localized with synuclein inclusions (Fig.
5
F). Furthermore, synuclein inclusions were found in the cell bodies of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra (Fig.
5
G). In addition, anti-α-synuclein immunoreactivity also presented in non-TH axons as well. Immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated that swollen dopaminergic axonal terminals interacted with reactive microglia in the striatum, while such microglia reactivity was less prevalent in SN (Fig.
5
H).

Tubular ER defects in axonal swellings of dopaminergic neurons
We then asked whether swollen dopaminergic axons were linked to abnormal tubular ER in knockout animals. Indeed, anti-RTN3 immunoreactivity was observed in swollen TH axonal terminals of dopaminergic neurons in brain regions innervated by dopaminergic axons (Fig.
6
A). In thalamus and striatum, large TH-positive axonal swellings were accompanied by RTN3 positive ER membrane aggregation. These swollen axonal terminals also exhibited fluorescence-lucent clefts, reminiscent of electron microscopic images of the abnormal axonal structures shown in Fig.
2
. In the cerebellum, smaller, scarce TH axonal swellings were associated with RTN3 aggregates that appeared as pearls on a string. In the SN, presynaptic RTN3 aggregates were closely linked to cell body of dopaminergic neurons, while no detectable intracellular RTN3 aggregates were found in the soma of TH neurons.

The axonal changes described in the striatum and cerebellum could represent a dying back neuronal degeneration. To further assess the integrity of dopaminergic neurons in SN, we performed stereological analysis of brain sections spanning the midbrain stained by anti-TH antibodies (Fig.
6
B). The results showed a notable decrease in the size of TH neurons in knockout mice, while the number of TH neurons in the SN remained unchanged (Fig.
6
C), a finding that is not unexpected as there is no striking axon terminal loss in the striatum that would reflect a loss of dopaminergic SN neurons. In the cerebellum, however, which also showed evidence of oxidative related iron accumulation in Fig.
2
and axonal spheroid development, there is a significant loss of Purkinje cells in the 18-month KO mice (Fig.
6
D–E). Although these brain regions demonstrate localized neurodegeneration and death, there is no change in brain weight even at 18 months between the KO and WT mice (data not shown), suggesting this mutation causes a more chronic condition and not acute neuronal death throughout the brain.
Age dependent decline in motor function
As the cerebellum and dopaminergic neuron containing brain regions are involved in motor function, we tested the motor performance of knockout animals in an age cohort from 3 months to 15 months. There was no noticeable difference in the physical appearance between knockout and wild-type animals at each time points. Knockout animals gained weight at the same rate as WT animals, from 3 months to 15 months (Fig.
7
A). In the rotarod task, knockout animals exhibited decreased performance in terms of best speed, average time, and best time on the cylinder of the unit at 6 months, which gradually declined until 15 months (Fig.
7
B-D). When animals were subjected to walk on a large beam, there was no difference in performance between wild-type and knockout animals (data not shown). However, the performance of knockout animals on the medium bar began to deteriorate at 9 months, while a significant difference was observed among 15-month-old knockout animals (Fig.
7
E–F).

Discussion
This study demonstrates that a rodent model of MPAN recapitulated several hallmarks of human MPAN neuropathology. First, we discovered an increase in redox iron levels in multiple brain regions, particularly in the cuneate nucleus. It was further confirmed by disturbed RNA transcription of iron metabolism regulators, suggesting abnormal iron homeostasis in brain of knockout mice. Second, we observed widespread neuroinflammation in mouse brain tissues. Indeed, reactive microglia were interacting with large swollen axons, while most the spheroids of small or medium size were spared from reactive microglia. This implies that the neuroinflammation was caused by the final stage of spheroid damage, characterized by a compromised structure integrity of axonal spheroid. We also identified characteristic α-synuclein inclusion in dopaminergic neurons especially within TH swollen axons in striatum. Interestingly, such α-synuclein pathology was not limited to anti-TH positive dopaminergic neurons in knockout mice, suggesting a similar widespread accumulation of pathological α-synuclein as observed in human MPAN. Finally, motor function impairment demonstrated using the rotarod and beam walk tests was also a prominent feature in the MPAN mouse model. Future study should devote to characterize the detailed biochemical features of α-synuclein pathology in this mouse model. These changes likely represented a dying back feature that correlated with neurodegeneration and neuronal loss in the SN and cerebellum respectively and was evident in motor function deficits.
There is a slight difference of C19orf12 protein sequence among primates and non-primate animals [
8
]. The long 152-aa isoform is unique in primates, suggesting an evolutionary adaption of C19orf12 protein among primate ancestors. Furthermore, the human 152-aa isoform prefers to express in brain tissues rather than peripheral tissues [
3
,
34
]. , suggesting that it is more essential for the homeostasis of central nervous system of human. Although mouse expresses less human brain relevant 141-aa isoform in brain, the knockout mice presented remarkably similar neurodegeneration as found in brains tissues of human MPAN, suggesting a conserved function of two isoforms from evolutional perspective. Furthermore, this knockout mouse model could be used to investigate the functional differences between the long and short isoforms of C19orf12 after delivery into brain tissues by viral vectors for rescue experiment.
Our study indicated that neuropathological phenotypes of the knockout mice was modified by age. Younger mice showed significant deficits in motor performance at 6 months of age by the rota-rod test, which gradually worsened with aging. In contrast, the beam-walking performance of knockout mice only significantly deteriorated at older ages, while young mice showed less extensive deterioration, possibly indicating that the 17 mm square beam might not be a suitable tool to catch early motor deficits in this model. This late onset of motor symptoms in knockout mice differed from human juvenile MPAN patients with severe symptoms onset much earlier, considering the longer life span of humans than rodents. It is likely that the knockout of C19orf12 homologs in mice might be mitigated by an unknown compensatory mechanism. Alternatively, it is possible that primate specific long 152-aa isoform in human brain bears unique function which is not shared by the short 141-aa isoform expressed in rodents and in peripheral tissues of human. Therefore, it is likely that loss of such 152-aa isoform is much more devastating in human rather in non-primate animals. Future exploration of biochemical difference among the two isoforms of C19orf12 protein should address this discrepancy.
Our study noted remarkable tubular ER deficit in axonal spheroid which likely explains the neuroaxonal dystrophy in knockout mice. Neuronal axons contain a network of tubular ER that continuously extends from neuronal soma to axonal terminals. Previous studies showed defects in tubular ER molecular machinery caused neuroaxonal dystrophy and related axonal spheroid as found in human HSP patients [
20
]. RTN3, a neuronal axonal ER protein, plays a role in neurodegeneration as a bystander or activator [
11
]. Enhanced immunoreactivity of RTN3 was found in brain tissues of sporadic cases of human neurodegenerative diseases [
9
]. RTN3 was specifically associated with dystrophic neurites of human/rodent AD tissues [
25
], WDR45/BNAN [
32
] and neuronal ATG5 deficits [
15
]. These observations align with our characterization of MPAN axonal spheroid, which exhibited high RTN3 immunoreactivity. However, the overall abundance of RTN3 protein, as determined by LC-MS/MS, showed mild changes, indicating possible abnormal distribution and following enrichment within the axonal spheroid. The precise mechanism underlying the association of spheroid with the expansion of the axonal ER membrane remains unclear in MPAN mice. Given that tubular ER is a major site for lipid biogenesis, and C19orf12 and its homologs likely regulate lipid metabolism by its association with ER and mitochondria membrane as has been described in cell and Drosophila models [
13
,
27
], it would be reasonable to suspect that abnormal ER membrane pathology in knockout animals might be secondary to disrupted lipid homeostasis due to C19orf12 deficiency.
We also illustrated the effects of axonal spheroid on a specific subset of neurons in vivo. While there was no brain weight loss in the KO mice, even at 18 months of age, and no apparent changes in hippocampus or cortical brain structures (data not shown), significant Purkinje cell loss was found in the cerebellum, which is a characteristic of human MPAN and related disorders. Due to the limitation of current antibody tools, we did not extend the investigation of the swollen axonal terminals in hippocampus, cortex and cerebellum, which should be addressed in the future study. Indeed, this study used anti-TH immunostaining to identify swollen axons in dopaminergic neurons in the KO mice. Though the overall intensity of anti-TH immunostaining was not changed in brain, there were many swollen dopaminergic axons found in brain regions innervated by dopaminergic neurons from SN such as the striatum, and indeed these SN neurons were smaller and degenerative but did not show increased cell death. Interestingly, we found that such swollen TH axonal terminals were featured by anti-RTN3 immunoreactivity as well, suggesting the deficits of tubular ER defect is one of the fundamental features of neuroaxonal damage in C19orf12 knockout mice. The observations of Purkinje cell loss and impaired dopaminergic system is consistent with the parkinsonism symptoms of MPAN patients, suggesting a critical effect of axonal ER deficit in the neuropathogenesis in this rodent model and likely in human MPAN.
Materials and methods
Animals and behavior tests
The mouse knockout of 1600014C10Rik was obtained from the Jackson Laboratory (046094-JAX). The mice were maintained in the university Animal Resource Center under normal conditions, and all experiments were conducted in accordance with the approved protocols by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Case Western Reserve University. Brain and spinal cord tissue was collected at 4 months (n≠≠ = 3 KO;
n
= 4 WT) and at 18 months (
n
= 11 KO;
n
= 13 WT) for morphological analysis. At different ages up to 18 months, behavior tests were performed among age and gender matched cohort (
n
= 12 KO;
n
= 12 WT). For beam walking test, animals were placed on one end of a beam (diameter 17 mm) and guided towards a dark box at the opposite end. The average time of 3 trials and best time taken to cross the beam from end to end were recorded. For Rota-Rod test (Ugo Basile), mice were placed on the cylinder of the unit with the speed accelerating from 4 to 40 rpm over 5 min. Each mouse was tested individually for the best speed of cylinder, average time and best time staying on the cylinder. After the behavior tests, animals were sacrificed and the brains dissected and immersed in 10% buffered formalin for fixation before paraffin embedding.
Brain tissue RNA extraction and realtime PCR
RNA was extracted from whole brain tissues by a commercial kit (Qiagen/ RNeasy Kits). cDNA synthesis was processed with high-capacity cDNA reverse transcription kit from ThermoFisher. Primer sequences were designed by Primer-Blast from NCBI, and primer information is available upon request.
Immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry staining
Immunocytochemistry was performed by the peroxidase anti-peroxidase protocol with formalin fixed paraffin embedded brain samples sectioned at 6 μm thickness as described before [
31
]. Hematoxylin and eosin staining was performed using routine methods. The redox iron staining method was performed as previously described [
26
]. Primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4 °C and fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies were incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Stained samples were mounted by Fluoromount-G mounting medium (Southern Biotech) with #1.5 cover slide. Confocal images were collected by Leica TSC S8 platform with an inverted DMI 6000 adaptive focus microscope equipped with 40x/1.3 (oil) objective. 3D reconstruction of confocal images was processed by 3D viewer plugin in ImageJ Fiji [
22
]. The following antibodies were used for immunostaining: anti-IBA1 (Wako #019-19741), anti-CD68 (Abcam #
Ab125212
), anti-PDHE1 (PTG #18068), anti-IP3R (Calbiochem #407143), Calnexin (CellSignaling #2679), anti-β-III-tubulin (CellSignaling #5666), anti-TH (Millipore Mouse #MAB318/Rabbit #AB152), anti-α-synuclein (Millipore #MABN633/ StressMarq#SMC5335), anti-ubiquitin (Millipore #MAB1510), anti-Myelin basic protein (MBP) (Abcam #216668), Anti-Calbindin (Swant #CB38a) anti-REEP2 (PTG #15684) and anti-REEP5 (PTG #14643). Antibodiy for RTN3 (mouse) was obtained from Dr. Yan lab.
Stereological counting of dopaminergic neurons
To quantify the number of TH-positive dopaminergic neurons in the SN, coronal sections spanning the entire midbrain were cut from 18 month old knock and wildtype mice (
n
= 5 per group). Every third slide was immunostained for TH, and 8 sections from each mouse representing the entire SN with the same Bregma location were imaged and the number of TH-positive cells qu8antified using Image J with constant size cutoff representing the lower and upper limit of neurons and thresholding. Total number of cells from 8 sections was compared. To accurately measure the neuron size, one section representing each mouse was opened in Image J and the scale set. Each TH-stained neuron was outlined manually and the mean size calculated.
Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS)
10ug of each sample was diluted into 100 µl of 2% SDS, 50 mM HEPES, 50 mM EDTA and 5mM DTT and incubated for 30 min at 60 °C to reduce any disulfide bonds, the free cysteine was alkylated with 20mM iodoacetamide for an hour at room temperature in the dark and followed by SP3 beads digestion with trypsin (sequencing grade, Thermo Scientific Cat#90058) in 100mM ammonium bicarbonate, 2 mM CaCl2 and incubated at 370 C overnight. Peptides were dried and solubilized in 0.1% TFA and stored at -80 C until ready for analysis. Peptides were analyzed by LC-MS using Nano LC-MS/MS (Dionex Ultimate 3000 RLSCnano System, Thermofisher) interfaced with Eclipse (Thermofisher). Raw data were analyzed with predicted library from uniprot mouse reference proteome for library-free search using DIA NN 1.8.1 with recommended settings.
Electronic microscopy analysis
Electron microscopic analysis was performed as previously described [
31
]. Mouse brains tissues were fixed with EM fixative of triple aldehyde-2.5% DMSO fixative solution. After washing, post-fixation and dehydration, 70 nm sections were cut on RMC MT6000-XL ultramicrotome. For various mouse brain regions, semi-thin sections were cut and stained with toluidine blue which was imaged with light microscopy and the neuronal region of interest marked for EM examination. The thin sections were mounted on Gilder square 300 mesh nickel grids (Electron Microscopy Sciences, PA) and then stained with acidified uranyl acetate and Sato’s triple lead stain [
6
]. Then coated on a Denton DV-401 carbon coater (Denton Vacuum LLC, NJ), the sections were examined in an FEI Tecnai Spirit (T12) with a Gatan US4000 4kx4k CCD.
Bioinformatic analysis, experimental design and statistical analysis
Protein sequences were retrieved UniProt database (
https://www.uniprot.org

4
