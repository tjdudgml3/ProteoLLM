Introduction
African swine fever (ASF) is a highly contagious infectious disease caused by African swine fever virus (ASFV) [
1
,
2
], which leads to both domestic pigs and wild boars of various breeds and ages with high morbidity and mortality [
3
]. In 1921, ASF initially appeared in Kenya [
4
]. Subsequently, ASFV has spread swiftly and extensively across multiple countries and regions globally, exacerbating its impacts on the affected areas [
5–7
]. ASF emerged in China in 2018 and soon spread to other Southeast Asian countries [
5
,
8
,
9
]. Without effective vaccines or agents, the only available control strategies are animal quarantine and large-scale slaughter, affecting the global agricultural economy and posing a significant threat to food security [
10
,
11
]
ASFV, the sole member of the genus
Asfivirus
within the
Asfarviridae
family [
12–14
], has a complex virion structure comprising an internal genome, nucleocapsid, inner envelope membrane, icosahedral protein capsid, and outer envelope membrane [
15
,
16
]. ASFV mainly infects the monocyte-macrophage system and can invade through macropinocytosis or the clathrin/dynamin mediated endocytosis, processes that involve the cell membrane [
17
,
18
]. After entering the cell, the virion is internalized through the endosomal pathway. The ASFV virion then begins to uncoat in the acidic endosomal environment, culminating in the fusion of the inner membrane of ASFV with the late endosomal membrane, thereby releasing the naked viral core containing the viral genome into the cytoplasm to initiate replication [
18–20
]. It has been reported that cepharanthine, a bis-benzylisoquinoline alkaloid, alkalizes late endosomes/lysosomes and hinders ASFV endosomal transport and uncoating, thereby inhibiting ASFV internalization [
21
]. ASFV replication primarily occurs within cytoplasmic viral factories, where newly synthesized virions are assembled and subsequently transported to the plasma membrane along microtubules
via
kinesin-1. During budding from the host cell, ASFV acquire an additional lipid envelope [
19
,
22
].
The interactions between the virus and the host cell membrane, as well as the structural and functional changes of the cell membrane, are crucial for virus infection, particularly when the infection begins with the entry of viral particles into the host cell. Profilin 1 (PFN1) is an actin-binding protein that regulates the formation of the cytoskeleton and is involved in various cellular activities, including cell migration and membrane transport [
23
]. Additionally, glycerophospholipids are the primary components of cell membranes, with phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) being the second most abundant glycerophospholipids in mammalian cells, constituting 15% to 25% of total glycerophospholipid [
24
]. PE plays significant roles in cellular processes, such as membrane fusion, the cell cycle, autophagy, and apoptosis [
25
]. It has been reported that PFN1 and PE can influence the replication of virus. For example, PFN1 facilitates the production of progeny human immunodeficiency virus type 1 (HIV-1) [
26
], while PE binding to TIM1 promotes virus entry [
27
].
Using our established platform for screening small molecules against ASFV [
28
], we discovered that rinderine (RIN) significantly inhibits the replication of ASFV. To date, antiviral research on RIN as a pyrrolizidine alkaloid (PA) has been limited [
29
]. PA is an important group of secondary metabolites in plants, notorious for their severe genotoxicity, hepatotoxicity, and neurotoxicity to both humans and animals [
30
,
31
]. However, PA exhibits anti-bacterial, anti-inflammatory, anti-cancer, and antiviral activities [
31
]. For example, various compounds, such as madhumidine A, lindelofidine benzoic acid ester, and minalobine B isolated from the leaves of
Madhuca pasquieri
, demonstrate anti-inflammatory activity by inhibiting lipopolysaccharide-induced nitric oxide production in macrophage RAW264.7 [
32
]. In addition, alexine isolated from
Alexa leiopetala
exhibits anti-HIV-1 activity, which is mechanistically associated with both inhibition of α-glucosidase I, a glycoprotein-processing enzymes, and suppression of the cleavage of the HIV-1 precursor glycoprotein gp160 [
31
].
In this study, we first found that RIN can effectively inhibit the replication of ASFV in primary porcine alveolar macrophages (PAMs) in a dose- and time-dependent manner. Furthermore, we demonstrated that RIN targeting of PFN1 exerts its anti-ASFV activity by competitively inhibiting the PFN1-actin interaction, which decreases the actin remodelling at the cell membrane. Moreover, RIN reduces the level of PE in the glycerophospholipid metabolism pathway to decrease the cell membrane permeability. Notably, both PFN1 and PE are critical regulators of cytoskeletal organization and the fluidity and stability of the cell membrane, influencing the viral infection. In summary, the study aims to provide new perspectives for the prevention and control of ASF.
Materials and methods
Ethics statements
All the experiments with live ASFV in this study were performed in the biosafety level 3 (BSL-3) facilities at Harbin Veterinary Research Institute (HVRI) of the Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (CAAS) and approved by the Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs, China. Additionally, the isolation of PAMs was conducted in compliance with the Animal Welfare Act and the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, and approved by the Laboratory Animal Welfare Committee of HVRI (approval number 241016-02-GR).
Cells, virus strains, and small molecules
PAMs isolated from 21- to 25-day-old specific-pathogen-free (SPF) pigs and wild boar lung (WSL) cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (catalog no. C11875500BT; Gibco) containing L-glutamine and sodium bicarbonate, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (catalog no. A5669701; Gibco) at 37°C in 5% CO
2
. Porcine kidney 15 (PK-15) cells were cultured in DMEM medium (catalog no. C11995500BT; Gibco) containing D-glucose, L-glutamine, and sodium pyruvate, supplemented with 10% FBS. The ASFV HLJ/18 strain (ASFV-WT) was isolated from field samples in China as described previously (GenBank accession no.
MK333180.1
). Additionally, various gene-deleted ASFV mutants (rASFV-ΔCD2v, rASFV-ΔMGF, and rASFV-ΔMGF/CD2v), as well as classical swine fever virus (CSFV) and fluorescent reporter pseudorabies virus (PRV) were preserved in our laboratory [
33–35
]. In this study, ASFV stocks were prepared from the supernatants and the cell lysates of infected PAMs, whereas CSFV and PRV stocks were derived solely from the supernatants of infected cells. RIN (catalog no. WKQ-0005121; Weikeqi), provided by VICA Bioscience (Shenyang, China), was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). PE (catalog no.
B74432
; Yuanye) and meclozine (catalog no.
T40652
; TargetMol) were dissolved in chloroform and DMSO, respectively.
Cytotoxicity assay
The cytotoxicity of RIN, PE, chloroform, and meclizine to PAMs or PK-15 cells was assessed using the CellTiter-Glo (CTG) kit (catalog no. G7572; Promega) following the manufacturer’s protocols [
28
].
Construction of plasmids
To construct the plasmid expressing the Flag-tagged
PFN1
gene, the porcine
PFN1
gene was cloned into the p3×Flag-CMV-10 vector to generate the expression plasmid pFlag-PFN1. The primers used for
PFN1
gene amplification are listed in Table S1.
Quantitative PCR (qPCR)
ASFV genomic DNA was extracted from the PAMs infected with ASFV or co-treated with RIN and ASFV using a TIANamp genomic DNA kit (catalog no. A0909A; Tiangen), following the manufacturer’s protocols. qPCR was performed to quantify the viral genome copies using the QuantStudio system (Applied Biosystems, USA) with primers recommended by the World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH) [
36
]. The qPCR reaction was performed in a 20-µL reaction mixture containing 2 µL of genomic DNA, 1 µL of forward primer, 1 µL of reverse primer, 0.5 µL of probe, and 5.5 µL of ddH
2
O. The amplification conditions were as follows: 50°C for 2 min, 95°C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 15 s, annealing at 58°C for 30 s, and extension at 58°C for 1 min. The viral genome copies in the samples were quantified by a standard curve.
Immunofluorescence assay
PK-15 cells were treated with 10-fold serial dilutions of RIN (5, 10, 20, 40, and 80 μM) in combination with CSFV at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.5 for 48 h. The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 30 min. After fixation, the cells were washed thrice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Following that, the cells were permeabilized with 0.3% triton X-100 for 15 min and washed three times with PBS. The cells were then blocked with 5% of non-fat milk in TRIS-buffered saline with 0.05% tween-20 (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature. After washing three times with PBS, the cells were incubated with anti-CSFV sera prepared by our laboratory at 37°C for 1 h and then stained with FITC-conjugated rabbit anti-pig IgG (catalog no. F1638; Sigma-Aldrich) at 37°C for 1 h to capture fluorescence images.
Viral titration
The cells were cultured in 96-well plates and inoculated with 10-fold serial dilutions of ASFV-WT, CSFV, or PRV for 5 days. ASFV titers were quantified by hemadsorption assay using porcine red blood cells (RBCs) (10
6
cells/well), with the 50% hemadsorption dose (HAD
50
) calculated based on the “rosette” formation of RBCs [
37
]. CSFV and PRV titers were determined by the 50% tissue culture infectious dose (TCID
50
) method [
37
]. CSFV-infected cells were analyzed by immunofluorescence assay [
38
], while PRV-infected cells were visualized under a fluorescence microscope.
Virucidal assay
To investigate whether RIN exerts direct virucidal effects on ASFV, ASFV-WT (MOI = 1.5) was mixed with RIN (80 μM), while ASFV-WT (MOI = 1.5) alone served as a control, followed by 2-hour incubation at 37°C. The mixture was then diluted 50-fold with RPMI 1640 medium to avoid inhibitory effects and added to PAMs, which were incubated at 37°C in 5% CO
2
for 72 h. Subsequently, the supernatants and the cell lysates were collected for quantification of viral genome copies and titers.
Time-of-addition assay
To determine which stages of the viral life cycle are inhibited by RIN, multiple time points were established for harvesting samples as a reference. PAMs were pretreated with 80 μM RIN for 6 h, followed by infection with ASFV-WT. At 3 hours postinfection (hpi), the medium was discarded, washed twice with PBS, and then replaced with RIN-containing culture solution at the following intervals (relative to infection): 0–3, 3–6, 6–9, 9–12, 12–15, and 15–18 hpi. Following this, the cells were incubated with fresh medium for 24 h, after which the supernatants and the cell lysates were collected for qPCR analysis.
Virus-cell attachment assay
PAMs were co-treated with RIN (80 μM) and ASFV-WT (MOI = 5) at 4°C for 1 h, followed by two washes with PBS. Subsequently, the cell lysates were collected for the qPCR assay [
39
,
40
].
Virus-cell internalization assay
To allow ASFV to be absorbed on the cell membrane, PAMs were first incubated with ASFV-WT (MOI = 5) at 4°C for 1 h. Subsequently, the cells were washed twice with PBS, followed by the addition of RIN (80 μM) and incubation at 37°C for 2 h. After three washes with PBS, the cells were treated with 0.05% trypsin for 2 min and proteinase K (50 μg/mL) for 30 s to remove the non-internalized virus. The cell lysates were then collected to quantify the viral genome copies [
39
,
41
].
Untargeted metabolomics profiling and liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis
To identify the key differential metabolites that mediate the inhibition of ASFV replication by RIN, the PAMs cultured in 24-well plates (10
6
cells/well) were subjected to treatment with ASFV-WT (MOI = 1) alone or co-treated with ASFV-WT (MOI = 1) and RIN (80 μM) at 37°C in 5% CO
2
for 24 h. After discarding the supernatant, PAMs were washed thrice with ice-cold PBS and collected into tubes that were maintained on ice. The samples were centrifuged at 500 ×
g
for 5 min at 4°C, and the resulting cell lysates fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde were utilized for untargeted metabolomics profiling and LC-MS/MS analysis conducted at 24 hpi by Maiteville Bioscience (Wuhan, China) [
28
].
Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA) and mass spectrometry sample preparation
PAMs were infected with ASFV-WT (MOI = 1) or co-treated with RIN (80 μM) and ASFV-WT (MOI = 1). The cells were digested with trypsin at 24 hpi and then centrifuged at 500 ×
g
for 5 min. After discarding the supernatant, the cell pellet was collected and resuspended in 20 μL of PBS containing 1 mM phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) (a protease inhibitor) (catalog no. BL507A; Beyotime). For thermal gradient profiling, we used a gradient thermal cycler PCR machine (Applied Biosystems, USA) to cover the temperatures 37.6, 41, 44.6, 47.5, and 50.4°C for 3 min. Following that, the samples were added 80 μL of NP-40 lysis buffer (catalog no. P0013F; Beyotime) containing 1 mM PMSF to subject to three freeze–thaw cycles. Subsequently, the lysates were centrifuged at 13,000 ×
g
for 20 min to collect the supernatants for SDS-PAGE, followed by silver staining for the identification of differentially expressed proteins
via
mass spectrometry by Bioprofile Bioscience (Shanghai, China). An appropriate amount of peptides from each sample was subjected to chromatographic separation using the Easy-nLC 1200 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). After peptide separation, the data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mass spectrometry analysis was performed with a Q-Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and the mass spectrometry data were analyzed using the MaxQuant v2.4.14.0.
Reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)
Total RNAs were extracted from the PAMs treated with RIN (80 μM) alone, co-treated with RIN (80 μM) and ASFV-WT (MOI = 1), or infected with ASFV-WT (MOI = 1) alone using an RNA isolation kit (catalog no. BSC52M1; BioFlux). For RT-qPCR, cDNA was generated using FastKing gDNA Dispelling RT SuperMix (catalog no. A0530A; Tiangen) and quantified using the ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (catalog no. Q311-02; Vazyme). The relative transcriptional levels of
PFN1
gene were measured using the comparative cycle threshold (2
-ΔΔCT
) method [
33
]. GAPDH was used as an internal reference control. The RT-qPCR primers are listed in Table S2.
Western blotting
PAMs were lysed in ice-cold NP-40 lysis buffer containing 1 mM PMSF at 4°C for 30 min. The lysates were then centrifuged at 12,000 ×
g
for 15 min at 4°C. The supernatants mixed with 5 × SDS loading buffer were boiled for 10 min and resolved by SDS-PAGE. The resulting proteins were transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes, which were then blocked with 5% skim milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature. The membranes were subsequently incubated with appropriate primary antibodies, followed by secondary antibodies. Finally, the protein bands were visualized using an Odyssey imaging system (Li-Cor, USA).
Co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) assay
HEK293T cells were co-transfected with pFlag-PFN1 (2 μg) and pMyc-
β
-actin (2 μg). The pMyc-
β
-actin plasmid was constructed by our laboratory, and the related data has not been published yet. At 24 h posttransfection (hpt), the cells were treated with RIN (80 μM) for 3 h. Subsequently, the cells were lysed with NP-40 lysis buffer (200 μL) containing 1% PMSF at 4°C for 30 min. The cell lysates were then centrifuged at 12,000 ×
g
for 15 min at 4°C. The supernatants were collected and mixed with anti-Flag resin (20 μL), and incubated with rotation at 4°C for 6 h. After centrifugation at 500 ×
g
for 5 min at 4°C, the supernatants were discarded and the resin was then washed thrice with ice-cold PBS by rotating at 4°C for 2 h. Finally, the samples were mixed with 40 μL of PBS and 10 μL of 5 × SDS loading buffer, boiled for 10 min, and subjected to Western blotting analysis.
Laser confocal microscopy
To assess the effects of RIN on cell membrane permeability, PAMs were cultured in confocal dishes and co-treated with RIN (80 μM) and ASFV-WT (MOI = 1) for 3 h, followed by incubation with calcein-AM (catalog no. 40747ES76; Yeasen) at 37°C for 20 min. To evaluate the actin remodelling after
PFN1
knockdown, PAMs were transfected with siPFN1-2. At 24 hpt, actin-tracker red-594 dye (catalog no. C2205S; Beyotime) was added to the cells. After incubation at 37°C for 1 h, the cells were imaged using a laser-scanning confocal microscope (Zeiss, Germany).
Statistical analysis
The unpaired
t
test was determined for statistical significance by using SPSS 22.0 software. The experiment was independently repeated in triplicates.
P
< 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*,
P
< 0.05; **,
P
< 0.01; ***,
P
< 0.001; ****,
P
< 0.0001), while
P
≥ 0.05 was considered statistically non-significant (ns).

RIN exhibits broad-spectrum antiviral activities
Considering the field application, it is also very important to investigate the antiviral activities of RIN on different gene-deleted ASFV strains, including rASFV-ΔMGF, rASFV-ΔCD2v, and rASFV-ΔMGF/CD2v. As detailed above, PAMs were co-treated with RIN (80 μM) and different gene-deleted ASFV (MOI = 1) at 37°C in 5% CO
2
for 24 h. Following that, the supernatants and the cell lysates were collected to detect the viral genome copies and titers. The results indicated that RIN significantly decreased the viral genome copies and titers of rASFV-ΔMGF (Figure S1A and B), rASFV-ΔCD2v (Figure S1C and D), and rASFV-ΔMGF/CD2v (Figure S1E and F).
In addition, to evaluate the inhibitory effects of RIN on other viruses, we determined its effects on the viability of PK-15 cells. PK-15 cells were incubated with different concentrations of RIN for 48 h, and the cytotoxicity of RIN to PK-15 cells was then assessed by the CTG kit. As shown in Figure S1G, RIN of up to 160 μM exhibited no cytotoxicity to PK-15 cells. Subsequently, PK-15 cells were co-treated with RIN (5, 10, 20, 40, and 80 μM) and CSFV (MOI = 0.5) or fluorescent reporter PRV (MOI = 0.5) for 48 h followed by quantification of the viral genome copies and titers. The results revealed that RIN inhibited the replication of CSFV (Figure S1H and I) and PRV (Figure S1J and K) in a dose-dependent manner. Taken together, these results indicate that RIN exerts broad-spectrum antiviral effects.

The results showed that the viral genome copies (

RIN targets PFN1 to inhibit ASFV attachment to PAMs
CETSA is used to study drug targets, based on the principle of ligand-induced thermal stability of target proteins. The structure of drug-bound target proteins is more stable compared with that of non-drug-bound proteins, making them less prone to denaturation at the same temperature [
43
]. Firstly, using this assay, PAMs were treated with ASFV-WT (MOI = 1) or co-treated with RIN (80 μM) and ASFV-WT (MOI = 1) for 24 h. The cells were then collected for gradient heating, followed by silver stain to determine the increased abundance of protein bands because of the presence of RIN, compared with the control group at the same temperature (Figure S3). We further employed mass spectrometry analysis to screen for potential host molecules involved in cell attachment of ASFV. The results revealed that PFN1, an important actin-binding protein, was a candidate target, showing higher abundance in the RIN-treated cells compared with the control group (Table S3). Subsequently, to validate the results of the mass spectrometry analysis, the CETSA-Western blotting with the temperature range experiment was performed. The results showed that PFN1 was more stable in the RIN-treated samples, suggesting that PFN1 is a potential target of RIN (
Figure 3
A).

RIN decreases actin remodelling
To investigate whether RIN targeting PFN1 reduces the expression of PFN1, PAMs were treated with RIN (80 μM), ASFV-WT (MOI = 1), or the mixture of RIN (80 μM) and ASFV-WT (MOI = 1) for 24 h, respectively. The cells were then collected to determine the expression of PFN1 by RT-qPCR and Western blotting analysis. The results showed that RIN does not decrease the mRNA levels (
Figure 4
A) and the protein expression of PFN1 (
Figure 4

Given that PFN1 is an actin-binding protein, we further examined whether RIN inhibits the binding of PFN1 to actin, thereby affecting virus attachment to cells. Structural analysis and co-IP assay revealed that RIN inhibited the interaction between PFN1 and actin (
Figure 4

To assess the effects of PE on ASFV replication, we first determined the safe concentration of PE for PAMs. The results showed that PE exhibited no obvious cytotoxicity to PAMs at concentrations up to 20 μM (

Figure 6

Figure 6

Figure 6

PAMs were then co-treated with different concentrations of meclozine (2.5, 5, and 10 μM) and ASFV-WT (MOI = 1) for 24 h, the supernatants and the cell lysates were collected to quantify viral genome copies and titers. The results indicated that meclozine inhibited ASFV replication in a dose-dependent manner (

Discussion
RIN, as a PA, is predominantly distributed in plant species of the
Boraginaceae
and
Asteraceae
families [
45
,
46
]. It has been reported that PA exhibits potential toxicological effects, including hepatotoxicity, genotoxicity, and neurotoxicity, posing substantial health risks to livestock, wildlife, and humans [
47
,
48
]. However, certain plants containing PA have been traditionally used in herbal medicine for treating various diseases. For instance,
Heliotropium subulatum
has been employed against insect stings, throat infections, ulcers, and snakebites. Interestingly, PA exhibits antiviral properties. For example, the heliotrine isolated from
H. subulatum
demonstrates antiviral activities against poliovirus and vesicular stomatitis virus at a concentration of 10 µg/mL [
49
]. Currently, there have been no reports on the antiviral effects of RIN. Given the current lack of safe and effective commercial vaccines against ASFV, the development of anti-ASFV agents remains an urgent necessity for the prevention and control of ASF. Using our previously established high-throughput screening platform for natural small molecules with activities against ASFV, RIN was identified as a potent inhibitor of ASFV replication. Further studies confirmed that RIN not only suppresses the replication of ASFV-WT (
Figure 1
D–H) but also significantly inhibits the replication of different gene-deleted ASFV strains (Figure S1A–F). These findings suggest that RIN is a promising candidate molecule against ASFV.
With the advancement of studies on the pathogenic mechanisms of ASFV, numerous host cell proteins have been found to regulate various stages of the viral life cycle, including attachment, internalization, genome replication, virion assembly, and release. Antiviral strategies targeting host proteins offer certain advantages, such as high target conservation and a reduced risk of inducing viral resistance. The study first demonstrated that RIN does not directly inactivate ASFV
in vitro
(
Figure 2
A and B), indicating that it exerts antiviral effects not by targeting viral proteins but likely through host proteins. Subsequently, the time-of-addition assay demonstrated that RIN pretreatment up to 6 h before ASFV infection effectively inhibits ASFV replication (
Figure 2
E), suggesting its potential use as a feed additive for the prevention and control of ASF, highlighting its potential clinical application for ASF prevention. Further
in vivo
experiments will be conducted to evaluate the anti-ASFV effects of RIN.
PFN1 is an important actin-binding protein that primarily participates in the dynamic regulation of the cytoskeleton. It has been shown that PFN1 can influence viral replication through interactions with actin, signalling molecules, and viral proteins. For example, PFN1 affects HIV-1 entry and release by regulating actin polymerization and depolymerization [
26
]. Based on the principle of CETSA, when the target protein was bound to a small molecule, the structure of protein becomes more stable, making it less prone to denaturation at higher temperatures. Therefore, in this study, CETSA combined with mass spectrometry was used to identify PFN1 as the target protein associated with RIN’s inhibition of ASFV attachment to PAMs. RNA interference and overexpression experiments revealed that
PFN1
knockdown significantly suppressed ASFV replication, whereas PFN1 overexpression markedly enhanced the expression level of the viral protein (p30). Notably, although RNA interference experiment indicated that PFN1 regulates ASFV replication, the statistical significance of viral genome copies (
Figure 3
B) was substantially higher than that of viral titers (
Figure 3
C), suggesting that
PFN1
knockdown may decrease ASFV virion release. These results highlighted RIN’s potential to exert multimodal antiviral effects with broad-spectrum activity and high potency.
Given that the critical role of metabolic regulation in drug efficacy and safety [
50
], we explored whether the anti-ASFV activity of RIN affects metabolism pathways associated with virus attachment to cells. Using untargeted metabolomic profiling and LC-MS/MS analysis, we discovered that RIN inhibits ASFV replication by closely interacting with the glycerophospholipid metabolism pathway and significantly reduces the PE level. Glycerophospholipids, including a glycerol backbone, a functional head group, and two fatty acids of varying lengths and degrees of unsaturation, are major components of cell membranes [
51
]. PE, one of the glycerophospholipids, is involved in various cellular processes, such as membrane fusion, cell cycle regulation, autophagy, and apoptosis [
52
]. Previous studies have reported that PE biosynthesis, mediated by phosphate cytidylyltransferase 2, ethanolamine (Pcyt2), is essential for the efficient assembly and replication of herpes simplex virus type 1 [
44
]. To confirm the impacts of PE on ASFV replication, we investigated the antiviral effects of exogenous PE addition and inhibition of PE biosynthesis. The results showed that PE promotes ASFV replication (
Figure 6
A–F), while PE decreased the anti-ASFV activities of RIN (
Figure 6
G). However, the precise detailed mechanism by which RIN suppresses ASFV replication
via
reducing PE biosynthesis requires further investigation.
In conclusion, we have revealed for the first time that RIN inhibits the replication of ASFV by targeting the host PFN1 protein, while PE, an important glycerophospholipid, contributes to the RIN’s anti-ASFV activities, highlighting the essential role of interactions between the virus and host cell membranes in viral infection. These findings provide novel targets, strategies, and theoretical support for the prevention and control of ASF.
