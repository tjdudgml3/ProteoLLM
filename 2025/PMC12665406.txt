Peripartum cardiomyopathy eripartum cardiomyopathy (PPCM) is a type of heart disease that leads to heart failure and occurs during the last month of pregnancy or up to 5 months postpartum. It is characterized by a weakening of the woman's heart muscle, especially in the left ventricle, which leads to a reduced ejection fraction and impaired ability of the heart to pump blood throughout the body effectively. It is a rare but serious pathophysiological condition with unknown cause, in which symptoms can mimic normal pregnancy, that makes them challenging for diagnosis.

It is important to note here that up-regulation is of genes associated with oxidative phosphorylation, fatty acid metabolism, and DNA damage repair—pathways presumed to enhance cardiomyocyte performance and survival. Concurrently, there is a down-regulation of genes involved in ERS, apoptosis, fibrosis, and inflammation—processes typically implicated in the progression of heart failure. Supportively, heart deficient in UFL1 failed to initiate this adaptive transcriptional program, resulting in cardiac dysfunction. These findings indicate the critical role of ufmylation in orchestrating gene networks essential for cardiac compensation during cardiac remodeling.

Lastly, this study by Tandra et al
5
establishes a link of PPCM to dysregulated mTOR signaling in the heart. In mice, the lack of ZFP36L2, a mRNA-destabilizing protein, led to mTOR activation and the development of PPCM. Importantly, treatment with rapamycin, an mTOR inhibitor, significantly improved postpartum cardiac function, highlighting mTOR hyperactivation as a maladaptive mechanism contributing to PPCM. This dysregulation suggests that the loss of UFL1 plays a critical role in promoting excessive mTOR activity, which may contribute significantly to the development of pregnancy-induced cardiac dysfunction. These findings define the importance of UFL1 as a regulatory factor in maintaining cardiac homeostasis during the postpartum period and highlight mTOR signaling as a potential therapeutic target for preventing or mitigating pregnancy-related heart conditions.
Together, it appears that women harboring genetic mutations on UFL1 exhibit an increased susceptibility to PPCM, highlighting the potential value of incorporating genetic screening and proactive cardiovascular monitoring into clinical management strategies for at-risk populations.

Limitations and Future Directions
As described by Tandra et al
5
in their study, there are several limitations that should be acknowledged and warrant further investigation. First, although the findings suggest a role for ufmylation in modulating PPCM, the direct connection between dysregulated ufmylation and PPCM in human patients remains unproven. This could be addressed by analyzing ufmylation-related protein expression and signaling pathways in ventricular tissue samples from individuals diagnosed with PPCM. Second, future study for detailed bioenergetic profiling and mitochondrial respiration assays would be needed to fully elucidate the role of ufmylation in regulating mitochondrial integrity and metabolic flexibility in the postpartum hearts. Lastly, systematic identification and validation of UFM1 substrates in cardiomyocytes, possibly through mass spectrometry-based proteomics and functional assays, could help clarify the biological role of ufmylation and its impact on cardiac physiology.
Conclusions
Ufmylation plays a vital role in facilitating physiological cardiac remodeling during the peripartum period. This process is proposed mechanistically through the suppression of ERS and maladaptive mTOR signaling, while simultaneously enhancing mitochondrial function. Through regulation of these key pathways, ufmylation contributes to the preservation of cardiac homeostasis and supports adaptive myocardial growth during this crucial stage. As such, modulating ufmylation may serve as a novel therapeutic approach for the prevention of PPCM.
