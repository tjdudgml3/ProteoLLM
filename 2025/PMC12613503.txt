Background
The immune system is a complex biological network of hierarchically organized genes, proteins and cellular components that protect the body from pathogens and foreign substances and monitor the onset of internal disease [
1
]. During aging, the immune system undergoes remarkable changes known as immunosenescence, which is a multifactorial and dynamic phenomenon involving the destruction and remodeling of the structure of the immune organs and the consequent dysfunction of both innate and adaptive immunity [
2
,
3
]. It is known to be an important contributing factor to the poor health status of older individuals, including increased susceptibility to infections, chronic inflammatory disease, cancer, and poor vaccination responses [
2
,
4
–
6
]. Recently, the theory of inflammaging has suggested that immunosenescence may be one of the drivers of ageing of solid organs and plays a crucial role in the ageing process, which is strongly associated with adverse immune-related events and outcomes in the elderly [
7
]. Thus, restoring the senescence of immune system represents a potential therapeutic strategy for age-related diseases such as cancer.
Natural killer (NK) cells, as innate lymphocytes and the first line of immune defence, play an indispensable role in infection control of viruses and especially in cancer immunosurveillance [
8
,
9
]. Notably, unlike adaptive immune cells such as T and B lymphocytes, NK cells operate without prior sensitization or antigen presentation, a unique property that allows them to play early roles in immune recognition and clearance of tumor cells [
10
]. In humans, NK cells can be divided into two main subsets, the majority (approximately 90%) of circulating NK cells are CD56
dim
CD16
+
subset, with the remaining 10% primarily consisting of CD56
bright
CD16
neg/dim
NK subset [
11
]. After activation, the CD56
bright
NK cells, which are considered as immature precursors, eliminate target cells through expression and release of death ligands (e.g., TNF-α, FasL and TRAIL) upon stimulating by killer activating receptors (KARs) [
12
]. Additionally, the more mature CD56
dim
subset rapidly lyse malignant cells via releasing cytotoxic granules containing perforin and granzymes [
8
,
13
]. Previous studies have revealed that the characteristics of the NK cell population are impacted by the process of aging, with NK cells skewing towards a mature CD16
+
CD56
dim
state, which in turn leads to a slight increase in the frequency of NK cells in the elderly [
14
]. Furthermore, the proliferative activity of circulating NK cells is greatly reduced in the healthy elderly, accompanied by a decrease in cytokine secretion and cytotoxicity compared to younger controls [
15
–
19
]. Similar to population studies, a significant reduction in functionally mature NK cells in the peripheral tissues of aged mice [
20
]. Of note, growing evidence suggests that NK cell function is progressively impaired as humans age, and is strongly associated with an increased risk of developing cancer [
18
,
21
]. Therefore, we predicted that reversing the senescence of NK cells could help promote their antitumor activity and improve the prognosis of elderly cancer patients.
In previous studies, embryo stem cells (ESCs) attenuate D-gal induced CD4
+
T cell senescence by targeting nuclear factor erythroid-2-related factor 2 (Nrf2)-mediated antioxidant defenses [
22
]. Systematic administration of ESC derived small extracellular vesicles (ESC-sEVs) can rejuvenate senescence of bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BM-MSCs) and prevent age-related bone loss [
23
]. Induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) are pluripotent stem cells with unlimited self-renewal and differentiation capabilities similar to ESCs [
24
]. Recently, we found that, ESC-sEVs delayed NSC senescence and improved cognitive impairment during natural aging [
25
] as well as in a rat model of vascular dementia (VD) [
26
], and also iPSC-sEVs attenuated the senescence of microglia, the key innate immune cells in the brain, and protected aged mice from ischemic stroke [
27
]. All these factors suggested sEVs from stem cell may be a promising alternative in age-related diseases including immunosenescence.
However, the protection effect and mechanism of on aging-related NK cell dysfunction remain unknown. Therefore, in the current study, we aim to explore whether sEVs from iPSCs could attenuate aging-related NK cell dysfunction, thereby clinically alleviating immunosenescence. We found that the natural aging C57 mice exhibited immunosenescence, as well as a time-related increasing in NK cells dysfunction and phenotypic subtype cluster changes. Treatment with iPSC-sEVs via the tail vein significantly alleviated immunosenescence, recovered NK cell mediated cytotoxicity (NKCC), and strengthened its ability to recruit other immune cells in the murine co-inoculation models, ultimately enhancing the anti-tumor immune response in vivo. Moreover, we constructed an aging model of NK92 cells to explore the mechanism through which iPSC-sEVs retarded NK cell senescence. Transcriptional analysis and gene co‐expression network analysis unveiled that iPSC‐sEVs rejuvenate NK senescence by inhibiting CISH-STAT3 signaling activation. Overexpression of CISH evidently accelerated the NK92 cellular senescence. Mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis identified 4019 proteins encapsulated in iPSC-sEVs. Bioinformatics analysis predicted that the protein components in the iPSC-sEVs function in a synergistic way to induce the activation of several canonical signaling pathways in NK92 cells, which results in the downregulation of CISH in NK cells and then the recovery of senescent NK cells’ function. Collectively, our findings reveal the effect of iPSC-sEVs in reversing immunosenescence and age-related NK cells dysfunction, thereby enhanced anti-tumor ability in the elderly, clarified the mechanism by which iPSC-sEVs retard the senescence of NK cells. iPSC-sEVs might be promising therapeutic candidate drugs for treating age-related diseases.
Materials and methods
Animal experimental procedures
8-month-old male wild type C57 mice were purchased from Shanghai B&K Laboratory Animal Co., LTD (China). All mice were housed under pathogen-free conditions and provided a standard diet and water. After a four-month acclimatization period, C57 mice (
n
= 20) in a stable condition were selected for the study. These C57 mice were randomly grouped using the Excel Rand function. Mice were divided into cages as five or four per cage. 12-month-old male C57 mice were treated with 100 µl vehicle control (PBS,
n
= 8 mice) or iPSC-sEVs (1 × 10
10
/ml particles, 100 µl per mouse, dissolved in PBS,
n
= 10 mice) by tail vein injection once weekly. In the natural aged model, mice that died naturally before reaching the experimental time point were not included in the group. The work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.
Co-inoculation models establishment
After 6 months of treatment, all mice were sacrificed to isolate purified splenic NK cells. These NK cells were then co-inoculated with B16 melanoma cells at a 5:1ratio (6 × 10⁶ cells/mice) subcutaneously in syngeneic C57 mice, establishing an in vivo co-inoculation model. In situ length and width of the tumor were measured using a caliper, and the tumor volume was calculated using the equation V = 0.5×(length×width
2
). At day13, the tumor mass was surgically harvested from B16 tumor-bearing mice under anesthesia for following histological evaluation and flow cytometry assay. Mice with complete tumor regression were represented as mice whose tumors could not be detected by manual operation techniques. The mice were euthanized if their tumor diameter exceeded 1.5 cm. At the experimental endpoint, intraperitoneal anesthesia was administered using a 2% working solution of Sodium pentobarbital (Sigma-Aldrich) at a dose of 200 mg/kg. After the entered unconsciousness, euthanasia was performed by exsanguination via the abdominal aorta. Blinding was not feasible during treatment; however, results were analyzed in a blinded manner whenever possible.
Histological evaluation and immunohistochemistry
Spleens and tumors were fixed with 4% PFA for 24 h and then dehydrated with a graded-ethanol series. The dehydrated samples were embedded in paraffin and then sectioned at a 5 μm thickness. For the immunohistochemistry analysis, tendon sections in each group were deparaffinized in xylene, hydrated through ethanol series and then perform antigen retrieval by using EDTA buffer. Subsequently, all the sections were blocked with 5% BSA for 30 min and incubated with primary antibodies against NCR1 (1:100,13395-1-AP, Cell Signaling Technology), CD206 (1:200, ab64693, Abcam), cleaved caspase3(1:200, ab32042, Abcam). Next, each section was incubated with the HRP-labeled secondary antibodies and DAB (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) was used as HRP-specific substrate. Images were captured using Leica DM6B (Leica Microsystems, Milan, Italy) and further processed with ImageJ software. The positive rate (%) of markers mentioned above in each group was calculated.

4

Splenocytes were stained with CD3-FITC and CD19-APC, B220-FITC, F4/80-PE-Cy7 and CD11b-PE for 30 min on ice to sort T and B cells and macrophages. The remained NK Cells (5 × 10
4
) were poured into FBS using a BD Aria III flow cytometer. Sorted cells were washed in 1× DPBS. Total RNA was isolated from cells using RNeasy kit and analysed for the expression of senescence and SASP markers as described above.
The uptake of iPSC-sEVs by spleen NK cells in vivo
We obtained iPSC-sEVs using ultracentrifugation then nano-flow cytometry was used to determine the number of EVs as 2 × 10
10

9

After 24 h, the mice were anesthetized, and transcardially perfused with ice-cold 0.9% saline to eliminate any remaining blood-derived sEVs in tissues. Spleens were excised, and frozen sections were analyzed using fluorescence confocal microscopy to visualize the fluorescent signals.

The isolated splenocytes were exposed to hypotonic treatment, washed, and suspended in RPMI-1640 medium. To deplete the adherent cells, the suspension was incubated for 1 h on nylon-wool columns at 37 °C in humidified 5% CO

RNA extraction and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)
For RNA-seq, RNAs were prepared using RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, 74106), and DNA contaminations were removed with RNase-Free DNase (Qiagen, 79254). cDNA library was constructed using TruSeq RNA Sample Prep Kit and then sequenced with HiSeq 2000 system (Illumina Inc.). The expression of transcripts was quantified as reads per kilobase of exon model per million mapped reads (RPKM). The change in splicing isoforms was analyzed using MISO software with the annotation of all known alternative splicing events, and we filtered the results based on the ‘percent spliced in’ values (selected by Bayes factor > = 2 and diff > = 0.2 or diff< = -0.2). We conducted Differential Expression Analysis (DEG) on the readable data, and used the KEGG and GSEA databases to perform cluster analysis on the DEGs.
Mass spectrometry (MS) data acquisition
The mass spectrometry proteomics of iPSC-sEVs was performed by the Shanghai Applied Protein Technology Company (Shanghai, China) as reported in our previous study [

Generation, identification of clonal CISH over expression of NK92
The lentivirus vector LV-CISH with GFP-tag (GeneChem) was designed to overexpress the level of CISH stably in NK92 cells, and LV-control was the control lentivirus. 24 hours after transfection, cells were selected with puromycin (2.5 ug/ml) (Sigma P8833) for 3 days. Then cells were seeded to 96-well plate at concentration of 1 cell/ml (100 µl per well) for single clone selection. Under fluorescence microscopy, cell clusters labeled with GFP were observed, indicating successful transfection. Further, we confirmed the overexpression of CISH by qPCR and Western Blots. The primer sets: 5’- CCTCGTCCTTCCAAGCTGTT-3’, 5’- CCCAGTACCACCCCCTGTA-3’.
Natural killer cell cytotoxicity assay
Cells were washed twice and then resuspended in 500 µl of resuspension buffer and applied to MACS column attached to a magnet. Column elution fraction was collected and then centrifuged and resuspended and incubated with 50 µl anti-NK1.1–biotin conjugate on ice for 30 min. Cells were washed with three column volumes of buffer before elution. Cells were then counted to determine ‘effector’ natural killer cell numbers for use in cytotoxicity assays. Approximately 1 × 10
6
K562 ‘target’ cells were incubated in 2 ml of complete medium with 20 µl of 3 mM CSFE stain for 1 h at 37 °C. Cells were then washed twice in 1× DPBS to washout the remaining DiOC18 stain and resuspend in cells in complete medium at a density of 1 × 10
6
per ml. Suspensions of effector cells were prepared in complete medium to yield effector: target ratios of 5:1, 1:1 and 0:1 by mixing 130 µl of effector cells with 10 µl of target cells. Cells were washed in 1× DPBS three times and lysis was measured by flow cytometry.
Flow cytometry analysis
For the flow cytometry analysis of Mouse NK cells, single cells were collected and re-suspended into suspension. After blocking non-specific antigens as mentioned above, sp-NK cells were incubated with antibody CD3-FITC (1:100; eBioscience), CD4-PE (1:100; eBioscience), CD8-APC (1:100; eBioscience) and NK1.1-PE (1:100; eBioscience) at 4 °C for 30 min. The stained cells were resuspended and analyzed using CytoFLEX flow cytometer as described above. Data were processed by FlowJo Software (TreeStar, Ashland, OR).
Western blot analysis
For the identification of sEVs using Western blot analysis, three positive markers including CD9, CD63, and TSG101, as well as one negative marker GM130, were detected. The protein samples of sEVs were collected using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, Jiangsu, China). The Western blot analysis was performed as previously described. Briefly, protein extracts were resolved by SDS-PAGE gels (10 µg protein/lane) and probed with the indicated antibodies. The following antibodies were used for Western blot analysis: CD63 (1:1000; ab134045, Abcam), CD9 (1: 2000; ab92726, Abcam), TSG101 (1: 1000; sc-7964, Santa cruz), GM130 (1: 1000; ab52649, Abcam). Anti-rabbit IgG or anti-mouse IgG, horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-linked antibody (1:2000; Cell Signaling Technology) was used as secondary antibody and chemiluminescent signals were visualized by the ECL Western Blot detection kit and Bio-RAD imaging system (Bio-RAD, CA, USA).
For the Western analysis of NK92 cells in the in vitro experiment, cells were seeded on a 6-well plate followed by iPSC-sEVs treatment with or without D-gal. Control cells were cultured with the same time as other groups, but without any treatment. After that, the protein samples of NK92 cells were collected using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, Jiangsu, China) and quantified using Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Cat#23225, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Total amount of 10-µg protein of cell lysates were run on SDS-PAGE gels and probed with following primary antibodies: Anti-γH
2
A.X (1:1000; 9718, Cell Signaling Technology), Anti-p53(1:1000; 4511 T, Cell Signaling Technology), Anti-GAPDH(1:1000; AF7021, Affinity), Anti-Vinculin(1:1000; ab129002, Abcam), Anti-CISH(1:1000; 8731, Cell Signaling Technology), HRP-linked antibody (1:2000; Cell Signaling Technology) was used as secondary antibody and the immunoreactive bands were visualized by the ECL Western Blot detection kit and Bio-RAD imaging system.
Quantitative RT-PCR
Cells were resuspended and stored in RNA protect cell reagent (Qiagen). RNA was extracted with the RNeasy mini kit (Qiagen) and contaminating DNA removed by on-column DNase digestion, with the RNase-Free DNase Set (Qiagen). cDNA was synthesized using random primers (Sango) and the Superscript III reverse transcriptase kit (Invitrogen). qPCR amplification was performed with either Taqman or SYBR Green reagents. For both, 10ng cDNA was used together with assay-specific primers (Supplementary Table 2), and either Power Up™ SYBR™ Green Master Mix (for SYBR Green method) or Taqman gene expression Mastermix (both Applied Biosystems). Amplification was carried out in a 7500 real-time PCR machine or a QuantStudio 5 machine (Applied Biosystems). Reactions were performed in triplicate, apart from the qPCR of cell sorted NK cell subsets where there was limited starting material and reactions were performed in duplicate. All technical replicate values were within 0.5 cycles. Fold change gene expression was calculated by the ΔΔCt method.
Statistical analyses
Data are expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean value (SEM) from at least three different experiments. T-tests and one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were performed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, California, USA) to evaluate the significance of the results. P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.
Results
iPSC-sEVs ameliorated the senescence of spleen NK cells from aged mouse and enhanced NK cells cytotoxicity in vitro
We observed the effect of iPSC-sEVs intervention on the general aging process (The properties of iPSC-sEVs are detailed in Supplementary Fig. 1). The Old-PBS group showed worse hair condition and higher body weight compared to the young group, while the iPSC-sEVs group had better hair condition and lower body weight closer to the young group (Fig.
1

The uptake experiment of sEVs in vivo demonstrated that spleen NK cells could take up iPSC-sEVs (Supplementary Fig. 2). To detect immunosenecence during natural aging process, we first inspected the spleens of young and aged mice. The immunofluorescence staining results showed that γH2AX and p21 were highly expressed in the spleens of aged mice whereas it was barely detectable in young mice. In contrast, iPSC-sEVs intervention resulted in reduced expression levels of these markers (Fig.
1
C). In the spleen of the Old-PBS group, the activity of β-gal was higher than that of the Young group (Fig.
1
D). Further, we utilized p21 and CD122c (mature NK cells’ marker) co-staining to ascertain that the spleen NK cells presented an aging phenotype. Absolute counts of hematology analyzer indicated that iPSC-sEVs intervention does not alter the counts of each blood cell subgroup (Supplementary Table 1). In the spleen of the Old group (Old-PBS), the double-positive cells were increased as compared with the Young group (Fig.
1
E). Next, we purified the NK cells with magnetic beads from single-cell suspension of the spleen. After purification, the proportion of NK cells ranged from 86% to 91%. (Fig.
1

The expression levels of these indicators in the spleen NK cells of aged mice were elevated compared to the Young group. The expression of IL6 the Old group was lower than that in the Young group (Fig.

To examine the functional alterations of NK cells during aging, we initially used flow cytometry to detect the receptors associated with NK cells function, such as KLRG1 and NKG2C. Compared with the Young group, the expression of KLRG1 and NKG2C in the purified NK cells of the Old-PBS group was increased (Fig.

The remaining YAC cells in the Old group was more than that of Young group, indicating a reduced cytotoxic ability of NK cells derived from old mice in comparison with Young group NK cells (Fig.

The expression levels of p21 and TNF-α in NK cells of spleen of mice in the iPSC-sEVs group were lower than those in the old group. The flow cytometry analysis of purified NK cells indicated that the expressions of KLRG1 and NKG2C declined following the intervention of iPSC sEVs compared to Old NK cells. (Fig.

1

iPSC-sEVs ameliorate D-gal mediated NK92 senescence and enhance anti-tumor activity
To further affirm the anti-aging function of iPSC-sEVs on the NK cells, D-gal(D-galactose) was cocultured with NK92 cells (a human NK cell line) to generate chemical-induced cell senescence in vitro as previously reported [

The experiments on the cytotoxicity of NK92 cells against K562 cells (Human lymphoma cells) under different treatments indicated that the cytotoxicity of NK cells induced to senescence by D-gal was weakened compared with the control group and the intervention of iPSC-sEVs could antagonize the senescence induced by D-gal and enhance the cytotoxicity of NK cells (Fig.

The expression of CISH significantly increased in NK cells isolated from Old mice, while returned to the extent comparable to Young group in cells from Old- iPSC-sEVs group (Fig.

H), indicating that iPSC-sEVs could repress the expression of CISH in vivo.
iPSC-sEVs proteins synergistically modulate the expression of CISH-STAT3 pathway in D-gal induced NK92 aging model
Existing studies have indicated that stem cell EVs function by delivering their content proteins. To ascertain the specific regulatory mechanism of iPSC sEVs to regulate CISH expression, we analyzed the proteins in iPSC-sEVs, which have been identified in our previous study [
28
]. KEGG analysis showed that these proteins were involved in multiple biological pathways, including metabolic, RNA degradation, and longevity regulation pathways (Fig.
6
A). Furthermore, we performed a GO enrichment analysis on the contents of exosomes and identified that they are highly enriched in RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) and proteasome components (Fig.
6
B). RNA-binding proteins play a crucial role at the level of gene expression by interacting with RNA molecules to regulate their fate and function; including splicing regulation, stability and degradation regulation, etc. Given that iPSC-sEVs treatment could reduce the mRNA level of CISH, we predicted the RNA binding protein for CISH by RBPsuite. Among the 223 potential RBPs, 149 kinds were also included in iPSC-sEVs (Fig.
6
C). We conducted KEGG clustering analysis on these 149 proteins and found that the protein clusters were associated with the pathways of spliceosome, mRNA surveillance and RNA degradation (Fig.
6
D). Figure
6
E shows the relative level of top 19 RBPs for CISH in the mass spectrometry results. The binding sites distribution of 8 RBPs with a relatively high binding potential were shown in Fig.
6
F. Consistent with this finding, a previous study reported that the mRNA and protein expression levels of CISH in the initial T cells with Mettl3 defect were increased [
32
]. In conclusion, iPSC-sEVs mitigate NK cells senescence might through transferring those RBPs to decrease the expression of CISH, thereby enhancing their cytotoxic capabilities.
Discussion
Aging of the immune system, also known as immunosenescence, is a natural process that occurs with ageing and leads to a progressive decline in immune function, which affects all aspects of the immune functional network, including NK cells [
18
]. Age-related impairment of NK cell function, termed NK cell immunosenescence, is associated with an increased susceptibility to viral infections and cancer [
4
,
15
,
33
]. Multiple lines of evidence suggest that NK cells are critical for anti-tumor immune responses [
9
,
34
]. Mice deficient in NK cells exhibited impaired rejection of various target tumor cells, including B16 melanoma [
35
,
36
]. NK cell-based immunotherapies have shown great promise as a new anti-cancer strategy in several clinical studies [
8
,
37
].
However, numerous studies have demonstrated that ageing is associated with substantial alterations in the frequency, phenotype, and distribution of NK cell subsets, as well as impaired NK cell immunosurveillance against cancer cells due to the reduced expression of activating receptors [
10
,
14
,
19
–
21
]. Cancer is largely a disease of the elderly, meaning that for older patients [
38
], NK cell-mediated cancer immunotherapies must develop strategies to overcome the limitations of senescent NK cell function and adapt to the aged microenvironment [
15
,
39
,
40
]. In this study, we reported for the first time that iPSC-sEVs effectively ameliorated senescence of NK cells both in vitro and in vivo, thereby alleviating their age-related functional decline. Mechanism studies revealed that iPSC-sEVs could elevate phosphorylation of STAT3 via inhibiting the expression of CISH, which were involved in regulating the senescence of NK cell. Briefly, iPSC-sEVs may be a promising therapy for mitigating NK cell senescence and thereby improving the efficacy of NK cell-based cancer immunotherapy in the elderly population.
Studies have shown that senescent NK cells display marked reductions in two key effector functions: global cytotoxicity as well as immunoregulatory cytokine and chemokine secretion. Based on the detection of general hallmarks of cellular senescence, we found that the administration of D-gal induced a senescent phenotype in NK cells, recapitulating the phenotype observed in NK cells isolated from aged mice (18 months). Additionally, we have characterized the hallmarks of NK cell senescence, with a particular focus on the impairment of cytotoxic function. Given the combination of epidemiological findings and laboratory data suggesting that changes in the biology of senescent NK cells are strongly associated with an increased risk of cancer in the elderly. This may be attributable to age-associated impairment of NKCC, in particular a decline in the ability to recognize and kill multiple cancer cells. Hence, recent studies have focuses on how the relative balance between activating receptor and inhibitory receptor signals required for NKCC shifts with advancing age. Nevertheless, there is a degree of heterogeneity among studies regarding their findings. Collectively, the ability of senescent NK to receive pro-activation signals from target cells, such as cancer cells, is altered, showing an overall shift towards a more inhibitory phenotype. Consistent with previous reports [
41
], we observed an upregulation of the inhibitory receptor KLRG1 on senescent NK cells. In contrast, another research team reported a decreased level of KLRG1 on the both CD3
−
CD56
dim
and CD3
−
CD56
bright
NK cell subsets in individuals aged over 60 years [
42
]. The authors hypothesized that the age-related loss of KLRG1 would disrupt the inhibitory control exerted by KLRG1-positive NK cell subsets, thereby potentially expanding the pool of NK cells with enhanced effector functions [
42
].
CD94/NKG2C plays a crucial role in NK-cell-mediated immune responses, particularly in antiviral and antitumor immunity. As aging occurs, the majority of adaptive NK cells express the lectin-like heterodimeric receptor NKG2C. This expression is correlated with the surface marker CD57 of NK cells and is rich in self-inhibitory KIRs and activating KIRs [
43
]. Although the exact mechanisms of receptors such as KLRG1 and NKG2C in regulating NK senescence have not been fully elucidated, interventions targeting age-related decline in NKCC would hold promise for enhancing outcomes in elderly cancer patients.
Over the past twenty years, the field of iPSC-based cell therapy has undergone significant advancements is now being explored for its potential to advance regenerative medicine [
44
,
45
]. Recent studies indicate that stem cells and stem cells derived extracellular vesicles (sEVs) possess anti-aging properties by rejuvenating senescent cells, tissues, and organs, thus mitigating the development of age-related diseases [
25
,
27
,
46
–
48
].
In our study, we demonstrated that iPSC-sEVs attenuated NK cell senescence both in vivo and in vitro, as evidenced by reduced expression of senescence-associated proteins and SA-β-Gal activity. This is consistent with our previous findings that iPSC-sEVs delays the senescence of microglia, a major type of innate immune cell in the brain [
27
]. We further validated that it played an important role in ameliorating the senescence of another type of innate immune cell, the NK cells. Additionally, we assessed the efficacy of iPSC-sEVs treated NK cells in an in vivo B16 melanoma model. Our findings revealed that these NK cells effectively inhibited tumor growth and promoted apoptosis in B16 cells. Furthermore, we observed a marked enhancement in the recruitment of other immune cells to the cancer site. These findings suggest that the adoptive transfer of rejuvenated NK cells may not only directly suppress tumor growth but also contribute to a more effective anti-tumor immune response by modulating the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME).
As for the exploration of the mechanism, bioinformatics analysis of RNA-seq data before and after sEVs treatment identified potential genes associated with delayed NK cell senescence. KEGG pathway analysis revealed that differentially expressed genes were significantly enriched in the top 3 pathways, including the TNF signaling pathway, T-cell receptor signaling pathway, and JAK-STAT signaling pathway ranking among the top three. Additionally, GSEA analysis indicated that inflammation-related pathways activated by LPS were prominently clustered. Among the signalling pathways enriched by these differential genes, we focused on the NK killing function and spotlighted a potentially critical gene, CISH. CIS, encoded by the CISH gene, is a member of the cytokine signaling inhibitor family [
49
]. It functions by negatively regulating cytokine signaling pathways, often through mechanisms such as inhibiting JAK-STAT activation [
50
,
51
]. In our experiments, we found that CISH was increased in D-gal induced senescent NK92 cells and NK cells of aged mice, indicating CISH may be involved in the regulation of NK cells senescence.
In fact, previous studies demonstrated that CISH is a key regulator of human NK cell survival and anti-tumor activity [
50
–
52
]. Briefly, CISH-deficient iPSC-derived NK cells, when adoptively transferred into xenograft models of acute myeloid leukemia (AML), exhibited enhanced survival and anti-AML activity [
50
]. Furthermore, NK cells from Cish
−/−
mice exhibited heightened sensitivity to IL-15 signaling, resulting in enhanced tumor control and resistance to lung metastasis in a variety of experimental tumor models (e.g., melanoma, prostate and breast) [
51
]. Similarly, another study reported that targeting CISH enhanced natural cytotoxicity receptor signaling and reduced NK cell exhaustion, thereby improving solid tumor immunity [
52
]. Together, these observations strongly suggest that therapeutic inhibition of CISH could enhance NK cell immunotherapy, which is associated with enhanced JAK-STAT signaling and improved metabolic adaptability mediated by the mTOR pathway [
50
–
52
]. Previous literature reports indicated that in NK cells, STAT3 not only affects their development and maturation, but also regulates their activation, cytotoxicity, and their interactions with other immune cells [
53
,
54
].
However, to date, there have been no reports regarding whether CISH is involved in regulating NK cell senescence and subsequently affecting NK cell functions. Interestingly, previous studies have reported that CISH enhances oxidative stress by inhibiting p-STAT3, thereby promoting age-related intestinal inflammation in mice [
55
]. Moreover, it has been previously shown that elevated CISH expression contributes to inflammatory senescence of T cells in older individuals through the impairment of lysosomal function [
56
]. Thus, in this study, we investigated whether CISH, a potential target gene of iPSC-sEVs, regulates ageing-related NK cell immunosenescence via the inhibition of STAT3. Firstly, overexpression of CISH in NK92 cells induced senescent phenotypes, including an increase of p21 and the expression of γH2AX, and the reduction of proliferation and killing properties, and signaling inhibition of NK activating receptors. SOCS proteins, which includes CISH, could suppress signaling at the level of the receptor-JAK complex [
57
]. CISH specifically binds to phosphorylated STAT3 through its SH2 domain [
58
], thereby preventing the dimerization and DNA binding of STAT3 and inhibiting its transcriptional activity [
59
]. This mechanism enables CISH to specifically inhibit the phosphorylation of STAT3, thereby affecting its function within the cell [
60
]. There was also other pathway reported, such as CISH forms a stable complex with the IL-3 receptor and the tyrosine-phosphorylated EPO receptor, preventing STAT from binding to the receptor [
61
].In our study, CISH inhibited the phosphorylation of STAT3, thereby promoting the senescence phenotype of NK cells and cytotoxicity.
In addition, the application of iPSC-sEVs reversed the upregulation of CISH in senescent NK cells. STAT inhibitors, STAT-in-12, could induce a model of NK cell senescence similar to that mediated by D-gal, and this can be reversed by iPSC-sEVs. Taken together, these data suggest that the therapeutic effect of iPSC-sEVs in restoring NK cell senescence is partially attributed to the downregulation of CISH, leading to increased p-STAT3 levels and consequently improved cytotoxicity of NK cells in the B16 melanoma model.
Previous studies indicated that, in a lymphopaenic mouse adoptive transfer model, naive Mettl3-deficient T cells failed to undergo homeostatic expansion and remained in the naive state for up to 12 weeks, thereby preventing colitis. Consistent with these observations, the mRNAs of SOCS family genes encoding the STAT signalling inhibitory proteins SOCS1, SOCS3 and CISH were marked by m6A, exhibited slower mRNA decay and showed increased mRNAs and levels of protein expression in Mettl3-deficient naive T cells [
32
]. CISH can bind to a phosphorylated site to prevent downstream phosphorylation or outcompete the STATs directly preventing them from entering the nucleus and inducing gene expression. Once bound to the phosphorylated receptor, the remaining components of the Cullin5 E3 ligase are recruited via their SOCS-box and subsequent ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of the receptor occurs [
62
]. The mass spectrometry results suggested that iPSC-sEVs contain a large number of RNA-binding proteins and proteasomes, which may be the major factors for iPSC-sEVs to regulate the expression of CISH.
However, our study has some limitations. We cannot exclude the possibility that the anti-tumor effects observed were partially due to the immune cells recruited by NK cells within the co-inoculation tumor, including autologous NK cells from the recipient mice. Nevertheless, these findings indirectly suggest that the NK cell function in aged mice can be effectively restored by iPSC-sEVs intervention.
Conclusion
In summary, our data demonstrated that age-dependent upregulation of CISH in NK cells can induce senescence of NK cells, impairing their proliferation and cytotoxicity, which may contribute to the impaired anti-tumor function of NK cells. iPSC-sEVs exhibited a positive effect on rejuvenating senescence of NK cells partly by suppressing CISH expression and subsequently promoting STAT3 phosphorylation. As a cell-free therapeutic tool, iPSC-sEVs offer a novel therapeutic strategy for treating cancer in the elderly and other immunosenescence-mediated diseases.
