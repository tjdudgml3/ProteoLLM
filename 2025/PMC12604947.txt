Papillomas are benign tumors often associated with papillomavirus infection or exposure to ultraviolet rays. They are characterized by the outward, papillary proliferation of squamous
epithelial cells on mucosal surfaces, commonly observed in dogs, cats, and birds [
10
,
12
]. In contrast, inverted
papillomas exhibit an inward growth pattern, with epithelial cells proliferating into the underlying stroma. In humans, inverted papillomas are most often reported in the nasal cavity, paranasal
sinuses [
8
], and urinary bladder [
4
], while in dogs, they typically occur in the skin [
6
]. Additionally, a single outbreak was reported in the conjunctiva of a pony [
1
]. We report a case of an oriental turtle dove
(
Streptopelia orientalis
) with an inverted papilloma with Civatte/Colloid bodies and identified keratin types in the basaloid tumor cells with liquid chromatography-tandem
mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis of formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissues.
An adult female oriental turtle dove was found deceased and transferred to the laboratory for veterinary diagnostic examination. There were three cutaneous masses in this case. A cutaneous mass
measuring approximately 5 cm in diameter was identified on the left neck (
Supplementary Fig. 1A
), with surface ulceration covered by
clots. Additional masses were observed on the thorax (approximately 2 cm in diameter) (
Supplementary Fig. 1B
) and medial side of the
right leg (approximately 1 cm in diameter) (
Supplementary Fig. 1C
). The cut surfaces of the masses were solid or lobulated,
displaying dark to pale brown coloration in an inverted growth pattern (
Supplementary Fig. 1A–C
). The masses were fixed in 10%
neutral-buffered formalin and routinely embedded in paraffin. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson trichrome. Immunohistochemistry was performed using antibodies
for α-smooth muscle actin (αSMA), desmin, ionized calcium binding adaptor molecule 1 (Iba-1), proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA), and Keratin 14 (see
Supplementary Table 1
for details).

To identify keratins in Civatte/colloid body-like structures, two areas in a section of a neck lesion were collected from a single FFPE specimen (Samples 1 and 2), with Sample 1 containing a
higher number of Civatte/colloid body-like structures than Sample 2 (
Supplementary Fig. 4A–D
). The protein composition of each sample
was analyzed using LC-MS/MS [
9
]. The acquired protein data were processed through the Mascot Server (Matrix Science Ltd., London, UK) and referenced against
the protein database for the Feral Pigeon (Columbidae, NCBI Taxonomy ID8930), due to limited protein data available for the oriental turtle dove. Protein identification and quantification were
assessed using the score (for qualitative analysis) and the Exponentially Modified Protein Abundance Index (emPAI) for quantification, as described in Supplemental Methods.

To identify papilloma virus in the cutaneous lesions, DNA was extracted from FFPE samples of cervical, thoracic, and leg lesions using the black PREP FFPE DNA Kit (50 rxn) (Innuscreen GmbH,
Berlin, Germany). Heminested-PCR was performed with primers of Puffin papillomaviruses, ATPU-Pap_F1, ATPU-Pap_F2, ATPU-Pap_R1 (

Supplementary Methods

Papillomavirus infection is a known cause of inverted papilloma in humans and dogs [
2
,
16
,
18
]. However, no papillomavirus was detected in the present case using LC-MS/MS and PCR. Currently, only eight species of avian papillomaviruses have been reported across
seven species of birds, with just three viruses known to cause skin tumors [
10
]. Recently, 12 additional avian papillomaviruses were identified in wild
birds [
7
]. However, no papillomaviruses have been identified in the pigeon orders, including the oriental turtle dove. This suggests that the inverted
papilloma in this case was likely due to non-infectious factors such as ultraviolet light exposure or genetic predisposition, rather than viral infection. Nonetheless, a viral etiology cannot be
entirely ruled out, given the limited research on pigeons. Further studies are required to explore potential viral factors in avian papillomavirus diseases.
A notable histopathological feature in this case was the presence of acidophilic Civatte/colloid bodies within the tumor. Originally reported in 1912, Civatte bodies became more widely
recognized in various oral and epidermal lesions during the 1960s and are now considered to represent apoptotic keratinocytes [
5
,
12
]. LC-MS/MS analysis of FFPE samples provides a powerful tool for comprehensive protein identification in specific pathological lesions [
15
]. We detected major Keratin 5- and 14-like proteins, along with minor Keratins 6a- and 4-like proteins in the neck lesion. The results suggested that Civatte bodies demonstrated a
predominance of Type II Keratin 5- and its partner Type I Keratin 14-like proteins, which could form filamentary structures by connecting each other [
13
,
14
]. In humans, Keratin 5 and Keratin 14 are typically expressed in undifferentiated basal cells of stratified squamous epithelia in the epidermis and
nonkeratinized stratified squamous epithelia in the mucosa [
13
,
14
]. These keratins were also expressed in inverted
papilloma derived from paranasal sinuses [
8
,
17
] and small salivary glands [
11
]. However, in the present avian samples, the antibody for Keratin 14 showed strong immunohistochemical positivity in the peripheral layers containing basal and squamous cells, but was
weak or absent in the central regions containing disrupted Civatte/colloid bodies. The absence of Keratin 14 staining in the central regions may be attributed to proteolysis or masking of
epitopes resulting from cellular degeneration and apoptosis during Civatte/colloid body formation [
3
]. Keratin 6a-like protein, typically expressed in
proliferative keratinocytes within keratinized and nonkeratinized stratified squamous epithelia, and accessory organs (follicles), is induced by stresses such as skin damage, inflammation,
hyperproliferative lesions, and cancers [
13
,
14
]. The high proliferative activity of basal cells demonstrated by
PCNA immunostaining could be associated with the presence of Keratin 6a-like protein. Keratin 4, found in non-keratinizing stratified squamous epithelia of the mucosa [
13
,
14
], is typically absent in normal epidermis but is expressed in squamous cell carcinoma [
13
], suggesting potential dysregulation of keratin expression in this case.
In conclusion, this case represents an inverted papilloma in an oriental turtle dove, distinguished by the presence of Keratins 14-, 5-, 6a-, and 4-like proteins detected with LC-MS/MS and the
presence of numerous Civatte/colloid bodies observed histopathologically. This rare case highlights an atypical inverted proliferation of basal cell-like tumor cells accompanied by mesenchymal
cell degeneration, osteogenesis, and melanosis. It offers valuable insight into the relationship between the inverted growth and stromal reaction, including blood vessel proliferation
surrounding tumor cords. Further investigation and sampling of similar cases are necessary to elucidate potential processes contributing to epidermal carcinogenesis.
