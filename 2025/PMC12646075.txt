Introduction
External factors that impinge negatively on plant metabolism, growth, and development are considered stressors (
Lichtenthaler 1998
). Exposure to a mild stress modifies the responses to a stress event that recurs after a stressless interval. This priming of stress responses has been described in plants and other organisms in response to abiotic and biotic stressors (
Hilker et al. 2016
;
Lämke and Bäurle 2017
). It is associated with the enhanced re-induction of stress-responsive genes after recurrent stress exposure and with chromatin modifications that mark these loci upon priming and remain in place while the memory lasts. In particular, histone H3 lysine 4 (H3K4) hypermethylation (H3K4me3), polymerase II phosphorylated at serine 5 of the C-terminal domain (Ser5P Pol II CTD), and reduced histone turnover are associated with stress priming and memory (
Jaskiewicz et al. 2011
;
Ding et al. 2012
;
Feng et al. 2016
;
Lämke et al. 2016
;
Pratx et al. 2023
;
Ge and Brickner 2024
).
Exposure to elevated temperature is a frequent abiotic stressor of plants, whose prevalence will increase with climate change. The general HS response (HSR) is evolutionarily highly conserved across eukaryotes (
Anckar and Sistonen 2011
) with heat shock transcription factors (HSFs) acting as key transcriptional regulators of the HSR. They form trimeric complexes and bind to cis-regulatory HS elements (HSEs) in the promoters of HS-responsive genes and activate their expression (
Scharf et al. 2012
;
Gomez-Pastor et al. 2018
). HSFs are negatively regulated by binding to chaperone proteins; upon HS, the chaperones dissociate from HSF to bind to other proteins in the cell, thus activating the HSFs and preventing those other proteins from denaturation (
Richter et al. 2010
). The number of HSFs in plant genomes generally exceeds that in other groups of organisms by far, while baker’s yeast has a single HSF and vertebrates have 4 (
Scharf et al. 2012
), angiosperm genomes typically contain more than 20 HSFs (
Wang et al. 2018
;
Wu et al. 2022
). Arabidopsis (
Arabidopsis thaliana
) has 21 HSFs, which are divided into 3 classes (A, B, and C) and 14 groups (A1-A9, B1-B4, C1) and are modularly organized (
Nover et al. 2001
;
Scharf et al. 2012
). At the N-terminus, they contain 2 evolutionarily conserved domains: the DNA-binding domain (DBD), consisting of 3 α-helices and 1 β-sheet, which binds to HSEs; and the oligomerization domain (OD), consisting of 2 heptameric repeats of hydrophobic amino acids which form a coiled-coil-like structure for oligomerization with other HSFs (
Peteranderl and Nelson 1992
;
Peteranderl et al. 1999
;
Scharf et al. 2012
;
Gomez-Pastor et al. 2018
). At the C-terminus, many HSFs contain a nuclear localization signal (NLS) and/or a nuclear export signal (NES) for shuttling between cytosol and nucleus, as well as one or more
a
romatic and large
h
ydrophobic amino acids embedded in an
a
cidic context (AHA) motifs that are crucial for transcriptional activation of target genes and presumably interact with components of the basal transcriptional machinery (
Döring et al. 2000
).
In Arabidopsis, 8 HSFs have been characterized regarding their involvement in different aspects of the HSR. Class A1 HSFs, in particular HSFA1A, HSFA1B, and HSFA1D, are master regulators of the HSR (
Liu et al. 2011
;
Yoshida et al. 2011
;
Ohama et al. 2017
) and act partially redundantly to mediate immediate responses to HS by transcriptional activation of target genes and transcriptional (co)activators (
Liu et al. 2011
;
Yoshida et al. 2011
;
Ohama et al. 2017
;
Peng et al. 2025
). They are constitutively expressed and regulated post-translationally. HSFA1D contains C-terminally an additional regulatory domain, the temperature-dependent regulatory domain (TDR) region, which inactivates its activity under non-HS conditions by interacting with chaperones of the HEAT SHOCK PROTEIN (HSP) 70/90 families (
Ohama et al. 2016
). HSFA1D integrates temperature and light and hormone signals and also plays a role in developmental responses to moderately elevated temperatures (thermomorphogenesis) (
Jung et al. 2013
;
Albertos et al. 2022
;
Gao et al. 2023
;
Tan et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2024
). HSFB1 and HSFB2B act as transcriptional repressors and are positive regulators of aTT (
Kumar et al. 2009
;
Ikeda et al. 2011
). In contrast, the HS-inducible
HSFA2
and
HSFA3
genes positively regulate HS memory (
Charng et al. 2007
;
Lämke et al. 2016
;
Friedrich et al. 2021
). Upon HS, transcription of
HSFA2
is directly induced by HSFA1s (
Liu et al. 2011
;
Nishizawa-Yokoi et al. 2011
;
Peng et al. 2025
), while induction of
HSFA3
is mediated by DEHYDRATION-RESPONSIVE ELEMENT BINDING PROTEIN 2A (DREB2A), which is in turn induced by HSFA1s, thus delaying the induction of
HSFA3
compared to
HSFA2
(
Sakuma et al. 2006
;
Schramm et al. 2007
;
Yoshida et al. 2008
,
2011
;
Liu et al. 2011
;
Friedrich et al. 2021
).
HS memory is associated with 2 types of transcriptional signatures that are found in a subset of HS-responsive genes: type I transcriptional memory or sustained induction, which describes sustained gene expression above non-stressed baseline expression levels after stress exposure has ceased (
Lämke and Bäurle 2017
;
Bäurle 2018
), and type II transcriptional memory, enhanced re-induction, which describes enhanced re-induction of gene expression after recurrent exposure to HS following a stress-free recovery period (
Lämke and Bäurle 2017
;
Bäurle 2018
). HSFA2 and HSFA3 jointly regulate type I transcriptional memory by directly binding to target genes (
Friedrich et al. 2021
;
Kappel et al. 2023
). They form heteromeric complexes with each other and additional members of the HSFA subfamily (
Friedrich et al. 2021
). In contrast, only HSFA2 is required for type II transcriptional memory, although HSFA3 can partially restore type II memory defects of
HSFA2
if it is expressed from the promoter of
HSFA2
, indicating that overall HSFA2 and HSFA3 share molecular functions that are absent in other HSFAs (
Lämke et al. 2016
;
Liu et al. 2018
;
Friedrich et al. 2021
). The transcriptional memory is associated with sustained enrichment of H3K4 di- and trimethylation, which is mediated by HSFA2 and HSFA3 through an as yet unknown mechanism (
Lämke et al. 2016
;
Liu et al. 2018
;
Friedrich et al. 2021
;
Oberkofler and Bäurle 2022
).
At the whole plant level, responses to heat stress (HS) include basal and acquired thermotolerance (
Mittler et al. 2012
;
Yeh et al. 2012
). Basal thermotolerance (bTT) is defined as the tolerance to severe HS without prior priming. Acquired thermotolerance (aTT) refers to a tolerance of severe HS that is conferred by a moderate HS (priming). After acquiring thermotolerance, Arabidopsis seedlings remains in a primed state for several days (
Charng et al. 2006
;
Stief et al. 2014
;
Friedrich et al. 2021
). This maintenance of acquired thermotolerance (maTT, HS memory), is genetically separable from aTT and several specific regulators have been characterized (
Charng et al. 2006
,
2007
;
Stief et al. 2014
;
Brzezinka et al. 2016
,
2019
;
Lämke et al. 2016
;
Urrea Castellanos et al. 2020
;
Friedrich et al. 2021
;
Crawford et al. 2024
).
Here, we asked which protein features determine the memory function of HSFA2. We investigated the role of the expression pattern by aiming to complement the
hsfa2
mutant with expression of HSFA1D under control of the
HSFA2
promoter. We then generated a series of chimeric HSF proteins, in which single domains of HSFA1D are swapped with their HSFA2 counterparts, and analyzed their ability to function in HS memory. We found that the presence of the TDR region abrogated HS memory, while the C-terminal regions and DBDs of HSFA1D and HSFA2 interchangeably mediated physiological HS memory. In contrast, the OD of HSFA1D could not replace its HSFA2 counterpart. In summary, our work provides insight into the molecular mechanisms at the base of HS memory and provides useful information to identify and/or engineer efficient HS memory transcription factors.
Results
HSFA1D cannot replace HSFA2 in HS memory
To investigate whether the HSFA1D protein can replace HSFA2 in HS memory when expressed under the control of the
HSFA2

(

,

and

) in the

mutant background. HSFA1D was selected as a representative of the HSFA1s group, as its domain structure is well studied (
Ohama et al. 2016
). When driving
FLAG-HSFA2
, this promoter complements the
hsfa2
mutant (
Friedrich et al. 2021
). Two independent homozygous lines (
pA2::FLAG-HSFA1D
#3 and #6) were tested for physiological HS memory (maTT) by priming 4-d-old seedlings with a 2-step acclimation treatment (ACC; 1 h 37 °C, 1.5 h 23 °C recovery, 45 min 44 °C) and, 3 d later, exposing them to an HS of 44 °C for 80 to 90 min (

). Neither line complemented the defects of

in HS memory (

and

), although immunoblotting confirmed the expression of FLAG-HSFA1D after ACC (

We next asked whether the memory-specific function of HSFA2 can be mapped to one of its functional domains. To this end, we created a series of transgenic lines that express FLAG-tagged chimeric proteins under control of
pA2

Fig. 1
,
Supplementary Table S1
); in

OD(A1D)
, the OD and NLS of HSFA1D replace their HSFA2 counterparts; in
CTDΔ(A1D)

CTD(A1D)
, the C-terminus of HSFA1D including the complete TDR replaced the C-terminus of HSFA2. Lines harboring these constructs were established in the
hsfa2
background either in combination with or without the

Brzezinka et al. 2016
). This allowed LUC-based screening of type I transcriptional memory.

Forty-nine hours after priming HS, OD(A1D) was no longer detected, while CTDΔ(A1D) and HSFA2 still were. This further supports the hypothesis that the OD(A1D) protein is less stable than HSFA2.

The OD of HSFA2 and HSFA1D determines interaction preferences
The functional differences between the ODs of HSFA1D and HSFA2 may also be caused by a difference in their interactions with other protein, in particular with other HSFs. To investigate this hypothesis, we performed immunoprecipitation followed by mass spectrometry analysis of HSFA2 and OD(A1D) at 1 or 4 h after priming HS (1 h 37 °C) and no-HS conditions. For HSFA2, we identified all 4 HSFA1s, HSFA3, HSFA6B, HSFA7A, HSFA7B, HSFA4A, and HSFC1 as interaction partners (
Fig. 7B
,
Supplementary Table S2
), in line with previous analyses (
Friedrich et al. 2021
). The highest numbers of unique peptides at P + 1 h were found for HSFA1B, HSFA7A, and HSFA7B. At P + 4 h, HSFA3 peptides make up a larger share of total co-immunoprecipitated peptides, likely reflecting the higher abundance of
HSFA3
protein 4 h after HS (
Friedrich et al. 2021
). Co-precipitating with OD(A1D), we identified all 4 HSFA1s, HSFA3, HSFA6B, and HSFA7A (
Fig. 7B
,
Supplementary Table S2
). To estimate quantitative differences between the abundance of interaction partners, we considered their relative presence among the pool of HSF peptides that were not derived from the bait proteins (
Fig. 7B
,
Supplementary Table S2
). HSFA4A and HSFC1 were found only in HSFA2 samples, however, due to their very low abundance, it is possible that they also interact with OD(A1D) and were not recovered in our experiment. In contrast, HSFA7B peptides were among the most frequently recovered peptides in HSFA2 samples but are completely absent from OD(A1D) samples. Thus, the OD of HSFA1D appears to have a reduced affinity for HSFA7B. Peptides derived from HSFA1s make up a larger proportion of OD(A1D) co-immunoprecipitated peptides than of HSFA2 co-immunoprecipitated peptides, and this is more evident at P + 4 h, where they make up almost 75% of interacting peptides (50% for HSFA2 samples) than at P + 1 h (∼70% in OD(A1D) and 50% in HSFA2) (
Fig. 7B
,
Supplementary Table S2
). Thus, OD(A1D) has a higher affinity for HSFA1s than HSFA2. This is in line with the model that HSF complexes that are active in HS memory contain only one HSFA1 and 2 other HSF molecules (
Friedrich et al. 2021
). This further suggests that HSFA7A, HSFA7B and HSFA6B may be neutral or even positively acting toward HS memory.
Aligning the amino acid sequences of the OD domains of HSFA2 and HSFA1D did not reveal major differences in conserved amino acid residues in their bipartite heptad motifs or in their hydrophobicity (
Supplementary Fig. S1B
and
C
). However, HSFA1D, but not HSFA2, contained 2 amino acid stretches with low complexity and potential prion-like amino acid composition (
Supplementary Fig. S1D
) (
Lancaster et al. 2014
). Both sequence stretches are juxtaposed to the OD and were included in the part of HSFA1D that was used to generate FLAG-OD(A1D) (
Supplementary Fig. S1B
). It is possible that they confer temperature regulation to the chimeric OD(A1D) protein through phase separation. Thus, both the identity of HSF interaction partners as well as additional protein domains may underlie the different activity of the chimeric protein in HS memory.
Discussion
Here, we investigated which protein domains of HSFA2 mediate HS memory in Arabidopsis. We demonstrated that the expression pattern is not a decisive feature and generated a series of chimeric proteins to delineate relevant protein domains. We selected HSFA1D in this approach, as HSFA1s are considered as master transcriptional regulators of the acute HSR in different species (
Mishra et al. 2002
;
Liu et al. 2011
;
Yoshida et al. 2011
). In addition, the domain structure of HSFA1D is well characterized (
Ohama et al. 2016
). An obvious difference between HSFA2 and HSFA1D is the presence of the TDR domain in HSFA1D, which is absent from HSFA2 (
Supplementary Fig. S1A
). The TDR is a temperature-dependent negative regulatory domain; partial deletion caused constitutive nuclear localization of HSFA1D and conferred transactivation capacity at ambient temperatures (
Ohama et al. 2016
). This was most evident when subregions 1–4 were deleted, which is why we deleted those regions in the
CTDΔ(A1D)
lines. This region also mediates the interaction with HSP70/90 chaperones (
Ohama et al. 2016
). We included the TDR in 2 chimeric proteins: A2+TDR(A1D), in which the complete TDR was inserted into
HSFA2 CDS
, and CTD(A1D), which contained the C-terminal region of HSFA1D, including the TDR. Neither of these proteins complemented the
hsfa2
mutant with respect to sustained induction of
HSA32-LUC
and physiological HS memory (
Supplementary Fig. S3
), although both proteins were detected up to 52 h after ACC treatment (
Supplementary Fig. S4
). These findings suggest that the TDR imposes gradual inactivation after HS also onto a heterologous HSF.
A surprising finding of this work was that the C-terminus of HSFA1D can fully replace that of HSFA2 (
Figs. 3
–
5
), although they are the most diverged regions at the sequence level (
Supplementary Fig. S1A
). HSFA1D lacks the signature AHA motifs, which mediate interactions with the basal transcription machinery (
Döring et al. 2000
). Thus, either the C-terminal domain of HSFA1D has AHA-independent domains that recruit the basal transcriptional machinery, or, its presence in a trimeric HSF complex is sufficient to reconstitute overall transactivation activity. The memory HSFs (HSFA2, HSFA3) recruit sustained H3K4 methylation at their target gene loci, and this property might be mediated by the C-terminal region. Hence, the presence of the
CTDΔ(A1D)
chimeric protein with the C-terminus of HSFA1D in a multimeric HSFA3-containing complex is sufficient to reconstitute overall memory functions to the complex. It is unknown which methyltransferase enzymes catalyze H3K4 methylation after HS, and whether they are recruited through direct interactions with memory HSFs or indirectly through other proteins. H3K4 methyltransferases act as part of the evolutionarily conserved COMPLEX PROTEINS ASSOCIATED WITH SET1 (COMPASS)-like complex which contains subunits ARABIDOPSIS ASH2 RELATIVE (ASH2R), HUMAN WDR5 (WD40 REPEAT) HOMOLOG A (WDR5A), and RBBP5 (
Jiang et al. 2011
;
Oya et al. 2022
). On the other hand, the bZIP transcription factors bZIP28 and bZIP60, master regulators of the unfolded protein response in the endoplasmic reticulum (
Iwata and Koizumi 2005
;
Liu et al. 2007
), interact with ASH2R and RBBP5 directly (
Song et al. 2015
). While it is an attractive hypothesis that the C-terminus of HSFA2 (and HSFA3) directly interacts with an H3K4 methyltransferase, our findings do not provide evidence for this.
The second unexpected finding is that the OD domains of HSFA1D and HSFA2 encode functional specificity, even though these domains share the highest degree of sequence similarity (
Supplementary Fig. S1A
). OD(A1D) did not complement the
hsfa2
mutant, suggesting that specific sets of interaction partners determine the function as a memory HSF. We thus identified interaction partners of HSFA2 and OD(A1D) by co-immunoprecipitation and mass spectrometry. HSFA2 interacted with HSFA1 isoforms and other HSFAs (A3, A6B, A7A, A7B), consistent with previous findings (
Friedrich et al. 2021
). In contrast, OD(A1D) samples were more enriched with HSFA1 peptides and less with other HSFAs (A3, A6B, A7A). Notably, no HSFA7B peptides were recovered with OD(A1D) as a bait, while HSFA2 samples recovered a substantial amount of HSFA7A and A7B (
Fig. 7B
,
Supplementary Table S2
). These findings support the idea that different complex compositions determine the potency of HSF complexes in HS memory. It might further implicate the identified non-A1 isoforms in HS memory. Indeed, mutants of HSFA3 (
Friedrich et al. 2021
) and HSFA7B (
John et al. 2024
) have compromised HS memory. Thus, the absence of the OD of HSFA2 in
OD(A1D)
lines could shift the HSF complex equilibrium toward complexes specialized in the immediate response to HS. Also, the OD region may affect protein stability, as the OD(A1D) protein levels resembled HSFA2 at ACC + 4 h, but were undetectable at 28 and 52 h after ACC. This effect may be mediated either by the affinity to other HSF interactors, or by adjacent domains within the chimeric protein that affect protein stability or localization, such as the prion domain (PrD)-like domains or the NLS flanking the OD of HSFA1D.
The core bipartite heptad repeats of the OD form a coiled coil-like structure (
Peteranderl and Nelson 1992
;
Peteranderl et al. 1999
); however, our sequence analysis did not identify obvious changes between the 2 proteins (
Supplementary Fig. S1B
and
C
). The crystallization of HSFs has been so far limited to the DBD (
Neudegger et al. 2016
;
Feng et al. 2021
), and structural prediction tools produce a very similar structure for both ODs. One difference between the HSFA1D and HSFA2 regions that were exchanged to generate the OD(A1D) chimeric protein is the presence of 2 PrD-like sequences in HSFA1D (
Lancaster et al. 2014
) (
Supplementary Fig. S1D
). PrDs in plants confer thermal responsiveness or liquid-liquid phase separation to proteins, for example in HSFA1A (
Peng et al. 2025
). Thus, this domain may be involved in regulating the stability or activity of the OD(A1D) protein. The exchanged region also contains the NLS, which in principle may be differentially regulated and impact nuclear localization.
In summary, our work reveals characteristics of memory HSFs at the protein domain level, and shows that protein stability and transactivation capacity are key determinants. The TDR and possibly a PrD-like domain close to the OD interfere with this and thus abrogate memory-specific functions of HSF proteins. This suggests that some active protein may be required during the memory phase. In addition, the composition of the HSFA2 complex is affected by the OD and likely plays an important role. Taken together, key features of HSFA2 (in comparison to HSFA1D) for its activity in HS memory are interaction partners, as selected through the OD, and the lack of a repression and PrD-like domains. Thus, a prediction from our study is that a modified HSFA1D with the OD domain of HSFA2 and deletion of its TDR domain should be active as a memory HSFs. It will be interesting to test this prediction to better understand the HSF protein code. Our findings will be useful to biotechnologically fine-tune and optimize transcriptional HS responses and memory to adapt plants to a changing environment.
Materials and methods
Plant materials, growth conditions, and HS treatments
The
hsfa2-1
(SALK_008978) allele was characterized previously (
Charng et al. 2007
;
Lämke et al. 2016
). The
pHSA32::HSA32-LUC
reporter line (
Brzezinka et al. 2016
) was introduced into the
hsfa2
background by crossing. The
hsfa1a/b/d
triple mutant was described (
Liu et al. 2011
). Primers for genotyping are listed in
Supplementary Table S3
. Seedlings were grown on GM medium (1% [w/v] glucose) under 16 h/8 h light/dark cycle at 23 °C/21 °C. For maTT, 4-d-old seedlings were exposed to a 3-step ACC treatment (1 h 37 °C, 1.5 h 23 °C, 45 min 44 °C), and, 3 d later, to HS of 44 °C for 80 to 90 min (
Fig. 2A
). For bTT, 4 d-old seedlings were exposed to HS of 44 °C for 15 to 25 min (
Supplementary Fig. S2D
). For aTT, 4-d-old seedlings were exposed to 37 °C for 1 h, recovered at 23 °C for 1.5 h, and then exposed to HS of 44 °C for 140 to 180 min (
Supplementary Fig. S2F
). Seedlings were imaged and evaluated 18 d after germination. To statistically evaluate seedling performance in HS assays, Fisher's 2 × 4 exact probability test was used (
http://vassarstats.net/fisher2x4.html
).

,

LUC imaging
Chemiluminescence emitted by luciferase was measured with NightOWL II LB 983 in vivo imaging system (Berthold Technologies). Seedlings were sprayed with 2 m
m
beetle luciferin (Promega) right before imaging. For data acquisition, signal acquisition time was set to 2 min. Data processing was done with Indigo Software for in vivo imaging (Berthold Technologies). Signal thresholds were set to the same interval for the whole length of the analyzed time series.
Total protein extraction, immunoprecipitation, and immunoblotting
For immunoblotting, total protein was extracted from whole seedlings and quantified by Bradford protein assay (
Smaczniak et al. 2012
). For immunoblot of total protein extract, 40 µg of total protein were separated by SDS-PAGE on a 12% gel. FLAG signal was assessed with anti-FLAG antibody (F1804, Sigma) followed by secondary anti-mouse antibody (926-32210, LI-COR Biosciences). H3 signal was assessed with anti-histone H3 antibody (ab1791, Abcam) followed by secondary anti-rabbit antibody (926-32211, LI-COR Biosciences). For isolation of FLAG-tagged proteins and their interactors by immunoprecipitation, 500 µg of total protein were processed with the µMACS DYKDDDDK isolation kit (Miltenyi Biotec). 15 µl of 50 µl total eluate were used for assessment of FLAG signal, while 20 µg of total protein input were used for assessment of H3 signal.
Protein mass spectrometry
Sample preparation for mass spectrometry was based on a modified protocol by (
Wiśniewski 2018
). Total protein was extracted from ca. 1 g whole seedlings and FLAG-tagged bait protein was immunoprecipitated with µMACS DYKDDDDK isolation kit (Miltenyi Biotec) and samples were eluted in 8
m
urea. In-solution protein digestion with Trypsin/LysC (Promega) was performed as follows: ammonium bicarbonate (AmBic) and DTT were added to the eluate to a final concentration of 50 m
m
and 5 m
m
, respectively, and samples were incubated at RT for 30 min. Iodoacetamide was added to a final concentration of 15 m
m
and samples were incubated at RT in the dark for 30 min. For in-solution protein digestion, 0.1 µg Trypsin/LysC mix resuspended in 50 m
m
AmBic was added. Samples were brought to 100 µl volume with 50 m
m
AmBic and digested at 37 °C for 4 h. Samples were brought to 400 µl with 50 m
m
AmBic and were incubated at 37 °C over night. Samples were acidified by addition of trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) to a final concentration of 1%. For desalting, Sep-Pak C18 96 well plates, 40 mg sorbent (Waters), were used. Wells were calibrated by addition of 1 mL 100% methanol, once by addition of 1 mL Solution B (80% acetonitrile, 0.1% TFA), and twice by addition of 1 mL Solution A (0.1% TFA). Samples were loaded and washed twice by addition of 1 mL Solution A. Each of these steps was followed by centrifugation at RT for 1 min at 100 g. Samples were eluted by adding 600 µl Solution C (60% acetonitrile, 0.1% TFA) followed by centrifugation, dried in a SpeedVac and stored at −20 °C. Purified peptides were resuspended in 50 μL of 0.1% TFA, and analyzed by liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (MS) carried out on a Exploris 480 instrument connected to an Ultimate 3000 rapid separation liquid chromatography nano instrument and a nanospray flex ion source (all Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA). Peptide separation was performed on a reverse-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) column (75 μm by 42 cm) packed in-house with C18 resin (2.4 μm; Dr. Maisch HPLC GmbH, Ammerbuch, Germany). The peptides were first loaded onto a C18 precolumn (preconcentration set-up) and then eluted in the backflush mode with a gradient from 98% solvent A (0.15% formic acid) and 2% solvent B (99.85% acetonitrile and 0.15% formic acid) to 25% solvent B over 40 min, continued from 25% to 35% of solvent B for another 20 min. The flow rate was set to 300 nL/min. The data were acquired in a data-dependent mode for the label-free quantification, and study was set to obtain one high-resolution MS scan at a resolution of 60,000 (m/z 200) with the scanning range from 350 to 1,650 m/z, normalized automatic gain control (AGC) target (%) of 300, 25 ms per scan in a positive mode. This was followed by 20 MS2 scans (top20 method), at the resolution of 15,000 and normalized AGC target (%) of 200.
For the database search, protein database for
Arabidopsis thaliana
was downloaded from Uniprot (
https://www.uniprot.org
, November 2021, 39,267 entries). Spectral counting analysis was performed using SEQUEST within the Proteome Discoverer platform v. 1.4.0.288 (Thermo Scientific). Search parameters included full tryptic digestion, 4 missed cleavages allowed, carbamidomethylation (C) set as a fixed modification and oxidation (M) and deamidation (N,Q) as a variable modification. Precursor ion tolerance was set to 10 ppm (Monoisotopic), and fragment ion tolerance to 0.02 Da (Monoisotopic). The mass tolerance was set to 10 ppm for precursor ions and 0.02 Da for high energy-collision dissociation fragment ions. The results were imported into Scaffold v.5.1.2 (Proteome Software), and a 1% false discovery rate was adjusted within the Scaffold software.
