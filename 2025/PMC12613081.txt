1
Introduction
Chicken breast is widely acknowledged as a high-quality animal protein source, valued for its high protein content and low cholesterol, fat, and calorie levels (
Jung et al., 2022
). However, at room temperature, the endogenous enzyme activity in chicken is relatively high, and microorganisms decompose nutrients and multiply rapidly, and their metabolic products may affect the flavor of chicken. Freezing serves as a critical method for preserving meat quality during processing, storage, and transportation, as it delays deterioration in odor, color, and texture (
Rocha et al., 2022
;
Zhang et al., 2023
). For frozen meat intended for further processing, thawing represents a critical prerequisite. During this process, the melting of ice crystals often causes protein degradation, lipid oxidation, color changes, and reduced water retention (
Arshad et al., 2023
). Although traditional thawing evaluation methods, such as texture analysis, color measurement, and water-holding capacity, can effectively describe changes in apparent quality, they struggle to comprehensively reveal the molecular mechanisms and metabolic pathways of these changes (
Ali et al., 2015
;
Oliveira et al., 2015
). The development of high-throughput omics technologies has provided a new perspective for in-depth understanding of the molecular changes during the chicken thawing process.
Metabolomics is an emerging and rapidly advancing discipline in the field of meat science. By systematically analyzing the composition and concentration changes of small-molecule metabolites under different physiological and environmental conditions, it provides an important basis for evaluating key attributes of meat quality, including tenderness, flavor and its precursor compounds, edible quality characteristics, and freshness-related features (
Ramanathan et al., 2023
;
Zhang et al., 2021
). In the field of chicken quality research, non-targeted metabolomics studies have revealed dynamic metabolic changes during refrigeration and identified key freshness-related biomarkers, including L-pyroglutamic acid, tyramine, and indole-3-carboxaldehyde (
Wen et al., 2020
). Metabolomic analysis of Beijing You chicken breast meat revealed that core flavor metabolites, including
l
-carnitine, L-methionine, and 3-hydroxybutyric acid, were significantly enriched with increasing age. The formation of its quality was mainly regulated by the pathways of arginine biosynthesis, purine metabolism， and amino acid metabolism (
Ge et al., 2023
). Additionally, metabolomic analysis of chicken exudate during the freeze-thaw process revealed elevated levels of phospholipids and their derivatives, which were highly susceptible to oxidation. The effective removal of these exudates may contribute to an extended product shelf life (
Xu et al., 2025
). These studies highlighted the significant potential of metabolomics in revealing the mechanisms of meat quality changes. While metabolomics has achieved notable progress in the field of meat storage and processing, its application to the investigation of the thawing process in frozen meat remains limited.
This study used widely targeted metabolomics to analyze the metabolic changes in frozen chicken breast meat at different thawing stages (frozen, half-thawed, and thawed) under air thawing conditions at 25 °C, aiming to identify key differential metabolites and their related metabolic pathways during the thawing process. The air-thawing method studied is economically viable and widely adopted, ensuring the scalability of our findings. Results indicated that the metabolic dynamics during Tibetan chicken breast thawing exhibited distinct phase-specific characteristics, involving the synergistic effects of lipid oxidation, protein hydrolysis, and flavor compound formation. This study revealed potential associations between thawing stages and metabolic dynamics in frozen Tibetan chicken, offering insights for future optimization of the thawing process to enhance the quality and value of frozen poultry products.
2
Material and methods
2.1
Material and chemicals reagents
A total of 15 Tibetan chickens, each with an average body weight of 1.2–1.4 kg and aged 500 days, were purchased from Bowen Agriculture and Animal Husbandry Technology Co., Ltd. All chemical reagents used for metabolomic analysis were of chromatographic grade. Specifically, methanol and acetonitrile were obtained from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). Formic acid was purchased from Aladdin (Shanghai, China). Reference standards (purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, Shanghai) were dissolved in methanol, stored at −20 °C, and diluted with 70 % methanol before mass spectrometry for gradient concentration preparation.
2.2
Tibetan chicken thawing treatment and collection
Firstly, the packaged Tibetan chicken samples were frozen at −18 °C for 72 h to ensure complete freezing. The thawing process was conducted under sterile conditions by placing the samples on a super-clean workbench and air-thawing them at 25 °C with a relative humidity of 70 %. A probe-type food thermometer (YHZ-3200) was inserted into the interior of the chicken breast to monitor and record the changes in the core temperature of the chicken carcass in real time (
Wang et al., 2020
). Samples were collected at three critical states, with five Tibetan chickens in each group: the first group consisted of fully frozen chicken breast with a core temperature of −18 °C. The second group was half-thawed chicken breast with a core temperature of 0.0 ± 0.3 °C and a thawing time of 220–240 min. The third group was fully thawed chicken breast with a core temperature of 20.0 ± 0.4 °C and a thawing time of 520–540 min (Fig. S1). Subsequently, samples of chicken breast were collected at three key thawing stages: the frozen chicken breast group (FCB); the half-thawed chicken breast group (H-TCB); and the thawed chicken breast group (TCB). All collected samples were stored in an ultra-low temperature freezer at −80 °C for further analysis.
2.3
Metabolomic analysis sample extraction
Metabolomics analyses were conducted by Wuhan Meteville Biotechnology Co. Samples were taken from the −80 °C refrigerator and thawed on ice. Subsequently, they were ground into powder under liquid nitrogen. A precise quantity of 20 ± 1 mg was weighed for each replicate and transferred into pre-cooled centrifuge tubes labeled for FCB, H-TCB, and TCB groups. Each tube was added with 400 μL of pre-cooled 70 % methanol internal standard solution. After vortexing and shaking (2500 r/min, 5 min), the tubes were placed on ice for 15 min, then centrifuged (12,000 r/min, 4 °C, 10 min). The supernatant (300 μL) was transferred to new tubes and stored at −20 °C for 30 min, followed by a second centrifugation (12,000 r/min, 4 °C, 3 min). Finally, 200 μL of the sample was transferred to vials for mass spectrometry analysis (
Xie et al., 2025
). Quality control (QC) samples were prepared by pooling equal volumes of all sample extracts and adjusting the final volume to match individual test samples.
2.4
UPLC-MS/MS analysis
The separation process was performed utilizing a Waters ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 C18 column (1.8 μm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm). Mobile phase A: ultrapure water containing 0.1 % formic acid; Mobile phase B: acetonitrile containing 0.1 % formic acid. Elution gradient: 0 min (95:5, water/acetonitrile,
v
/v), 2.0 min (80:20), 5.0 min (40:60), 6.0 min (1:99), 7.5 min (1:99), 7.6 min (95:5), 10.0 min (95:5). Experimental conditions: flow rate 0.4 mL/min, column temperature 40 °C, injection volume 2 μL. ESI source temperature: 500 °C, voltages: +5500 V and −4500 V. Gas pressures: GS I 55 psi, GS II 60 psi, CUR 25 psi. CAD in high-sensitivity mode. Ion pairs were scanned in Qtrap with optimized DP and CE (
Jia et al., 2025
).
2.5
Qualitative and quantitative principles of metabolites
Qualitative analysis was achieved by comparing retention time, daughter ion pairs, and secondary mass spectrometry data to the self-constructed MWDB (MetWare Database). Quantitative analysis used multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) on a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer. In MRM mode, precursor ions were filtered by the first quadrupole, fragmented in the second, and characteristic fragment ions were selected by the third to minimize interferences. Metabolite peak areas were integrated and normalized across samples for accurate quantification. The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG, with species-specific filtering for chicken (
Gallus gallus
) and database version updates as of October 2024) was used for metabolic pathway analysis (
Zhang et al., 2024
).
2.6
Statistical analysis
Each sample group was repeated five times. Results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. One-way ANOVA was used for comparisons among groups, with significance at
p
< 0.05. Data visualization was performed using Origin software.

4
Discussion
4.1
Analysis of the potential biochemical processes of quality changes in Tibetan chicken breast during thawing
During the early stage of thawing (FCB vs. H-TCB), the DAMs were mainly polar lipids and lipid-related metabolites, including 13 “glycerophospholipids” and 12 “fatty acyls”. This profile was consistent with the significant enrichment observed in the “glycerolipid metabolism” and “necroptosis” pathways (
Fig. 4
). Among the top 5 DAMs ranked by VIP value, 18β-glycyrrhetinic acid has been reported to be capable of preventing the generation of free radicals and lipid peroxidation (
Sun et al., 2019
). Its significant accumulation in H-TCB suggested that the cells had initiated an antioxidant defense mechanism to cope with oxidative damage. Free fatty acid FFA (22:6), a typical polyunsaturated fatty acid released through phospholipase A
2
(PLA
2
)-mediated hydrolysis of the sn-2 ester bond in membrane phospholipids (
Astudillo et al., 2019
), was significantly accumulated (
p
< 0.05), indicating activation of the PLA
2
-mediated membrane phospholipid degradation pathway. Meanwhile, carnitine C3:0, a key carrier for short-chain fatty acids to enter mitochondria for β-oxidation, underwent significant changes in content (
p
< 0.05), suggesting that the intracellular fatty acid transport and energy metabolism processes were disrupted (
Alhasaniah, 2023
;
Houten et al., 2020
). The changes in these two types of key lipid regulatory molecules jointly indicated that the degradation of cell membrane phospholipids and the lipid oxidation process were initiated in the early stage of thawing (
Tatiyaborworntham et al., 2022
).
In the late thawing stage (H-TCB vs. TCB), “amino acid and its metabolites” accounted for the highest proportion of differential metabolites (33 out of 54), which was consistent with the significantly enriched “ABC transporters” and “phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan biosynthesis” in the KEGG pathways (
Fig. 4
;
p
< 0.05). The top 5 DAMs based on VIP ranking included Trp-Ile, carnitine C5:1, L-methionine, Ile-Glu, and Phe-Gly, indicating that the core process in the later stage of thawing gradually shifted from membrane lipid degradation to extensive protein hydrolysis and amino acid metabolism. The significant enrichment of the “ABC transporter” pathway suggested that cells were actively transporting the small peptides and free amino acids produced by hydrolysis. The significant enrichment of the “phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan biosynthesis” pathway was more likely due to the large release of these aromatic amino acids as protein degradation products, which then entered subsequent metabolic conversion pathways. For example, as a precursor for the microbial synthesis of aromatic compounds. These findings further suggested that the quality deterioration of Tibetan chicken breast during thawing may be regulated by a complex biochemical signaling process associated with necroptosis. Specifically, the thawing stress of chicken breast triggered necroptosis, leading to cell membrane rupture and lipid hydrolysis. Previous studies have shown that the formation of internal ice crystals in meat significantly affects the sensitive ultrastructure of proteins. Thawing causes the melting of ice crystals, thereby transferring intracellular water to the extracellular region, resulting in excessive water loss, deterioration of texture characteristics, and protein denaturation and oxidation (
Ballin & Lametsch, 2008
;
Barido et al., 2022
;
Leygonie et al., 2012
). Subsequently, the thawing process triggers the release of proteases, lipases, and lysosomes from damaged cells, thereby affecting biochemical homeostasis. These hydrolytic enzymes further activate proteolytic degradation, generating a large number of small peptides and free amino acids, which are then actively transported and subsequently transformed (
Yang et al., 2019
). However, the specific molecular pathways and key regulatory targets involved require further validation and elucidation through follow-up studies employing proteomic and transcriptomic approaches.
4.2
The dual role in flavor formation during the thawing process of Tibetan chicken breast
The flavor change in chicken breast during thawing is a complex dynamic process, involving the generation of umami substances and the accumulation of off-flavor compounds (
Yi et al., 2025
). The results of this study showed that “amino acid and its metabolites” was the most abundant metabolic category (30.62 %–30.68 %), with a significant increase of 12.05 % (
p
< 0.05) in TCB. This change was mainly attributed to 165 small peptides and 58 amino acid derivatives produced by protein degradation. In the TCB group, the contents of the umami dipeptides Val-Ile and Ile-Leu were significantly higher than those in the FCB and H-TCB groups (
p
< 0.05), indicating that the enhanced proteolysis resulting from the combined action of endogenous enzymes and microbial metabolism positively contributed to flavor improvement. Flavor peptides, generated through protein degradation, contribute to the unique taste profiles of meat. According to previous studies, combined peptides, including glycine (Gly), leucine (Leu), lysine (Lys), isoleucine (Ile), and others, contribute to the umami taste (
Dashdorj et al., 2015
). Meanwhile, the total abundance of “nucleotide and its metabolites” significantly increased by 15.06 % (
p
< 0.05) after thawing, further enhancing the complexity of flavor. Among the top ten most abundant “nucleotide and its metabolites”, inosine, guanosine, 5′-deoxy-5′-(methylthio), and adenosine significantly accumulated in the TCB group (
p
< 0.05). These compounds are widely recognized as key precursors of umami flavor in meat products (
Wen et al., 2020
;
Xu et al., 2022
). It is worth noting that the accumulation of certain metabolites, such as carnosine (accounting for 63.19 % of small peptides) and ornithine, may also bring health-related bioactive benefits, including antioxidant and buffering properties (
Jairath et al., 2024
). The synergistic interactions among these flavor compounds collectively enhanced the overall flavor complexity and umami perception in thawed chicken breasts, constituting the “positive aspect” of their flavor quality (
Qi et al., 2021
). Our previous metagenomic studies further support this conclusion, revealing that the degradation of proteins and carbohydrates is the most active metabolic pathway for microorganisms. Particularly, pathways such as “alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism” and “glycine, serine and threonine metabolism” were significantly active during thawing, directly promoting the accumulation of umami amino acids (
Shui et al., 2025
).
However, the thawing process is also accompanied by the formation of off-flavor compounds. The by-products of proteolysis, such as bitter peptides containing hydrophobic amino acids and 2-amino-methylpyrimidine produced by the degradation of nucleotides (a known enhancer of bitter compounds), increased significantly (
p
< 0.05). Meanwhile, when lipid oxidation products (e.g., aldehydes and ketones) accumulate to a certain threshold, they can participate in the Maillard reaction to generate heterocyclic aromatic amines, and this process may further trigger the production of off-flavors (
Chang et al., 2025
;
Wu et al., 2021
). Liu et al. reported that ultrasound thawing treatment significantly reduced the content of 2-aminomethylpyrimidine, thereby reducing the bitterness of thawed Tibetan pork (
Liu et al., 2025
). Shin et al. found that after freezing and thawing, the contents of sweet, bitter, and umami amino acids in chicken increased by approximately three to four times. Although it was difficult to assess the specific impact of these amino acids on the overall flavor, the increase in the total content of free amino acids in thawed chicken compared to fresh chicken may enhance its flavor intensity (
Shin et al., 2024
). Our previous metagenomic results also revealed a positive correlation between
Acidovorax
and
Pseudomonas
and bitter amino acids (including valine and methionine), suggesting that enhanced microbial metabolic activity may play a key role in the generation of bitter precursors (
Shui et al., 2025
). Additionally, the contents of benzene derivatives (e.g., 4-aminophenol and
p
-coumaric acid) and heterocyclic compounds (e.g., 3-aminoquinoline) were also significantly increased in the TCB group (
p
< 0.05), and their accumulation may be associated with amino acid degradation and lipid oxidation (
Luo et al., 2022
). These results collectively indicated that the degradation of proteins and nucleotides in Tibetan chicken breast during thawing, while enhancing umami taste, may also promote the formation of bitterness and other off-flavors through lipid oxidation and aberrant metabolic pathways. Therefore, the dynamic changes in flavor precursor compounds during thawing can be vividly described as “an intense competition between flavor enhancement and quality deterioration”.
4.3
The formation of harmful compounds and potential risks during the thawing process of Tibetan chicken breast
During the thawing process, the rise in temperature creates favorable conditions for the proliferation of microorganisms and their metabolic activities, leading to a significant accumulation of harmful compounds such as biogenic amines, thereby posing a potential food safety risk. In this study, three biogenic amines (histamine, putrescine, and tyramine) were detected in Tibetan chicken breast during thawing, collectively accounting for 0.52 % of the total abundance of small molecule metabolites. Notably, in the TCB group, the abundance of biogenic amines was 13.72 % higher than that in the FCB group. These biogenic amines mainly originate from the decarboxylation of amino acids such as histidine, ornithine, and tyrosine by microorganisms. Among these, histamine (converted from histidine) and tyramine (converted from tyrosine) exhibit relatively high biological activity, and their accumulation may trigger allergic reactions or foodborne diseases, posing a potential threat to human health (
Gardini et al., 2016
;
Shashank et al., 2021
), while cadaverine and putrescine are regarded as histamine enhancers (
Onyango et al., 2016
). These findings were highly consistent with our previous metagenomic study, which demonstrated that microbial communities were significantly involved in amino acid metabolism during thawing and that the abundance of microorganisms closely associated with biogenic amine formation (such as
Klebsiella
and
Chromobacterium
) increased (
Shui et al., 2025
). In addition to biogenic amines, the significant accumulation of benzene derivatives (e.g., 4-aminophenol) and heterocyclic amine precursors (e.g., 3-aminoquinoline) suggested the potential existence of a more complex microbial metabolic network and associated safety risks during thawing. In conclusion, the thawing process of Tibetan chicken breast irreversibly initiates microbiologically mediated food safety risks. Monitoring biogenic amines (particularly cadaverine and putrescine) and specific microbial metabolic markers (e.g., 4-aminophenol) could serve as critical indicators for evaluating the safety of thawed frozen chicken meat.
This study is subject to certain limitations. The use of a 70 % (
v
/v) methanol aqueous solution for extraction in broad-targeted metabolomics may not effectively recover complex lipids and highly non-polar compounds, potentially resulting in the underrepresentation or omission of key lipid metabolites associated with off-flavor development. Furthermore, although the observed correlations among metabolites, microbial activities, and quality changes are informative, they do not establish causality, and thus require further functional validation. Future research should employ an integrated multi-omics approach by combining proteomics with metagenomics or metatranscriptomics to more precisely delineate the roles of specific enzymes and microbial processes in the quality deterioration of thawed chicken, thereby supporting the development of targeted intervention strategies.
5
Conclusion
In this study, the changes in water-soluble small molecule metabolites in Tibetan chicken breast during room-temperature thawing were analyzed using widely targeted metabolomics. A total of 788 metabolites were identified. The differentially abundant metabolites (DAMs) primarily included “amino acid and its metabolites”, “nucleotide and its metabolites”, “organic acids and its derivatives”, “glycerophospholipids”, and “fatty acyls”. Key DAMs such as 18β-glycyrrhetinic acid and FFA (22:6) were screened out. KEGG enrichment analysis showed that during the thawing process at room temperature, the main metabolic pathways in Tibetan chicken breast meat were “glycerolipid metabolism”, “necroptosis”, “ABC transporters”, and “phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan biosynthesis”. In the early stages of thawing, lipid metabolism disorder was dominant, characterized by membrane phospholipid degradation and lipid peroxidation. In the later stage, protein hydrolysis and amino acid metabolism became prominent. These findings provide a theoretical basis for clarifying the correlation mechanism between the degree of thawing and the dynamic changes of metabolites.
