Background
The signal peptides (SPs) are peptide segments of about 20–40 amino acids sequences located in the N-terminal of protein that play a crucial role in guiding the newly synthesized protein to its proper location within a cell or for secretion outside the cell. A complete signal peptide typically consists of three distinct regions, including a positively charged n-region, a hydrophobic h-region, and a c-region with the cleavage site for signal peptidase (SPase) (Peng et al.
2022
; von Heijne
1998
). When the SP emerges from the ribosome, they are bound by a signal recognition particle (SRP). The SRP is important for targeting the nascent protein to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) membrane. After translocation of the protein to the ER lumen, the SP of the exported protein is cleaved off by the signal peptidase complex (SPC). The mature protein within the ER then undergoes further processing, folding, and modification (Liaci and Förster
2021
). Hence, the N-terminal signal peptides (SPs) are responsible for guiding secretory proteins into the general secretory (Sec) pathway.
Currently, studies on the functions of SPase are predominantly focused on bacteria, yeast, and animals. In bacteria, SPases are classified into three categories: types I, II, and IV (Auclair et al.
2012
). Among them, signal peptide peptidase I (SPase I) cleaves the SP of non-lipoproteins (Musik et al.
2019
). Inhibition of bacterial SPase I function leads to the accumulation of preproteins in the membrane, ultimately resulting in compromised cell growth (Zhbanko et al.
2005
). In
Staphylococcus aureus
, the type I signal peptidase SpsB participates in the removal of the N-terminal leader of the accessory gene regulator (Agr) AgrD which is the autoinducing peptide (AIP) precursor modulating the expression of extracellular virulence factors (Kavanaugh et al.
2007
; Novick and Geisinger
2008
). Bacterial type I signal peptidases can also serve as suitable antibiotic targets because of the difference in structure between humans and bacteria (Smitha Rao and Anné
2011
). In yeast, the signal peptidases complex (SPC) is composed of four components named Spc1, Spc2, Spc3, and Sec11. Among them, Spc3 and Sec11 are essential for signal peptidase activity in the yeast endoplasmic reticulum and are lethal genes (Fang et al.
1997
; Mullins et al.
1996
). While, either Spc1 or Spc2 could be deleted, both of which are important for efficient signal peptidase activity (Fang et al.
1996
; Mullins et al.
1996
). In the model organism
Drosophila
, SPC member Spase12 contributed to development and cell differentiation (Haase Gilbert et al.
2013
). In mammals, the SPC consists of five members including Spc18 and Spc21 (the homologs of yeast Spc1), Spc22/23 (the homologs of yeast Spc3), Spc12 (the homologs of yeast Spc1) and Spc25 (the homologs of yeast Spc2). The SPC18, SPC21, and SPC22/23 proteins were implicated with roles in signal peptidase activity (Shelness et al.
1993
). Importantly, a novel function of the human signal peptidase complex (SPC) on the removal of ER signal peptides acts as a quality control enzyme for membrane proteins was reported (Zanotti et al.
2022
). However, little is known about the function of SPase in filamentous fungi. Thon et al. identify a gene named
CPR1
(the yeast
SPC3
homolog) encoding a putative signal peptidase that is required for pathogenicity of
Colletotrichum graminicola
to both stalks and leaves by using restriction-enzyme mediated insertional (REMI) mutagenesis, but they were unable to generate
CPR1
null mutant indicating CPR1 is an essential gene (Thon et al.
2002
). We previously characterized the SPase FoSpc2 of the soilborne fungal pathogen
Fusarium odoratissimum
causes
Fusarium
wilt of banana. FoSpc2 contributes to fungal growth, conidiation, stress response, and virulence. Interestingly, we also found a relationship between the SPase FoSpc2 and fungal light response (Yang et al.
2023
). Compared to soil-borne plant pathogenic fungi, airborne pathogens need to face more challenges brought by environmental factors, such as severe weather events, the use of chemical pesticides, air pollution, and so on. These situations may require a high secretory capacity for airborne pathogens to cope with environmental challenges. Rice blast is one of the most devastating airborne diseases threatening global food security caused by the fungus
Magnaporthe oryzae
. The contribution of SPase to both the physiology and pathogenesis of the rice blast fungus is unknown.
M. oryzae
secretes a large amount of effector proteins to host cells during early infection to suppress the host immune response and facilitate successful colonization. During the infection stage, effector proteins are mainly delivered extracellularly in two ways: cytoplasmic effectors are efficiently secreted through the exocyst components and rice clathrin-mediated endocytosis facilitates the internalization of cytoplasmic effectors into rice cells, while apoplastic effectors required the conserved ER-Golgi pathway for secretion (Giraldo et al.
2013
; Oliveira-Garcia et al.
2023
). According to the comparative analysis of blast fungal genomes, it was found that among 45 reported effector proteins, 35 (77.8%) possess predicted typical signal peptide sequences and cleavage sites(Gómez Luciano et al.
2019
), indicating that signal peptide processing may play an important role in the secretion of fungal effector proteins.
In this study, the role of the SPC component MoSpc2 in
M. oryzae
was identified and functionally characterized. We found that MoSpc2 is crucial for fungal development and pathogenicity. Deletion of
MoSPC2
caused defects in suppressing host-produced reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation and the activities of extracellular peroxidases and laccases. Importantly, MoSpc2 contributed to protein secretion and is involved in the accumulation and secretion of effector MoSlp1. Moreover, we identified the MoSpc2-interacting proteins by affinity purification and provided the potential interactor of SPC for further study.
Materials and Methods
Strains and Culture Conditions
The
M. oryzae
wild-type (WT) strain Guy11 was obtained from the Fungal Genetics Stock Centre (FGSC 9462) and served as the genetic background for all transformation experiments. The Δ
Mospc2
mutant was constructed in the Guy11 background, and the complemented strain (Δ
Mospc2/MoSPC2
) was subsequently generated based on the Δ
Mospc2
mutant. All strains were cultured on complete medium (CM) agar plates (CM: 10 g D-glucose, 2 g peptone, 1 g yeast extract, 1 g casamino acids, 50 ml 20 × nitrate salts, 1 ml trace elements, 1 ml vitamin solution, 15 g agar, add distilled water to 1 L) at 28˚C in the dark. Liquid CM medium was used to prepare the mycelia for DNA, RNA and protein extraction. The protoplast-mediated transformation of
M. oryzae
was performed for gene deletion and complementation assays by using hygromycin B (250 mg/ml, Calbiochem, La Jolla, CA) and bleomycin (200 mg/mL, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) as a selective marker as described (Sweigard et al.
1992
). Selection of transformants was performed on TB3 agar containing 3 g yeast extract, 3 g casamino acids, 200 g sucrose, and 7.5 g agar per liter, supplemented with respective antibiotics.
The fungal vegetative growth was tested on CM, minimal media (MM, 6 g NaNO
3
, 0.52 g KCl, 0.152 g MgSO
4
·7H
2
O, 1.52 g KH
2
PO
4
, 0.01 g thiamine, 1mL 1000× trace elements, 10 g glucose, and 15 g agar and distilled water to 1 L), rice decoction & corn medium (SDC, 100 g of rice straw, 40 g of corn flour, and 15 g of agar in 1 L of distilled water) and oatmeal agar media (OM: 50 g of oatmeal and 15 g of agar in 1 L of double-distilled water) at 28˚C in the dark for 7 days.
Targeted Gene Deletion and Complementation in
M. oryzae
The entire
MoSPC2
gene was replaced with the hygromycin resistance cassette gene (
HPH
). The upstream and downstream flanking sequences of the
MoSPC2
gene were amplified from
M. oryzae
genomic DNA, and the
HPH
cassette were PCR amplified from pCX62 (annealing temperature: 56 °C; number of cycles: 32). The double-joint PCR approach was used to generate the gene replacement construct for the
MoSPC2
gene which contained the flanking sequences and
HPH
gene for
MoSPC2
were transformed into protoplasts of the WT Guy11 (Yu et al.
2004
). Fungal transformation and genomic DNA isolation were performed as previously described (Talbot et al.
1993
). We obtained 78 transformants, and through PCR verification and Southern blot analysis, one successful gene knockout mutant was identified. For Southern blot analysis, probe labeling, hybridization, and detection were performed with the DIG High Prime DNA Labeling and Detection Starter Kit (Roche Applied Science, Penzberg, Germany). In brief, the genomic DNA of the WT and the Δ
Mospc2
mutant was respectively digested with the restriction enzyme
Nru
I, separated according to size by electrophoresis through a standard agarose gel, and transferred to a nylon membrane by using the capillary transfer method. The membrane was then hybridized with a digoxigenin-labeled gene-specific probe, and signals were detected via chemiluminescence after incubation with an anti-DIG antibody.
The complement fragments, which contain the entire
MoSPC2
gene and their native promoter regions, were amplified by PCR with primers and inserted into pYF11 (bleomycin resistance) to generate MoSpc2-GFP fusion construct which was used to complement the mutant strain. Transformants expressing the construct were identified by PCR and confirmed by fluorescent microscopy. Primers used are shown in Supplemental Table
S1
.
Assays for Conidiation and Appressorium Formation
For sporulation, strain blocks were maintained on SDC agar media at 28˚C for 7 days in the dark followed 3 days of continuous illumination under fluorescent light. Conidia were collected in 5 mL of distilled water. The suspension was then filtered using three-layer lens paper, and the conidia were counted using a hemocytometer under a light microscope. For calcofluor white staining, the conidia were then stained with 10 µg ml
− 1
Calcofluor white for 5 min, de-stained in ddH
2
O for 5 min and observed under Nikon A1 plus confocal microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). Appressorium induction and formation on artificial hydrophobic surface were performed as described previously (Tang et al.
2020
). Appressoria were obtained by holding germinated conidia on a hydrophobic coverslip for 4 h, 8 h, and 16 h, and at least 100 appressorium structures were counted during appressoria formation assays.
Pathogenicity and Plant Infection Assays
Rice and barley are grown in nutrient soil in a greenhouse at 25 °C and 60% humidity. For spray or injected inoculation, conidia of the WT Guy11, Δ
Mospc2
, and the complemented strain Δ
Mospc2/MoSPC2
were harvested and resuspended to a concentration of 5 × 10
4
spores per milliliter in 5 mL of 0.2% (w/v) gelatine solution. A conidial suspension was sprayed onto 2-week-old seedlings of rice (
Oryza sativa
cv. CO39) or injected into the rice sheath. Inoculated plants were kept in a growth chamber at 25 ˚C with 90% humidity and in the dark for the first 24 h, followed by a 12 h/12 h light/dark cycle for 7 days. The infected plants were observed for lesion development at 7 days post-infection (dpi).
For detached plant leaves inoculation, 2-week-old seedlings of rice (
O. sativa
cv. CO39) or 5-day-old barley (
Hordeum vulgare
cv. Golden Promise) leaves were cut by scissors and laid into 15 cm dishes under humid conditions. Leaves were wounded by a syringe needle, if necessary. 10 µL of conidial suspension (5 × 10
4
spores/mL) was repeatedly dropped on detached rice or barley leaves. Lesion development in the infected rice and barley leaves was observed at 7 and 5 dpi, respectively. For microscopic observation of hyphal expansion in plant cells, droplets with the same concentration of conidia were placed onto the epidermis of barley leaves, maintained in the dark. Detached rice sheaths were injected with the same concentration of conidia (5 × 10
4
spores per milliliter), followed by incubation at 28 ˚C for 48 h, and then observed and photographed under a light microscope with a 40x objective lens. Host-derived ROS was detected by staining with DAB (3,3-diaminobenzidine, D-8001, Sigma-Aldrich, United States) as described (Wang et al.
2017
).
Fluorescence Microscopy, Protein Extraction, Mass Spectrometry, and Western Blot
A Nikon A1 plus confocal microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) was used to fluorescence imaging. Total proteins were isolated from vegetative hyphae. Protein extraction was performed as described previously (Yang et al.
2024
). Total proteins isolated from the complemented strain Δ
Mospc2/MoSPC2
and the control strain harboring the empty GFP vector were respectively incubated with 20 µL of anti-GFP magarose beads (GFP-Trap
®
_A kit, ChromoTek, Germany) for 4 h at 4 °C to immunoprecipitate the GFP-fusion proteins. The beads were then washed three times with 500 µL of cold wash buffer (50 mM Tris, 0.15 M NaCl, pH 7.4) using a magnetic frame, and finally resuspended in 100 µL of SDS-loading buffer. Proteins eluted from anti-GFP magarose beads were analyzed by using immunoblotting with anti-GFP antibodies (M20004, Abmart, China), followed by mass spectrometry (BGI company, Beijing, China). The identification of protein gel strips involves separating the sample proteins by gel electrophoresis, then obtaining the protein gel strips at different positions on the film, extracting the peptides after enzymatic digestion, and then using mass spectrometry to obtain the mass spectrum of the proteins in these gel strips, and finally uses the protein identification software to identify the proteins in the samples. The peptides separated by liquid phase chromatography were ionized by a nanoESI source and then passed to a tandem mass spectrometer Q-Exactive HF X (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA) for Data Dependent Acquisition (DDA) mode detection. The main parameters were set: ion source voltage was set to 1.9 kV, MS1 scanning range was 350 ~ 1,500 m/z; resolution was set to 60,000; MS2 starting m/z was fixed at 100; resolution was 15,000. The ion screening conditions for MS2 fragmentation: charge 2 + to 6+, and the top 30 parent ions with the peak intensity exceeding 10,000. The ion fragmentation mode was high-energy collision dissociation (HCD), and the fragment ions were detected in Orbitrap. The dynamic exclusion time was set to 30 s. The automatic gain control (AGC) was set to: MS1 3E6, MS2 1E5.
For Western blot, the proteins separated on SDS-PAGE gels were transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane with a Bio-Rad electroblotting apparatus (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., California, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk blocking buffer and immunoblotted with a primary anti-GFP antibody (M20004, Abmart, China) or anti-MYC antibody (
M20002
, Abmart, China) and a secondary antibody (
M21003
, Abmart, China). The antibody dilutions are 1:5000.
Extraction of Secreted Proteins
Secreted proteins were prepared as described previously with minor modifications (Jung et al.
2012
). In brief, mycelial cultures were grown under shaking conditions (150 rpm, 28 °C) in liquid minimal medium (MM) for 48 h. The culture filtrate was harvested via centrifugation (3500 × g, 10 min), followed by vacuum filtration through membrane filter (0.45 µM) to eliminate the mycelium and any other particles. Fourfold sample volume of pre-chilled (-20 °C) acetone was added to clarified culture filtrates and incubate for 60 min at -20˚C for protein precipitation. The precipitated protein was spun down at 13,000 rpm for 10 min and properly dispose of the supernatant. Protein pellets were dried and resuspended in 1×PBS buffer and stored at -20˚C for the downstream process. Protein gel staining was achieved using Protein Fast Silver Stain Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) following the product manual.

Results
Identification and Characterization of MoSpc2 in
M. oryzae
Using the amino acid (aa) sequence of
Saccharomyces cerevisiae
Spc2 as a query, we identified the locus for MoSpc2 in the
M. oryzae
genome by using basic local alignment search tool of the Fungi and Oomycetes genomics resources database (
https://fungidb.org/fungidb/app/workspace/blast/new
) (Alvarez-Jarreta et al.
2024
). The putative amino acid sequence of MGG_10832 shared 30% identity to
S. cerevisiae
Spc2 and gain the highest score, we designated it as MoSpc2. MoSpc2 is predicted to encode a 232-aa protein with a putative SPC25 domain (Fig.
S1
A). A phylogenetic tree of Spc2 proteins of various origins revealed that MoSpc2 is conserved in other fungi, sharing a 60% aa identity to that of
Fusarium odoratissimum
, 57% to
F. graminearum
, 56% to
Verticillium dahlia
, 47% to
Neurospora crassa
, 46% to
Botrytis cinerea
, 41% to
Aspergillus fumigatus
, and 30% to
S. cerevisiae
(Fig.
S1
B). To gain insight into the potential function of MoSpc2, we analyzed the relative gene expression patterns of the SPC genes including
MoSPC1
,
MoSPC2
,
MoSPC3
, and
MoSEC11
via the published transcriptome profiling of
M. oryzae
and rice during infection (Jeon et al.
2020
). Notably, the transcriptional activity of the
MoSPC2
gene, measured by its FPKM (fragments per kilobase of exon model per million mapped reads) value, was significantly higher during the infection stages (particularly at the early time points of 18 and 27 h post-inoculation in planta) compared to the mycelial stage, suggesting that the component MoSpc2 in the SPC may plays a key role in the pathogenesis of
M. oryzae
(Fig. S2).
Targeted Gene Deletion and Complementation of
MoSPC2
To address the role of the
MoSPC2
gene, constructs for gene replacement were generated and transformed into the wild-type (WT) strain Guy11. The
MoSPC2
null mutant was obtained and confirmed by PCR and Southern blot analysis conducted with gene-specific probe and primers (Fig. S2 and Table
S1

Mospc2
mutant are caused by the loss of
MoSPC2
, complementation transformants were generated by transforming the native promoter-driven
MoSPC2
-GFP fusion construct into the Δ
Mospc2
mutant. At least three complemented strains with GFP signals were generated and recovered all the phenotypic defects, one of which, termed Δ
Mospc2/MoSPC2#3
, was randomly selected for further study.
MoSpc2 Is Crucial for Hyphal Growth, Conidium morphology, and Conidiation

Mospc2

Mospc2/MoSPC2

Mospc2

Mospc2/MoSPC2

M. oryzae

Mospc2

Mospc2

The Loss of Pathogenicity in Δ
Mospc2
Strain Is Caused by Defects in Penetration and Invasive Hyphae Growth
To investigate the reasons for the compromised pathogenicity of the Δ
Mospc2
mutant, the rice leaf sheaths were respectively inoculated with the conidial suspensions of the same set of strains. After incubation with spore suspensions in rice leaf sheaths for 30 h, the WT and complemented strains infected the host cells and expanded freely into neighboring cells, but the Δ
Mospc2
mutant failed to develop any penetration structures at 30 h. We further extended the incubation time and found that the Δ
Mospc2
mutant still cannot penetrate the rice leaf sheath cells at 84 h (Fig.
3
G). This result indicated that MoSpc2 contributed to penetration into the rice cells. Given that deletion of MoSpc2 blocked the development of penetration structures on rice, we further inoculated barley (
Hordeum vulgare
cv. Golden Promise) which is more susceptible to rice blast fungus than rice to assess the role of MoSpc2 on the invasive hyphal growth. Notably, the Δ
Mospc2
mutant can penetrate the barley epidermal cells, and all four types of invasive hyphae were obtained in barley tissues after incubation with spore suspensions for 36–48 h (Fig. S4A). In WT and the complemented strains, approximately 95% of cells displayed type 4 invasive hyphal growth. Nonetheless, less than 20% of cells showed type 4, and 73% showed type 1 and type 2 in the Δ
Mospc2
mutant at 36 h (Fig. S4B). A similar result was observed when four types of invasive hyphal growth were counted at 48 h (Fig. S4C). These results suggested that MoSpc2 is vital for penetration and invasive hyphal growth in the host cells, and these defects in penetration and invasive hyphal growth might be responsible, at least in part, for the impaired pathogenicity.
The Defects in Penetration and Invasive Hyphal Growth in the Δ
Mospc2
Strain Are Due To Impaired Suppression of Host-Produced Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) Accumulation
Plant cells can promptly accumulate abundant reactive oxygen species (ROS) around the infection site at the initial stage of pathogen infection, which is considered as the primary disease resistance response (Liu and Zhang
2022
; Zhang et al.
2020
). To determine why the deletion of
MoSPC2
impaired penetration and invasive hyphal growth, we next tested host ROS production by 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining of rice sheaths following
M. oryzae
infection. DAB staining showed the accumulation of ROS at the infection site of the Δ
Mospc2
mutant, but not the WT and complemented strains after inoculation for 30 h (Fig.
4
A). Fungal extracellular peroxidases and laccases were reported to be important for virulence and inhibition of ROS accumulation from the host cells (Chi et al.
2009
; Liu and Zhang
2022
). To validate whether MoSpc2 is involved in the regulation of peroxidases and laccases activity, we detected the enzyme activity in the extracellular culture filtrate of the WT, Δ
Mospc2
mutant and the complemented strains. Enzyme activity assay using 2, 2’-azino-di-3-ethylbenzthiazoline-6-sulphonate (ABTS) as substrate showed a significant reduction of peroxidases activity in the culture filtrate of the Δ
Mospc2
mutant compared with that of the WT and complemented strains (Fig.
4
B). Moreover, the ABTS oxidation test without H
2
O
2
revealed that the level of laccase activity was also decreased in the culture filtrate of the Δ
Mospc2
mutant compared with that of the WT and complemented strains (Fig.
4
B). These results revealed that MoSpc2 is pivotal for suppressing host-produced reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation and the activities of extracellular peroxidases and laccases.

Mutant’s Failure To Suppress Host-Produced ROS Results from Defects in Protein Secretion
Plant fungal pathogens secrete effector proteins to inhibit host-produced ROS and then promote infection (Lin et al.
2025
; Liu et al.
2021
). Since signal peptidase is responsible for the cleavage of precursor proteins targeted to the rough endoplasmic reticulum (ER) during biosynthesis and secretion (Walker and Lively
2013
), we further investigated the secretome of the WT and Δ
Mospc2
strains by extracting the secreted proteins from culture supernatants. SDS-PAGE analysis of the secreted proteins revealed that several bands were absent in the Δ
Mospc2
strain compared with those of the WT, which indicated an overall reduction in secreted protein levels of the Δ
Mospc2
strain culture supernatants (Fig.
5
A). Mass spectrometric (MS) analysis of these secreted proteins identified a total of 281 proteins. Among them, 60 proteins overlapped in the WT and Δ
Mospc2
strain, while 221 were non-overlapping proteins. And 154 (70%) only existed in the WT but were absent in the Δ
Mospc2
strain, confirming an alteration of secreted protein patterns in the Δ
Mospc2
strain compared with the WT culture supernatants and revealing that these proteins could be MoSpc2-dependent secreted proteins (Fig.
5
B; Table
1
).

Potential fungal effector proteins are defined as small cysteine-rich secreted proteins, < 400 amino acids (aa) and > 4 cysteine residues (Klosterman et al.
2011)
. Based on these features, we did an in-depth analysis of the protein sequence of the 154 WT-specific proteins through online prediction of signal peptides, transmembrane (TM) domains, protein localization and effectorP websites. The result showed that 21 proteins annotated with signal peptides without TM domains were likely to be secreted and predicted to be effector candidates by effectorP program (Sperschneider and Dodds l.
2022
) (Table
1
). Among them, several proteins were commonly identified in many previously characterized secretory proteins and had been reported to be involved in the regulation of pathogenicity of
M. oryzae
and plant defense response. For example, MoSPD11 (MGG_05344) was identified as a suppressor of plant cell death (SPD) effector from
M. oryzae
that was able to block the host cell death reaction induced by Nep1 and is also required for
M. oryzae
virulence (Jeong et al.
2007
; Sharpee et al.
2017
). The effector MoNIS1 (MGG_02347) suppressed both hypersensitive response (HR) cell death triggered by the oomycete PAMP INF1 and ROS generation triggered by flg22 and the fungal PAMP chitin in
Nicotiana benthamiana
, and plays a vital role in host infection (Irieda et al.
2019
). The candidate effector MoCEP6 (MGG_09842) can also suppress flg22-triggered ROS burst in
N. benthamiana
(Liu et al.
2023
). The secreted protein MoCDIP2 (MGG_05531) can induce cell death in rice cells (Chen et al.
2013
). The secreted chitin deacetylates, MoCDA1 (MGG_14966) were found to be dispensable for pathogenicity but crucial for fungal vegetative growth under stress conditions (Sun et al.
2017
). These results collectively revealed that MoSpc2 is involved in the regulation of pathogenicity-related effector protein secretion in
M. oryzae
.
MoSpc2 Is Involved in the Accumulation and Secretion of Effector MoSlp1
In
M. oryzae
, the secretory effector protein MoSlp1 localizes to the extra invasive hyphal membrane (EIHM) during infection and competes with CEBiP in rice for binding of chitin oligosaccharides to suppress rice immune responses (Mentlak et al.
2012
). Given that MoSpc2 is vital for suppressing host-produced ROS accumulation and protein secretion, we determine whether
MoSPC2
disruption affects the accumulation and secretion of MoSlp1. The MoSlp1::6×MYC tag fusion construct was respectively introduced into the WT and Δ
Mospc2
mutant to obtain transformants overexpressing MoSlp1::6×MYC. On western blots of total proteins isolated from the vegetative hyphae of the resulting transformant, three forms of MoSlp1 can be detected with an anti-MYC antibody in both the WT and Δ
Mospc2
mutant transformants overexpressing MoSlp1::6×MYC (Fig.
6
A), as reported previously (Chen et al.
2014
). Nonetheless, the level of each of the three forms was significantly lower in the Δ
Mospc2
transformant than that in the WT transformant (Fig.
6
A and B). This result indicated that MoSpc2 disruption disturbed the accumulation of adequate MoSlp1. To further validate if MoSpc2 affected the secretion of effector MoSlp1 during infection, the
MoSLP1
gene fused with a C-terminal GFP was expressed in the WT Guy11 and the Δ
Mospc2
mutant. The MoSlp1-GFP signal was normally observed in the EIHM of the WT, but a partial MoSlp1-GFP signal appeared to be distributed in the intracellular of the invasive hyphae produced from the Δ
Mospc2
mutant on barley epidermal cells (Fig.
6
C). Collectively, these observations illustrated that MoSpc2 is involved in the accumulation and secretion of effector MoSlp1.

MoSpc2 Localizes To the ER at Different Stages of Fungal Development
To determine the cellular localization of MoSpc2 at different developmental stages, we fused a green fluorescent protein (GFP)-encoding gene at the C-terminal of the MoSpc2 coding sequence using the native promoter and transformed it into the Δ
Mospc2
mutant. Results obtained from microscopy analysis revealed an ER-like perinuclear and cortical network in
M. oryzae
(Fig.
7
). MoLhs1 was reported to be an ER marker protein in
M. oryzae
(Yi et al.
2009
; Zhang et al.
2022
). We then transformed the RFP-targeted MoLhs1 construct (pYF11-MoLhs1-RFP) into the protoplasts of the fluorescent strain expressing MoSpc2-GFP and screened for strains co-expressing MoLhs1-RFP and MoSpc2-GFP. Co-localization between MoSpc2-GFP and MoLhs1-RFP supported our assumption that MoSpc2 associates with the ER network in conidia, appressorium and invasive hyphae of
M. oryzae
(Fig.
7
). These results indicated that MoSpc2 functions in the ER during fungal development of the rice blast fungus.

Identification of MoSpc2-interacting Proteins by Affinity Purification
To determine the interacting proteins of MoSpc2, MoSpc2-GFP was immunoprecipitated from hyphal protein extracts from the Δ
Mospc2
expressing MoSpc2-GFP fusion construct using a GFP-Trap
®
_A kit (ChromoTek, Germany). Following affinity purification, proteins bound to the GFP-Trap beads from three independent biological repeats (Δ
Mospc2-C-1
, Δ
Mospc2-C-2
and Δ
Mospc2-C-3
) were eluted and digested with trypsin and analyzed by mass spectrometry (MS) analysis. The GFP fluorescent strain was used as a background control. After removing the intersection proteins between the MoSpc2-GFP and GFP control, total 63 proteins were identified, and 15 of them including MoSpc2 itself were repeatedly identified as MoSpc2-interacting proteins in the three independent biological repeats (Fig.
8
; Table
2
). The information of these proteins and their homologs in yeast were listed in Table
2
. Importantly, we found that all three of the remaining signal peptidase components (Spc1, Spc3 and Sec11) were identified by coimmunoprecipitation with MoSpc2, indicating the signal peptidase complex is conserved from yeast to the rice blast fungus. Besides, other proteins which have different biological functions were also presented as MoSpc2-interacting proteins (Table
2
). For example, MGG_06979 is orthologous to yeast dynamin-like GTPase Sey1 that mediates homotypic ER fusion (Anwar et al.
2012
); MGG_05203 is orthologous to yeast ran-specific GTPase-activating protein YRB1 involved in nuclear protein import and RNA export, ubiquitin-mediated protein degradation during the cell cycle (Künzler et al.
2000
,
2001
); MGG_03556 and MGG_05999 are orthologous to yeast GAR1 and NHP2 which are protein component of the H/ACA snoRNP pseudouridylase complex involved in 18 S rRNA processing (Girard et al.
1992
; Henras et al.
1998
), and so on. These data indicated that MoSpc2 may be involved in multiple signaling pathways in
M. oryzae
.

Discussion
SPs act like a “tag” that guides the newly synthesized protein to specific locations within or outside the cell. SPase plays a crucial role in processing newly synthesized proteins by cleaving the SP from the nascent polypeptide chain. This cleavage process mediated by the SPC ensures that the protein can function in its mature form. It is critical for the proper function of many cellular processes, including protein secretion and membrane protein insertion. Electron cryo-microscopy and structural proteomics of the human SPC reveal two structural motifs required for SP binding region, including a conserved c-region binding pocket similar to P-type SPases and a specialized transmembrane window (TM window) formed by all subunits, which locally thins the membrane and functions as a molecular ruler that separates SPs from TM segments based on helix length (Liaci et al.
2021
). A recent study identified a previously unknown role of the human SPC as a quality control enzyme for membrane proteins (Zanotti et al.
2022
). In addition, our previous study on SPase of the soilborne fungal pathogen
F. odoratissimum
revealed that the SPase FoSpc2 also contributed to fungal light response (Yang et al.
2023
). These results collectively suggested that SPase, in addition to its conserved role in signal peptide cleavage, also possesses other unknown functions. In this study, we identified and characterized the function of MoSpc2 from the phytopathogenic fungus
M. oryzae
. Our studies indicated that MoSpc2 is crucial for vegetative growth, conidiation, pathogenesis and protein secretion of the rice blast fungus.
The infection cycle of the rice blast fungus critically depends on conidial production and dissemination. The deletion of
MoSPC2
significantly reduced sporulation and caused abnormal conidial morphology (Fig.
2
), indicating that MoSpc2 is critical for the asexual reproduction in
M. oryzae
. The Δ
Mospc2
mutant completely lost pathogenicity and could not be restored even by wound inoculation on rice leaves. However, the complemented strain fully rescued these phenotypic defects. Further investigations revealed that the mutant exhibited delayed appressorium formation and failed to penetrate the rice sheath tissues (Fig.
3
). These findings suggest that MoSpc2 not only regulates the normal development of conidia and appressoria but also governs the invasive growth of
M. oryzae
within host cells. Collectively, these results were similar to our previous study on SPase FoSpc2 in the banana Fusarium wilt pathogen
F. odoratissimum
(Yang et al.
2023
), demonstrating that the homologues of Spc2 in phytopathogens may have conserved effects on fungal growth and pathogenicity.
In yeast, the SPC components Spc3 and Sec11 are indispensable for catalytic activity and cannot be deleted, whereas other components Spc1 and Spc2 are not essential and can be deleted. The structure of the human SPC also revealed that Spc12 (named Spc1 in yeast) is the only subunit that does not immediately interact with SEC11, demonstrating why it was reported to be the least important for SPase activity (Liaci et al.
2021
). We attempted to generate knockout mutants for each of the four SPC components in
M. oryzae
. However, except for the
MoSPC1
and
MoSPC2
, no deletion mutant of
MoSPC3
or
MoSEC11
can be obtained, which is similar to our previous works on
F. odoratissimum
, indicating the SPC has a relatively conserved function across different eukaryotic organisms and highlights the dominant roles of Spc3 and Sec11 on SPase activity. Although Spc2 is not a lethal gene of the SPC, loss of Spc2 in yeast caused a notable reduction of in vitro SPase activity (Antonin et al.
2000
), revealing that Spc2 is required for full enzymatic activity of the SPC in vitro. In this study, the secretory capacity of the
MoSPC2
gene deletion mutant was considerably compromised compared to the WT under axenic conditions. Among the differentially secreted protein profile data of the WT Guy11 and Δ
Mospc2
mutant, we found that about 70% of the proteins were present only in the WT but were absent in the Δ
Mospc2
strain, suggesting that MoSpc2 is involved in the regulation of the protein secretion in
M. oryzae
.
Fungal pathogens secrete numerous effectors into the host cell to disturb the host immune response and promote infection (Zhang and Xu
2014
).
M. oryzae
employ two distinct effector secretion systems to deliver effector proteins into the rice cells. Secretion of cytoplasmic effectors are mediated via the biotrophic interfacial complex (BIC), while the apoplastic effectors are secreted from invasive hyphae (IH) into the extra-invasive hyphal membrane (EIHM) compartment by the conventional fungal ER-Golgi-dependent secretory pathway (Giraldo et al.
2013
).
M. oryzae
Slp1 is an apoplastic effector specifically expressed during infection which can bind to chitin, and is able to suppress chitin-induced plant immune responses, including generation of ROS (Mentlak et al.
2012
). In this study, the Δ
Mospc2
mutant was defective in suppressing host-produced reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation (Fig.
4
A). Given that the secretory capacity of the Δ
Mospc2
mutant was compromised, we then investigated whether loss of MoSpc2 had effects on the functions of MoSlp1, and the results showed that MoSpc2 is involved in the accumulation and secretion of effector MoSlp1 (Fig.
6
). This result is in agreement with previous findings that an ER membrane-bound protein mutant Δ
Mosec62
is impaired in the accumulation of adequate MoSlp1 (Zhou et al.
2016
), and we additionally provided a role for SPase in the effector accumulation and secretion of phytopathogenic fungi.
Conclusion
In summary, we have shown that the signal peptidase complex component MoSpc2 contributes to hyphal growth, asexual reproduction, and pathogenicity of the rice blast fungus. In addition, MoSpc2 localizes to the ER and is implicated in the regulation of protein secretion, the accumulation and secretion of the effector MoSlp1, and host-produced ROS accumulation. Our study indicates the importance of the signal peptidase in the development and pathogenicity of
M. oryzae
, and provides new insights into the role of MoSpc2 in promoting the morphological and infection-related morphogenesis in various fungi. We found that MoSpc2 is an important pathogenicity-related protein in
M. oryzae
, which could serve as a potential drug target for the development of novel fungicides. The relevant findings may also provide valuable insights for research on the signal peptidase complex in other pathogenic fungi. Given these important roles in
M. oryzae
, further investigation of MoSpc2-interacting protein networks is warranted.
