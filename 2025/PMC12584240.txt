Introduction
Japanese encephalitis virus (JEV) is a single-stranded, positive-sense RNA virus belonging to Flaviviridae. JEV is categorized into five genotypes (GI-GV) and is predominantly endemic in the Southeast Asia and Western Pacific regions[
1
]. JEV demonstrates a broad host range, capable of infecting both humans and a diverse array of animals. The virus primarily circulates among pigs, waterfowl, and mosquitoes. More than 30 species of mosquitoes, such as Aedes, Anopheles and Culex pipiens, are potential vectors of JEV, but their vector abilities are different[
2
]. Factors such as species, genotype and even intraspecific variation contribute to the efficiency of virus transmission. For example, Culex pipiens is a particularly effective vector of JEV, while Aedes is a more effective vector of dengue virus. It is mainly transmitted by mosquito vectors, especially Culex tritaeniorhynchus, with humans serving as the primary host[
3
]. These mosquitoes predominantly breed in aquatic habitats such as paddy fields, ponds, swamps, and ditches. Previous studies have confirmed the stability of JEV in mosquito vectors for periods exceeding 14 days[
4
].
Derived from the female Asian tiger mosquito, C6/36 cells are a highly susceptible insect cell line for numerous viruses, particularly Japanese encephalitis virus, Dengue virus, and West Nile virus. This susceptibility makes them a valuable tool for studying various aspects of these viruses, including their life cycle, replication mechanisms, pathogenicity, and the development of antiviral drugs. C6/36 cells have broad applications in virology research, vaccine development, gene editing, drug screening, and disease control efforts. Understanding the interplay between arboviruses (arthropod-borne viruses) and their mosquito vectors is crucial for controlling virus transmission. Diptera (flies and mosquitoes) possess a robust innate immune system, the first line of defense against pathogens. This system comprises diverse mechanisms, including signaling cascade pathways like the Toll, IMD, and Jak/STAT pathways, as well as RNA interference (RNAi) pathways such as the siRNA and PIWI pathways[
5
,
6
]. Label-free proteomic quantification is a new technique for protein quantification that does not rely on isotope labeling. On the contrary, it analyzes proteolytic peptides through liquid-liquid coupling, and compares the signal intensity of peptides in different samples to relatively quantify the corresponding proteins. Compared with the traditional isotope labeling methods, 4D label-free quantitative proteomics has many advantages. It is simpler and faster, and it does not require expensive stable isotope labels. Additionally, label-free proteomic quantification can be used to quantify proteins from a variety of sample types, including tissues, cells, and body fluids[
7
].
JEV is transmitted by mosquitoes, which play a crucial role in the JEV transmission cycle. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying JEV replication in mosquitoes remain largely unknown. This study aimed to investigate the mechanisms of DNA replication, innate immune responses, and metabolism in JEV-infected C6/36 cells using 4D label-free quantitative proteomics approach. By identifying JEV-induced perturbations in DNA replication signaling pathways, this study aims to provide a theoretical basis for understanding the molecular mechanisms of JEV replication in mosquitoes. These findings are expected to inform the development of novel strategies for the prevention and treatment of JEV infection.
Materials and methods
Cell lines and viral strains
JEV strain used in this study was preserved by the Changchun Veterinary Research Institute, CAAS. C6/36 cells were cultured in Minimum Essential Medium (MEM; Cytiva) supplemented with heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco). The cells were incubated at 28 °C in a humidified atmosphere (Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing 5% CO
2
.
Reagents, protein isolation and quantification
UNlQ-10 Column Trizol Total RNA Isolation Kit, qPCR reagents, RTqPCR reagents, and siRNA were purchased from Shanghai Shenggong Bioengineering Technology Service Co., Ltd. Lipofectamine RNAiMAX transfection reagent was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Larval mosquito cells were cultured in 10% MEM medium at 28 °C and 5% CO
2
until confluent. Upon reaching confluence, the cells were inoculated with JEV at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.5 using serum-free MEM medium. After a 2-hour incubation, the medium was replaced with 1% MEM. Cells were harvested at four distinct time points: 0,6,12, and 24 hours post-infection (HPI), using a cell scraper. The harvested cells were lysed in IP lysis buffer and stored at −80°C for subsequent analyses.
For protein extraction, samples were thawed from −80°C storage and supplemented with 1% protease inhibitor. Cell lysates were subjected to ultrasonication and subsequently centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 minutes at 4°C. The supernatant was collected into a fresh centrifuge tube, and protein concentrations were determined using the Bicinchoninic Acid (BCA) assay kit.
Trypsin digestion
Protein samples were prepared for enzymatic digestion by taking equal amounts of protein from each sample and adjusting the volume with lysis buffer. Precipitation was induced by adding one volume of pre-cooled acetone, followed by vortex mixing and the addition of four volumes of pre-cooled acetone. Samples were incubated at −20°C for 2 hours for precipitation. Centrifugation was performed at 4,500 g for 5 minutes, after which the supernatant was discarded. The precipitate was washed twice with pre-cooled acetone. After air-drying the precipitate, it was resuspended in 200 mM Triethylammonium bicarbonate (TEAB) and subjected to ultrasonication for disaggregation. Trypsin was added at a mass-to-mass ratio of 1:50 (protease to protein) for overnight digestion. Dithiothreitol (DTT) was added to a final concentration of 5 mM and incubated for 30 minutes at 56 °C for reduction. Subsequently, iodoacetamide (IAA) was added to a final concentration of 11 mM and incubated for 15 minutes at room temperature in the dark for alkylation.
Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis
Peptides were resuspended in mobile phase A and subjected to separation using a NanoElute Ultra Performance Liquid Chromatography (UPLC) system. Mobile phase A consisted of an aqueous solution with 0.1% formic acid and 2% acetonitrile, while mobile phase B contained 0.1% formic acid in 100% acetonitrile. The liquid chromatography gradient was programmed as follows: 0–70 min, 7%−25% B; 70–82 min, 25%−32% B; 82–86 min, 32%−80% B; and 86–90 min, 80% B. The flow rate was set at 350 nL/min. Peptides were ionized using a capillary ion source and analyzed by mass spectrometry on a timsTOF Pro system. The ion source voltage was set at 1.7 kV. Parent ions and their corresponding fragment ions were detected using high-resolution Time-of-Flight (TOF) analysis. The scanning range for the secondary mass spectra was set between 400 - 1500 m/z. Data acquisition was performed in Parallel Accumulated Serial Fragmentation (PASEF) mode. After each primary mass spectrum was acquired, 10 secondary spectra were collected in PASEF mode with parent ion charges ranging from 0 to 5. Dynamic exclusion time for tandem mass spectrometry scans was set to 30 seconds to prevent redundant acquisition of the same parent ion.
RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)
C6/36 cells infected with JEV were lysed using Trizol reagent. Total RNA was extracted following the manufacturers protocol provided with the Sangong Bioengineering kit. The extracted RNA was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the Moloney Murine Leukemia Virus (MLV) reverse transcription system. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed to assess the expression levels of specific cytokines, employing the fluorescent dye-based detection method. Each experimental condition was replicated three times to ensure statistical validity. The primer sequences are listed in Table
1.
. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: 10 min at 95 ℃, followed by 40 cycles of 95 ℃ for 5 s and 60 ℃ for 1 min. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and gene expression levels are presented relative to those of β-actin.

Gene knockdown using small interfering RNA (siRNA)
According to the instructions of Lipofectamine® RNAiMAX Reagent transfection reagent, siRNAs targeting POLε3 and Prim2 were transfected into C6/36 cells. 30 pmol of siRNA was transfected into C6/36 cells in a 6-well plate. Samples were collected 24 hours after transfection, and nucleic acids were extracted and the interference effect was detected (Table

Bioinformatics, proteomics data analysis methods and statistical analysis
Proteomic data were subjected to Principal Component Analysis (PCA) using R programming language to assess data reproducibility. Further statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8 software. The statistical significance of differences between groups was evaluated using a one-tailed Students t-test. Significance levels were set as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001. All data reported in this study were derived from three independent experiments to ensure robustness and reproducibility. Other proteomics data analysis methods are shown in the Appendix.

Infection of C6/36 cells by JEV
The C6/36 cells were infected with JEV at a gradient of MOI, and the JEV load was detected by qPCR. The results showed that the JEV load increased exponentially at 6, 12, and 24 hours after infection (HPI). Under the condition of 0.5 MOI, the highest JEV load was observed at 24 HPI, and there was a significant difference in the JEV expression at 12 and 24 HPI compared with 6 and 12 HPI, indicating that JEV replication increased significantly over time (Fig.

Discussion
JEV is an arthropod transmitted virus (arbovirus), which mainly circulates between mosquitoes and vertebrate hosts, especially pigs and birds[
8
]. Among the domestic animals in rural areas, cattle, chickens, dogs, ducks, goats, horses and pigs can be infected with JEV, and the serum prevalence of pigs and birds is the highest. Therefore, close contact between humans and animals is the main risk factor for human infection[
9
]. Birds and spiders also play a role in the spread of JEV to new geographic locations[
10
]. In addition to the horizontal transmission of mosquitoes, JEV can also be transmitted to offspring through eggs (vertical transmission). Although viremia can be detected in many vertebrate hosts, including cattle, horses and humans, only pig and bird hosts have high enough viremia levels to infect mosquitoes[
11
]. The Aedes albopictus C6/36 cell line is an important tool for studying arboviruses. Its short doubling time allows for easy amplification, and it is tolerant to a variety of mosquito-borne viruses (members of the Flaviviridae, Togaviridae, and Bunyaviridae families), including Zika, dengue, and chikungunya viruses. Consequently, this cell line is widely used to study the mechanisms of virus-host interactions and develop antiviral strategies. The natural transmission cycle of JEV involves humans, pigs, and mosquitoes. C6/36 cells, widely used in arbovirus research, harbor a defective RNAi pathway due to Dicer mutations, rendering them highly susceptible to mosquito-borne viruses, including JEV[
12
,
13
]. This feature makes C6/36 cells an efficient model for viral isolation and propagation, and they have substantially advanced our understanding of JEV pathogenesis and antiviral strategies[
14
,
15
]. Notably, JEV maintains high titers after at least 10 passages in C6/36 cells, exceeding replication observed in human RD and porcine PS cells (6–8 passages)[
16
,
17
]. Moreover, C6/36 cells appear to impose selective pressures that accelerate viral evolution, mimicking aspects of the natural mosquito environment.
This study utilized 4D label-free quantitative proteomics to systematically analyze protein expression dynamics in C6/36 cells at different time points (0, 6, 12, and 24 HPI) following infection with JEV. This technique offers high throughput, high sensitivity, and high accuracy, enabling the effective detection of low-abundance proteins and making it suitable for studying complex processes involved in JEV infection, such as immune evasion[
18
,
19
]. The results showed that the JEV viral load increased significantly over time, peaking at 24 HPI, indicating efficient viral replication. Using this strategy, we have initially revealed DNA replication, innate immune responses, and related molecular mechanisms during JEV infection, identifying key proteins that may be involved in viral proliferation and immune evasion. These findings not only deepen our understanding of JEV pathogenicity but also provide a theoretical basis for subsequent target screening and drug development. In summary, 4D label-free quantitative proteomics is a powerful tool for analyzing protein functions and cellular processes during viral infection, and is of great value in revealing the mechanisms of viral replication and immune evasion[
20
].
Given their permissiveness and relevance, we applied 4D label-free quantitative proteomics to characterize host processes exploited by JEV in C6/36 cells. A total of 4,829 proteins were quantified, with statistical rigor ensured by Pearson’s correlation, RSD analyses, and fold-change thresholds (≥1.5 or ≤0.67, p < 0.05)[
21
]. This approach identified 159 upregulated and 156 downregulated proteins at 24 HPI. Functional annotation (GO, COG/KOG) and enrichment analyses (GO, KEGG, protein domains) revealed significant involvement of proteins in signal transduction, immune response, metabolism, protein transport, and post-translational modification. These findings provide novel insights into virus–vector interactions and establish a theoretical foundation for mechanistic studies of JEV adaptation in mosquito-like environments. Proteomic analysis revealed that PCNA, a central component of the DNA replication pathway, was significantly downregulated in JEV-infected C6/36 cells. PCNA is a multifunctional protein essential for DNA synthesis, damage bypass, repair, and chromatin assembly, and its versatility reflects evolutionary adaptation[
22
,
23
]. When DNA damage occurs, classical polymerases POLδ and POLε cannot efficiently engage damaged templates, leading to replication fork stalling, collapse, and strand breaks. Traditionally viewed as a replication cofactor, PCNA is now recognized as a hub coordinating numerous protein interactions at the replication fork[
24
–
27
]. Our findings suggest that JEV infection suppresses PCNA, PRI2, and POLε3, disrupting host DNA replication, inducing fork arrest and genomic instability, and ultimately promoting viral replication. By impairing host replication, JEV may create a cellular environment that favors viral propagation, maintaining high viral titers during culture.
In parallel, JEV infection inhibited Toll-like receptor (TLR) expression, further compromising innate immune defenses. TLRs recognize pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and activate downstream adaptors such as MyD88, leading to NF-κB signaling and cytokine induction. Functional studies have linked TLR downregulation with enhanced viral replication, as shown with TLR6 in La Crosse virus infection[
28
,
29
]. In C6/36 cells, innate immunity is mediated through Toll, IMD, and JAK–STAT pathways, which are also disrupted by flaviviruses such as ZIKV and JEV[
30
]. Our proteomic data revealed downregulation of TLR2, 4, 7, and 13, together with MyD88, IκBα, and NF-κBp110, indicating that JEV dampens PAMP recognition and downstream signaling. This suppression weakens antiviral responses, enhancing viral replication and transmission. Together, these results highlight a dual strategy by which JEV impairs both DNA replication and innate immune pathways in C6/36 cells. This not only facilitates viral proliferation but also provides a valuable model for dissecting mechanisms of immune evasion and identifying potential targets for intervention.
This study, through loss-of-function experiments, uncovered a counterintuitive finding: two essential components of the host DNA replication machinery—the catalytic subunit POLε3 of PRI2—act as restriction factors against JEV replication. Knockdown of either gene markedly increased viral RNA accumulation and protein synthesis, suggesting that POLε3 and PRI2 may contribute to the innate antiviral defense of mosquito cells during JEV infection[
31
]. Although the precise mechanisms remain unclear, we propose two potential explanations. (1) Resource competition hypothesis. Both host DNA replication and viral RNA replication are highly energy-demanding processes that require abundant nucleotide precursors, ATP, and translation machinery. While DNA synthesis occurs in the nucleus and viral RNA replication in the cytoplasm, these processes share a common pool of metabolic resources[
32
–
36
]. We speculate that knockdown of POLε3 or PRI2 reduces nuclear DNA replication efficiency, thereby releasing nucleotides, energy, and other critical factors that can be redirected to the viral replication complexes (RCs). This redistribution of resources may indirectly create a more permissive intracellular environment for JEV, leading to enhanced viral production. Conceptually, this resembles “robbing Peter to pay Paul,” where weakening host DNA synthesis fortifies viral RNA replication. (2) Direct modulation of viral replication complexes. Alternatively, POLε3 and PRI2, or their associated protein complexes, might directly interfere with the activity of JEV’s RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp). For instance, they could be recruited to viral replication sites through protein–protein interactions, where they act as molecular barriers to RdRp function[
31
,
37
,
38
]. Depletion of these host factors would remove this inhibitory effect, thereby enhancing viral RNA synthesis. Although DNA and RNA polymerases differ substantially in function, such cross-regulatory interactions between host replication proteins and viral polymerases have been observed in other virus–host systems[
38
].
Our findings align with reports that inhibition of host DNA synthesis can promote the replication of certain RNA viruses. However, they diverge from the prevailing view in mammalian systems, where many viruses exploit or hijack host DNA replication factors to support their own replication. This discrepancy may reflect fundamental evolutionary differences between mosquito vectors and mammalian hosts. In mosquitoes, antiviral defenses may rely less on interferon-driven pathways and more on the fine-tuned allocation of cellular resources and maintenance of basal immunity.
Conclusion
This study is the first to apply 4D label-free quantitative proteomics to systematically investigate the impact of Japanese encephalitis virus (JEV) infection on protein expression in C6/36 cells. JEV significantly suppressed proteins essential for DNA replication (PCNA, PRI2, POLε3) and innate immune signaling (TLRs, MyD88, IκBα, NF-κBp110). Notably, POLε3 and PRI2 were identified as host restriction factors critical for the JEV–mosquito interaction. These findings highlight a viral strategy of antagonizing host DNA replication and immune pathways to enhance replication and transmission. The results provide new insights into flavivirus–host interactions and suggest potential molecular targets for controlling mosquito-borne diseases.
