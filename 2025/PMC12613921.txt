Introduction
CHARGE syndrome (CS) is a complex autosomal dominant disorder that disrupts the development of craniofacial structures, cranial nerves, and multiple organ systems [
1
]. It is estimated that CS occurs in approximately 1 in 15,000 to 17,000 births and significantly impacts survival and long-term quality of life, with a high mortality rate—approximately 30% of affected children do not survive beyond the age of five [
1
,
2
]. CS is characterized by a distinct constellation of clinical manifestations, including ocular coloboma (80–85%), congenital heart defects (60%), choanal atresia (60–65%), growth and/or development retardation (80%), genital and/or urinary anomalies (70–75% in males), and ear anomalies (including deafness and vestibular disorders) (80%) [
3
]. These manifestations underscore the multisystem nature of the disorder, posing significant challenges in its diagnosis and management.
Notably, CS is characterized by nonrandom associations of clinical features and has a diverse clinical presentation, which can lead to a wide range of congenital defects [
4
]. Typically, CS is diagnosed during the neonatal period, primarily due to airway obstruction caused by posterior nasal atresia or stenosis, a hallmark feature of the syndrome. Other critical clinical phenotypes include ocular abnormalities, external ear malformations, and hearing loss, all of which are pivotal for early diagnostic considerations [
2
]. Despite these distinctive features, distinguishing CS from other genetic conditions, such as Kabuki syndrome, 22q11.2 deletion syndrome can be challenging because of overlapping phenotypic traits [
1
]. Such overlaps can lead to misdiagnosis or a missed diagnosis, highlighting the importance of thorough clinical evaluation and genetic testing to ensure accurate differentiation of CS from phenotypically similar syndromes.
An additional key aspect of CS is its strong association with idiopathic hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (IHH), which occurs in 60–80% of CS patients. IHH is further classified into normosmic IHH (nIHH) and Kallmann syndrome (KS) depending on the presence or absence of olfactory dysfunction [
5
,
6
]. Molecularly, the chromodomain helicase DNA-binding protein 7 (CDH7) gene is located on chromosome 8 (region 8q12), spans 188 kb, contains 38 exons and encodes a chromodomain helicase DNA-binding protein essential for chromatin remodeling and gene regulation [
1
,
7
]. The CHD7 gene was the first pathogenic gene associated with CS to be identified and remains the most extensively studied gene. It is also the causative gene shared between CS and IHH. Thus, the triple correlation among the CHD7 gene, CS and IHH has emerged as increasingly significant [
8
]. The closer the overlapping clinical characteristics and genetic commonalities between IHH and CS are, the greater the likelihood that IHH may be either an important complication of CS or allelic disorder with CS [
6
,
9
]. Given these complexities, delayed or incorrect diagnosis - especially problematic in the neonatal period - can potentially hinder early interventions, which is vital for optimizing outcomes.
Few studies have focused on neonates with CS who also exhibit IHH, particularly those born very preterm (< 32 weeks gestation). Additionally, CS patients with polydactyly are often misdiagnosed. Here, we present a case of a very preterm male infant with a de novo CHD7 variant, displaying marked abnormalities of the genitalia (micropenis) and limbs (polydactyly). This uncommon case expands the known genetic and clinical spectrum of CS and enhances neonatologists’ understanding of its association with IHH and its diverse manifestations.
Case presentation
A very preterm male infant was delivered at 28 weeks and 2 days of gestation, with a birth weight of 1.15 kg and Apgar scores of 6, 8, and 9 at 1, 5, and 10 min, respectively. He required prolonged mechanical ventilation due to complications related to prematurity. On admission to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) of the West China Second Hospital of Sichuan University, the infant presented with notable congenital anomalies. A micropenis (penis length less than 1.5 cm, approximately 1.3 cm) was identified (Fig.
1
A), significantly below the expected range for neonates of comparable gestational age [
10
]. No abnormalities were found on palpation of the corpora cavernosa. Additionally, right radial polydactyly was observed through physical examination and orthopantomograms of the hand (Fig.
1
B, C).

During hospitalization, the patient underwent an extensive set of hematological and biochemical tests. Sex hormone tests revealed subnormal levels of testosterone, luteinizing hormone (LH) and follicle stimulating hormone (FSH) (Table S1). On the basis of these clinical characteristics and laboratory data, the infant was initially suspected to have IHH. To further confirm this diagnosis, cranial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed, which revealed no apparent abnormalities in the hypothalamic-pituitary region. We subsequently performed a series of comprehensive diagnostic evaluations. An electroencephalogram revealed convulsive wave activity. Chest radiography demonstrated findings consistent with neonatal respiratory distress syndrome (NRDS) and an enlarged cardiac silhouette (Supplementary Figure A). Echocardiography identified a patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) measuring 2.7 mm in diameter and a slightly narrow descending aortic arch measuring 3 mm, both of which are confirmed by computed tomography angiography (CTA) (Supplementary Figure B-E); An electrocardiogram revealed sinus tachycardia, and suspected abnormalities of both the atria and left ventricular hypertrophy. Urinary ultrasound revealed partial separation of the left renal pelvis (2 mm) (Supplementary Figure F). Bedside funduscopy and otolaryngologic endoscopy revealed no significant abnormalities. The patient’s karyotype was normal male (46, XY) and the results of a newborn screening test and tandem mass spectrometry were normal. In light of the clinical presentation and diagnostic findings, a preliminary diagnosis of IHH was established for this infant (Table S2) [
10
].
Because multiple congenital malformations are generally not associated with IHH, we performed family-based whole-exome sequencing (WES) on this family to investigate the genetic etiology (Fig.
2
A). WES successfully identified a de novo heterozygous splicing variant [c.5405-7G >A (
NM_017780.4
)], which has been previously reported in the literature, and is a popular variant site for research on the CHD7 gene (OMIM: 608892; chr8-:60850486), a known disease-causing gene for both IHH and CS [
11
–
13
]. Then, we chose to utilize Sanger sequencing to validate this candidate variant, precisely confirming the CHD7 variant site (Fig.
2
B). The variant was classified as “Likely Pathogenic” (LP) according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines [
14
],. Thus, to further evaluate the functional impact of the variant, a Mini-gene Assay was conducted to assess the c.5405-7G >A variant in CHD7 on splicing in vitro. The reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) was performed via RNA from blood leukocytes of the proband and his family [used as the wildtype (WT)] to amplify a fragment of CHD7 mRNA, and the products displayed a measurable increase in the molecular weight of variant-CHD7 [679 base pairs (bp)] compared with the normal fragment (674 bp) using electrophoresis (Fig.
2
C). Subsequent analysis of the amplified cDNA showed an abnormal integration of 5 bp bases of intron 25 (CACAG) in the c.5405-7G >A variant clone, resulting in a frameshift and introduction of a premature termination codon (p.Gly1802Alafs*30) compared with the WT sequence (Fig.
2
D, E). All sequences of primers used in this study are presented in Table S3.

As the preterm infant grew, additional facial features consistent with CS became increasingly evident, including external ear malformations (bilateral dysplastic ears, absent earlobes, and triangular concha) (Fig.
1
D, E) and craniofacial abnormalities (square face with a broad forehead, hypertelorism, broad nasal root, collapsed nasal bridge and a short neck) (Fig.
1
F). Ultimately, the patient was revised to be diagnosed with CS according to the most recent diagnostic criteria proposed by Hale et al. [
4
] (Table S4), which integrate the criteria of Blake et al. [
15
], Verloes et al. [
16
] and Bergmann et al. [
17
].
After the neonate’s diagnosis was confirmed, management focused on symptomatic treatment for complications, such as feeding intolerance, gastroesophageal reflux, hemodynamically significant patent ductus arteriosus (hsPDA), and edema. Supportive interventions, including blood transfusions, dobutamine, furosemide, and albumin, help temporarily stabilize the infant’s condition. The hsPDA failed to close with ibuprofen and acetaminophen. At 35 days after birth, the infant underwent surgical interventions consisting of patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) ligation and lateral thoracotomy for aortic coarctation repair; postoperative evaluation revealed persistent patent ductus arteriosus (PFO) and mild pericardial effusion. At two months, the patient developed a high fever, and blood cultures confirmed carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE) sepsis, which was controlled through aggressive antibiotic therapy. Initially, the patient was treated with cefoperazone-sulbactam, and ampicillin. Subsequently, due to changes in the patient’s condition, polymyxin B nebulizer was administered. Finally, the antibiotic regimen was adjusted to include linezolid, along with cefoperazone-sulbactam. Despite comprehensive medical interventions, the infant’s prognosis remained poor. After detailed discussions involving the hospital’s perinatal palliative care team, who provided counseling regarding prognosis, facilitated family decision-making, and ensured comfort care, the family chose to withdraw life-sustaining treatment. The infant died shortly thereafter.
Discussion
We present a unique and complex case of a very preterm infant initially diagnosed with IHH initially, but ultimately confirmed to have CS through the identification of multiple congenital anomalies, clinical examinations and genetic testing. Prominently, this case confirms the similarity of the phenotypic features of IHH and CS, combined with the masking effect of prematurity on classic CS features, significantly complicating the diagnostic process. This finding also highlights that IHH is more likely to serve as a specific phenotype for CS. This is in line with the hypothesis of Balasubramanian et al. [
9
].
Accurate early diagnosis of CS in very preterm infants is inherently challenging. While craniofacial anomalies are key diagnostic markers, the immature facial structures of preterm infants can obscure subtle dysmorphic features, such as underdeveloped external ears, leading to underrecognition of CS. In this patient, the initial presentation of micropenis and minor-features consistent with IHH-misguided the diagnostic focus toward IHH. However, the subsequent emergence of additional dysmorphic features, alongside genetic testing, redirected the diagnosis to CS. This case underscores the diagnostic ambiguity posed by prematurity and emphasizes the importance of being vigilant for CS in addition to considering IHH in premature infants with genital malformations.
Our patient presented with reproductive anomalies, including micropenis and sex hormone abnormalities, which fulfilled the diagnostic criteria for IHH. Available evidence suggests that reproductive anomalies in IHH stem from gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) deficiency, at least in part, CHD7 variants. This is because the CHD7 gene may have an impact on neuroendocrine developmental pathways in GnRH neurons in addition to neurodevelopmental effects [
5
,
18
,
19
]. Therefore, any newborn presenting with reproductive abnormalities, such as micropenis and cryptorchidism should first be evaluated for IHH thorough hormonal testing and via cranial MRI to assess anterior hypothalamic–pituitary function. In our case, the presence of polydactyly—an extremely rare limb anomaly in IHH—coupled with progressively emerging facial features not typically associated with IHH, prompted us to reconsider the diagnosis. Subsequent molecular genetic analyses, including whole-exome sequencing (WES), identified a suspected pathogenic CHD7 variant. This variant was further confirmed as a splicing mutation through Sanger sequencing and a Mini-gene Assay, ultimately shifting the clinical diagnosis toward CS.
To date, seven patients were initially diagnosed with IHH and subsequently confirmed as CS. The shared pathogenic gene variants underlying IHH and CS are thought to account for their overlapping phenotypes (Table
1
). This reasoning was reinforced by the fact that CHD7 variants have been implicated as the primary genetic cause in more than 90% of CS cases and approximately 10% of IHH cases [
1
,
6
,
17
]. In general, patients with CS tend to have nonsense, frameshift and other null variants, whereas IHH patients tend to have missense variants of the CHD7 gene [
18
]. Previous studies have demonstrated that a large number of CHD7 variants and different types of CHD7 variants can result in diverse clinical manifestations. As hypothesized by Jongmans et al. [
12
] and Bergman et al. [
17
], there is an absence of correlation between genotype and phenotype in patients with CS. This finding is analogous to the conclusion we found in the present study. As more CHD7 variants are discovered, studying their effects on GnRH neuron development may reveal the molecular mechanisms behind the phenotypic differences between IHH and CS.

CS is a typically sporadic condition characterized by extensive genetic and phenotypic variability, often involving multiple organ systems (e.g., cardiac, auditory, and endocrine) [
4
]. Genetic variations can be categorized into distinct types, exhibiting both distinctive characteristics and shared traits. For example, genetic similarity and clinical heterogeneity between CS and IHH are shown in these seven cases of CHD7 variants in Table
1
(c.7282 C >T, c.4234T >G, c.2966G >A, c.5210 + 3 A >G, c.115 C >T, c.5405-7G >A, etc.) [
6
,
8
,
20
,
21
]. Table S5 presents a comparison of the clinical features between CHARGE syndrome and IHH. However, no genotype-phenotype associations were found, despite the fact that IHH is a shared phenotype. Currently, this clinical heterogeneity has led to the absence of standardized treatment protocols for CS. Instead, a highly individualized, multidisciplinary approach involving neonatology, endocrinology, cardiology, otolaryngology, and medical genetics is needed. In our patient, supportive therapies, surgical correction of a hemodynamically significant patent ductus arteriosus, and targeted interventions for respiratory failure and infection led to temporary clinical improvement. However, the severity of systemic involvement poses significant therapeutic challenges, highlighting the need for comprehensive, collaborative care to address the multifaceted nature of CS.
Conclusions
This case illustrates the diagnostic and management complexities of CS in a very preterm neonate. Clinically, prematurity can obscure classic CS features, whereas phenotypic overlap with IHH further complicates early diagnosis. Concurrently, the presence of polydactyly in this patient serves to increase the specificity of the clinical presentation of CS patients and this finding thereby strengthens the correlation between polydactyly and the diagnosis of CS, particularly during the early neonatal period. Additionally, the identification of a de novo CHD7 splicing variant emphasizes the utility of advanced molecular diagnostics in guiding clinical decision-making. Further studies of preterm infants with pathogenic CHD7 variants are warranted to explore potential links between prematurity, CHD7 dysfunction, and the phenotypic spectrum of CS. Ultimately, establishing a multidisciplinary, evidence-based framework is essential to optimize outcomes in these medically fragile patients.
