Introduction
The secretory pathway is a fundamental cellular process in which proteins synthesized in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) are transported through the Golgi apparatus and ultimately delivered to the extracellular space. Approximately one-third of all proteins synthesized in the cell are transported via this pathway. The budding of proteins from the ER is mediated by COPII factors at specialized ER subdomains known as ER exit sites (ERES). The COPII machinery is highly conserved from
Saccharomyces cerevisiae
to humans and has traditionally been considered essential for the formation of COPII-coated vesicles. The classical model describes a process in which the small GTPase Sar1 is activated by its guanine nucleotide exchange factor (GEF) Sec12, leading to its recruitment to the ER membrane. Subsequently, the inner coat proteins Sec23/Sec24 and outer coat proteins Sec13/Sec31 assemble to form COPII-coated vesicles, which then transport cargo proteins through the ER-Golgi intermediate compartment (ERGIC) to the Golgi apparatus
1
.
However, recent super-resolution microscopy studies have suggested that ERES in mammalian cells may not always generate discrete vesicles. Instead, a model has been proposed in which tubular structures extend from ERES toward the ERGIC, facilitating cargo transport
2
–
4
. Despite this structural variation, COPII factors remain essential for secretion from ERES, underscoring the importance of elucidating the precise roles of ERES-associated factors in mammalian cells.
Our previous studies demonstrated that the formation of ERES in mammalian cells requires the interaction between TANGO1 and Sec16A
5
. Sec16A functions as a scaffold for COPII factors, recruiting cytoplasmic components to ERES
6
, while TANGO1, which is anchored to the ER membrane, serves as a scaffold for recruiting other ER-localized ERES factors, including cTAGE5 and Sec12
7
–
9
. The interaction between TANGO1 and Sec16A is, therefore, crucial for proper ERES assembly
10
. Furthermore, we previously reported that TANGO1 regulates the disassembly of ERES during mitosis
11
. Specifically, the phosphorylation state of TANGO1 is dynamically regulated throughout the cell cycle, and its phosphorylation during mitosis weakens its interaction with Sec16A, leading to ERES disassembly. CK1δ and CK1ε act as the kinases responsible for TANGO1 phosphorylation, while PP1 functions as the phosphatase that dephosphorylates TANGO1. During mitosis, the reduced activity of PP1 results in a relative increase in TANGO1 phosphorylation, promoting ERES disassembly. However, the regulatory mechanisms controlling the activities of either kinase or phosphatase have remained unclear. In this study, we demonstrate that CK1α at the ER exit sites forms a complex with FAM83A, and that its kinase activity is spatially regulated, allowing phosphorylation of the TANGO1 and Sec16A/Sec13 complex in interphase cells. We also show that PPP1R15B, a regulatory subunit of PP1 localized to the ER membrane, is responsible for dephosphorylating TANGO1, whereas PPP6R3, which is localized in the cytoplasm, functions as a regulatory subunit of PP6 and binds to Sec16A to promote its dephosphorylation. These findings reveal the presence of an autoregulatory mechanism that maintains ERES in a secretion-competent state through the spatially coordinated activities of kinases and phosphatases.
Results
Sec13 is required for Sec16A dimerization and localization to ERES
Previous studies suggested that the interaction between Sec16A and Sec13 is required during the early stages of COPII vesicle formation
12
; however, the details of this process remain unclear. To investigate this further, we first examined the state of Sec16A upon Sec13 expression. As shown in Fig.
1a
top panel, co-expression with Sec13 induced a band shift in Sec16A, indicating a potential modification. Given that several post-translational modifications of Sec16A have been reported, we first examined whether phosphorylation was responsible for this shift. To test this, we performed Phos-tag blot analysis on immunoprecipitated Sec16A fractions. The results showed a marked increase in Sec16A phosphorylation upon Sec13 expression (Fig.
1a
).

Sec16A is a protein of more than 2000 amino acids, and several isoforms have been reported. In this study, we used a 2332 aa construct registered in the database as Sec16A transcript variant X12. A schematic of Sec16A domain organization is shown in Fig.
1b
. Sec16A contains an intrinsically disordered region (IDR) at the N-terminus, which is known to co-phase separate with various ERES components
13
. In addition, Sec16A possesses an ERES localization domain (ELD), which is required for its interaction with TANGO1 and plays a crucial role in Sec16A localization to ERES
5
. Sec16A also contains a central conserved domain (CCD) involved in Sec16A-Sec16A interactions and a C-terminal conserved domain (CTCD) that mediates binding to Sec23
6
. Previous studies have reported that Sec13 interacts with Sec16A in the region between the ELD and CCD domains
12
. To examine whether Sec16A phosphorylation mediated by Sec13 interaction is independent of phase separation, we performed the same analysis using a Sec16A construct lacking the IDR region (Sec16A 1101–2332 aa). The results showed that even when using Sec16A 1101–2332 aa, Sec16A phosphorylation was significantly increased in the presence of Sec13 (Fig.
1c
).
Next, we examined the phosphorylation status of Sec16A in cells with Sec13 knockdown. In stable cell lines expressing 3×FLAG-Sec16A full-length, depletion of Sec13 markedly suppressed Sec16A phosphorylation (Fig.
1d
). This phenomenon was also observed when using FLAG-Sec16A (1101–2332 aa) stable cell lines (Supplementary Fig.
1
).
Sec16A has been reported to form homodimers
14
, and in control knockdown cells, endogenous full-length Sec16A was co-immunoprecipitated with FLAG-Sec16A (1101–2332 aa) (Fig.
1e
). However, in Sec13-depleted cells, the amount of endogenous Sec16A co-precipitated with FLAG-Sec16A (1101–2332 aa) was markedly reduced. These findings strongly suggest that Sec13 is not only required for Sec16A phosphorylation but also plays a crucial role in Sec16A homodimerization. Next, we examined the localization of Sec16A and TANGO1 in Sec13-depleted cells. As shown in Fig.
1f
, in cells with Sec13 knockdown, TANGO1 localization was confined to a juxtanuclear region, where it co-localized with Sec16A, suggesting that ERES were restricted to the juxtanuclear area. Furthermore, Sec16A was also observed to localize at sites other than TANGO1-positive ERES, showing a dispersed distribution. Indeed, quantification of the co-localization ratio of TANGO1 and Sec16A revealed that suppression of Sec13 expression resulted in a decrease in their co-localization ratio (Fig.
1g
). These findings strongly suggest that Sec13 is essential for Sec16A phosphorylation, dimerization, and localization to ERES.
CK1α phosphorylates Sec16A in a Sec13-dependent manner
To identify kinases that phosphorylate Sec16A in a Sec13-dependent manner, we screened for factors interacting with Sec16A. Specifically, we performed mass spectrometry analysis on proteins present in Sec16A immunoprecipitates from HeLa cells using an anti-Sec16A antibody, as well as FLAG immunoprecipitates from a stable 3x FLAG-Sec16A full-length cell line. Additionally, we conducted a TurboID proximity labeling assay for Sec13, enriched biotinylated proteins using streptavidin beads, and identified them by mass spectrometry. We further supplemented our dataset with reported Sec16A interactors from the BIOGRID database and generated a Venn diagram integrating these four datasets (Fig.
2a
). Among the 12 proteins identified across all datasets, we detected Sec23A, Sec23B, all four isoforms of Sec24, and Sec13, confirming the reliability of our screening approach. Notably, CSNK1A1(CK1α) was the only kinase present in this core set. In addition to CK1α, we selected 38 candidates based on their occurrence across multiple screening methods or their relevance to our kinase cDNA library. To assess their potential involvement in Sec16A phosphorylation, we co-expressed each candidate with Sec16A (1101–1890 aa) in 293 T cells and analyzed Sec16A phosphorylation status using Phos-tag. As a result, CK1α, which received the highest score in Fig.
2a
, was found to strongly enhance Sec16A (1101–1890 aa) phosphorylation (Supplementary Fig.
2a
). Moreover, as shown in Fig.
2b
, phosphorylation of full-length Sec16A was strongest when CK1α was co-expressed with Sec13. A similar trend was observed when analyzing the phosphorylation of Sec16A (1101–1890 aa) (Supplementary Fig.
2b
). Interestingly, the FLAG blot showed a clear upward band shift that correlated with enhanced phosphorylation upon co-expression of CK1α and Sec13. This mobility shift was more pronounced when the shorter Sec16A fragment (1101–1890 aa) was used compared with full-length Sec16A, likely because the phosphorylation-induced change has a greater impact relative to the overall molecular weight of the truncated protein (Fig.
2b
; Supplementary Fig.
2b
).

To further validate these observations, we performed an in vitro kinase assay using 3×FLAG-Sec16A immunoprecipitates from stable cell lines. In cells treated with control siRNAs, Sec16A was efficiently phosphorylated, whereas phosphorylation was markedly reduced in immunoprecipitates from CK1α-depleted cells (Fig.
2c
). A similar reduction in phosphorylation was observed when the assay was performed using FLAG-Sec16A (1101–2332 aa) stable cell lines (Supplementary Fig.
2c
).
Taken together, these results demonstrate that CK1α functions as a kinase that phosphorylates Sec16A. To further investigate the interaction sites of CK1α and Sec13 on Sec16A, we mapped the CK1α-binding region with various Sec16A deletion constructs (Fig.
2d
). These results revealed that the CK1α-binding region of Sec16A is largely confined to the ELD, while the Sec13-binding region is located between the ELD and CCD, consistent with previous reports (Fig.
2e
). Furthermore, the phosphorylation sites on Sec16A were found to be mainly concentrated within the ELD region (Fig.
2e
). We previously identified CK1δ and CK1ε as kinases that phosphorylate TANGO1 during mitosis, leading to a weakened interaction between TANGO1 and Sec16A
11
. To determine whether CK1δ and CK1ε also contribute to Sec13-dependent Sec16A phosphorylation, we next examined their effects. As shown in Fig.
2f
, CK1α expression significantly increased Sec16A (1101–1890 aa) phosphorylation, whereas CK1δ and CK1ε had a minor effect. Furthermore, when CK1α was co-expressed with Sec13, Sec16A (1101–1890 aa) phosphorylation was further enhanced. In contrast, co-expression of CK1δ or CK1ε with Sec13 did not significantly increase Sec16A (1101–1890 aa) phosphorylation beyond the level observed with Sec13 alone (Fig.
2f
). These findings indicate that CK1α, rather than CK1δ or CK1ε, functions as the kinase responsible for Sec16A phosphorylation in a Sec13-dependent manner.
CK1-mediated Sec16A phosphorylation enhances its dynamics and facilitates efficient ER export
To identify the phosphorylation sites on Sec16A modified by CK1α in the presence of Sec13, we performed phosphorylation proteomics analysis on Sec16A (1101–1890 aa) phosphorylated under these conditions (Fig.
3a
). This analysis revealed that 14 serine and threonine residues were potential phosphorylation sites. To further validate their functional significance, we generated non-phosphorylatable SA mutants, in which the identified phosphorylation sites were substituted with alanine, and performed an in vitro kinase assay. FLAG-Sec16A (1101–2332 aa) stable cell lines were also generated for the SA mutant, and FLAG immunoprecipitates from these cells were subjected to an in vitro kinase assay. As shown in Fig.
3b
, while phosphorylation was detected in cells expressing wild-type Sec16A (1101–2332 aa), it was markedly suppressed in the SA mutant (1101–2332 aa). These results allowed us to identify specific phosphorylation sites on Sec16A.

To investigate the impact of Sec16A phosphorylation on its localization, we generated GFP-Sec16A full-length stable cell lines expressing wild-type (WT), SA mutant, and phosphomimetic SE mutant and analyzed their localization. All Sec16A full-length variants colocalized with TANGO1, indicating that Sec16A localizes to ERES regardless of phosphorylation status (Supplementary Fig.
3
).
Next, to determine whether Sec16A phosphorylation affects its turnover at ERES, we performed FRAP analysis. Representative FRAP traces, showing the recovery of fluorescence signal at individual ERES over time, are presented in Fig.
3c
, and the calculated initial rate, t1/2, and plateau level are summarized in Table

These results indicate that in the absence of phosphorylation, the immobile fraction of Sec16A increases, suggesting that Sec16A phosphorylation enhances its turnover on the ERES membrane. The comparable FRAP recovery rates between the WT and SE mutants further imply that Sec16A undergoes efficient phosphorylation under steady-state conditions.

We next examined whether Sec16A phosphorylation affects secretion from ERES. To assess this, we evaluated ManII transport using a RUSH assay in stable cell lines expressing either WT or SA mutant 3×FLAG-Sec16A full-length. As shown in Fig.
3e

3f

To determine whether the effects of Sec16A phosphorylation on turnover and secretion are directly mediated by CK1α, we measured Sec16A turnover using FRAP in CK1α-depleted cells. Compared with control knockdown, CK1α knockdown caused a reduced initial rate, prolonged t1/2, and decreased plateau level (Fig.
3g, h
; Table
2
), indicating an increased immobile fraction and impaired Sec16A turnover. A similar reduction was observed when cells were treated with the CK1 inhibitor D4476; however, unlike CK1α depletion, D4476 treatment did not prolong t1/2. This is likely because the 4 h inhibitor treatment does not mimic the prolonged effects of CK1α depletion on Sec16A. Nevertheless, D4476 treatment decreased both the initial rate and plateau level, indicating an increase in the immobile fraction and supporting the conclusion that CK1α-mediated phosphorylation is essential for Sec16A turnover at ERES (Supplementary Fig.
4a–c

FAM83A acts as a scaffold for CK1α-mediated phosphorylation of Sec16A
CK1α is known to regulate various cellular functions, including membrane trafficking, cell cycle progression, apoptosis, autophagy, and circadian rhythm, and has numerous identified substrates. Although CK1α is constitutively active in biochemical assays, the mechanisms governing its activity regulation remain largely unknown
15
. Recent studies have reported that FAM83 family proteins interact with CK1 through their DUF1669 domain, contributing to its localization to various intracellular compartments
16
,
17
. Based on this, we sought to identify a FAM83 protein involved in Sec16A phosphorylation by examining the intracellular localization of CK1α in the presence of different FAM83 family members. We co-expressed FAM83A, FAM83D, FAM83G, and FAM83H with CK1α in cells and analyzed their localization (Fig.
4a
). Consistent with previous reports, CK1α exhibited strong colocalization with all tested FAM83 proteins, localizing to various intracellular compartments (Fig.
4a
). When compared with Sec16A localization, however, only FAM83A-expressing cells exhibited partial colocalization of FAM83A and CK1α with Sec16A (Fig.
4a
). Given that kinase-substrate interactions are often transient and weaken upon phosphorylation, we examined the colocalization of Sec16A with a kinase-dead CK1α mutant (CK1α K46A) in the presence of FAM83A. As shown in Fig.
4b
, when CK1α K46A was expressed alone, it localized to the nucleus, as previously reported
18
. In contrast, when co-expressed with FAM83A, CK1α and FAM83A strongly colocalized with Sec16A. In contrast, FAM83D, FAM83G and FAM83H failed to colocalize with Sec16A when co-expressed with CK1α K46A (Supplementary Fig.
6
). These results suggest that FAM83A may be involved in Sec16A phosphorylation. To further investigate the interactions among Sec16A, CK1α, and FAM83A, we performed Sec16A immunoprecipitation in cells expressing CK1α K46A and FAM83A, either individually or together. The results revealed that FAM83A binds to Sec16A, and the amount of CK1α K46A associated with Sec16A increased in the presence of FAM83A (Fig.
4c
, lanes 3 and 4). Next, we mapped the FAM83A binding region on Sec16A and found that, similar to CK1α, FAM83A binds to the ELD domain of Sec16A (Fig.
4d
). These findings suggest that CK1α interacts with Sec16A through FAM83A. To further examine the effect of CK1α on the FAM83A-Sec16A interaction, we performed FAM83A immunoprecipitation in cells co-expressing FAM83A, Sec16A WT (1101–2332 aa) or SA mutant (1101–2332 aa), and either wild-type or kinase-dead CK1α. The results showed that the interaction between Sec16A WT (1101–2332 aa) and FAM83A was significantly reduced in the presence of wild-type CK1α, but not in the presence of kinase-dead CK1α (Fig.
4e
). Furthermore, when using the Sec16A SA mutant (1101–2332 aa), which cannot be phosphorylated by CK1α, the addition of wild-type CK1α did not significantly reduce the interaction between FAM83A and Sec16A (Fig.
4e
). These findings indicate that phosphorylation of Sec16A by CK1α weakens its interaction with FAM83A. Therefore, FAM83A likely functions as a scaffold that facilitates CK1α-mediated phosphorylation of Sec16A.

Excessive phosphorylation of Sec16A by CK1α leads to the disassembly of ERES
To investigate the effects of FAM83A and CK1α on ERES organization, we analyzed the localization of various ERES components in cells co-expressing both proteins. The results showed that expression of FAM83A and CK1α caused dispersion of cytoplasmic ERES factors; however, Sec16A, Sec23, and Sec31 also tended to accumulate in a perinuclear region. In contrast, membrane-associated ERES proteins such as Sec12 and TANGO1 were distributed throughout the ER (Fig.
4f
). To determine whether this dispersion of ERES components is caused by CK1α-mediated phosphorylation of Sec16A, we examined the effect of FAM83A and CK1α co-expression in cells expressing either wild-type Sec16A full-length or the Sec16A full-length SA mutant. As shown in Fig.
4g
, co-expression of FAM83A and CK1α in cells expressing wild-type Sec16A full-length resulted in the dissociation of Sec16A and TANGO1, consistent with previous observations. However, in cells expressing the Sec16A full-length SA mutant, not only Sec16A and TANGO1 but also FAM83A and CK1α remained co-localized at ERES, and ERES disassembly was suppressed (Fig.
4g
). These findings suggest that while moderate phosphorylation of Sec16A by CK1α is required to promote ERES turnover and support secretion, excessive phosphorylation disrupts ERES integrity. Thus, a balanced level of CK1α-mediated phosphorylation of Sec16A appears to be critical for maintaining functional ERES (further discussed in the Discussion section).
Proper phosphorylation of TANGO1 is critical for ERES function and efficient ER export
We previously reported that during mitosis, TANGO1 is phosphorylated by CK1δ and CK1ε, isoforms of CK1, and that this phosphorylation disrupts the interaction between TANGO1 and Sec16A, leading to ERES disassembly
11
. Here, we investigated whether CK1α also contributes to TANGO1 phosphorylation during mitosis. In cells with CK1δ and CK1ε knockdown, the co-localization of Sec16A and Sec31 during mitosis was significantly higher than in control knockdown cells, indicating partial inhibition of ERES disassembly. A similar phenotype was observed in CK1α knockdown cells (Supplementary Fig.
7
), indicating that CK1α also contributes to ERES disassembly during mitosis.　Based on this, we hypothesized that the FAM83A–CK1α complex may also be involved in the phosphorylation of TANGO1, in addition to Sec16A. To test this, we examined the effect of co-expression of FAM83A and CK1α on TANGO1 phosphorylation. TANGO1 exists as two isoforms: TANGO1L, which contains a long luminal domain that recognizes collagen, and TANGO1S, which primarily consists of the cytoplasmic domain
7
. The cytoplasmic domains of TANGO1L and TANGO1S are shared, and based on our findings that TANGO1L and TANGO1S functions in ER exit site organization are redundant
5

5a

The ER-localized PP1 regulatory subunit PPP1R15B is involved in the dephosphorylation of TANGO1
While this study has provided insights into the phosphorylation-dependent regulation of TANGO1 and Sec16A, the mechanism by which PP1 mediates TANGO1 dephosphorylation remains unclear. Discussion
We previously demonstrated that the interaction between TANGO1 and Sec16A is crucial for the formation of ERES
5
. In the present study, we identified CK1α as a kinase responsible for phosphorylating Sec16A and showed that this phosphorylation is regulated by FAM83A. Since the interaction between FAM83A and Sec16A is weakened upon Sec16A phosphorylation, this interaction likely functions as a negative feedback loop to prevent excessive Sec16A phosphorylation. Furthermore, we found that the phosphorylation of TANGO1 by FAM83A/CK1α requires the presence of Sec16A, suggesting that once TANGO1 becomes hyperphosphorylated and dissociates from Sec16A, this phosphorylation process is terminated. Thus, the phosphorylation of both TANGO1 and Sec16A by FAM83A/CK1α is finely tuned to avoid excessive modification (Fig.
7a–c
).
Fig. 7
Model of ER exit site autoregulation.
a
Low phosphorylation state. The FAM83A/CK1α complex binds to Sec16A and phosphorylates it to initiate functional ERES formation. Once Sec16A is phosphorylated, FAM83A dissociates. TANGO1 is also phosphorylated by FAM83A/CK1α, but this requires Sec16A to be associated with TANGO1.
b
Moderate phosphorylation state. Functional ERES are maintained by keeping TANGO1 and Sec16A in a moderately phosphorylated state.
c
High phosphorylation state. Phosphorylated TANGO1 is dephosphorylated by the ER-localized PPP1R15B/PPP1C complex and returns to the ERES. Sec16A is dephosphorylated by the cytosolic PPP6R3/PPP6C complex.
Previously, we reported that hyperphosphorylated TANGO1 diffuses throughout the ER membrane
11
. In this study, we demonstrate that the phosphatase PPP1R15B/PPP1C, localized to the ER reticular membrane, can dephosphorylate TANGO1, enabling it to return to the ERES. Similarly, when Sec16A becomes hyperphosphorylated, it is dephosphorylated by PPP6R3/PPP6C, which are cytoplasmically localized. The dephosphorylated Sec16A can then re-bind to FAM83A and re-enter the phosphorylation cycle mediated by CK1α (Fig.
7
). Together, these observations support a model in which ERES in interphase cells are maintained in a secretion-competent state via an autoregulatory mechanism involving a dynamic balance between phosphorylation by FAM83A/CK1α and dephosphorylation by PP1 and PP6, without requiring external stimuli (Fig.
7
).
Importantly, we found that either excessive Sec16A phosphorylation promotes ERES disassembly or excessive Sec16A dephosphorylation disrupts secretion. Similarly, while TANGO1 hyperphosphorylation promotes its diffusion from ERES, its excessive dephosphorylation suppresses Sec16A turnover and delays secretion. Therefore, maintaining the phosphorylation balance of TANGO1 and Sec16A via autoregulation is critical for preserving ERES functionality (Fig.
7
).
In this study, we initially isolated CK1α as a kinase activity dependent on Sec13. This finding suggests that the formation of a complex between Sec16A and Sec13 is important for proper secretion. In contrast to this stable complex, the interaction between TANGO1 and Sec16A appears to be more transient. In wild-type ER exit sites where TANGO1 and Sec16A interact, FRAP analysis revealed that the turnover rate of Sec16A is comparable to that of the phospho-mutant Sec16A, indicating that Sec16A is rapidly exchanged. Therefore, rather than forming a stable complex, TANGO1 and Sec16A likely maintain their interaction in a dynamic, continuously exchanging manner.
DYRK3, previously identified as a kinase that phosphorylates Sec16A, is thought to regulate secretion via the phase separation of Sec16A
13
. Among the 14 phosphorylation sites we identified as CK1α targets on Sec16A, five residues—within the range we could verify—overlap with sites phosphorylated by DYRK3. Additionally, Sec16A is known to exist in multiple transcript variants, and recent findings suggest that alternative splicing of Sec16A may influence COPII transport efficiency
21
. In our study, we used the full-length transcript variant X12 of Sec16A (6996 bp). In contrast, the DYRK3-related study employed an isoform initially cloned as “Sec16AL,” as full-length, which corresponds to various C-terminal fragments found in multiple transcript variants (covering the sequence from amino acid 179 onward in transcript variant X12)
14
. Whether our findings and the phase separation regulated by DYRK3 are influenced by the specific transcript variant of Sec16A remains an intriguing question for future investigation. Although our results highlight FAM83A as a regulator of CK1α-mediated phosphorylation of Sec16A, this regulation may still be compatible with a phase separation-based mechanism. Whether phase separation contributes to the newly proposed autoregulatory model or whether DYRK3, a kinase specialized for phase separation, is controlled by specific upstream stimuli remains an important question. It is also possible that DYRK3 activity is integrated into the FAM83A–CK1α, PP1, and PP6 autoregulatory axis, warranting further investigation.
We identified PP6 as a phosphatase responsible for dephosphorylating Sec16A. PP6 was previously implicated in the dephosphorylation of COPII components in budding yeast and is known to interact with CK1δ in mammalian cells to regulate ER-to-Golgi transport
22
. Our identification of PP6 as a Sec16A phosphatase in mammalian cells suggests that PP6 may also dephosphorylate other COPII components localized at ERES. Interestingly, PPP6R3/PPP6C does not dephosphorylate TANGO1, while PPP1R15B/PPP1C dephosphorylates TANGO1 but not Sec16A, suggesting that even among ERES-localized proteins, distinct phosphatases may be utilized depending on whether the substrate is membrane-anchored or cytoplasmic. In the aforementioned DYRK3 study, PP1 was suggested as a candidate for Sec16A phosphatase
13
. However, the inhibitors used in that study, such as okadaic acid and calyculin A, also inhibit PP6
23
. Moreover, the observed reduction in GFP-Sec16A turnover upon PPP1CC overexpression may reflect PPP1CC targeting TANGO1 phosphorylation, similar to what we observed in Fig.
5d
. It will be important to clarify whether the phosphatases involved in Sec16A dephosphorylation differ depending on the upstream kinase, whether PP1 and PP6 function cooperatively, or whether only PP6 is involved. To date, three regulatory subunits of PP6 have been identified. Determining whether these are used selectively and how ANKRD family proteins, known to associate with PP6 complexes, contribute to specificity will be an important direction for future research
24
. In addition, we found that not only CK1δ and CK1ε, but also CK1α, contributes to TANGO1 phosphorylation during mitosis (Supplementary Fig.
7
). The potential contribution of FAM83 family members to mitotic TANGO1 phosphorylation, as well as the involvement of phosphatase regulatory subunits in re-establishing ERES after mitotic exit, represent intriguing directions for future research.
Sec16A has previously been shown to act as a sensor that transmits extracellular environmental signals, such as growth cues and nutrient deprivation, to the secretory machinery via phosphorylation and other modifications
25
–
27
. However, the regulation of Sec16A and TANGO1 phosphorylation revealed in this study is distinct from such stimulus-driven regulation. Instead, it is an autoregulatory mechanism that operates constitutively under steady-state conditions. The question of why cells maintain a system that continuously consumes energy to preserve an intermediate phosphorylation state of TANGO1 and Sec16A is intriguing. One possibility is that this system enables rapid responses to environmental changes or cargo load variations by keeping components primed for either increased phosphorylation or enhanced dephosphorylation. In this study, we elucidated an autoregulatory mechanism that maintains optimal phosphorylation of TANGO1 and Sec16A, thereby creating a secretion-competent ERES during interphase. Future studies should address how this secretory environment adapts to various external signals, stresses, and changes in cargo levels.

RUSH assay
Stable HeLa cells expressing either Str-KDEL_ManII-SBP-mCherry or Str-KDEL_SBP-EGFP-GPI under the control of a doxycycline-inducible promoter were seeded onto coverslips. The cells were then treated with doxycycline (8 ng/ml for Str-KDEL_ManII-SBP-mCherry, 40 ng/ml for Str-KDEL_SBP-EGFP-GPI). After 24 h, biotin was added to the cell culture medium at a final concentration of 40 µM. After 20 min, the cells were fixed with methanol and stained with an anti-GM130 antibody for immunofluorescence. Images were acquired using confocal laser scanning microscopy (LSM 900; Carl Zeiss). Quantification of the percentage of ManII-mCherry or EGFP-GPI signals in the GM130-positive region was then quantified among all ManII-mCherry or EGFP-GPI signals was performed using Fiji-ImageJ. The number of quantified cells (n) is indicated in the Figure.
FRAP assay
Cells expressing GFP-Sec16A full length or GFP-Sec16A full length SA mutant or GFP-Sec16A full length SE mutant were seeded onto 3.5-cm glass-bottom dishes (Matsunami) and induced with doxycycline 24 hours prior to imaging. Before the assay, the culture medium was replaced with phenol red-free DMEM (Nacalai Tesque). FRAP experiments were performed using confocal laser scanning microscopy (Plan Apochromat 63×/1.40 NA oil immersion objective lens; LSM 980; Carl Zeiss) equipped with an incubation chamber (Tokai Hit, Shizuoka, Japan) with the following parameters: temperature, 37 °C; humidity, 100%; and CO
2
concentration, 5%. Images were acquired and processed with Zen Blue software (RRID:SCR_013672; Carl Zeiss). A circular region of interest (ROI) with a diameter of 2.15 µm was selected and photobleaching was performed using a 488 nm laser at 60% power for 0.51 second. Time-lapse imaging was conducted at a rate of one frame every 0.6304 seconds for a total of 100 frames. Fluorescence intensity within the bleached ROI was quantified using Zen Blue software and normalized to pre-bleach and background intensities. To correct for overall fluorescence decay during image acquisition, fluorescence intensity from a non-bleached ROI was used as a reference for normalization. FRAP recovery curves were analyzed using GraphPad Prism software. Normalized fluorescence intensity values were fitted to a single exponential model using the One phase association function. The fitted parameters included the plateau value and the rate constant (k), from which the half-time of recovery (t1/2) was calculated as ln(2)/k. The initial rate of recovery was determined as the product of the plateau and k.

Mitotic assay
HeLa cells treated with siRNAs were incubated for 22 h and then treated with 2.5 mM thymidine for 16 h. The cells were washed three times with PBS and released for 8 h. Then, the cells were incubated further with 2.5 mM thymidine for 16 h, washed three times with PBS and released for 10 h before fixation. Stained cells were analyzed by confocal laser scanning microscopy (Plan Apochromat 63×/1.40 NA oil immersion objective lens; LSM 900; Carl Zeiss) and processed with Zen software (Carl Zeiss). Intensity scanning and calculating coefficiency were performed by colocalization plugin Coloc2 in Fiji-ImageJ
