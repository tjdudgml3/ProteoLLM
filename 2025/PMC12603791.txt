BACKGROUND
Two pathological hallmarks of Alzheimer's disease (AD) are the accumulation of extracellular amyloid beta (Aβ) deposits and soluble species and paired helical filament tangles composed of hyperphosphorylated tau inside neurons.
1
The two pathologies are strongly related.
2
The current amyloid hypothesis
3
,
4

Multiple proteins are known to bind to different forms of Aβ through prior non‐human studies, including apolipoprotein E (APOE), low‐density lipoprotein receptor‐related protein 1 (LRP1), α7‐nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (α7nAChR), and prion protein (PrP

However, caveats are in order when it comes to generalizing animal studies’ findings to humans. For example, the use of overproduction of Aβ in animal models does not mirror human sporadic AD or the difference between animal models and human Aβ species. Therefore, to determine relevance to AD, potential Aβ receptors should be validated using human data to complement preclinical models.
Here, we examined whether these putative Aβ binding partners could be identified in the human brain and whether they mediated the relationship between Aβ and tau tangles. We used
post mortem
pathological and proteomic data from the brains of participants in one of two longitudinal community‐based clinical pathological studies of aging, the Religious Orders Study and the Rush Memory and Aging Project, collectively known as ROSMAP.

METHODS
ROS recruitment began in January 1994 and MAP in September 1997; both are ongoing. The ROS participants are older clergy men and women who join the study across the United States. The MAP participants are seniors living in the community (retirement facilities and personal accommodations) who join the study across the greater Chicago metropolitan area. In both studies, participants are without known dementia at enrollment and consent to annual clinical examination and brain donation at the time of death. The two cohorts share many identical study protocols and collection data instruments, implemented by the same personnel of the Rush Alzheimer's Disease Center, facilitating joint analyses. More information about the studies is provided elsewhere.
18
An Institutional Review Board of Rush University Medical Center approved the studies.
Here, we used
post mortem
neuropathological and brain proteomic data. Through March 2024, 2393 ROSMAP participants had died, of whom 2054 underwent autopsy. The study analytic sample included 850 participants who had data for both pathology and brain proteomics.
2.1
Post mortem
pathological assessment
The median
post mortem
interval was 6.5 h (interquartile range [IQR]: 4.9 to 8.8). During the autopsy, the brain was removed, and the brain hemispheres were separated. One hemisphere was frozen for omics study, and the other hemisphere was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. The fixed hemisphere was used for pathological assessment of AD.
2.1.1
Aβ load
Monoclonal antibodies specific for Aβ were used for quantifying Aβ deposits in the brain using immunohistochemistry methods. The antibodies were 4G8 dilution 1:9000 (Covance Labs, Madison, WI, USA), 6F/3D dilution 1:50 (Dako North America Inc., Carpinteria, CA, USA), and 10D5 dilution 1:600 (Elan Pharmaceuticals, San Francisco, CA, USA). The stained sections were prepared from eight brain regions: anterior cingulate cortex, superior frontal cortex, midfrontal cortex, inferior temporal cortex, hippocampus, entorhinal cortex, angular gyrus/supramarginal cortex, and calcarine cortex. To quantify Aβ load, the stained sections were digitally scanned using the Aperio Image Analysis Toolbox Positive Pixel Count algorithm to calculate the percentage of the area occupied by the immunohistochemical staining. The calculated percentages were averaged within each brain region and then across the eight brain regions to yield the overall Aβ load.
19
2.1.2
Tau tangle density
Sections were prepared from the same eight brain regions used for quantifying Aβ load and stained using an antibody against phosphorylated tau, AT8 dilution 1:1000 (Innogenetics, Alpharetta, GA, USA). Immunohistochemically stained sections were digitally scanned using the Aperio Image Analysis Toolbox Nuclear algorithm, and the nuclei with tau tangles were counted and divided by the analysis area, which were averaged within each brain region and then across the eight regions to yield the overall tau tangle density.
19
2.1.3
Pathological diagnosis of AD
Amyloid immunohistochemistry for Thal staging, Modified Bielschowsky silver stain for assessment of CERAD neuritic plaque score, and neurofibrillary tangles (Braak score) were examined for adjudicating the presence of pathological AD.
20
2.2
Proteomic analysis‐Aβ binding partners
Tandem mass tag (TMT) mass spectrometry‐based proteomic analysis was used for the quantification of proteins in the frozen tissue of dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC). The DLPFC was chosen for omics quantification because of its vulnerability to the accumulation of AD pathology and its contribution to human behavior. Details of the TMT proteomic analysis have been provided elsewhere.
21
,
22
,
23
The samples of DLPFC tissue were homogenized, digested by peptidases such as endopeptidase and trypsin, and prepared for labeling. Isobaric TMT peptide labels were introduced, followed by high‐PH fractionation. The peptides of the fractions were analyzed by liquid chromatography coupled to mass spectrometry. The derived spectra were searched against the canonical UniProtKB human proteome database, and peptides were assigned and combined into proteins. After implementing the quality control criteria, 10030 proteins were identified that were searched for outlining Aβ binding partners suggested in the literature.

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT
Systematic review
: We reviewed the literature using PubMed. Through searching keywords including amyloid, amyloid beta, or amyloid‐β and receptor and examining the references of the retrieved review articles, we identified 52 proteins that were suggested in the experimental non‐human studies to be an Aβ binding partner acting as an Aβ receptor. However, we found no human studies that had systematically examined levels of Aβ binding partners as the mediators between Aβ and tau in the human brain.
Interpretation
: The strongest mediations between Aβ and tau tangles were observed for RAC1 and ATP1A3, which collectively mediated 10.1% of the association between Aβ and tau tangles. In contrast, Aβ mediated >70% of the associations of matrix proteins with tau tangles.
Future directions
: Future studies should test whether targeting RAC1 or ATP1A3 may slow down accumulation of Aβ‐induced tau tangle accumulation.
2.3
Total Aβ protein
Targeted liquid chromatography‐selected reaction monitoring (SRM) proteomic analysis was used to quantify Aβ protein.
24
,
25
About 20 mg of frozen DLPFC tissue was homogenized, digested using trypsin, and washed. Stable isotope‐labeled synthetic peptides were created for peptides of interest and mixed with the tryptic‐washed samples, and the mixture was examined using liquid chromatography SRM mass spectrometry. The relative abundance of a tryptic peptide was calculated using the peak area ratio of the peptide to that of its corresponding synthetic peptide. The transformed peak area ratio of total Aβ protein (LVFFAEDVGSNK) was used in the current study.

Sex, race/ethnicity, and years of education were also obtained at the baseline. Quantification of RNA level from DLPFC follows the methods described in eMethods and elsewhere.

2.5
Statistical analyses
Linear regressions were used to examine the associations of the Aβ binding partners with Aβ or tau tangle density. For Aβ binding partners related to tau tangles, we used mediation analysis to examine whether Aβ binding partners mediated the association between Aβ and tau tangles
29
or whether it was Aβ that mediated the association between Aβ binding partners and tau tangles. For mediation analysis, we used the procedure
Causalmed
in SAS and included interaction terms between Aβ binding partners and Aβ load to account for both mediation and moderation.
30
In both the regression and mediation analyses, we applied Benjamini and Hochberg's false discovery rate (FDR)
31
to adjust for multiple testing.
Based on the results of mediation analyses, a conceptual model was created between the Aβ measures (Aβ load and Aβ proteins), Aβ binding partners, and tau tangles. To develop the conceptual model, we used principal component analysis to examine whether Aβ binding partners could be summarized into factors; factors with an eigenvalue ≥ 1 were retained. The conceptual model was tested using a structural equation modeling (SEM) technique to assess whether the model fitted the data. The goodness‐of‐fit index (GFI) and the root mean square error of the approximation (RMSEA) assess model performance. All the models, including the SEM model, were controlled for age at death and sex, and the analyses were done using the procedure
Calis
in SAS (version 9.4, SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Aβ binding partners and the association of Aβ with tau tangles

However, the sequence of the events resulting in tau tangle formation following the potential binding of Aβ with a binding partner is unclear: Does the Aβ binding partner mediate the association of Aβ with tau tangles through its downstream effects, or does Aβ mediate the association of Aβ binding partner with tau tangles (Figure

In our primary analysis, we used total Aβ protein, quantified by SRM proteomic analysis, as the Aβ measure to examine its association with tau tangles, together with the 15 proteins also associated with tau tangles. All 15 proteins, except integrin subunit alpha M (ITGAM), were correlated with total Aβ protein (Figure

In 15 separate mediation analyses, total Aβ protein was the exposure, each of the 15 proteins was the mediator, and tau tangle density was the outcome, controlling for age at death and sex. We found seven proteins that mediated the association between total Aβ protein and tau tangles (Table
2
). However, the mediated percentages were low, ranging from 3.1% for glutamate receptor 2 (GRIA2) to 7.4% for Ras‐related C3 botulinum toxin substrate 1 (RAC1). Second, we examined whether Aβ mediated the associations of the Aβ binding partners with tau. In 15 separate mediation analyses, each of the Aβ binding partners was the exposure, total Aβ protein was the mediator, and tau tangle density was the outcome. We found that the associations of four Aβ binding partners with tau tangles were mediated by total Aβ protein with >70% mediation: Apolipoprotein E (APOE), Clusterin (CLU), Integrin beta‐2 (ITGB2), and pro low‐density lipoprotein receptor‐related protein 1 (LRP1) (Table

Although three of these four proteins were also among the seven proteins that mediated the association of Aβ with tau tangles (first group of mediation analyses described previously), more than 70% mediation by Aβ was 8 to 20+‐fold larger than any potential mediation by an Aβ binding partner, which was 3% to 7%. Therefore, the two groups of mediation analyses identified two groups of proteins: four proteins (RAC1, sodium/potassium‐transporting ATPase subunit alpha‐3 [ATP1A3], Ras/Rap GTPase‐activating protein SynGAP [SYNGAP1], GRIA2] that mediated the association of Aβ with tau tangles, and four other proteins [APOE, CLU, ITGB2, and LRP1] whose association with tau tangles was mediated by total Aβ protein. Interestingly, the four proteins (RAC1, ATP1A3, SYNGAP1, GRIA2) that mediated the association between total Aβ protein and tau tangles are on the plasma membrane, supporting their potential action as the linkage between extracellular Aβ and intracellular tau tangles. The mediations did not change when we further controlled for housekeeping proteins, an indirect measure of the variation of the cellular content (Table

A model of Aβ, Aβ binding partners, and tau tangles
Based on the commonality of the Aβ binding partners mediating the associations of total Aβ protein or Aβ load with tau tangles or the commonality of the Aβ binding partners whose associations with tau tangles were mediated by total Aβ protein or Aβ load, we hypothesized a model illustrating the connections between Aβ, Aβ binding partners, and tau tangles (Figure
Se‐2
) and used SEM analysis to test whether the model fit the data. The model included a latent variable, Aβ, inferred from observed measures of total Aβ protein and Aβ load and their correlation. The model also included another latent variable for matrisome, which was inferred from the protein abundance for APOE, CLU, and ITGB2. This is because the three proteins’ associations with tau tangles were mediated by both total Aβ protein and Aβ load, the three proteins loaded on just one factor with an eigenvalue ≥1, explaining 55% of their variance, and because the three proteins are members of matrix proteins. The model also included the association between age at death and the latent variable Aβ and tau tangles
37
and the association of sex with tau tangles based on prior reports.
38
The SEM analysis revealed that SYNGAP1 was not associated with tau tangles (standardized estimate = −0.022, SE = 0.030,
p
= 0.46) with RAC1 and ATP1A3 included in the model (Table
Se‐6
). Therefore, we removed SYNGAP1 from the model. The final model adequately fit the data (Figure
3
; Table
Se‐7
): adjusted GFI = 0.90, RMSEA = 0.10 (90% confidence limit: 0.08 to 0.11). In this model, the associations of matrix proteins (e.g., APOE) with tau tangles are mediated by Aβ, whose association with tau tangles in turn is mediated by membrane‐associated Aβ binding partners, including RAC1 and ATP1A3. Although both the direct (estimate = 0.477, SE = 0.031,
p
< 0.001) and indirect (estimate = 0.054, SE = 0.011,
p
< 0.001) associations of Aβ with tau tangles were significant, the indirect association accounted for only 10.1% of the total Aβ‐tau association, suggestive of additional mediators yet to be captured.

DISCUSSION
In 850 well‐characterized participants, we quantified 34 proteins previously identified in non‐human studies as Aβ binding partners, with 15 of them associated with tau tangles. A series of mediation and SEM analyses identified two proteins, RAC1 and ATP1A3, mediating the association between Aβ and tau tangles. Although matrix proteins such as APOE and CLU were previously suggested as Aβ receptors, our analyses indicated that while both APOE and CLU levels were associated with tau tangles, it was Aβ that mediated the associations of APOE and CLU with tau tangles, rather than APOE and CLU serving as the mediators. Of the examined proteins, only RAC1 and ATP1A3 mediated a small proportion (∼10%) of the association between Aβ and tau tangles, suggesting that other mediators remain to be identified.
An estimated 6.9 million Americans are living with AD dementia today, which is expected to reach 13.8 million by 2060.
39
Immunotherapy against Aβ is the only AD treatment approved by the US Food and Drug Administration with the potential to modify the disease progression, but it shows modest clinical efficacy and major side effects.
40
Therefore, new avenues must be explored for finding better treatment targets.
Prior studies showed that cognitive decline was faster when both Aβ and tau tangles were present in the brain compared with the presence of each one alone.
41
Moreover, the accumulation of Aβ occurs earlier than the distributed accumulation of tau tangles,
3
and Aβ mediates the association of APOE with neocortical tau tangles,
42
suggesting that Aβ may initiate the progression of tau tangles. Therefore, identifying proteins that link Aβ with tau tangles offers a potential novel therapeutic strategy.
2
The current study is among the first human studies that systematically examined proteins identified as Aβ binding partners in prior experimental studies and supported two membrane‐associated proteins as Aβ binding partners linking Aβ with tau tangle accumulation in community‐dwelling older adults.
RAC1 is a Rho GTPase‐binding protein located in the plasma membrane. Upon binding to GTP, RAC1 is activated and through binding with some effector proteins affects its downstream target, actin and myosin involved in cellular functions such as locomotion, cell division, and morphogenesis.
43
Therefore, one possible pathway for mediating the association between Aβ and tau tangles by RAC1 is through RAC1‐downstream target, actin, because of the cross‐talk between actin and microtubule and interactions of tau with both proteins.
44
Other possible pathways are internalization of Aβ through macropinocytosis and its modification by RAC1,
9
RAC1‐induced translocation of SET protein and hyperphosphorylation of tau,
45
and modulation of inflammatory pathways.
46
Future studies should investigate the molecular pathways underlying the association of Aβ with tau tangles mediated by RAC1.
ATP1A3 was the second protein, after RAC1, that mediated the association between Aβ and tau tangles.
ATP1A3
gene encodes the α3 subunit of sodium‐potassium‐ATPase present on the neuronal cell membrane, which is responsible for maintaining sodium balance across cell membrane and preserving resting action potential.
47
Previous experimental studies showed direct interaction between ATP1A3 and amyloid
48
or tau species
49
and phosphorylation of tau by glycogen synthase kinase‐3β after binding amyloid species to ATP1A3.
50
Specificity of ATP1A3 to neurons
47
supports its possible mediatory role in the association between extracellular Aβ and intraneuronal hyperphosphorylated tau tangles.
Matrix proteins such as APOE and CLU were previously shown to bind to Aβ and affect its aggregation and clearance.
51
Further, previous experimental evidence demonstrated that accumulated neuropathology induced upregulation of APOE and CLU.
52
Our mediation and SEM analyses suggest that matrix proteins, such as CLU and APOE, provide an environment for the build‐up of Aβ, which will be later linked to the intraneuronal tau tangles through proteins such as RAC1 and ATP1A3. This finding can explain the dominant association between matrix proteins and Aβ in the prior studies.
53
,
54
Impaired Aβ clearance due to interrupted glymphatic pathways is a mechanism through which matrix proteins contribute to AD.
55
Matrix proteins such as APOE and CLU provide a nidus for deposition and aggregation of Aβ, which results in slow bulk flow of the extracellular fluid and restriction in the diffusion across the extracellular space,
56
causing attenuation of the glymphatic system and impairing clearance of Aβ protein that ends in the formation of Aβ plaques. Because the intercellular matrix is built with many proteins, it is uncertain if modulation of one or two proteins can significantly change the accumulation of Aβ in the extracellular space.
Total Aβ protein, quantified using SRM proteomic analysis, cannot differentiate between different Aβ species. More than 30 Aβ peptides with different C‐ and N‐terminal truncations have been identified in the brain,
57
,
58
such as Aβ1‐42, Aβ1‐40, and Aβ5‐42. These Aβ peptides are heterogeneous in the associations with the pathologies. For example, Aβ species ending after position 40 are more strongly associated with Aβ plaques in the brain, while Aβ species ending at position 40 or before are more strongly associated with cerebral amyloid angiopathy.
34
,
35
,
59
,
60
,
61
In addition, the specificities of anti‐Aβ antibodies for different Aβ species vary, which results in different efficacies at removing Aβ plaques from the brain.
62
Therefore, in future studies, different Aβ proteoforms
57
in soluble material and insoluble aggregates

The Aβ binding partners included in this study were quantified using bulk brain tissue, which did not have resolution to identify the cell origin of the examined proteins. Considering tau tangles as intraneuronal deposits of tau, future studies should examine neuronal levels of Aβ binding partners, such as RAC1 or ATP1A3, in relation to Aβ or tau tangles to confirm the current study's findings. Cultures of human induced pluripotent stem cell‐derived neurons
63
can be used to examine the neuronal levels of candidate Aβ binding partners in relation to Aβ and hyperphosphorylated tau levels in neuronal cultures.

The application of the current study's findings is limited. The study is an observational study that relied on statistical techniques to infer directionality between Aβ, the proteins including Aβ binding partners, and tau tangles. To confirm that modulation of RAC1 and ATP1A3 may interrupt Aβ‐induced tau tangle accumulation and distribution, experimental studies and clinical trials are needed. The participants were not representative of the general American population; they were mostly White with more years of education than the average American. Therefore, the study findings should be replicated in more diverse samples. Aβ has many proteoforms with varied solubility
57
that were not examined in the current study; future studies should examine different proteoforms of Aβ to identify if they have different binding partners in relation to tau tangles. Several strengths make the study's findings trustworthy, including its large sample size, the community setting of the participants with very high follow‐up and autopsy rates, a systemic collection of data by staff blinded to other participant data, and availability of the AD pathology and proteins, including Aβ protein, from the same participants and the same brain region.
