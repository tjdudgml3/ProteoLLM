Background
Ischemic stroke remains a major global health challenge as the second leading cause of death worldwide [
1
]. Currently, clinical treatment primarily relies on vascular recanalization; however, its application is severely limited by a narrow therapeutic time window and the risk of hemorrhagic transformation. More importantly, even after successful recanalization, approximately 40% of patients still suffer from poor outcomes due to secondary neuronal injury [
2
,
3
]. Recent studies have identified microglia-mediated inflammatory responses are involved in the entire pathological process of ischemic stroke, making them one of the most critical factors contributing to secondary brain injury. As the resident immune cells of the brain, microglia normally surveil the neural environment to maintain the brain in an “immune-privileged” state. However, they are highly sensitive to pathological changes and can become activated within minutes of ischemia onset. Once activated, microglia release large amounts of pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6) and reactive oxygen species, which directly contribute to neuronal apoptosis, blood-brain barrier breakdown, and infarct expansion [
4
,
5
]. Both clinical and preclinical studies suggest that suppressing inflammation or inhibiting microglial overactivation can significantly mitigate brain injury, highlighting microglial modulation as a promising therapeutic strategy to improve stroke outcomes [
6
–
8
].
Macroautophagy (hereafter referred to as autophagy) is the predominant form of autophagy and a conserved intracellular degradation and recycling process. It involves the formation of autophagosomes that sequester damaged proteins or organelles, which subsequently fuse with lysosomes to degrade the contents, thereby maintaining cellular homeostasis [
9
]. In ischemic stroke, autophagy has been implicated in regulating microglia-mediated inflammation, though findings remain controversial. Some studies have shown that inhibiting autophagy with 3-methyladenine (3-MA) reduces microglial autophagic activity and inflammation, while also significantly decreasing infarct size, edema, and neurological deficits [
10
]. In contrast, other studies reported that promoting microglial autophagy using steroidal saponins like Polyphyllin I suppresses pro-inflammatory responses and protects against ischemic brain injury [
11
]. These discrepancies likely stem from the dynamic nature of the autophagic process—specifically, the importance of autophagic flux. Rather than simply promoting autophagosome formation, maintaining a functional autophagic flux is critical [
12
,
13
]. In general, enhanced autophagic flux in immune cells exerts anti-inflammatory effects, whereas blocked flux tends to promote a pro-inflammatory phenotype [
14
,
15
].
Notably, the completion of autophagic flux is highly dependent on lysosomal function. As the terminal degradative organelles of the autophagy pathway, lysosomes are essential for efficient autophagosome-lysosome fusion and degradation, requiring an acidic environment (pH < 5.0) to maintain hydrolase activity. When lysosomal function is impaired, accumulated autophagosomes cannot be efficiently degraded, resulting in disrupted autophagic flux [
16
]. This “blocked” state not only compromises the protective role of autophagy but also sustains pro-inflammatory signaling. Both pharmacological and genetic strategies that enhance lysosomal function have shown neuroprotective effects in stroke models, underscoring the therapeutic potential of targeting the microglial autophagy-lysosome axis [
17
–
19
]. Therefore, elucidating the key molecular regulators of lysosomal function in microglia is critical for understanding post-stroke neuroinflammation.
NOD-like receptor family caspase recruitment domain-containing protein 5 (NLRC5) is a key member of the NOD-like receptor family. Structurally, it consists of an N-terminal CARD-like domain, a central NOD domain, and an unusually long C-terminal leucine-rich repeat (LRR) region, making NLRC5 the largest known NLR protein [
20
]. Unlike most NLRs that function exclusively in the cytoplasm, NLRC5 possesses a nuclear localization signal and can shuttle between the nucleus and cytoplasm. It is highly expressed in immune-related tissues such as the bone marrow and spleen, and is a critical transcriptional regulator of major histocompatibility complex class I genes [
21
]. NLRC5 is thought to function both as a pattern recognition receptor and as a regulatory NLR, acting as either a positive or negative modulator of inflammatory signaling cascades [
22
]. However, its role in inflammation appears to be context-dependent. For instance, NLRC5 expression is upregulated in
Helicobacter pylori
-induced gastritis, and its deficiency exacerbates inflammation [
23
]; in high-fat diet-induced cardiac inflammation, its absence promotes NF-κB activation and myocardial fibrosis [
24
]. Conversely, in diabetic nephropathy, NLRC5 deficiency alleviates kidney injury via NF-κB suppression [
25
]. Similarly, NLRC5 is upregulated in the substantia nigra-striatum axis in MPTP-induced Parkinson’s disease models, and its deletion protects dopaminergic neurons and improves motor dysfunction [
26
]. These contradictory findings may be attributed to differences in cell types and stimuli across disease models. However, the role of NLRC5 in ischemic stroke, particularly in the regulation of microglial lysosomal function and the autophagy-inflammation axis, remains completely unexplored.
In this study, we systematically investigated the role and mechanism of NLRC5 in ischemic stroke. We found that NLRC5 expression is significantly upregulated in the ischemic penumbra of stroke mice and in the brains of stroke patients, with a predominant localization in microglia. Using microglia-specific
Nlrc5
conditional knockout (CKO) mice, we demonstrated that
Nlrc5
-deletion effectively suppressed microglial activation and inflammation after ischemia, thereby reducing neuronal death and ischemic brain injury. Proteomic analyses and verification experiments revealed that
Nlrc5
deficiency restored lysosomal function in microglia and promoted autophagic flux. Further mass spectrometry and molecular experiments showed that NLRC5 interacts with and stabilizes interferon-stimulated gene 15 (ISG15) via its CARD domain, preventing ISG15 degradation through the autophagy-lysosome pathway. NLRC5 promotes lysosomal dysfunction and inflammatory responses in microglia in an ISG15-dependent manner. This study identifies NLRC5-ISG15 axis as a critical regulator of microglia-driven neuroinflammation and a potential target for treating ischemic stroke.
Materials and methods
Postmortem human brain tissues
Prior to donation, written informed consent was obtained from all donors or their legal guardians. Comprehensive details for human postmortem cerebral specimens are summarized in Table S1. In total, brain tissues from 2 non-stroke patients and 2 stroke patients were analyzed in this study. The peri-infarct regions of these specimens were dissected and immersed in fresh fixative solution (0.1 M phosphate buffer containing 15% formalin, pH 7.4) for 24–48 h, and then underwent sequential dehydration through immersion in 20% and 30% sucrose for 48–72 h. Processed tissues were maintained at − 80 °C until required for sectioning and subsequent immunostaining [
27
].

HEK293T, HT22 neuronal cells and BV2 cell lines maintained in our laboratory were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; 11965092, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; 10099141 C, Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (15070063, Gibco, USA) [

Primary cortical neurons were obtained from E15-17 embryos of C57/BL6J mice, and then seeded on plates after poly-D-lysine coating. They were cultured in Neurobasal Medium containing GlutaMAX and B27 for 7–8 days before being given treatment. Primary microglia were obtained from the cerebral cortex of C57/BL6J mice born within 48 h, underwent sequential enzymatic digestion, centrifugation and 70-µm filtration, and then seeded in 75-cm² flasks. They were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin for 14 days. Primary microglia were collected after shaking and reseeded for further experiment [

Virus and plasmids
For NLRC5 knockdown in BV2 cells, lentivirus (OBiO Tech Inc., China) encoding shRNA targeting NLRC5 was employed with non-targeting shRNA serving as negative control. BV2 cells were transduced at multiplicity of infection 40 (MOI = 40) for 8 h, followed by complete medium replacement and 72-hour culture. Selection was performed using 2 µg/ml puromycin for another 72 h with knockdown efficiency validated.
Autophagic flux monitoring and NLRC5 overexpression were achieved using mRFP-GFP-LC3 adenovirus (GeneChem Co., China) and HA-tagged NLRC5 adenovirus (GeneChem Co., China) respectively. Primary microglia or BV2 cells were transduced at MOI = 50 for 8 h, followed by complete medium replacement and 48–72 h culture.
FLAG-tagged ISG15 lentivirus (OBiO Tech Inc., China) were utilized for ISG15 overexpression. HEK293T cells were transduced at MOI = 1 for 8 h, followed by complete medium replacement and 72 h culture. Selection was performed using 1 µg/ml puromycin for another 72 h with knockdown efficiency validated.
HA-tagged NLRC5 domain plasmids (Bioworld Technology, USA) were transiently transfected into ISG15-overexpressing HEK293T cells for 8 h using Lipofectamine 2000 (11668019, Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s manuals. Cells were maintained in fresh medium for 36 h and prepared for subsequent experiment.
tMCAO and laser speckle contrast imaging (LSCI)
Transient focal cerebral ischemia was induced in experimental cohorts through tMCAO, with sham-operated controls receiving identical surgical exposure without vascular occlusion. Surgical procedures commenced with intraperitoneal administration of 2.5% 2,2,2-tribromoethanol (125–150 µl/10 g,
T48402
, Sigma-Aldrich, USA). A 6/0 monofilament nylon suture (Doccol Corporation, USA) was advanced through the internal carotid artery to the MCA origin under laser Doppler flowmetry (Perimed Corporation, Sweden) guidance, with successful occlusion confirmed by a steep reduction in regional cerebral blood flow (CBF). After 60-minute ischemia, reperfusion was initiated by filament withdrawal. Core body temperature was maintained at 37.0 ± 0.5 °C throughout procedures via a heating pad.
In addition, LSCI provided hemodynamic verification across surgical phases. Using a laser (Perimed Corporation) directed at the skull, CBF images at different stages (preoperative, perioperative and postoperative stages) were visualized and collected for further analysis.
Behavioral testing
Neurological functional assessments in tMCAO models were conducted at 1, 3 and 7 days post-stroke through 4 standardized behavioral paradigms: modified neurological severity score (mNSS), forepaw grip strength quantification, rotarod test and foot fault test. The mNSS evaluation (scale: 0–18) systematically assessed sensory function, motor function, reflex responses and balance deficits, with elevated scores correlating with functional impairment severity. Forepaw grip strength was quantified using a digital dynamometer (GS3, Bioseb, France) through 5 consecutive trials per subject, with maximum strength values recorded for statistical analysis. Preoperative rotarod (RWD Life Science, China) acclimatization was performed over 3 days prior to tMCAO procedure. In the formal test, the speed of rotarod was increased linearly from 4 to 40 rpm over a 300-second trial period. The fall latency (≤ 300 s) of mice was recorded. The foot fault test evaluated sensorimotor deficits by calculating left forefoot misstep frequency (%) during 5-minute explorations of elevated stainless-steel grids (12 × 12 mm
2
).
Staining
Cerebral infarction volumes were assessed via 2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) staining. Brains were sectioned coronally into six 2-mm-thick slices and incubated in 2% TTC solution (T8877, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) at 37 °C for 15 min. Stained sections were photographed and analyzed with ImageJ. Cerebral edema correction employed the formula: Corrected Infarction (%) = (contralateral hemisphere area - non-infarcted ipsilateral hemisphere area)/(2× contralateral hemisphere area) × 100%.
At 24 and 72 h post-tMCAO, mice underwent sequential transcardial perfusion with 0.9% NaCl and 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) under deep anesthesia. Brains were post-fixed in 4% PFA, dehydrated with sucrose, and sectioned at 20 μm for subsequent staining. Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) assays were performed using the One-Step TUNEL Apoptosis Kit (A112-03, Vazyme Biotech, China). After permeabilization, sections were incubated with TUNEL reaction mix (2 h, RT) and counterstained with DAPI. The TUNEL-labeled cells were observed under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus BX51, Japan), and the percentage of TUNEL-positive cells was calculated.
For immunofluorescence staining, brain sections were fixed with 4% PFA, permeabilized (0.2% Triton X-100), and blocked with 2% bovine serum albumin. Primary antibodies included NLRC5 (ab105411, abcam, USA), Iba-1 (OB-PGP049-01, Oasis biofarm, China), GFAP (ab4674, abcam, USA), NeuN (abcam, ab104224), MAP2 (ab5392, abcam, USA), microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3 (MAP1LC3, 12741 s, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), sequestosome 1 (SQSTM1, sc-48402, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA) and ISG15 (703132, Invitrogen, USA). Sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by species-matched Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen, USA) for 2 h at RT. After being counterstained with DAPI, images were captured using a confocal laser-scanning microscope (Olympus FV3000, Japan).
Microglial process complexity was analyzed through skeleton analysis of Iba1
+
cells. 3D reconstructions of images were generated via Imaris (Bitplane AG) with semi-automated quantification of morphological data by MotiQ Analyzer of ImageJ software.
Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)
Total RNA was extracted from both peri-infarct cortical tissues and cultured cells using TRIzol reagent (15596026CN, Invitrogen, USA), followed by reverse transcription with the Evo M-MLV RT Mix Kit (AG11728, Accurate Biotechnology, China) according to the manufacturer’s manuals. RT-qPCR analysis was conducted using a SYBR Green kit (AG11733, Accurate Biotechnology, China) on Roche LightCycler 96 PCR instrument (Roche, Sweden). Tissue homogenates were filtered through a 70-µm cell strainer to obtain single-cell suspensions. Myelin debris was removed via discontinuous percoll density gradient centrifugation (30–70%), with microglia-enriched fractions collected for subsequent FACS analysis.
For immunophenotypic characterization, cellular aliquots were stained with fluorophore-labeled anti-CD45 (Rat, 1:1000, 103114, BioLegend, USA) and anti-CD11b (Rat, 1:500, 53–0112−82, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for 30 min at 4 °C. The CD45

Western blot analysis
Total proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined via bicinchoninic acid assay (BCA Protein Assay Kit, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Equal protein quantities were resolved through SDS-PAGE and electrotransferred to 0.2 μm PVDF membranes using semi-dry transfer apparatus. After being blocked with 5% non-fat dried milk, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting β-actin (AP0060, Bioworld, USA), GAPDH (AP0063, Bioworld, USA), NLRC5 (72379s, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), autophagy related 7 (ATG7, 10088-2-AP, Proteintech, China), autophagy related 3 (ATG3, 11262-2-AP, Proteintech, China), LAMP1 (ab24170, abcam, USA), Cathepsin B (CTSB, 12216-1-AP, Proteintech, China), cathepsin D (CTSD, A19680, ABclonal, China), LC3 (12741s, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), SQSTM1 (5114s, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), HA (3724s, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), FLAG (AE005, ABclonal, China) and ISG15 (15981-1-AP, Proteintech, China). Following TBST washes, the membranes were probed with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. The bands were finally visualized by a GelPro system (Tanon Technologies, China) using an ECL Detection Kit (Millipore, USA). Band intensities were analyzed by ImageJ software.
Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
For sample preparation, protein lysates extracted from cerebral ischemic penumbra tissues and medium from cultured cells were centrifuged. Proinflammatory cytokine levels (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) in tissue lysates and cell supernatants were quantified via ELISA using commercial kits: IL-1β (CEK1788, Bioworld, USA), IL-6 (CEK1785, Bioworld, USA), and TNF-α (CEK1783, Bioworld, USA), strictly adhering to manufacturer’s protocols. Absorbance measurements were obtained using a microplate reader (Tecan Trading AG, Switzerland).
Proteomics analysis
Total proteins were extracted from primary microglia after OGD/R and the cerebral ischemic penumbra tissues 3 days after tMCAO. Comprehensive proteomic profiling was performed by OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) underwent unsupervised hierarchical clustering. Subsequent functional annotation of DEPs incorporated kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis and Gene Ontology (GO) term classification.
Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)
Co-immunoprecipitation assays were performed using Protein A/G magnetic beads (30 µL/sample; HY-K0202, MedChemExpress, USA). Beads were pre-washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (PBST) and conjugated with primary antibodies (2 µg/sample): anti-HA (3724s, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), anti-ISG15 (15981-1-AP, Proteintech, China), anti-FLAG (AE005, ABclonal, China), or rabbit IgG (2729s, Cell Signaling Technology, USA) control via 2-h rotation at 4 °C. Bead-antibody complexes were magnetically separated and washed 4 times with PBST. Precleared protein lysates were then incubated with antibody-bound beads under constant rotation (4 °C, 2 h). Immunoprecipitation complexes were isolated magnetically and rigorously washed 4 times with PBST. Bound proteins were eluted in 30 µL 2× loading buffer through 5-minute denaturation at 95 °C. Then the supernatants were collected for subsequent western blot analysis.
Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)
Ultrastructural characterization of autophagic processes was conducted through TEM. Cellular specimens underwent primary fixation in ice-cold 2.5% glutaraldehyde (0.1 M cacodylate buffer, pH 7.4) for 30 min, followed by post-fixation with 1% osmium tetroxide. Epoxy resin-embedded samples were sectioned to 70–80 nm thickness and counterstained with 2% uranyl acetate/lead citrate prior to imaging on a Hitachi 600 electron microscope (Hitachi High-Technologies, Japan). Quantitative assessment of autophagic vacuoles was conducted by counting autophagosomes across 16 randomly selected fields per samples.
Autophagic flux assay
Microglia were transduced with adenoviral mRFP-GFP-LC3 vectors for 48 h preceding OGD/R or LPS stimulation to monitor autophagic flux dynamics. Acid-stable mRFP fluorescence was interpreted as reflecting autophagosome-lysosome fusion events, wherein LC3-positive vesicles became incorporated into acidic lysosomal compartments to form autolysosomes, indicating a smooth autophagy flux. Conversely, persistent GFP fluorescence signified impaired lysosomal fusion capacity with accumulated autophagosomes which couldn’t be converted to autolysosomes, indicating autophagic flux blockade. Therefore, in this study, an increase in the RFP: GFP ratio of the mRFP-GFP-LC3 reporter, was interpreted as evidence of restored or enhanced autophagic flux, whereas GFP persistence indicates autophagic flux blockade.
Lysosomes staining with lysotracker and lysosensor
To visualize lysosomes and assess their functional activity, two fluorescent probes were employed: LysoTracker Red DND-99 (A66439, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and the pH- sensitive LysoSensor Green DND-189 (A66436, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). LysoSensor Green displays enhanced fluorescence in response to decreasing pH levels, whereas LysoTracker Red maintains fluorescence relatively independent of pH changes. For labeling, primary microglia were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h in culture medium containing 1 µM of each dye. Following incubation, the cells were washed twice with HBSS (14175-079, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) to eliminate excess dye. Imaging was conducted on four biological replicates per group using consistent acquisition settings.

Primary microglia undergoing OGD were concurrently treated with inhibitors, including 3-MA (5 mM, HY-19312, MedChemExpress, USA), CQ (40 µM, HY-17589 A, MedChemExpress, USA), Bafilomycin A1 (BAF, 50 nM, HY-100558, MedChemExpress, USA), or MG132 (10 µM; HY-13259, MedChemExpress, USA). Cycloheximide (CHX)-chase assays
Primary microglia were activated through 18-hour LPS stimulation (0.1 µg/mL, L6529, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) followed by medium replacement with CHX-containing solution (50 µg/mL, S2619, Selleck, USA). Cell samples were collected at designated intervals (0, 3, 6, 9, and 12 h post-treatment) for subsequent western blot analysis. Protein lysates were subjected to western blot to quantify the expression levels of NLRC5, ISG15 and β-actin. ISG15 band intensities were normalized to corresponding β-actin values at each time point. The degradation rate of ISG15 in NLRC5-expressing versus NLRC5-deficient conditions were then statistically analyzed.

Results
NLRC5 is upregulated in microglia within the peri-infarct region following cerebral ischemia
To investigate the function of NLRC5 in ischemic stroke, we first examined its dynamic expression in a mouse model of tMCAO. Western blot and RT-qPCR analyses revealed that both the protein and RNA levels of NLRC5 in the ischemic penumbra were significantly elevated from day 1 to day 7 post-tMCAO compared with the sham group (Fig.
1
A-D). We then explored the localization and cell-type-specific expression of NLRC5 in the brain. Immunofluorescence staining combined with Pearson correlation coefficient analysis demonstrated that, three days after tMCAO, NLRC5 was primarily colocalized with microglia (Iba1⁺) in the peri-infarct region, with much less colocalization observed in astrocytes (GFAP⁺) or neurons (NeuN⁺) (Fig.
1
E-I). Moreover, immunofluorescence staining showed that NLRC5 expression in peri-infarct microglia increased on days 1, 3, and 7 post-tMCAO, with the highest expression observed on day 3, and remained elevated on day 7 (Fig.
1
J, K). To assess clinical relevance, we performed immunofluorescence analysis on the postmortem brain tissues from patients with ischemic stroke. Compared to non-stroke controls, NLRC5 expression in microglia appeared higher in the peri-infarct region of stroke patients (Fig.
1
L). Together, these findings indicate that NLRC5 is predominantly activated in microglia during the acute phase of ischemic stroke and may contribute to the development of ischemic brain injury.

To further support these findings, we investigated the role of NLRC5 in the BV2 cell line. Following ischemic stress, microglial autophagy contributes to the maintenance of homeostasis and mitigation of neuroinflammation. Proper lysosomal function is essential for autophagic flux [
33

Thus, we treated primary microglia under OGD/R conditions with BAF, a lysosomal inhibitor that impairs lysosomal enzyme activity and promotes autophagosome accumulation. RT-qPCR results revealed that BAF treatment exacerbated the inflammatory response of microglia following OGD/R (Fig.

Nlrc5 knockout restores lysosomal function impaired by OGD/R in microglia and promotes autophagic flux
We first investigated the effect of
Nlrc5
knockout on OGD/R-induced autophagy in microglia. Primary microglia were isolated from
Nlrc5
KO and WT mice and subjected to OGD/R treatment, with or without BAF to block autophagosome degradation. The expression of MAP1LC3, referred to as LC3, a marker for autophagosomes, and SQSTM1/p62, which mediates delivery of ubiquinated cargo to autophagosomes and is degraded with its cargo, were significantly upregulated in primary microglia following OGD/R (Fig.
6
A-C), which was also observed in microglia from the ischemic penumbra of the tMCAO model (Fig. S6A-C). This suggests that autophagy flux in microglia was inhibited due to enhanced autophagic induction and impaired autophagosome clearance after ischemic stroke. In contrast,
Nlrc5
knockout reduced the accumulation of LC3-II and SQSTM1 in both OGD/R-treated primary microglia and in penumbral microglia of the tMCAO model (Fig.
6
A-C, Fig. S6A-C). To further clarify whether NLRC5 influences autophagosome formation or degradation, BAF was added during OGD/R treatment. In the presence of BAF after OGD/R, there was no significant difference in LC3-II accumulation between
Nlrc5
KO and WT groups (Fig.
6
A, C), and the expression levels of autophagy initiation-related proteins ATG3 and ATG7 after OGD/R were also unaffected by
Nlrc5
knockout (Fig. S7A-C), suggesting that
Nlrc5
knockout does not affect autophagosome formation. This finding was further validated in LPS-stimulated BV2 cells, where NLRC5 knockdown reduced LPS-induced SQSTM1 accumulation without altering BAF-induced LC3-II accumulation (Fig. S8A-C). Collectively, these results suggest that NLRC5 does not regulate autophagosome formation, but rather modulates autophagosome degradation.

Discussion
In this study, we systematically investigated the mechanism by which NLRC5, a member of the NOD-like receptor family, contributes to ischemic brain injury by regulating lysosomal function in microglia, and proposed its potential as a therapeutic target. The main findings of our study include the following: (1) NLRC5 was significantly upregulated in the peri-infarct region following ischemic stroke, predominantly localized in microglia, and maintained a pro-inflammatory state during the acute phase; (2) Microglia-specific deletion of
Nlrc5
markedly attenuated neurological deficits, infarct volume, and neuronal apoptosis in stroke mice, accompanied by suppression of microglial overactivation and pro-inflammatory cytokine expression; (3) Under various in vitro stimulation models (including OGD/R, LPS, neuronal supernatant, and debris),
Nlrc5
deficiency alleviated microglia-mediated inflammatory responses and neuronal injury; (4) Mechanistically,
Nlrc5
knockout improved lysosomal acidification and hydrolase expression under inflammatory conditions, promoting autophagosome degradation and restoring autophagic flux; (5) Further studies revealed that NLRC5 binds specifically to ISG15 via its CARD domain, stabilizes ISG15 protein levels, and inhibits its degradation via the autophagy-lysosome pathway, thereby amplifying the pro-inflammatory response of microglia. Notably, the pro-inflammatory effect of NLRC5 was significantly attenuated in the absence of
Isg15
. Taken together, the NLRC5-ISG15 axis exacerbates lysosomal dysfunction in microglia, which impairs autophagic flux and enhances neuroinflammation, serving as a critical mechanism of sustained microglial activation after stroke and offering a potential therapeutic target for post-stroke intervention (Fig.
10
).
Fig. 10
Schematic diagram illustrating the role of microglial NLRC5 after ischemic stroke
Previous studies have described the expression pattern of NLRC5 in the CNS, primarily during development or in non-stroke disease models, but lacked a systematic analysis of its cell-type-specific expression in the adult brain. Early reports showed that NLRC5 is mainly expressed in neurons and microglia in neonatal mouse brains, with minimal expression in astrocytes [
36
]. In this study, immunofluorescence staining confirmed that NLRC5 is predominantly localized in microglia in both postmortem brains of stroke patients and adult mouse brains, with limited expression in neurons or astrocytes. More importantly, we observed for the first time that NLRC5 expression in microglia within the ischemic penumbra increased in a time-dependent manner during the acute phase of stroke, suggesting its key role in microglial activation and functional regulation post-stroke. Supporting evidence from other models, such as ischemic retinopathy and Parkinson’s disease models [
22
,
26
], also revealed upregulation of NLRC5 in microglia closely linked to disease progression, indicating a conserved role of NLRC5 in central immune cells.
NLRC5 has been widely studied in various immune and inflammatory diseases and exhibits functional heterogeneity under different pathological conditions. In our ischemic stroke model, NLRC5 was upregulated and played a pro-inflammatory role, while its deletion conferred neuroprotection by inhibiting microglial activation and inflammatory factor release. This pro-inflammatory role of NLRC5 is consistent with its function in Parkinson’s disease models, where NLRC5 expression was elevated in MPTP-induced mice, promoting activation of microglia and astrocytes and enhancing neuroinflammation. Knockout of
Nlrc5
alleviated dopaminergic neuronal damage and motor dysfunction [
26
]. Similarly, in an ischemic retinopathy model, NLRC5 was shown to promote ganglion cell death by inducing microglial pyroptosis [
22
]. In an acute colitis mouse model,
Nlrc5
deletion reduced colonic ulcers and inflammation [
37
]. However, in certain chronic or infectious diseases, NLRC5 may play the opposite role. For example, in a chronic gastritis model induced by Helicobacter pylori,
Nlrc5
deficiency exacerbated inflammation in the gastric mucosa [
23
]. Moreover, in an HIV-Tat protein-stimulated macrophage model, miR-34a was found to downregulate NLRC5 and thereby enhance NF-κB activation and inflammation, suggesting an anti-inflammatory role for NLRC5 in some virus-related conditions [
38
]. This dual role (pro- or anti-inflammatory) highlights the complex regulatory nature of NLRC5 as a modulatory NLR. We hypothesize that this functional divergence may arise from cell-type specificity and pathological and temporal context. As the resident immune cells of the CNS, microglia exhibit unique signaling pathways and immune responses distinct from peripheral immune cells. Furthermore, acute ischemic events (e.g., stroke or retinal ischemia) trigger cellular responses that fundamentally differ from those induced by chronic inflammatory stimuli.
Autophagy and inflammation are closely linked processes with fine regulatory mechanisms that remain incompletely understood. Previous studies have shown that autophagy in microglia exerts bidirectional regulatory effects on inflammation after ischemic stroke, exhibiting a “double-edged sword” phenomenon [
39
]. Autophagy is a highly conserved intracellular degradation process involving a cascade of steps including initiation, autophagosome formation, fusion with lysosomes, and content degradation, collectively referred to as “autophagic flux” [
40
,
41
]. In intact autophagic flux, LC3-II is typically upregulated, while SQSTM1/p62 is reduced. Accumulation of SQSTM1 often indicates blocked flux due to impaired degradation. In several brain injury models, simultaneous elevation of LC3-II and SQSTM1 suggests activated autophagy with impaired autophagosome clearance. For instance, in hypertensive rats subjected to MCAO, both LC3-II and SQSTM1 were increased in thalamic vasculature, indicating disrupted autophagic clearance post-stroke [
42
]. Similarly, in a mouse model of traumatic spinal cord injury, LC3-II and SQSTM1 levels were elevated in microglia at the lesion site, while trehalose (a natural autophagy inducer) reduced SQSTM1 expression, alleviated tissue damage, and improved motor function [
43
]. Consistent with these findings, our experiments revealed that OGD/R or ischemic stroke led to concurrent elevation of LC3-II and SQSTM1 in microglia, with BAF treatment further exacerbating LC3-II accumulation, suggesting sustained autophagy activation but impaired autophagic flux. We propose that this may result from enhanced autophagy induction coupled with insufficient clearance. Using a series of assays (including western blot for marker proteins, TEM, and tandem fluorescent-tagged LC3 reporter), we demonstrated that
Nlrc5
deletion did not affect autophagosome formation but significantly promoted autophagosome degradation, thereby restoring impaired autophagic flux in microglia under OGD/R or LPS stimulation. These findings align with the hypothesis that disrupted autophagic flux exacerbates inflammation.
The latter stages of autophagy rely on lysosomal function, which has been shown to be impaired by OGD/R. For example, in hippocampal neurons, OGD/R caused accumulation of LC3-II and SQSTM1, upregulation of LAMP1, and relatively decreased expression of lysosomal hydrolases such as CTSD and CTSB, indicating reduced degradative capacity [
44
]. In stroke models, CTSD expression and activity were shown to decrease in a time-dependent manner following OGD/R, and CTSD knockdown further impaired lysosomal function and increased cell death, whereas CTSD overexpression was protective [
17
]. Our study also revealed lysosomal dysfunction in microglia following OGD/R, evidenced by reduced mature CTSD/CTSB expression and weakened acidification, which was significantly improved by
Nlrc5
deletion. Pharmacological inhibition of lysosomal activity using BAF exacerbated microglial inflammation under OGD/R, highlighting lysosomal impairment as a key driver of inflammatory amplification. Unlike prior studies that focused on NLRC5 in antigen presentation or classical inflammatory signaling, we demonstrate for the first time that NLRC5 modulates microglial lysosomal function, thereby regulating autophagic flux and maintaining a pro-inflammatory phenotype. This regulation of lysosomal degradation capacity not only enhances our understanding of microglial immunometabolism but also provides a new perspective on the “double-edged sword” nature of autophagy—wherein the integrity of autophagic flux, rather than merely autophagy levels, determines its inflammatory outcomes.
ISG15 is a ubiquitin-like protein that can be induced by interferon stimulation. Under physiological conditions, ISG15 is expressed at low levels, but it is significantly upregulated in response to pathogen infection [
45
], traumatic brain injury [
46
], cancer [
47
], and neurodegenerative diseases such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis and Alzheimer’s disease (AD) [
35
,
48
]. Traditionally, ISG15 is thought to exert its pleiotropic functions either by modifying proteins through ISGylation or by acting in its free form to mediate signaling pathways [
49
]. In recent years, accumulating evidence suggests that ISG15 can participate in disease progression by interfering the autophagy-lysosome pathway [
50
]. For instance, during respiratory syncytial virus infection, ISG15 negatively regulates the activity of Beclin1 and thus inhibits autophagy, whereas the respiratory syncytial virus nonstructural protein NS2 promotes viral replication by suppressing Beclin1 ISGylation, thereby reactivating autophagy [
51
,
52
]. AD, a common neurodegenerative disorder, is characterized by autophagic dysfunction and the abnormal accumulation of microtubule-associated protein tau (MAPT). Elevated ISG15 expression has been observed in the brains of sporadic AD patients and in AD models. Its overexpression impairs autophagic flux by inhibiting histone deacetylase 6 activity, leading to autophagic defects and abnormal MAPT aggregation. Conversely, ISG15 knockdown restores autophagic flux, reduces MAPT accumulation, and improves cognitive function [
35
]. These studies highlight the diverse roles of ISG15 in autophagy regulation; however, how ISG15 is regulated by upstream signals and its cell type-specific effects remain poorly understood. In this study, we demonstrate that, in the context of ischemic stroke, microglia-specific NLRC5 binds directly to ISG15 via its CARD domain, preventing ISG15 degradation through the autophagy-lysosome pathway and leading to ISG15 accumulation. This interaction impairs lysosomal acidification and decreases the activity of hydrolases such as CTSB and CTSD, ultimately blocking autophagic flux. This unique regulatory mechanism provides a molecular explanation for autophagy impairment in post-stroke microglia and offers new insights into how lysosomal dysfunction may exacerbate neuroinflammation. Importantly, microglia-specific deletion of
Nlrc5
significantly reduces ISG15 accumulation, restores lysosomal function, and improves stroke outcomes. These findings not only expand our understanding of ISG15’s pathological regulatory network but also suggest that targeting the NLRC5-ISG15 interaction may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for mitigating post-stroke neuroinflammation.
Despite unveiling the crucial role of the NLRC5-ISG15 axis in aggravating neuroinflammation during the acute phase of ischemic stroke, several limitations and future directions remain. First, this study was conducted exclusively in male mice to control for the confounding effects of estrogen. Future investigations in female models, incorporating estrous cycle monitoring or ovariectomy, are essential to validate the generalizability of our findings. Second, the use of injectable anesthesia precluded continuous monitoring of arterial blood pressure and blood gases. Although the comparable reduction in CBF (as verified by LSCI) across experimental groups mitigates concerns about unequal ischemic insults, the potential influence of unmonitored physiological parameters cannot be entirely ruled out. Third, the translational relevance of our findings is supported by preliminary evidence from postmortem human brains; however, this conclusion is constrained by a small sample size (
n
= 2 per group) and a lack of precise post-mortem interval data. Fourth, our study primarily focuses on the acute phase (1–7 days) following tMCAO. The role of the NLRC5-ISG15 axis in chronic recovery and neurorepair phases warrants long-term investigation. Finally, while our computational and Co-IP data suggest a direct interaction between NLRC5 and ISG15, future studies employing site-directed mutagenesis are needed to definitively map the binding interface and validate the contribution of key residues. Elucidating the precise molecular mechanisms downstream of ISG15 that lead to lysosomal dysfunction also represents a critical direction for future research. Addressing these questions will help establish a more robust foundation for targeting this pathway therapeutically.
Conclusion
In conclusion, NLRC5 promotes microglial inflammation and exacerbates post-stroke brain injury by stabilizing ISG15 and disrupting lysosomal function and autophagic flux. NLRC5-ISG15 axis is a therapeutic target for immune modulation in ischemic stroke.
