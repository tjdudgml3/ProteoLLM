Autophagy functions as a conserved metabolic pathway in most eukaryotic cells to eliminate detrimental or dysfunctional components, and it is highly induced upon stress conditions (
1
–
5
). This process involves the formation of a double-membrane compartment called an autophagosome for the engulfment and delivery of harmful materials into the vacuole. Multiple autophagy-related (ATG) proteins and non-ATG regulators are recruited for either autophagosome formation and/or cargo recognition. ATG8 family proteins are conjugated onto the autophagosome membrane and play a pivotal role throughout the autophagic process. In a general model for autophagosome development, the ATG machinery is activated and recruited to the phagophore assembly site for phagophore development. Subsequently, ATG8 proteins are conjugated to the phagophore membrane through a ubiquitin-like conjugation process, which is essential for cargo recognition and autophagosome closure. Autophagy can be classified into bulk (nonselective) autophagy and selective autophagy, with the latter targeting specific cargo, such as proteins or organelles, which are recognized directly or indirectly by ATG8 proteins through selective autophagic receptors (SARs) (
1
–
4
).
A variety of novel autophagic regulators containing ATG8-family interacting motif (AIM) or LC3-interacting region (LIR) have been identified and extensively studied in yeast, mammals, and plants (
6
,
7
). Additionally, noncanonical ATG8 binding modes independent of AIM have also been reported in recent years, such as UIM and sAIM in plants (
8
,
9
). These ATG8-binding proteins either collaborate with the ATG machinery to facilitate autophagosome formation or exert selectivity as SARs in selective autophagy, highlighting the intricate autophagy regulation network that governs cellular homeostasis in response to various stress conditions. NBR1 represents one of the extensively studied SARs in plants that binds to ATG8 in a classical AIM-dependent manner, and it has been implicated in multiple types of selective autophagy (
10
–
16
). In recent years, advances in proteomic analysis have allowed researchers to identify several novel plant ATG8-interacting proteins for different types of selective autophagy, like C53 in ERphagy, TraB in mitophagy, and clathrin in Golgiphagy (
17
–
19
). These findings have greatly advanced our understanding of selective autophagy in plants in response to diverse stress conditions. Furthermore, progress in studying ATG8-interacting regulators has provided insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying autophagosome formation, encompassing phagophore initiation, autophagosome maturation, and vacuolar delivery (
20
–
26
). Several studies have suggested that endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and ER contact sites, as well as ATG9 vesicles and COPII vesicles, serve as important membrane sources for autophagosome initiation, which requires the assembly of both ATG and non-ATG regulators (
27
–
32
). Recent advances in studying non-ATG regulators that bind to ATG8 have also shed new light on the underlying molecular mechanisms for autophagosome formation, ranging from phagophore initiation, autophagosome maturation, and vacuolar delivery (
20
–
26
). For instance, a plant-unique ESCRT (endosomal sorting complex required for transport) subunit, named FREE1/FYVE1, has been reported to function in autophagosome closure (
20
). Another ESCRT subunit ALIX has also been shown to undergo phase separation with CaLB1 that interacts with ATG8 for autophagosome maturation (
26
). Nevertheless, plant-specific ATG8-binding regulators for autophagosome initiation are largely unexplored, and the molecular properties and regulatory mechanisms underlying phagophore initiation in plants remain elusive.
By employing a proximity labeling approach using
Arabidopsis
ATG8e tagged with TurboID as bait, we found a protein family of unknown function, consisting of two homologs (AT4G02880 and AT1G03290). Both proteins contain coiled-coil domains that are predicted to be closely related to the mammalian ELKS/Rab6-interacting/CAST (ERC) protein family, which acts as a central scaffold for forming a multidomain protein complex involved in synaptic vesicle secretion (
33
–
35
). Due to the limited information available about this protein family in plants, we named these two proteins ERC1 and ERC2, respectively. Interaction mapping revealed a physical interaction between ERC1 and ATG8e through a C-terminal AIM-like motif of ERC1. We observed that ERC1 responds to autophagic induction and localizes onto the autophagosome membrane. Loss of ATG8 conjugation activity leads to overaccumulation of ERC1 and ATG8e on large spherical condensates. Using correlative-light electron microscopy (CLEM), immuno-electron microscopy (EM), 3D electron tomography (ET), and dynamic analysis, we elucidated the liquid-like property and morphological features of the ERC1 condensate, unveiling its complex spatial architecture and close association with the ER membranes. Furthermore, we demonstrate that ERC1 interacts with the autophagic receptor NBR1. Loss of ERC1 suppresses NBR1 turnover and the degradation of ubiquitinated substrates upon heat stress in
Arabidopsis
. Collectively, these findings suggest that ERC1 acts as a scaffold through biomolecular condensation with ATG8 and NBR1, likely serving as an assembly platform to enhance autophagy for plant heat resistance.

ERC1 Interacts with ATG8e via a C-terminal AIM.
To date, most reported ATG8-interacting proteins feature a short peptide known as AIM, also referred to as the LIR, characterized by a consensus sequence “W/F/Y-X-X-L/I/V” for binding to the LIR/AIM docking site (LDS) in ATG8 proteins (
3
,
37
,
38
). Since ERC1 displays a physical interaction with ATG8 proteins, we first searched for AIM-like motifs in ERC1. Based on the prediction from the iLIR tool (
https://ilir.warwick.ac.uk
) (
39
), we selected two AIM-like motifs with high scores for substitution mutation and tested with CNX-RFP-ATG8e in the recruitment assay. However, mutations in these two predicted AIM-like motifs did not disrupt the colocalization between ERC1 fusion proteins and CNX-RFP-ATG8e (
SI Appendix
, Fig. S1
D
). To further map the molecular determinants required for ERC1–ATG8 interaction, we generated several GFP-tagged ERC1 truncations (T1, T2, and T3) as depicted in
Fig. 2
A
, for a recruitment assay with CNX-RFP-ATG8e or colocalization analysis with mCherry-ATG8e. Interestingly, we observed that the C-terminal T2 truncation, but not the N-terminal T1 truncation, colocalized with CNX-RFP-ATG8e or mCherry-ATG8e (
Fig. 2
C
and
SI Appendix
, Fig. S1
E
). However, when the T3 truncation, which lacks a short C-terminal region, was coexpressed with CNX-RFP-ATG8e, their association was greatly reduced. We further conducted an in vitro pull-down assay to validate their interactions with ATG8e (
Fig. 2
B
). In agreement with the recruitment assay results, His-tagged ATG8e only interacts with the T2 truncation, but not with the T1 or T3 truncation.

Subcellular Localization of ERC1 in
Arabidopsis
Plants.
Next, we generated ERC1-GFP transgenic plants driven by a ubiquitin promoter and then crossed with the autophagosome marker mCherry-ATG8e to further characterize their subcellular association. As shown in
SI Appendix
, Fig. S2
A
, under normal conditions, ERC1-GFP primarily displayed a punctate pattern, partially overlapping with the autophagosome marker mCherry-ATG8e. We then subjected the seedlings to autophagic induction using Acibenzolar-S-methyl (BTH) treatment (
27
,
32
). As shown in
SI Appendix
, Fig. S2
A
, the number of puncta labeled by ERC1-GFP was significantly upregulated, with some displaying cup-shaped or ring-like structures that overlapped to varying degrees with mCherry-ATG8e. This suggests ERC1 might participate in different steps of autophagosome formation.
As most of the ERC1-positive puncta are devoid of ATG8e signals under normal conditions, we hypothesized that ERC1 might reside on other subcellular compartments. Therefore, we crossed ERC1-GFP with the Golgi marker (ST-RFP), the TGN marker (VHA-a1-RFP), or the PVC/MVB marker (mCherry-Rha1) for confocal observation. However, we barely detected pronounced colocalization between ERC1 and ST-RFP, VHA-a1-RFP, or mCherry-Rha1 (
SI Appendix
, Fig. S2
B
). In addition, when we applied Brefeldin A (BFA), which induces the formation of BFA bodies by targeting the GTPase involved in Golgi-related trafficking (
42
), the punctate pattern of ERC1-GFP seems unchanged (
SI Appendix
, Fig. S2
C
). When treated with wortmannin (Wort), a compound that inhibits PI3K activity and induces the formation of enlarged ring-like PVC/MVB structures (

SI Appendix

Our previous study showed that the recruitment of different ATG proteins occurs sequentially (

Hence, to gain more insights into the temporal relationship between ERC1 and ATG8 proteins during autophagosome formation, we conducted a real-time imaging analysis to monitor the dynamic behavior of ERC1-GFP and mCherry-ATG8e using a spinning disk confocal microscope. A representative montage is presented in

ERC1 Interacts with NBR1 and Responds to Heat Stress.
Recent studies in mammalian cells have suggested that biomolecular condensation of SARs, including p62 and NBR1, occurs prior to phagophore initiation, serving as an assembly platform for phagophore and ATG machinery (
52
,
53
). However, the assembly mechanism for phagophore initiation, as well as the related adaptors, remains poorly characterized in plants. Given that ATG8e proteins are directed to ERC1-positive condensate structures in the absence of ATG8 conjugation activities (
Fig. 4
A
), we hypothesized that ERC1-positive condensates might serve as an assembly site for ATG8 proteins and other adaptors before ATG8 proteins are conjugated onto the phagophore membrane. Typically, components within biological condensates are categorized as scaffolds and clients (
54
,
55
). In order to identify the potential clients for the ERC1-positive condensates, we generated transgenic plants expressing TurboID-tagged ERC1 for proximity labeling in the
atg7-2
background, which would allow us to identify the clients independently of autophagosome association. TurboID-coupled mass spectrometry analysis revealed that, in addition to ATG8e, another well-known SAR, NBR1, was significantly enriched in the neighboring proteome of ERC1 (
Fig. 6
A
). To explore the possible relationship between ERC1 and NBR1, we first carried out a recruitment assay using CNX-mCherry-ERC1 and YFP-tagged NBR1 variants. Notably, except the ΔUBA truncation, NBR1 FL and the UBA domains were both redirected by CNX-mCherry-ERC1 (
Fig. 6
B

Discussion
Autophagy requires a sophisticated regulatory network to exert accurate response to different environmental stimuli for maintaining cellular homeostasis. In this study, we have identified
Arabidopsis
ERC1 as a plant autophagic regulator that promotes autophagosome formation via biomolecular condensation. We demonstrated that the
Arabidopsis
ERC protein family directly interacts with ATG8e in an AIM-dependent manner (
Figs. 1
and
2
). Interaction mapping combined with the complex modeling revealed that an WDFF motif in the extended C-terminus of ERC1 is essential for ATG8 interaction (
Fig. 2
). The first W is a conserved residue in many canonical AIM sequences (
6
). Notably, the fourth F has only been implicated in the AIM sequence of yeast
Pichia pastoris
pexophagy receptor ATG30 and its other homologs, but has not been found in yeast
Saccharomyces cerevisiae
(
57
,
58
). This suggests the WDFF motif of ERC1 might represent a diversified AIM, conferring ATG8-binding specificity in plants. On the other hand, we still observed the signal of ERC1 condensates was encapsulated by that of CNX-RFP-ATG8e when the WDFF motif was removed or substituted (T4 and 2A), but an ERC1 truncation lacking a predicted IDR region (T3) displays a punctate pattern and is partially associated CNX-RFP-ATG8e (
Fig. 2
C
). One possible explanation is that phase separation of ERC1 also contributes to its binding affinity to ATG8 or the ER membrane, as both T4 and 2A contain the two IDRs. Additionally, given that ERC1 is a coiled-coil domain containing protein, which is known to form dimers or tetramers, the partial association between T3/T4/2A with CNX-RFP-ATG8e may be mediated by the self-interaction between T3/T4/2A with endogenous ERC1 in the cell. Finally, it is also possible that other intermediate molecule(s) or undefined interaction regions on ERC1 might contribute to their partial association with ATG8.
We further demonstrate that prior to ATG8 conjugation to the membrane, ERC1 is capable of forming liquid-like condensates with ATG8 and NBR1 proteins (
Figs. 4
and
6
). It has been previously reported that ATG8-positive large foci, likely to be protein aggregates, were occasionally observed in
atg4a/b
and
atg7-2
mutants (
12
,
59
). Our results reveal that these ERC1-positive condensates exhibit liquid-like behavior including fusion and diffusion (
Fig. 4
E
–
H
). In addition, ATG8 and NBR1 proteins are uniformly distributed in the ERC1 condensates, rather than associated only with the outer layer as a ring-like pattern, indicating fluidity within the ERC1 condensates (
Fig. 4
A
and
SI Appendix
, Fig. S3
). Furthermore, our ultrastructural analysis unveils a distinct spatial architecture of the ERC1-positive condensates, which exhibit close interaction with other membrane structures. Morphologically, ERC1-positive condensate structures are membraneless but closely associated with ER membranes (
Fig. 5
and
Movies S3
and
S4
). Remarkably, tiny vesicles were also clustered adjacent to or embedded within the ERC1 condensates, representing a previously undescribed multiphasic condensate in plant cells. However, one limitation of this study is that we primarily used the ERC1-GFP fusion proteins to assess the condensate property. It will be worthwhile for future studies to investigate the functionality of ERC1-GFP fusion proteins in the
erc1
mutant.
The interaction between ERC1 and the autophagic receptor NBR1 further outlines a possible mechanistic framework for cargo recruitment via biomolecular condensation prior to phagophore initiation (
Fig. 6
and
SI Appendix
, Fig. S5
). It is tempting to propose that prior to ATG8 conjugation, ERC1 functions as a scaffold to facilitate the cocondensation of ATG8, NBR1, and cargoes. This process subsequently recruits membrane sources, such as ER and ATG9 vesicles, for phagophore initiation. In mammalian cells, protein condensates have been reported to interact with membranes for either membrane recruitment or membrane remodeling for autophagosome formation (
52
,
53
). Recent findings have highlighted a close interplay between biomolecular condensation and membranes in plants. A recent study demonstrated that phase separation facilitated by the endocytic TPLATE complex on the cell membrane promotes the recruitment of other endocytic adaptors for clathrin-coated vesicle formation (
47
). Additionally, phase separation of VPS41, a homotypic fusion and protein sorting (HOPS) subunit, has been implicated in inducing the formation of VPS41-associated phagic vacuoles to modulate autophagy degradation (
60
). An endosomal sorting complex required for transport (ESCRT) protein, ALIX undergoes phase separation with CaLB1 to facilitate autophagosome closure (
26
). More recently, phase separation of the plant-specific ESCRT subunit FREE1 has been reported to drive membrane bending and scission for MVB biogenesis through a membrane-wetting interaction (
61
). Droplet-membrane wetting has previously been implicated in autophagosome formation in mammalian cells (
52
). Our dynamic and ultrastructural analysis reveals that ERC1 seems to undergo a transition from condensate to delineate the phagophore membrane, whereas the condensates are in close association with ER membranes and vesicles. This raises a possibility that ERC1 condensate might function in membrane remodeling through membrane wetting.
Another major finding of this study is that ERC1 modulates NBR1 turnover and plant resistance to heat stress (
Fig. 7
A
). Although the ATG8 lipidation level and the number of autophagosomal structures were comparable in the WT and
erc1-1
mutant under heat stress (
Fig. 7
C
–
E
), we observed that dysfunction of ERC1 leads to the accumulation of NBR1 and total ubiquitinated proteins (
Fig. 7
F
–
H
). It is possible that an alternative ERC-independent autophagic pathway(s) is induced under heat stress in the
erc1-1
mutant. A recent study reported that autophagic flux in the
nbr1-2
mutant is indistinguishable from that of WT upon heat stress, as YFP-ATG8e is still processed through bulk autophagy (
12
). It is very likely that, similar to NBR1, ERC1 is dispensable in bulk autophagy. It is noteworthy that
erc1-1
mutant is sensitive to nitrogen starvation and its autophagic flux is slightly reduced (
SI Appendix
, Fig. S7
C
–
F
). It remains possible that ERC1 still participates in general autophagy in a nonessential manner. Notably, neither NBR1 nor ERC1 dysfunction compromises the puncta formation of ERC1 or NBR1 (
Fig. 7
I
and
SI Appendix
, Fig. S5
C
). In the
nbr1-2
mutant, the ERC1-positive puncta exhibited a reduction in size following heat stress, but ring-like structures persisted and were capable of being processed into the vacuole (
SI Appendix
, Fig. S5
C
). On the other hand, we found that both the
erc1-1
and the
atg5-1
mutants exhibited much larger NBR1-positive puncta with a less pronounced effect in the
erc1-1
mutant (
Fig. 7
I
and
J
). Given that condensate size is modulated by the ratio of scaffolds to the clients, it is possible that ERC1 forms smaller condensate due to a reduction in client molecules like NBR1. Conversely, the degradation of NBR1 and its associated cargoes is delayed in the
erc1-1
mutant, which leads to the formation of larger puncta. Future investigation using an
erc1
x
nbr1
double mutant should be helpful to provide more insights into the assembly mechanism of NBR1 and ERC1. It has been noted that NBR1 is also engaged in heat stress memory in response to recurrent heat stress conditions (
11
). Considering the close relationship between ERC1 and NBR1 during heat stress, it will be interesting to investigate whether ERC1 contributes to heat stress memory.
The ERC protein family shares a high degree of homology in the coiled-coil domains with its orthologs in most representative land plant species (
Fig. 1
B
). The coiled-coil domain has been well known as scaffold for structural rigidity in molecular assembly (
62
). The mammalian ERC protein family (including ELKS1/ERC1 and ELKS2/ERC2) has been extensively implicated as an important scaffold in the active zone of neurons by forming cytoplasmic condensates with several major active zone proteins for capturing cargo and organizing synaptic vesicles (
63
,
64
). Another study reported that ELKS1 interacts with the cytoplasmic LC3 (the ATG8 homolog in mammals) independent of autophagy (
65
). However, different from the mammalian homolog,
Arabidopsis
ERC protein family encodes a shorter coiled-coil domain (
SI Appendix
, Fig. S1
B
) and lacks a unique C-terminal IWA motif that mediates the interaction of mammalian ERC with the presynaptic active zone protein RIM1 (
35
). Additionally, there is a highly conserved N-terminal region (in gray color) shared across most of the representative land plants (
Fig. 1
B
), described as a CUE (coupling of ubiquitin conjugation to ER degradation) domain for ubiquitin binding. The
Arabidopsis
ERC protein family was referred to as CESAR in a recent publication, showing that it might serve as a conserved SAR in the land plants, binding to ubiquitin for protein aggregate turnover under stress conditions (
66
). Undoubtedly, it will be interesting to further elucidate the interaction network of ERC/CESAR under other stress conditions or developmental stages. Additionally, whether condensate formation or ERC1–ATG8 interaction is a prerequisite for the involvement of ERC1 in autophagy promotion requires further investigation. It would be necessary to generate ERC1 mutant versions with reduced/impaired condensation capacity or ATG8 binding affinity to further decipher the role of ERC1 in autophagosome formation and stress tolerance in plants. In summary, this work uncovers an ERC1-mediated assembly mechanism via biomolecular condensation to promote NBR1 and ATG8 recruitment in response to heat stress conditions.
