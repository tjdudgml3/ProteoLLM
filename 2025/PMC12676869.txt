Background
Anti-N-methyl-D-aspartete (NMDA) receptor encephalitis, an autoimmune disease associated with IgG antibodies against the NMDA receptor (NMDAR) NR1 subunit [
1
], is a rare disease that mostly affects children and adolescents. An underlying neoplasm is detected in 25–40% of the individuals diagnosed with anti-NMDAR encephalitis (NMDAR-E), which are primarily young females, and 90% of the associated tumors are identified as ovarian teratomas [
2
]. Studies have shown a correlation between ovarian teratomas and NMDAR-E, with some cases specifically mentioning the presence of teratomas in patients diagnosed with the disease [
3
–
5
]. Our current understanding of the pathogenesis and molecular regulatory networks underlying NMDAR-E-associated ovarian teratomas remains limited. Transcriptional and proteomic profiling, which are powerful high-throughput technologies, have been widely employed to elucidate the molecular mechanisms of diseases [
6
,
7
]. The integration of transcriptomics and proteomics can comprehensively and thoroughly unravel the molecular regulatory networks of ovarian teratoma-associated NMDAR-E, to further explore the mechanisms underlying the development of this disease and identify potential biomarkers. The primary objectives of our study were to identify significantly differentially expressed genes and proteins to provide a preliminary molecular characterization of NMDAR-E associated with ovarian teratomas, thereby generating hypotheses on potential pathogenic mechanisms.
Briefly, total RNA and protein were extracted from the sample groups. A portion of the extracted material was utilized for library preparation and mRNA sequencing analysis; another portion was subjected to trypsin digestion and labeling, and then the same amount of labeled sample was mixed for chromatographic separation. RNA sequencing and LC-MS/MS analysis were subsequently performed. The bioinformatics analysis was conducted after screening the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and proteins (DEPs) between the CONTROL and NMDAR-E groups. Finally, the proteins of interest were selected for further validation using qPCR and western blotting. The technology workflow is illustrated in Fig.
1
.

Materials and methods
Ethics statement
This study was approved by the institutional ethics committee of Fudan Obstetrics and Gynecology hospital, Fudan University and Shanghai Key Laboratory of Female Reproductive Endocrine Related Disease, Shanghai, China (2021 − 114). All patients who donated teratoma tissues provided written informed consent, and the experiments were conducted according to approved protocols.
Patients and samples
In this study, the teratoma samples were collected from three patients (Patient IDs: P1, P2, and P3) diagnosed with mature ovarian teratomas with NMDAR-E (Sample ID: NMDAR-E-01, NMDAR-E-02 and NMDAR-E-03). NMDAR-E was diagnosed based on the criteria of Graus et al. [
8
] and neurological signs during the clinical progression. Clinical diagnoses were confirmed by a positive anti-NMDAR antibody titer in the patient’s cerebrospinal fluid. Another group of samples was collected from six patients (Patient IDs: P4, P5, P6, P7, P8 and P9) diagnosed with mature ovarian teratomas who did not have NMDAR-E (Sample IDs: CONTROL-01, CONTROL-02, CONTROL-03, CONTROL-04, CONTROL-05 and CONTROL-06). Samples were rinsed with cold PBS and frozen in liquid nitrogen immediately after surgical resection. All teratoma tissues were obtained from the patients at Obstetrics and Gynecology Hospital, Fudan University between April 2021 and June 2023.
mRNA sequencing
RNA isolation and library Preparation
Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, CA, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA purity assessments and quantification were performed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). RNA integrity was evaluated using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Subsequently, libraries were constructed using the VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Transcriptome sequencing and analyses were performed by OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).
mRNA sequencing analysis
The Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencing platform was utilized to sequence the libraries and generated 150-bp paired-end reads. Approximately 50 million raw reads were obtained per sample. The raw reads in FASTQ format were processed using the fastp [
9
] software (version 0.20.1) to remove low-quality reads, resulting in 48.66G clean reads for subsequent data analysis. The HISAT2 [
10
] software (version 2.1.0) was employed for reference genome alignment, and gene expression levels (FPKM) [
11
] were calculated, with the parameter of “--rna-strandness rf --fr”. The HTSeq-count [
12
] was used to obtain read counts for each gene, with the parameter of “-s reverse.” Principal component analysis (PCA) and visualization were conducted using R (version 3.2.0) to assess biological replicates. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 software (version1.22.2) [
13
]. Genes with a q-value < 0.05 and a fold change (FC) >2 or < 0.5 were defined as DEGs. Hierarchy clustering analysis of DEGs was carried out using the “pheatmap” (version 1.0.12) package in R, and gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis for [
14
] and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway [
15
] were performed by using the “clusterProfiler”(version 4.10.0) package [
16
], based on the hypergeometric distribution algorithm to identify significantly enriched functional terms. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was conducted using the GSEA software (version 4.1.0) [
17
], and the statistical significance and degree of enrichment were quantified using the nominal p-value and normalized enrichment score (NES). Bar plots, chord diagrams, or enrichment analysis circle plots were created using R for the significantly enriched functional terms by using the “ggplot2” (version 3.5.1) and “GOplot”(version 1.0.2) packages in R.
Protein extraction and digestion
Each sample was suspended in 300 µL lysis buffer and protease inhibitor PMSF, and tissues were sonicated on ice for 3 min. After sonication, the samples were centrifuged at 15,000 g for 15 min to remove particles, and this process was repeated once to further exclude precipitation. Finally, the supernatants were collected, the concentration of the protein were detected using the bicinchoninic acid assay method [
18
], and samples were aliquoted and stored at −80℃. Lysis buffer was used to adjust 50 µg protein of each sample to the same concentration and volume. DTT solution was added to the prepared protein solution at a final concentration of 5mM, and the solution was mixed and incubated at 55℃ for 30 min. A corresponding volume of iodoacetamide was added to the solution at a final concentration of 10 mM and incubated in the dark for 15 min at room temperature. Six times the sample volume of acetone were added to precipitate the protein, and the mixture stored at −20℃ overnight. The precipitate was collected by centrifugation at 8,000 g for 10 min at 4℃, and the acetone was volatilized for 2–3 min. Proteins were resuspended in 100 µL of 100 mM TEAB, and 1 mg/mL trypsin-TPCK was added to each protein sample and incubated at 37℃ overnight. The digested samples were lyophilized and store at −80℃.
Protein labeling and LC-MS/MS analysis
For iTRAQ labeling, the lyophilized samples were resuspended in 30 µL of 100 mM TEAB buffer and mixed with 140 uL of isopropanol at room temperature. The samples were vortexed, mixed, and centrifuged, and the steps were repeated once. Each sample was labeled with 76 µL iTRAQ reagent, and 100 µL of HPLC water was added and incubated with for 30 min to terminate the reaction. Samples were then lyophilized and stored at −80℃. Reversed-phase liquid chromatography (RPLC) was performed on an Agilent 1100 HPLC System with an Agilent Zorbax Extend RP column (150 mm ×2.1 mm, 5 μm). Mobile phases A (2% acetonitrile in HPLC water) and B (98% acetonitrile in HPLC water) were used for the RP gradient. The solvent gradients were set as follows: 0–8 min, 8% A; 8–8.01 min, 98%−95% A; 8.01–48 min, 95%~75% A; 48–60 min, 75 − 60% A; 60–60.01 min, 60 − 10% A; 60.01–70 min, 10% A; 70–70.01 min, 10–98% A; 70.01–75 min, 98% A. Tryptic peptides were separated at a flow rate of 300 µL/min and monitored at 210 and 280 nm. Samples were collected for 8–60 min, and the eluent was collected every minute in centrifugal tubes 1–15. Samples were recycled in this order until the end of the gradient. The separated peptides were lyophilized for mass spectrometry. The analyses were performed by a Q-Exactive HF mass spectrometer (Thermo, USA) equipped with a Nanospray Flex source (Thermo, USA). The samples were loaded and separated using a C18 column (15 cm × 75 μm) on an EASY-nLC 1200 system (Thermo Fisher, USA). The flow rate was 300 nL/min, with a 75-min linear gradient of mobile phase A (0.1% FA in water) and B (0.1% FA in ACN), as follows: 0–63 min, 5–45% B;63–65 min, 45–90% B; and 65–75 min, 90% B). Full mass spectrometry (MS) scans were acquired in the mass range of 300-1,600 m/z with a mass resolution of 60,000. The 20 most intense peaks on MS were fragmented using higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) with collision energy of 30. MS/MS spectra were obtained with a resolution of 15,000 with and AGC target of 2e5, and a maximum injection time of 80 ms. The Q Exactive HF dynamic exclusion was set for 30.0s and run under positive mode.
Database search and bioinformatics analysis
The raw data were searched thoroughly by ProteomeDiscoverer 2.4.1.15 (ThermoFisher), and the database search was performed with trypsin digestion specificity. The main parameters used to search database were set as Table S1. According to the criteria of unique peptide ≥ 1, all proteins with non-zero expression value were retained. The trusted proteins were identified after median normalization and log2 transformation. The DEPs were screened using the criteria of the global false discovery rate (FDR) of 0.01 and FC ≥ 1.2 or ≤ 1/1.2 (
P
-value < 0.05). Ferroptosis-related genes were obtained from the FerrDb database (
http://www.zhounan.org/ferrdb/current/

Western blotting
Total protein extracted from all samples was separated on 12% SDS-PAGE gels (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and then electro-transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. The membranes were blocked for 2 h at room temperature in TBST buffer containing 5% non-fat dry milk and then incubated overnight at 4℃ with different primary antibodies: GGH (13264-1-AP, 1:2000; Proteintech), FKBP11(A23048, 1:1000; Abclonal), SLC40A1(26601-1-AP, 1:1000; Proteintech), CCDC80 (bs-7992R, 1:1000; Bioss), ANK3(27980-1-AP, 1:1000; Proteintech), and ACTB (66009-1-Ig, 1:1000; Proteintech). The membranes were then incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody HRP (P8002, 1:10000; NCM Biotech) for 1 h at room temperature. The bands were detected with an enhanced chemiluminescence agent in the dark and analyzed using a ChemiDoc MP imaging system (Bio-Rad, CA, USA). The relative quantitative analysis of bands was performed using Image J software.
Histology and pathology conformation
Paraffin sections of the teratoma tissues from all the patients were obtained from the Department of Pathology of Obstetrics and Gynecology Hospital, Fudan University. The tumor location was carefully reviewed and evaluated independently by two pathologists.
A mature teratoma was defined as a tumor composed exclusively of mature tissues derived from two or three germ layers (ectoderm, mesoderm, and/or endoderm). The diagnostic criteria for a mature teratoma were a benign germ cell tumor composed of mature tissues from two or three germ layers (ectoderm, mesoderm and/or endoderm) (at least two germ layers) [
19
]. The most common form of necrosis was fat liquefaction necrosis, or infarction due to tumor ischemia (e.g. cyst torsion or local compression). There is no malignant tumor-related coagulative necrosis (malignant tumors grow rapidly, and new blood vessels are insufficient to supply tumor cells for survival and proliferation).
Statistical analysis
Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The Student’s t-test was used, and
P-
values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. GraphPad Prism 7.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) was used for the relative quantification of mRNA and protein levels. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± SD Measurement data were analyzed using the Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA, according to the data distribution. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used for normality testing and Levene’s test was used for homogeneity of variance testing. Count data were analyzed using the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test.
P-
values < 0.05 were considered to be statistically different, and “ns” represents no statistically difference. The mRNA sequencing data analysis process using R is detailed in
mRNA sequencing analysis
.
Study limitations and bias mitigation
Given the rarity of anti-NMDAR encephalitis (NMDAR-E) associated with ovarian teratomas, the present study was necessarily limited by a small cohort size (
n
= 9, including 3 NMDAR-E cases). To minimize technical and analytical bias, transcriptomic and proteomic data were subjected to rigorous normalization, quality control, and batch effect correction. Histological evaluations were independently performed by two experienced pathologists in a blinded manner, and inter-observer discrepancies were resolved by consensus. While these measures aimed to enhance reliability, we acknowledge that the exploratory nature of the study, the absence of normal ovarian tissue controls, and the limited sample size remain important constraints. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings and underscore the need for validation in larger, independent cohorts.

Combined analysis reveals Inhibition of ferroptosis in NMDAR-E-associated ovarian teratomas
The nine-quadrant plot is a graphical representation that facilitates the examination and comparison of the differential ploidy of transcriptome genes and protein expression in the proteome. The vertical coordinates correspond to the log2 values of the differential ploidy of transcriptome genes, and the horizontal coordinates represent the log2 values of the differential ploidy of the protein expression in the proteome. The dashed line on the horizontal coordinate represents the FC threshold for the transcriptome, while the dashed line on the vertical axis represents the FC threshold for the proteome. Genes or proteins outside these threshold lines were considered significantly differentially expressed, while those within the threshold lines are considered non-significantly differentially expressed. The concordance or discordance between gene and protein expression changes could be determined by examining the positional relationship of genes and proteins within the nine quadrants. As shown in Fig.
6
A, quadrants 1, 2, and 4 showed a lower protein abundance than RNA abundance. Quadrants 3 and 7 showed a correspondence between RNA and the associated proteins. Quadrant 5 showed no significant difference in expression between proteins and RNA. Quadrants 6, 8, and 9 showed a higher protein abundance than RNA abundance, which could be attributed to post-transcriptional or translational regulation.

Discussion
NMDARs play a crucial role in modulating synaptic transmission, synaptic plasticity, neuroexcitotoxicity, learning and memory [
23
]. Previous studies have highlighted the clinical association between ovarian teratomas and NMDAR-E [
24
,
25
], but little is known about the molecular mechanisms.
Our integrative research suggests potential involvement of ferroptosis, immune regulation, and antigen presentation pathways. Through a combined RNA-seq and proteomic analysis, we identified five candidate genes and proteins, including SLC40A1, GGH, FKBP11, CCDC80 and ANK3, which provided preliminary molecular signatures for expression patterns that were consistent with the sequencing results using qPCR and western blotting.
SLC40A1
encodes for the solute carrier family 40 member 1 (SLC40A1), also known as ferroportin. This transmembrane protein is primarily responsible for the export of cellular iron and, plays a crucial role in iron homeostasis. Dysfunction or mutations in
SLC40A1
can disrupt iron metabolism and has been associated with ferroptosis [
26
]. NMDAR activation has been shown to induce ferroptosis in vascular endothelial cells via the PP2A-AMPK-HMGB1 pathway, indicating a potential mechanistic coupling between NMDAR signaling and iron-dependent cell death [
27
]. However, there is currently no direct evidence linking ferroptosis to anti-NMDAR encephalitis in published studies, our findings raise the hypothesis that altered iron regulation via SLC40A1 may contribute to the pathogenesis of NMDAR-E, which warrants further functional investigation.
In this study, we focused primarily on differential expression and bioinformatics analyses of ferroptosis-associated proteins, specifically SLC40A1. However, it is important to emphasize that this approach does not establish a direct causal link between NMDAR-E-associated ovarian teratomas and ferroptosis. Owing to the rarity of cases and the limited availability of resources, we were unable to perform further functional assays to validate the biological relevance of these findings. This is a notable shortcoming of the present study. More comprehensive functional studies are needed in the future to better elucidate the potential role of ferroptosis in the disease pathogenesis.
As for the other DEPs,
GGH
encodes gamma-glutamyl hydrolase, which is a key enzyme in the folate metabolism pathway [
28
]. Studies have indicated that gamma-glutamyl hydrolase may play a role in the development of certain neurodegenerative conditions [
29
], but further research is needed to determine the exact mechanisms involved. In our study, FKBP11 was highly expressed at both the mRNA and protein levels in the NMDA-E group. FKBP11 has been linked to autoimmune diseases, such as systemic lupus erythematosus. Moreover, CCDC80 is involved in various cellular processes, including the inhibition of cell migration [
30
], glycosaminoglycan binding activity, and extracellular matrix organization [
31
]. Our study indicated a possible connection between CCDC80 and NMDAR-E-associated ovarian teratomas through cell adhesion or the extracellular matrix, which requires further research and validation.
Another potential biomarker is ANK3, which facilitates the connections between integral membrane proteins and the spectrin-actin cytoskeleton. Several genome-wide association studies have confirmed that multiple SNPs in ANK3 are associated with bipolar disorder, schizophrenia and post-traumatic stress disorder [
32
,
33
].
Furthermore, there were 2,524 DEGs between the NDMAR-E and CONTROL groups. However, only 34 DEPs were found, suggesting that translational regulation occurs after transcription. Posttranscriptional modifications, such as N6-methyladenosine (m6A), play a crucial role in autoimmune diseases. These modifications regulate posttranscriptional gene expression and are important regulators of autoimmune responses [
34
]. Nevertheless, studies investigating posttranscriptional modifications related to NMDAR-E-associated ovarian teratomas are lacking. Further research is needed to fully understand the mechanisms by which posttranscriptional modifications contribute to autoimmunity and identify potential therapeutic targets.
In the present study, we used transcriptional and proteomic data to deduce various biological processes and pathways associated with this disease. qPCR results demonstrated that the DEGs GGH, FKBP11, and CCDC80, were upregulated in the NMDARE group, whereas SLC40A1 was downregulated. This finding was further supported by the western blotting results, which revealed high protein expression levels of FKBP11 and CCDC80 and low expression levels of SLC40A1 and ANK3. Based on the integration of our results with the aforementioned description, we hypothesize that these genes may serve as biomarkers for NMDAR-E. Nonetheless, it is important to acknowledge that the limited sample size of this study may have introduced certain limitations and experimental flaws. NMDAR-E is a rare disease that occurs most frequently in children and adolescents; however, the exact incidence is unknown. Studies have shown that the incidence of NMDAR-E in patients with ovarian teratomas is from 1.17% [
35
]−8.3% [
36
], indicating that the incidence of this disease may be underestimated and the actual incidence may be higher. Moreover, during the bioinformatics analysis, we identified outliers and potential sources of variability, which can arise from various factors. Biological variability, such as genetic differences or sample heterogeneity, can introduce outliers, and technical factors, including batch effects and measurement errors, may further contribute to their presence. Additionally, inadequate normalization or data processing, along with low expression levels prone to noise, can generate unreasonable results. Statistical artifacts, such as false positives from multiple tests, and small sample sizes can exacerbate this problem. With small sample sizes, natural variability in the data can cause individual data points to appear as outliers, even if they are not true reflections of biological differences. Hence, further investigations with a larger sample size are warranted to validate the current findings, considering the intricate etiology and mechanisms underlying NMDAR-E-associated ovarian teratomas.
Conclusions
In conclusion, our study integrated transcriptomic and proteomic analyses to provide a preliminary molecular characterization of ovarian teratomas associated with NMDAR-E. These findings offer preliminary insights into potential pathways, such as iron regulation and immune activation, and generate hypotheses for further mechanistic studies. However, given the small sample size and the lack of functional validation, these results should be interpreted with caution. Future research will involve the validation of our findings using a larger cohort, as well as the implementation of experimental models to further elucidate the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms of NMDAR-E-associated ovarian teratomas.
