Introduction
Polyhydroxybutyrate (PHB) and related polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHAs) are natural polyesters produced by diverse prokaryotic species, serving mainly as a reservoir for carbon and energy. These polymers have attracted interest in academia and industry as plastic materials due to their biodegradability and production from renewable resources [
1
].
PHAs are accumulated in the cytoplasm as insoluble inclusions (granules) that can constitute up to 90% of the cellular dry weight [
2
]. The granules are constituted by a core of polymer surrounded on its surface by a group of granule‐associated proteins (GAPs). Some of these are the enzymes involved in PHA metabolism, either in its synthesis or its degradation [
2
,
3
,
4
]. In addition to these enzymes, there are other GAPs termed phasins (
phbP
or
phaP
genes). Phasins are small proteins composed of a hydrophobic domain associated with the surface of PHB granules and a hydrophilic domain exposed to the cytoplasm [
5
,
6
]. Despite not presenting apparent catalytic activity, they constitute a major component of the granules and play important roles in their formation. The function of the most abundant phasins is structural, forming a barrier between the hydrophobic polymer and the cytoplasm, thus preventing the nonspecific binding of other proteins to the surface of the granules and avoiding granules coalescence [
5
,
6
]. In relation to this role, these phasins have been shown to affect the surface/volume ratio of the granules, thus determining the number and size of the granules in the cell [
7
,
8
,
9
]. Other phasins have been shown to drive granule segregation during cell division [
10
,
11
,
12
].
Phasins also function in the mitigation of bacterial stress [
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
]. For example, the phasin PhaP
Az
from
Azotobacter
sp. FA‐8 has a stress‐reduction action against oxidative stress and heat shock. This role is independent of the synthesis of PHB and also occurs when the phasin is expressed in recombinant
Escherichia coli
, even reducing the expression of stress‐related proteins [
13
,
15
]. These phasins have been shown to have chaperone‐like activities, so the stress‐protective effect could be related to this capacity [
13
,
15
,
18
], in addition to the stress‐relieving role of PHB that provides energy and reducing power to support different physiological processes [
5
,
6
]. In addition, some phasins influence PHB metabolism by affecting the expression of biosynthetic or depolymerizing enzymes or other phasins (reviewed in Velázquez‐Sánchez et al. [
19
]), or by effects on their activity, as has been documented for the PHA synthase [
5
,
6
,
18
,
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
], or PHA depolymerizing enzymes [
7
,
27
]. However, for many phasins—especially the less abundant ones—their specific functions remain undetermined.
A. vinelandii
is a bacterium known for its high accumulation of PHB and exceptionally high molecular weight, producing more resistant plastics [
28
]. Three phasins have been previously identified in the PHB granules of
A. vinelandii
: PhbP1 (
avin_23670
or
phbP1
), PhbP2 (
avin_03930
or
phbP2
), and PhbP3 (
avin_34720
or
phbP3
) [
3
,
8
]. Canales‐Herrerias [
29
] demonstrated that PhbP1 serves as the principal phasin in the PHB granule of
A. vinelandii
, affecting the number and size of granules. However, the structural and functional roles of the
Azotobacter vinelandii
phasins PhbP2 and PhbP3—both of which are present in the PHB granule, albeit in minor amounts—have not yet been determined. Therefore, this study aimed to characterize these phasins and evaluate their impact on the PHB metabolism of
A. vinelandii
. This study aims to provide a better understanding of the mechanisms through which these proteins influence PHB accumulation and could consequently contribute to the design of improved strains capable of producing more PHB or improving specific characteristics such as molecular size.

2.2
Construction of Plasmids and Mutant Strains
Gene amplification was carried out using Phusion High‐Fidelity DNA Polymerase (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Restriction enzymes and DNA purification kits were also obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific and used according to the manufacturer′s instructions. Plasmid isolation and DNA purification were performed according to standard protocols.
A. vinelandii
strain OP was used as the wild‐type reference. To construct the OP‐PhbP2⁻ strain, the
phbP2
gene was PCR‐amplified from OP genomic DNA using oligonucleotides FW3930PB and RV3930MPB, and cloned into the EcoRV site of vector pJET1.2, yielding plasmid pJET‐P2. A tetracycline resistance cassette from plasmid pHP45Ω‐Tc [
31
] was inserted into a BstXI site within
phbP2
, generating plasmid pJET‐P2::Tc. This plasmid was linearized with ScaI and used to transform competent
A. vinelandii
OP cells. A tetracycline‐resistant transformant was selected and designated OP‐PhbP2
−
. Its genotype was confirmed by PCR using the same primer pair.
For OP‐PhbP3
−
, the
phbP3
gene was amplified using oligonucleotides Av34720F and Av34720R, cloned into the EcoRV site of pJET1.2, and the
phbP3
gene in the resulting plasmid (pJET‐P3) was disrupted with a kanamycin resistance cassette from plasmid pBSL99‐Km [
32
], inserting it into an internal HincII site, forming pJET‐P3::Km. This construct was linearized with XmnI and used to transform competent OP cells. A kanamycin‐resistant transformant was selected (OP‐PhbP3⁻) and verified by PCR using primers PCR34720F and PCR34720R. To construct complemented strains OP‐PhbP2C and OP‐PhbP3C, the
phbP2
and
phbP3
genes were amplified using PhbP2com‐F/R and PhbP3com‐F/R primers, respectively. The resulting PCR products were cloned into pJET1.2 to generate pJETP2com and pJETP3com. Inserts were excised using BamHI and XbaI and subcloned into plasmid pLA07, which contains a σ⁷⁰ promoter and the ATG start codon of a gentamicin resistance gene [
33
], yielding pLA07‐P2com and pLA07‐P3com. These were then digested with BglII, and the fragments containing
phbP2
or
phbP3
under the σ⁷⁰ promoter were cloned into plasmids pUMA‐Tc and pUMA‐Km, which allow chromosomal integration into the
melA
locus of
A. vinelandii
[
34
]. The resulting plasmids—pUMA‐P2com and pUMA‐P3com—were sequenced to confirm the absence of mutations, then linearized with PvuI (pUMA‐P2com) or ScaI (pUMA‐P3com) and transformed into the respective mutant strains. Double crossover integration was confirmed by PCR in the resulting complemented strains.
To evaluate the effect of
phbP2
and
phbP3
expression on PHB synthesis in
E. coli
, the following plasmids expressing the
Avinelandii phbBAC
operon and the
phbP2
or
phbP3
phasin genes were transformed into
E. coli
BL21 (DE3) competent cells. The
phbBAC
operon was amplified using primers phbB_EcoRI_FW and phbC_HindIII_RV, cloned into pJET1.2 (yielding pJGphbBAC), and excised with EcoRI and HindIII for subcloning into vector pMPM‐T6Ω, generating pMPMT6/phbBAC. This plasmid was used to transform
E. coli
BL21 (DE3). A tetracycline‐resistant transformant was selected and named strain BAC. BAC cells were further transformed with empty vector pET24a(+) to generate strain BAC‐pET, resistant to tetracycline and kanamycin.
For
E. coli
BAC‐P2, the
phbP2
gene was amplified with primers fw3930C and rv3930C and cloned into pET24a(+), yielding plasmid pJG03930. BAC was transformed with this plasmid to obtain a double‐resistant transformant (BAC‐P2).
For
E. coli
BAC‐P3, the
phbP3
gene was amplified using primers EAv34720F and EAv34720R, cloned into pJET1.2 (pJET‐P3E), and a NdeI‐XhoI fragment containing
phbP3
was subcloned into pET24a(+), producing plasmid pET‐P3E. BAC cells were transformed with pET‐P3E to obtain strain BAC‐P3.
The plasmids pMPMT6/phbBAC, pUMA‐P2com, pUMA‐P3com, pJG03930, and pET‐P3E were sequenced to confirm the absence of mutations.

2.4
Electron Microscopy Assay
The
A. vinelandii
cells were grown in BS medium supplemented with MgSO
4
. After 48 h (late‐stationary‐phase), 30 mL of culture was harvested and centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 7 min; the supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was washed with 0.2 M sodium cacodylate buffer, pH 7. Subsequently, the cells were fixed for 2 h with a solution containing 1X PBS, 0.1 M sodium cacodylate, and 3% glutaraldehyde at pH 7. Finally, the samples were centrifuged three times for 5 min at 1500 rpm and stored at 4°C for further processing. The samples were processed at the Microscopy Unit of the Institute of Neurobiology at UNAM, using an RMC MT‐x ultramicrotome and a JEOL JEM‐1010 transmission electron microscope.
2.5
Evaluation of the PHB Phenotype of the Mutants and Complemented Strains
PHB accumulation in the liquid medium of the different
A. vinelandii
mutants were analyzed and compared with the OP (WT) strain. It was determined as follows: strains were grown at 30°C and 200 rpm for 48 h (late‐stationary‐phase) in 250 mL flasks containing 50 mL of BS medium supplemented with MgSO
4
and the corresponding antibiotic. Samples were harvested for quantification of total protein concentration and PHB accumulation. Protein determination was performed using the method described by Lowry et al. [
36
], and the PHB content in the bacteria was assessed using the spectrophotometric method of Law and Slepecky [
37
].
2.6
Purification of PHB Granules From
A. vinelandii
A. vinelandii
PHB granules were purified using glycerol gradients [
38
]. Cells were grown for 48 h (late‐stationary‐phase) in 1.8% BS medium supplemented with 9.1 mM MgSO
4
and were collected by centrifugation at 6900
g
for 7 min. Cells were resuspended in 50 mM TRIS‐HCl, pH 7.5, and lysed with a French press at 900 PSI. PHB granules were purified by two discontinuous density gradients of glycerol in 50 mM TRIS‐HCl, pH 7.5: 87% and 50%; and 87%, 80%, 60%, and 40% glycerol. Gradients were centrifuged using an Ultra‐Clear ultracentrifuge (Beckman) at 68,589
g
at 4°C for 40 min. PHB granules were recovered and stored at 4°C until use.
2.7
Determination of the PHB Synthase Activity
For the determination of PHB synthase activity, 5 mL of cultures of strains grown for 48 h (late‐stationary‐phase) were harvested by centrifugation. The cells were washed with 10 mM MgSO
4
and resuspended in 1 mL of 25 mM potassium phosphate buffer, pH 7.2, 137 mM NaCl, 20 mM KCl, and 1 mM EDTA. The cells were lysed by ultrasonication treatment at 4°C (6 cycles of 15 s ON/30 s OFF at 6 W, Virtis Virsonic 60), and cellular debris was removed by centrifugation at 8647
g
for 20 min at 4°C. The total protein in the cell extract was quantified using the method described by Lowry et al. [
36
]. PHB synthase activity was determined by spectrophotometric assay at 412 nm and 29°C, quantifying the CoA released. The reaction mix contained 10 μL of soluble cell extract (≈0.03 mg of protein), 25 mM of potassium phosphate buffer pH 7.0, 0.1 mM of 5,5‐dithio‐bis‐(2‐nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB), and 55 μM of β‐hydroxybutyryl‐CoA substrate. The reaction was started by adding DTNB and the β‐hydroxybutyryl‐CoA substrate. The free thiol of the CoA produced cleaves DTNB, realising the yellow‐colored 5‐thio‐2‐nitrobenzoate, measurable at 412 nm. The assays were performed in 96‐well plates on the Synergy H1 microplate reader, with a total reaction volume of 200 μL. One unit was defined as the activity to produce 1 nmol of TNB anion per minute (ε
412
: 13,600 M
−1
cm
−1
) [
3
,
28
,
39
].
2.8
Determination of the PHB Depolymerase Activity
PHB depolymerase activity was analyzed by spectrophotometric measurement of the change in turbidity at 650 nm of a PHB granule suspension using a Synergy H1 microplate reader. The decrease in absorbance was considered PHB degradation [
27
]. The assay was performed using native PHB granules containing the GAPs, purified by density gradients from late‐stationary‐phase cultures of OP, OP‐PhbP2⁻, OP‐PhbP2C, OP‐PhbP3⁻, and OP‐PhbP3C strains. The PHB granules were dialyzed at 4°C in 50 mM Tris‐HCl buffer, pH 7.5, for approximately 12 h to remove glycerol from the samples. The reaction mix contained 300 μL of pure PHB granules (45 μg of PHB) in 50 mM Tris‐HCl buffer pH 7.5, adjusted to an OD
650nm
of 0.9, equivalent to 0.055 mg of GAPs per mL. The degradation of PHB granules was measured through the decrease in absorbance at 650 nm at 37°C, using a calibration curve. One unit of PHB depolymerase activity was defined as the micrograms of polymer degraded per mg of protein per minute (μg of PHB degraded mg protein
−1
min
−1
).
2.9
GAPs Analysis by SDS‐PAGE
Pure PHB granules, derived from 150 mL of culture in late‐stationary‐phase, were dialyzed in 50 mM Tris‐HCl buffer, pH 7.5, to remove glycerol used during the purification process. For the identification of total GAPs, SDS‐PAGE‐Tricine was performed with three layers: a stacking gel, a spacer gel, and a separating gel, to achieve better resolution of the proteins [
40
]. A total of 400 µg of protein was loaded into each well, as quantified using the Bradford method [
41
]. For sample preparation, lysis buffer was added, and the samples were heated at 95°C for 10 min. The gel was run at 100 V for approximately 10 h and then stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue to visualize the proteins.
For the identification of proteins of specific bands in the gel, a glycine SDS‐PAGE12% was used, in which 60 µg of proteins from the purified native PHB granules were loaded.
2.10
LC–MS/MS Exploratory Analysis of the PHB Granules
Sample preparation: Native PHB granules from late‐stationary‐phase cultures containing 400 μg of GAPs were loaded onto a discontinuous SDS‐PAGE‐Tricine, divided into three fractions with different concentrations of polyacrylamide, allowing both large and small proteins to be separated.
The separated GAPs were initially digested in‐gel using a 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate buffer. First, a reduction step was carried out by incubating for 45 min with 10 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) at 60°C. This was followed by an alkylation step with 55 mM iodoacetamide (IAA) for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. Next, trypsin digestion was performed using Trypsin Mass Spec Grade (Promega) with an overnight incubation at 37°C. After digestion, the peptides were desalted employing a ZipTip C18 (Sigma Aldrich), concentrated in a Speed Vac, and acidified with 1% formic acid, achieving a final concentration of 0.1%.
LC–MS/MS Analysis: Peptide separation was achieved using a Thermo Scientific Dionex UltiMate 3000 RSLC system equipped with a PepSep C18 column (150 µm × 8 cm, 1.5 µm particle size, 100 Å pores; PepSep, Denmark) maintained at 40°C. A 5 µL injection, corresponding to 1 µg of total peptide, was performed. Chromatographic separation was carried out over a 60‐min run at a flow rate of 0.520 µL/min using two mobile phases: Phase A consisted of water with 0.1% formic acid, and Phase B was 80% acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid. The separated peptides were then analyzed on an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with the following parameters: a spray voltage of 1.8 kV, an RF lens setting of 46%, and an ion transfer tube temperature of 275°C. Operating in data‐dependent acquisition mode, a full MS scan was recorded in the Orbitrap over an m/z range of 375–1600 at a resolution of 60,000 (at m/z 200), with an automatic gain control (AGC) target set to 4 × 10⁵ and an ion filling time of 50 ms. In a 3‐second cycle, the most intense ions with charge states +2 and +3 were selected using a 1.2 m/z isolation window and fragmented via collision‐induced dissociation (CID) at 30% normalized collision energy. The resulting fragments were detected in the IonTrap operating at a rapid scan rate. For the MS/MS scans, the AGC target was adjusted to 5 × 10³ with an ion filling time of 35 ms, and dynamic exclusion was applied for 50 s within a 10‐ppm mass window. This LC–MS/MS analysis was performed using duplicate samples.
2.11
PHB Synthesis in
E. coli
To determine PHB synthesis in
E. coli
, the inocula were grown at 37°C/overnight/200 rpm. These cultures were then used to inoculate 50 mL of LB medium supplemented with 2% glucose, and they were incubated at 30°C/200 rpm until reaching an OD
600nm
of 0.5–0.7. Arabinose at a final concentration of 10 mg mL
−1
was added to induce the
phbBAC

phbP2
and
phbP3

2.12
Aklylresorcinols Production
The strains were grown in encystment‐inducing medium solid (Burk butanol medium) for 48 h (late‐stationary‐phase). Then, the cells were collected, and a fraction of the pellet was used to quantify protein content by the Lowry method, while the rest was used for alkylresorcinols extraction. This was done by extracting the wet biomass three times with acetone to obtain the lipid extracts, then they were concentrated at 60°C using an Eppendorf Vacufuge Plus centrifuge. Finally, determination was made by Fast Blue spectrophotometric method. The samples and the orcinol standard curve were read at 520 nm [
42
].

4
Discussion
Moreno et al. [
8
] characterized that the main phasin PhbP1 of
A. vinelandii
has a role as a barrier preventing the coalescence of the granules, thus determining the number and size of the granules, as has been shown for other main phasin proteins [
7
,
8
,
9
,
46
,
47
]. The present results provide some insights into the roles of two additional phasins present in
A. vinelandii
.
The two new phasins have low overall sequence similarity, although PhbP1 and PhbP2 share a conserved Phasin_2 domain. This suggests a diversification of functions within these proteins. This is also suggested by the differences in their relative abundance in the granules, with phasin PhbP1 being the most abundant protein and PhbP2 and PhbP3 of low abundance (Figure
1
). PhbP1 and PhbP2 belong to the same family, but for phasin PhbP3 the lack of sequence homology and absence of the Phasin_2 domain indicate that it represents a novel phasin family, with potentially different functional properties. This new family of phasins is only present in
Azotobacter
spp. and a few genera of Gammaproteobacteria (Figure
3C
). In all cases, the products were annotated as hypothetical. None of these proteins has been studied, and therefore, they have not been related to PHB metabolism. Interestingly, in most of these bacteria, these genes are also linked to genes coding for putative PHAs depolymerases (annotated as
phaZ
in Figure
3C
), as in
A. vinelandii
, suggesting a possible involvement in PHAs degradation.
Other non‐canonical phasins described in bacteria also lack the Phasin_2 domain. For example, PhaM from
C. necator
not only binds to PHB granules but also mediates their attachment to the nucleoid, thereby controlling segregation during cell division [
11
,
12
]. Another example is phasin PhaH from
Caulobacter crescentus
, which contains a helix‐hairpin‐helix (H3TH) motif but lacks canonical phasin domains [
51
]. Both PhaM and PhaH share very low sequence identity with PhbP3 (15.5% and 11.6%, respectively) and, unlike PhbP3, they contain predicted DNA‐binding domains absent in PhbP3. In this context, PhbP3 broadens the known diversity of phasins, representing a novel subgroup restricted to
Azotobacter
spp. and certain Gammaproteobacteria.
Although PhbP3 belongs to a different and new family of phasins, it shares some structural characteristics with other phasins. It has hydrophobic regions (see Kyte‐Doolittle hydropathicity plots, Figure
4
[
45
]) that could be involved in the binding to the granules; it also has a secondary structure consisting of a‐helices and disordered regions [
55
]. Structural predictions (via AlphaFold) show helical structures, which could imply interaction capabilities, possibly with other proteins. It additionally has an alanine‐rich region at the C‐terminus predicted to be disordered, which is a structural characteristic of many phasins [
44
].
The observation that mutants lacking PhbP2 or PhbP3 do not differ from the wild‐type in granule morphology suggests that these low‐abundance phasins do not have a direct role in determining granule structure. This finding is consistent with previous studies reporting that those phasins affecting granule morphology are the main highly abundant proteins of the granules (e.g., PhbP1 in
A. vinelandii
or PhaP1 in
C. necator
[
8
,
9
]). Thus, only PhbP1, being the most abundant protein in the granules (Figure
1
), functions in the prevention of granule coalescence, influencing the size and number of the granules [
8
].
Moreno et al. [
8
] also showed that the inactivation of
phbP1
negatively affected the accumulation of PHB in
A. vinelandii
, as is the case with PhaP1, the main phasin of
C. necator
[
9
],
Azospirillum brasilense
Sp7 [
46
], and
Sinorhizobium fredii
NGR234 [
47
]. The differential effects on PHB accumulation—where disruption of
phbP2
leads to increased PHB levels, while that of
phbP3
results in reduced accumulation—would imply that PhbP2 and PhbP3 may have opposing regulatory roles in PHB metabolism. However, both mutations had similar effects on the enzyme activities tested in cell extracts of the mutants, with PHB synthase activity remaining unaltered, but PHB depolymerizing activity diminished in both mutants. The significant decrease in PHB depolymerase activity in the OP‐PhbP2
‐
and OP‐PhbP3
‐
mutants indicates that these phasins are important for promoting PHB degradation. This effect is consistent with the changes observed in the molecular weight distribution of PHB in mutants with altered expression of these two phasins [
56
]. This role is in line with findings in other organisms, such as the enhancement of depolymerase activity by the phasin ApdA in
Rhodospirillum rubrum
[
27
]. The importance of a phasin protein for normal PHB degradation was also observed for the phasin PhbP1 of
Cupriavidus necator
, because the corresponding mutants degraded PHB at a higher rate [
7
]. Therefore, PhbP2 and PhbP3 may enhance PHB depolymerase activity either by directly interacting with and stabilizing depolymerizing enzymes on the granule surface or by indirectly influencing the granule‐associated proteome. Further protein–protein interaction studies will be required to elucidate their precise mechanism of action. The decrease in PHB degradation could also have a relation with the localization of both
phbP2
and
phbP3
genes linked to genes coding for PHB depolymerases, suggesting possible coordinated regulation of gene expression. At least for the OP‐PhbP2
‐
mutant, the observed changes in PHB accumulation are likely due to the reduced PHB degradation, rather than impaired synthesis, but for the OP‐PhbP3
‐
mutant, a more complex mechanism is probably involved. It could be that PhbP3 may influence the activity or stability of biosynthetic enzymes (e.g., ketothiolase or acetoacetyl‐CoA reductase) by interacting with them, thereby affecting PHB synthesis, as suggested by co‐expression experiments of phasins with the biosynthetic operon in
E. coli
. Alternatively, the altered composition of GAPs observed in the mutant (Table
3
) could impair the proper organization or accessibility of the biosynthetic enzymes (Figure
10
).

The co‐expression of phasin PhbP2 or PhbP3 in recombinant

E. coli
by acting as a barrier that separates the PHB from the cytoplasmic components, and this could have an indirect effect on PHB production [
13

57
].

,

,
52
] (Table
3
). Concerning PHB‐related proteins, previous studies in
A. vinelandii
showed the presence of the PHB biosynthetic enzymes PhbB and PhbC [
3
,
8
]. In this study, we also found PhbA attached to the granules; therefore, all the biosynthetic steps could be directly catalyzed there.
Although a statistical analysis of the differences in abundance of the GAPs would be required, an altered protein composition of PHB granules was evident in the absence of PhbP2 and PhbP3, as suggested by SDS‐PAGE and mass spectrometry. This suggests that these phasins may be involved in organizing or anchoring other GAPs, as proposed by Pötter and Steinbüchel [
60
].
The changes in the GAP profile could have implications for granule functionality and may be related to the differences in PHB accumulation observed in the OP‐PhbP2⁻ and OP‐PhbP3⁻ mutants. In relation to the phenotype of reduced PHB depolymerization activity, despite the identification of two additional PHB depolymerases in the granules (Avin_44010 and Avin_27080) (Table
3
), no decrease in the abundance of any of the identified PHB depolymerases was detected. Thus, the diminished activity observed in the OP‐PhbP2⁻ and OP‐PhbP3⁻ mutants is likely attributable to effects on enzyme activity rather than on expression. Notably, PhbZ2 (Avin_34710), the PHB depolymerase encoded adjacent to
phbP3
, was not detected in the granules of any strain. PhbZ2 expression may be condition‐dependent, as we have observed for other PHB depolymerases of
A. vinelandii
(PhbZ1 and PhbZ3), which are differentially expressed depending on the growth phase or culture conditions [
3
]. Also concerning PHB utilization, the enzyme 3‐hydroxybutyrate dehydrogenase, catalyzing the second step in PHB degradation (oxidation of β‐hydroxybutyrate to acetoacetate), was present only in the granules of strain OP. This absence is coincident with the low activity of PHB depolymerization detected in the OP‐PhbP2⁻ and OP‐PhbP3⁻ mutants, which would agree with a low mobilization of the polymer. Interestingly, Avin_33510—a putative transcriptional regulator whose gene is adjacent to that of the PHB depolymerase Avin_33500 (PhbZ3), suggesting a regulatory relationship—was detected exclusively on the granules of strain OP. The absence of these two proteins in the OP‐PhbP2⁻ and OP‐PhbP3⁻ mutants could contribute to decreased PHB degradation in these strains.
Concerning proteins not directly related to PHB metabolism, we found several outer membrane proteins like OprF, OprI, OprL, and OprG‐2. Other studies have also found these kinds of proteins associated with the PHA granules [
49
,
52
]. It has been argued that this may be artefactual and due to the purification procedure [
61
]. However, for some of these proteins, their presence on the granules and a role in PHB physiology have been confirmed. This is the case for the lipoprotein OprL in
P. putida
[
49
] and OprI in
A
.
vinelandii
[
8
]; in both cases, the proteins have been previously found on the granules, and the corresponding mutants had a lower PHB production, indicating an unknown role in PHB metabolism.
Although many of the identified proteins are not related to PHB metabolism and are possibly the result of artifacts during granule purification, several others may be present in vivo—for example, those involved in pathways or processes related to central carbon metabolism—and their presence could have a functional role. In this regard, we identified the pyruvate dehydrogenase E1 component associated with the granules, although no considerable effect in their abundance was detected in the OP‐PhbP2⁻ and OP‐PhbP3⁻ mutants. The localization of this subunit of this enzymatic complex to the granules has been previously reported in
P. putida
, where interaction with the phasin PhaI was also documented. This localization was hypothesized to contribute to the carbon supply for fatty acid synthesis; interestingly, overexpression of the E1 subunit resulted in a significant increase in PHA production [
49
]. The location of phosphoenolpyruvate synthase or succinate dehydrogenase subunits in the granules of
A. vinelandii
could also have some metabolic significance.
Along similar lines, the presence of ArsA and ArsC proteins—enzymes involved in the synthesis of alkylresorcinols (ARs) in
A. vinelandii
(Table
3
)—was unexpected and remains to be confirmed. However, their localization may have biochemical significance, potentially linking PHB metabolism with the synthesis of these phenolic lipids, which are derived from acetyl‐CoA. Our findings suggest that phasins may influence the abundance of ARs biosynthetic enzymes on the granules. The increased abundance of ArsA and ArsC correlates with an increase in ARs levels in the OP‐PhbP2⁻ mutant, supporting the hypothesis of a possible link between the synthesis of these compounds and the catabolism of PHB on the granules, which may provide the precursors for their production.
Collectively, these findings contribute to a better understanding of the complex interplay among phasins in regulating PHB metabolism in
A. vinelandii
and open avenues for further investigation into the distinct roles of PhbP2 and PhbP3.
Beyond their fundamental biological roles, manipulating the expression, stoichiometry, and identity of phasins holds significant biotechnological potential for enhancing both the productivity and the material properties of PHB. Phasins can modulate the activity of PHB depolymerases, PHB synthase or enzymes involved in polymer metabolism, thereby influencing net polymer accumulation and turnover [
6
,
7
,
14
,
18
,
27
,
59
]. Consequently, overexpression or tuning of specific phasins can raise total PHA content and can shift molecular weight distributions, as has been shown specifically for both phasin PhbP2 and PhbP3 [
62
], and this can modify the thermomechanical properties of the polymer. In addition, some phasins can even influence the composition of the PHAs [
63
]. Altogether, the rational engineering of phasin combinations ‐or introducing heterologous phasins‐ offers a biotechnological route to tailor PHB/PHAs for target applications—balancing yield and material properties.
All these insights provide a solid foundation for further exploration of phasin diversity and function, as well as their potential applications in optimizing microbial bioplastics production.
