Introduction
RNA transcription is an essential mechanism of the cell. In eukaryotes there are 3 main nuclear DNA-dependent RNA polymerases (RNAPI, RNAPII, and RNAPIII) (
Sydow and Cramer 2009
;
Osman and Cramer 2020
); plants encode 2 additional RNA polymerases not strictly required for survival, the RNAPIV and RNAPV (
Onodera et al. 2005
;
Chen et al. 2018
). Among these, the RNAPII is responsible for the production of protein-coding transcripts. The core of RNAPII consists of highly conserved structural elements (
Sydow and Cramer 2009
;
Osman and Cramer 2020
). During the transcriptional elongation process, the incorporation of correct ribonucleotides promotes forward translocation of the transcriptional complex (
Nedialkov et al. 2003
;
Bar-Nahum et al. 2005
;
Sydow and Cramer 2009
), while transcriptional errors cause rearrangements of the active core leading to arrests and backtrackings (
Kettenberger et al. 2003
;
Nedialkov et al. 2003
;
Sydow and Cramer 2009
). The active core of RNAPII possesses an intrinsic 3′-5′ exonuclease activity, which can cleave off the incorrectly incorporated (di)nucleotide(s) upon arrests/backtrackings (
Kettenberger et al. 2003
;
Sydow and Cramer 2009
). The nuclease activity of RNAPII is potentiated by the elongation cofactor TFIIS (
Kettenberger et al. 2003
;
Ruan et al. 2011
). The C-terminal zinc-finger domain of TFIIS complements the RNAPII active site and accelerates the excision of mis-incorporated nucleotides (
Jeon and Agarwal 1996
;
Kettenberger et al. 2003
). Diverse conditions may alter the error rates of RNAPII. Mutagens or aging can cause error-prone transcription in yeast and animals (
Saxowsky et al. 2008
;
Chung, Verheijen, Navapanich, et al. 2023
). The presence of TFIIS markedly boosts RNAPII fidelity under oxidative stress or nucleotide depletion (
Grasser et al. 2009
;
Koyama et al. 2010
). Recent studies in plants have shown that TFIIS is required for both efficient transcription and proper alternative splicing (AS), but the molecular basis of its actions, particularly its contributions to transcriptional fidelity remained unexplored (
Grasser et al. 2009
;
Al Khateeb et al. 2019
;
Antosz and Deforges 2020
;
Obermeyer et al. 2022
;
Szadeczky-Kardoss et al. 2022
;
Ahmad et al. 2024
).
Despite the existence of co-transcriptional proofreading and other nuclear quality control mechanisms, some faulty transcripts remain undetected in the nucleus and are exported to the cytoplasm. These inaccurate mRNAs can be identified and eliminated by diverse cytoplasmic quality control systems. Nonsense-mediated decay (NMD) is a translation-termination-coupled eukaryotic mRNA quality control pathway that recognizes and destabilizes premature termination codon (PTC)-containing aberrant mRNAs (
Kurosaki et al. 2019
;
Luha et al. 2024
). NMD identifies PTC-containing mRNAs by the presence of NMD-eliciting cis elements including upstream open reading frames (uORF) or unusually long and/or intron-containing 3′UTRs (
Arciga-Reyes et al. 2006
;
Kertesz et al. 2006
;
Kerenyi et al. 2008
;
Kalyna et al. 2012
;
Kurosaki et al. 2019
). NMD is indispensable in higher eukaryotes (
Kurosaki et al. 2019
;
Luha et al. 2024
). In
Arabidopsis thaliana
, mutations of NMD factors (including UP FRAMESHIFT1 (UPF1), UPF3, SUPPRESSOR OF MORPHOGENESIS IN GENITALIA7 (SMG7)) cause a range of developmental abnormalities, even seedling lethality (
Arciga-Reyes et al. 2006
;
Riehs-Kearnan et al. 2012
). In plants, NMD efficiency is controlled by different regulatory circuits, including an autoregulation by UPF3 and SMG7 or by the translation termination factor eRF1-1 (
Kerenyi et al. 2008
;
Gloggnitzer et al. 2014
;
Degtiar et al. 2015
;
Nyiko et al. 2017
).
Frameshifts caused by insertions, deletions, or nonsense (STOP-codon generating) substitutions in the ORF can lead to the formation of PTC, thus 1 to 2 nucleotide transcription errors can induce NMD. Alternative splicing can also generate transcripts possessing NMD-eliciting features (
Lewis et al. 2003
;
Kalyna et al. 2012
;
Drechsel et al. 2013
;
Tabrez et al. 2017
;
Fair et al. 2024
). By destabilizing these aberrant transcripts, NMD prevents the production of potentially detrimental truncated proteins (
Gout et al. 2017
;
Fritsch et al. 2018
;
Chung, Verheijen, Navapanich, et al. 2023
;
Chung, Verheijen, Zhang, et al. 2023
). The role of plant NMD during abiotic stress conditions is understudied (
Kurosaki et al. 2019
;
Luha et al. 2024
). It was suggested that NMD regulates cytoplasmic protein response (
Sugio et al. 2009
), light signaling (
Shi et al. 2012
) or salt stress response (
Drechsel et al. 2013
;
Vexler et al. 2016
). Whether NMD has roles during heat stress (HS) adaptation has not been considered yet.
Heat is a major abiotic stressor that impacts growth, viability, and reproductive fitness of most organisms (
Liu and Charng 2013
;
Ohama et al. 2017
;
Charng et al. 2023
;
Bakery et al. 2024
). Elevated temperature damages cellular structures, impairs enzymatic pathways, and leads to proteotoxic stress through protein misfolding and aggregation. To relieve or revert the negative impacts of HS, several heat stress transcription factors (HSF) are activated. These subsequently drive the expression of secondary transcription factors, heat shock proteins (HSP), and other participants. These components assemble into a coordinated network to ensure proteostasis (
Liu and Charng 2013
;
Ohama et al. 2017
;
Charng et al. 2023
;
Bakery et al. 2024
). Different classes of ATP-dependent chaperones, including HSP70 and HSP90 are expressed in the early phase of HS and are involved in refolding of denatured proteins. Upon prolonged heat, the low molecular weight ATP-independent small HSP holdases (sHSP, 16 to 42 kDa) are produced; these bind to the unfolded proteins and keep them in a ready-to-refold soluble state, preventing their aggregation (
McLoughlin et al. 2016
;
McLoughlin et al. 2019
;
Waters and Vierling 2020
). Protein aggregates are disassembled by the interplay between disaggregase HSP101, the HSP70 chaperones, and different classes of sHSPs (
McLoughlin et al. 2016
;
McLoughlin et al. 2019
;
Waters and Vierling 2020
). Transcriptional mistakes and inaccurate splicing may lead to the generation of aberrant proteins on a global scale, enhancing the demand for HSP activities to preserve cellular protein homeostasis (
Vermulst et al. 2015
;
Chung, Verheijen, Zhang, et al. 2023
;
Xie et al. 2024
).
The biological relevance of transcriptional proofreading and transcriptome quality control during high-temperature adaptation has not been explored in plants. In this work, we use TFIIS and NMD mutant
A. thaliana
lines to gather evidence supporting the notion that TFIIS- and NMD-mediated cooperative safeguarding of transcriptional fidelity and correct AS are essential for proper development and HS adaptation in plants. Findings of the study establish transcriptome quality control mechanisms as pillars of HS adaptation and raise its relevance in agriculture and adaptation to climate change.
Results
To get an insight into transcription fidelity changes during HS, we performed a preliminary error rate survey on our previous RNAseq, in which we analyzed the transcriptomes of wild-type (
A. thaliana
Columbia-0 ecotype, Col-0) and
tfIIs-1
T-DNA insertion mutant plants grown under normal and HS conditions (
Szadeczky-Kardoss et al. 2022
) (
Supplementary Fig. S1
). Although in Col-0 the total error and the substitution rates were not changed (
Supplementary Fig. S1, A
and
B
), we observed increased insertion rate change during HS exposure (thermotolerance to moderately high temperatures regime, 1 day exposure to 37 °C, TMHT/1d; see Materials and Methods) that was further elevated in the
tfIIs-1
mutant (
Supplementary Fig. S1C
). Moreover, an increasing trend in deletions at TMHT/1d was also observed (
Supplementary Fig. S1D
). Most of the indels were of 1 to 2 nucleotide lengths (
Supplementary Fig. S1, E
and
F
), potentially leading to the production of PTC-containing aberrant transcripts. Even though error rate estimation by this approach is poor (
Fritsch et al. 2018
), these observations raise the following assumptions: (i) safeguarding transcriptional fidelity is needed, (ii) TFIIS regulates fidelity in plants, and (iii) the 1 to 2 nt indel containing transcripts may be posttranscriptionally regulated by NMD.
CirSeq unravels the transcriptional error landscape in
A. thaliana

bona fide
RNA errors. CirSeq is the most reliable method for transcriptional error estimation that was used to determine transcriptional error rates in several organisms, including yeast, worm, fly, mouse, or human cells, but not plants (
Acevedo et al. 2014
;
Vermulst et al. 2015
;
Fritsch et al. 2018
;
Chung, Verheijen, Navapanich, et al. 2023
;
Chung, Verheijen, Zhang, et al. 2023

(

TFIIS binds to RNAPII complex but not to other polymerases (

Fang et al. 2024

To unpick the spectrum of errors, we divided the errors into groups, such as substitutions, insertions, and deletions. In correlation with metazoan data (

The rate of insertions was increased in all
tfIIs-1

Fig. 1C

tfIIs;upf1
(vs

Next, we assorted the substitutions into exchange types (

,

-

We examined the type of indels: a significant ∼3-fold enrichment was observed for the insertion of adenine at the expense of the other nucleotides, especially in
tfIIs-1

Supplementary Fig. S4A
), while the type of deleted bases was random (
Supplementary Fig. S4B

The potential impact of transcription errors at proteome level
To estimate their downstream impact, we studied the effects of transcriptional mistakes on amino acid changes in silico. ∼25.8% to 31.4% of substitutions caused silent mutations (
Supplementary Fig. S5
). These originated mainly from nucleotide replacement at the third position of the codons. ∼63.4% to 70.5% of substitutions caused missense mutations, resulting in amino acid changes (
Supplementary Figs. S6
and
S7
). These changes were rooted in the combined consequence of the number of codons encoding the particular amino acid and the bias of erroneous transcription by RNAPII for a particular substitution: (i) amino acids encoded by multiple codons were converted as the consequence of a single nucleotide substitution (e.g. Ala→Ser, Gly→Val, etc.); (ii) the prevalent nucleotide exchanges have driven the particular amino acid changes such as C→U (e.g. Ala→Val, Pro→Leu, etc.), G→A (Ala→Thr, Glu→Lys, Val→Ile etc.), or G→U (e.g. Ala→Ser, Val→Phe, etc.). Some of the prevalent amino acid exchanges are converted into amino acids having functionally similar side chains, while other swaps may hold the potential to strongly impact the protein structure/activity (such as Asp→Arg, Glu→Lys, etc., converting the negatively charged side chain into a positive one) (
Supplementary Figs. S6
and
S7
). ∼2.3% to 8.0% of nucleotide substitutions lead to the generation of nonsense mutations (STOP codons) (
Supplementary Fig. S5
). These types of errors were surprisingly numerous, mainly generated by G→U changes (Glu→STOP, Gly→STOP), by C→U changes (Gln→STOP), or by G→A changes (Trp→STOP) (
Supplementary Figs. S6
and
S7
).
Distribution of transcriptional errors within the genome and along the transcripts
To discern the topology of transcriptional quality regulation, we investigated the genomic distribution of errors (
Fig. 2
). The transcriptional mistakes were evenly scattered throughout the entire genome: centromeric and pericentromeric regions are depleted due to the absence of RNAPII transcripts, not due to error rate changes (
Fig. 2A
). Errors were equally partitioned between the 5 chromosomes (
Fig. 2B
). We performed a gene ontology (GO) analysis of error-containing transcripts. The GO results reflect the transcription pattern of the given condition: under NT, transcripts supporting growth were enriched, while at high temperature (TMHT/1d) transcripts involved in heat acclimation were overrepresented (
Supplementary Table S1
). These results show that errors are characteristic of current transcriptomes and support the observation that errors are scattered randomly.

tfIIs-1

TFIIS genetically interacts with the NMD pathway
If both TFIIS and NMD contribute to the reduction of downstream negative effects of faulty transcription, then the TFIIS and NMD mutants should genetically interact. To analyze the interaction between the TFIIS and NMD pathways, we studied the development and HS-sensitivity of

Arciga-Reyes et al. 2006
). The
upf3-1
displays stronger developmental alterations, with well-marked rosette leaf shape and floral morphological changes, and slightly retarded growth compared to wild type (
Arciga-Reyes et al. 2006
) (
Fig. 4A
). The developmental phenotype of the
nmd

tfIIs;upf1

tfIIs;upf3
plants showed dramatic growth phenotype, they were stunted and failed to flower at normal temperature (LD, 21 °C). Their leaf shape alterations were similar as in the
tfIIs;upf1

tfIIs-1
(

and

NMD is needed for efficient HS response
In plants, biotic and abiotic stresses can modify NMD activity, but the roles of NMD in HS adaptation were not analyzed before (
Shi et al. 2012
;
Kurosaki et al. 2019
;
Luha et al. 2024
). To test if NMD maintains its activity during elevated heat, we measured the level of selected NMD targets in plants under control conditions or exposed to short-term (TMHT/1h) or persistent HS (TMHT/1d) and following two-day recovery period (RE) (see Materials and Methods).
eRF1-1
(
Nyiko et al. 2017
) and
SMG7
(
Nyiko et al. 2013
) are NMD-regulated transcripts bearing NMD-inducing introns in their 3′UTR, while
At3g53400
and
At5g45430
are NMD targets possessing uORFs (
Degtiar et al. 2015
). All tested NMD targets were maintained at low level in Col-0 and
tfIIs-1
(∼2-6-fold lower vs
upf1-5
or
upf3-1
background plants) suggesting that they are efficiently destabilized by NMD under ambient conditions (
Supplementary Fig. S12
). Moreover, in the NMD-functional lines (Col-0 and
tfIIs-1
), all these NMD targets remained at a low level throughout all heat treatments (∼3-6-fold lower vs
upf1-5
or
upf3-1
plants), showing that NMD functions during HS. The TFIIS and NMD factors (UPF1, UPF3) do not regulate each other at the transcriptional level during ambient or HS conditions (
Supplementary Fig. S13
), except that
UPF3
mRNA was increased in
tfIIs-1
background at TMHT/1d, implying a TFIIS-UPF3 regulatory loop.
TFIIS
mRNA HS-changes in Col-0 and

confirmed previous findings (

As shown, quantitative and qualitative differences of transcriptome may cause developmental alterations and/or HS-sensitivity of TFIIS and NMD mutants (

;

;

;

;

Discussion
Several studies on RNAPII transcriptional regulation have been published in yeast and metazoan, but plant systems have remained less explored despite their importance in view of climate change. Here, we demonstrate that TFIIS nuclear and NMD cytoplasmic quality control systems act cooperatively to safeguard transcriptome homeostasis in planta, and we evidence its biological relevance during development and stress adaptation in the dicot model
A. thaliana
.
The error landscape of Arabidopsis transcriptome is altered by environmental conditions
To understand transcriptome quality regulation, we examined transcriptional fidelity. For this, we implemented CirSeq analysis under NT and HS conditions and characterized the error content of Arabidopsis transcriptome at single-nucleotide resolution. Our error data are in strong correlation with independent findings (
Li et al. 2025
). In addition, we provide evidence that transcriptional errors are continuously generated and depend on internal/external conditions, including HS and nucleotide imbalance stress.
CirSeq assays revealed that substitutions are the predominant error type (∼90%), with some being constant while others are environment sensitive. The most prevalent substitutions detected were the C→U and G→A exchanges. Since ATP and UTP are the most abundant ribonucleotides (
Straube et al. 2021
), their high concentration may predispose a preference for these swaps.
C→U and G→A mistakes were also detected frequently in other organisms (
Chung Verheijen, Navapanich, et al. 2023
). These substitutions may be favored due to the similarity of their ring structures. Notably, the frequency of the C→U substitution is high and constant, whereas G→A substitution rate is low in Col-0 plants but dramatically increased in
tfIIs-1
and HS conditions. Since G→A formation is slow but efficiently extended (
Sydow and Cramer 2009
), heat may primarily promote its initial formation. GTP depletion by MPA treatment also caused G→A substitutions in
tfIIs-1
but did not lead to G→U swaps, confirming the specificity/bias of given nucleotide exchanges. C→U errors could result from a dG nucleotide (G within the DNA template) pairing with U (instead of C), similar to a G-U wobble base-pair in RNA duplexes. This substitution could be more frequently incorporated, as proposed in yeast (
Sydow and Cramer 2009
).
The possibility arises that some of the substitutions are the consequence of RNA editing events (
Meng et al. 2010
;
Chung, Verheijen, Zhang, et al. 2023
). In Arabidopsis the main nuclear RNA editing types are the U-to-C, A-to-G, A-to-C, and G-to-U. The U→C, A→G, and A→C substitutions are very rarely detected, but the G→U substitutions are relatively common. It was shown that G-to-U editing tends to occur at conserved cis element (
Meng et al. 2010
); however, in our dataset the G→U swaps are distributed randomly suggesting that these changes are mainly caused by transcriptional errors instead of editing. The C→U substitutions could also be the consequence of editing. C-to-U editing is most frequent in the organelles of flowering plants, but not on nuclear transcripts (
Meng et al. 2010
). Our raw material consists mainly of RNAPII products (
Supplementary Fig. S2A
); therefore, C-to-U editing might not considerably alter C→U error rates defined by us in plants. Regardless, this intriguing possibility needs to be further explored.
Insertions and deletions are rarer (∼5% to 10%) than substitutions, suggesting that RNAPII transcription is less likely to continue transcription following indels. We observed a bias for adenine insertion (
Supplementary Fig. S4
). ATP has the highest cellular concentration of NTPs (
Straube et al. 2021
), perhaps explaining this preference. Notably, insertion rates are genotype-, temperature- and MPA-dependent (varying ∼10-fold), but deletion rates remain low and constant (
Fig. 1
,
Supplementary Fig. S3
). We speculate that the unpaired nucleotide on the DNA template strand (e.g. the deletion fault) is recognized by RNAPII through alternative means, as compared to substitutions and insertions. Insertions (but not deletions) likely derive from substitutions: the mismatched nucleotide is erroneously elongated by a correct one, without forward translocation of the RNAPII. Consequently, the proofreading events triggered by substitutions and insertions (RNA-sided errors) at the polymerization core or +1 position may be mechanistically similar.
Transcriptional fidelity may have different causes, since it varies across cellular age, tissues, developmental stage, or under diverse environments (
Vermulst et al. 2015
;
Chung, Verheijen, Navapanich, et al. 2023
;
Chung, Verheijen, Zhang, et al. 2023
). While our study focuses on seedlings, future research on mature plants grown in soil and in the field could provide a more comprehensive understanding of fidelity regulation dynamics in plants.
The transcription velocity and fidelity are connected, as shown in yeast and human cells: high temperature could increase transcription speed, which may lead to higher error rates (
Muniz et al. 2021
;
Chung, Verheijen, Zhang, et al. 2023
). We postulate that in plants, high temperature may result in faster and consequently more erroneous transcription. Heat-induced destabilization of chromatin structure (
Pecinka et al. 2010
) may contribute to increased elongation speed and consequently elevated error rates. It was also demonstrated that the speed of RNAPII continuously increases within the gene body (
Danko et al. 2013
;
Jonkers et al. 2014
). In correlation with this, we observed a mild increase in error rate toward the 3′ end of CDS (
Fig. 2, C
and
D
). Interconnection of fidelity and speed of transcription in plants needs in-depth examination.
Transcriptome fidelity safeguarding occurs co- and posttranscriptionally
Next, we have shown that transcriptional errors are constantly surveyed co- and posttranscriptionally to be eliminated by the nuclear co-transcriptional fidelity factor TFIIS and the cytoplasmic post-transcriptional quality control NMD pathway (
Fig. 8D
). Importantly, TFIIS eliminates both substitutions and indels, while NMD specifically targets for decay the frame-shifted transcripts caused by indels within the CDS. Notably, the insertion rate is significantly higher in the
tfIIs;nmd
double mutants compared to the
tfIIs-1
, while the substitution rate is unchanged (
Fig. 1
,
Supplementary Fig. S3
). This suggests that the nuclear and cytoplasmic fidelity pathways cooperatively eliminate the indels. In sum, while TFIIS plays a dominant role in ensuring high fidelity of transcription, NMD acts as a backup control layer that eliminates the transcription error-generated PTC-containing faulty transcripts, which remained undetected by TFIIS proofreading (
Fig. 8D
).
Besides, all types of transcriptional errors can create faulty mRNAs lacking STOP codons or having other aberrant features. Aberrant mRNAs are targets of diverse RNA quality control (RQC) pathways at several levels. Nuclear RQC pathways could degrade aberrant transcripts on-chromatin (e.g. XRN- or nuclear exosome-mediated co-transcriptional decay) (
van Hoof et al. 2002
;
Nagarajan et al. 2013
;
Lange et al. 2014
;
Bhat et al. 2024
) or off-chromatin (e.g. posttranscriptional RNA quality checks to inhibit export of aberrant/immature RNA species) (
Garland and Jensen 2020
), while XRN4 and the cytoplasmic exosome degrade faulty mRNAs after the nuclear export. No-Go Decay and Non-Stop Decay (NGD and NSD), like NMD, are translation-coupled cytoplasmic RQCs. NGD eliminates faulty mRNAs containing translation elongation inhibiting sequences and structures, whereas NSD degrades early transcription termination or endonucleolytic cleavage-generated aberrant mRNAs lacking the STOP codon (
Hwang and Maquat 2011
;
Szadeczky-Kardoss, Csorba, et al. 2018
;
Szadeczky-Kardoss, Gal, et al. 2018
). Amongst the translation-coupled RQCs, NMD likely identifies and decays the largest pool of erroneous transcripts (having non-sense substitutions or indels causing frameshifts) that are particularly harmful for proteostasis (
Vermulst et al. 2015
). To what extent the alternative RQC pathways contribute to the erroneous transcript elimination under normal and stress conditions, and how TFIIS activity interacts with these RQCs deserves further dedicated research.
TFIIS and NMD cooperatively control DEGs and AS of plant transcriptomes
Besides the transcriptional fidelity control (e.g. transcriptional error survey and decay), TFIIS–NMD pathway interaction may regulate other aspects of transcriptomes, including DEGs and AS patterns. To better understand this, we performed an RNA transcriptome analysis; we show that both DEGs' regulation (mRNA steady state levels) and AS patterns are cooperatively controlled by TFIIS and NMD. We unraveled that a large portion of AS products could bear PTC, therefore are potentially an NMD target. By comparing transcriptomes of Col-0 and
tfIIs-1
plants, we demonstrated that the AS pattern is significantly changed in
tfIIs-1
, especially under HS (
Fig. 6
,
Szadeczky-Kardoss et al. 2022
). Furthermore, AS products accumulated significantly in the
tfIIs;nmd
mutants (vs
tfIIs-1
), implying that many AS transcripts are indeed eliminated by NMD following their cytoplasmic export and translation.
Molecular changes at the basis of HS-phenotypic alterations
It was shown previously that TFIIS regulates transcriptional reprogramming during HSR in plants (
Szadeczky-Kardoss et al. 2022
;
Obermeyer et al. 2023
). Adding to this, now we demonstrate that NMD pathway and TFIIS–NMD interaction are needed for both proper development and HS tolerance. Notably, the molecular basis of synergistic phenotypes of double mutants at different temperature conditions is different (
see below
).
At ambient (NT) temperature immunity-related transcripts are destabilized by NMD in wild-type plants (
Gloggnitzer et al. 2014
). However, the
tfIIs;upf3
double mutant has a strong autoimmune phenotype (at NT) due to accumulation of the immunity-related transcripts (vs Col-0,
tfIIs-1
, or
upf3-1
). At high temperatures immunity-related transcripts are at a low level (
Gloggnitzer et al. 2014
) (
Supplementary Fig. S11
), and the phenotype is partially rescued, indicating a causal relationship between the expression of immunity factors and developmental alterations. This suggests that TFIIS and NMD cooperate to suppress autoimmunity that could be the main cause of developmental alterations of
tfIIs;upf3
plants at ambient temperatures.
At HS the molecular basis of HS sensitivity of mutants is distinct. First, by performing RT-qPCR and RNA transcriptome, we have shown that the NMD pathway is functional during early (TMHT/1h) and persistent (TMHT/1d) heat stress. In addition, NMD absence causes HS sensitivity. Secondly, we combined
tfIIs-1
with
nmd
mutants and have revealed synergistic impacts on both molecular changes and HS phenotypes. Likely, several layers of transcriptome regulation, including production of erroneous transcripts, DEGs and AS patterns (but unlikely autoimmunity), contribute to HS sensitivity of
tfIIs;nmd
lines. We propose that the HS-phenotype of
tfIIs;nmd
double mutants is due to the proteotoxic stress caused by DEGs and accumulation of aberrant proteins translated from the faulty transcript, including erroneous and PTC-containing AS species. Due to the amplification of genetic information during translation, the impact of transcriptome faults is further amplified at the proteome level (
Marguerat et al. 2012
). In addition, under HS conditions a specific transcriptome is expressed; concentration of errors within this transcript set further outsizes the impact of mistakes on the proteome (
Supplementary Table S1
).
In conclusion, transcription quality control is crucial for both ambient conditions and adapting to HS, operating on several levels. First, TFIIS acts as a fidelity factor, efficiently resolving RNA Polymerase II (RNAPII) halts. This reduces the time RNAPII spends arrested, thus contributing to the efficient completion of the transcription cycle (a quantitative aspect). Second, the cooperation between TFIIS and NMD pathways ensures transcriptome quality by suppressing the generation and accumulation of faulty transcription and splicing products. This is vital for proteostasis and overall cellular function (a qualitative aspect). Given that HS alters transcriptional accuracy and AS, the induction of TFIIS during elevated temperatures (
Szadeczky-Kardoss et al. 2022
) likely represents an adaptive response to enhance transcriptional and splicing fidelity. Ultimately, the transcriptome probably aims for an optimal trade-off between the quantity and quality of transcripts to ensure proper development and stress adaptation.
Materials and methods
Plant materials
Arabidopsis seeds were bleach sterilized, stratified for 2 days in the dark and plated on Murashige and Skoog (Duchefa M0222,
https://duchefa-biochemie.com

The
tfIIs-1;upf3-1
plants are sterile at 21° C but can produce very low amounts of viable seed at 25 °C; this gives us the opportunity to obtain seeds. Further molecular works using these seeds were performed under standard conditions (on seedlings grown at 21 °C).

Phenotyping analysis

Three-week-old plants were examined using an automatized PlantScreen Modular high-throughput phenotyping system (Photon System Instruments Ltd, PSI, Brno). The measurements consisted of a 20-min dark adaptation period at 21 °C inside the acclimation chamber followed by chlorophyll fluorescence, RGB, and hyperspectral imaging. Heat stress treatments
For HS treatment regime, TMHT, 7-day-old seedlings were placed in a growing cabinet preheated to 37 °C and kept for 1 to 5 days. During the treatment, long-day conditions were used (16 h light/8 h dark). Treatments were started at midday (Zeitgeber time, ZT8). For molecular works, samples were collected immediately after treatment lasting for 1 hour (1 h), 1 day (1 d), or following a 2-day recovery period (RE). For phenotyping, plants (on agar plates) were treated as described above, then kept at 21 °C for 1 or 2 wk for post-growth, and then photographed. Statistical analysis is shown in
Supplementary Table S4

Mycophenolic acid treatment
For MPA treatments, seedlings were pre-grown for 7 days on 0.5× MS containing 0.5% sucrose and 1% agar media plates; on 7th day the seedlings were treated with 0.4 mg/mL MPA (ethanol stock solution 10 mg/mL), or solvent (CTL, ethanol), 2 mL/plate, respectively. For CirSeq library preparation, CTL- or MPA-treated seedlings were collected the next day (24 h treatment); for phenotyping experiments, the CTL- or MPA-treated seedlings were placed in plant growing cabinets and were grown at standard conditions for 14 days. Statistical analysis is shown in
Supplementary Table S4

CirSeq library preparations
All materials and reaction conditions for circle-sequencing were done as described previously (
Fritsch et al. 2018
). In brief, after total RNA extraction and polyadenylated RNA purification (to enrich for RNAPII products), the mRNA was enzymatically fragmented and then circularized. The circular RNAs were reverse transcribed during a rolling cycle reaction with a strand-displacing reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen SuperScript III RT; the product contains multiple concatenated units within a long single-molecule cDNA). After this, the single-stranded cDNA was converted into double-stranded form, end-repaired, dA-tailed, and finally adapter-ligated for library construction and sequencing. Sequencing service was performed by Novogene Ltd. If transcription mistakes are present in the original RNA template, the error will be detected on every concatenated copy, periodically in the very same position; if errors are made by technical inaccuracies of the methods (by reverse transcription, PCR amplification, or sequencing), they will be scattered along the cDNA concatenated units in different positions; therefore can be easily eliminated by bioinformatic selection (
Fritsch et al. 2018
) (
see below
).
For CirSeq sample preparation we have used non-treated (NT, plants grown at 21 °C, 16 h/8 h light/dark) and 1-day 37 °C-treated seedlings. The HS time-point was chosen because (i) transcription is generally suppressed at early time points of HS (TMHT/1h) but later (at TMHT/1d) broadly resumed (
Szadeczky-Kardoss et al. 2022
), and (ii) RNA-seq indicated that erroneous transcripts accumulate to higher levels after 1 d HS (
Supplementary Fig. S1C
). Notably, the TMHT/1d regime impacts very mildly the HS-sensitivity phenotype of
tfIIs-1
or
upf
single and double mutant plants (
Szadeczky-Kardoss et al. 2022
), reducing the chance of pleiotropic indirect effects.
Bioinformatic analyses of circle-sequencing data
Raw circle-sequencing data of at least 3 biological replicates was analyzed according to previously described pipelines and codes (
Chung, Verheijen, Zhang, et al. 2023
),
https://github.com/
LynchLab/TranscriptErrors. Raw paired-end reads were trimmed with Cutadapt (
https://doi.org/10.14806/ej.17.1.200
). Tandem repeats were identified and consensus sequences of the repeats from the same read were created. The consensus sequences were mapped to the
A. thaliana
TAIR10 (v.56) cDNA dataset and the breakpoints (the ends of the initial RNA fragments before circularization) were identified. The consensus sequences were realigned to the RNAPII transcriptome with Tophat2 according to the breakpoints (
Kent 2002
). A refining step was implemented for the non-perfectly aligned sequences to search for a better breakpoint position before re-mapping these reads with Tophat2. Lastly, every mapped base was examined for identifying substitutions, insertions, and deletions, with the following conditions: (i) it needs to be part of at least 3 repeats on the same read; (ii) it is at least 3 nucleotides away from the ends of consensus; (iii) the sum of quality scores for the base is higher than 100; (iv) the identity of the repeats containing the base is at least 90%; (v) less than 1% of all base calls for the given position are different from the reference genome.
The Circo plot was created with Circos software (
Krzywinski et al. 2009
). Data were calculated for the metagene plots with deepTools (
Ramirez et al. 2016
). Sections of the metagenes were adjusted to the
A. thaliana
average transcript section lengths, namely the 5′UTR, the CDS, and the 3′UTR ratios (155:1391:242 nt,
Srivastava et al. 2018
;
Pucker et al. 2019
). The possible impact of errors on the amino acid changes was analyzed with SNPEFF software (
Cingolani et al. 2012
). We used the data from our previously published RNAseq dataset (
Szadeczky-Kardoss et al. 2022
) for the preliminary error rate analysis with the help of the webtool Alfred (

https://biit.cs.ut.ee/gprofiler/gost
