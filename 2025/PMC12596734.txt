Background: PARP enzymes in the DNA damage response
Every human cell is subject to 10 000–1 000 000 DNA damage events every day, due to spontaneous DNA damage (e.g. oxidation and hydrolysis), replication errors, and/or environmental factors (e.g. reactive oxygen species, UV irradiation, and chemicals) [
1
,
2
]. To protect the integrity of their genomes, cells have evolved robust DNA damage repair mechanisms. Two key components of these DNA repair mechanisms are the “first responder” proteins poly-(ADP ribose) polymerase 1 and 2 (PARP1/2) (reviewed in [
3
–
6
]). PARP1/2 are enzymatically activated by binding to a variety of DNA lesions including single and double strand DNA breaks (SSBs and DSBs). Upon activation, PARP1/2 utilize NAD
+
as a substrate to post-translationally add chains of ADP-ribose (ADPR) to a large number of targets including themselves and other nuclear proteins such as histones (Fig.
1A
). ADP-ribosylation activity can manifest in the addition of a single ADPR (MARylation) or chains of ADPR (with some branching) that may be as long as 200 residues (PARylation) (Fig.
1A
). In addition to the enzymatic activity of adding ADPR to itself and other proteins, PARP1/2 can also generate free ADPR (i.e. treadmilling) [
7
] as well as free PAR, not attached to any protein [
8
]. All reactions of PARP1/2 concomitantly generate nicotinamide (Fig.
1A
). The N-terminal regions of PARP1 and PARP2 are significantly different, with PARP1 having multiple DNA-binding Zn-fingers and a BRCT (BRCA1 C-Terminus) domain; all absent in the smaller PARP2. However, PARP1 and PARP2 share a WGR domain and a conserved HD-CAT domain, the latter of which is composed of a helical subdomain (HD) and the catalytic domain (Fig.
2A
). The WGR domain in PARP1 is important for interacting with DNA and transmitting a conformational change upon binding of DNA at the N-terminus to the catalytic domain. The catalytic domain is shared by a diverse family of 17 different ADP-ribosyltransferase diphtheria toxin-like proteins (ARTD, a unifying and mechanistically more correct name for all “PARP” family members) that all have in common an H-Y-[E/D/Q] active site motif. This motif constitutes the core of the NAD
+
-binding site (donor site) that also includes an adenine binding pocket deeper within the protein (Fig.
2B
). Next to the donor site is the acceptor site where an ADPR that is already attached to an amino acid side chain or to another ADPR binds. This acceptor site is then utilized for building PAR chains (Fig.
2B
).

MARylation and PARylation takes place both in
cis
, on PARP1 itself (autoPARylation), and on other proteins, primarily the histone components of nucleosomes. Modification of histones has two outcomes. First, the addition of negative charges from multiple ADPR additions (two charges per ADPR residue; see Fig.
1A
) onto the positively charged histones facilitates the opening of chromatin due to steric repulsion with the negatively charged DNA. Second, either MAR or the chains of PAR serve as recruitment sites for DNA repair proteins containing ADPR- or PAR-binding motifs (e.g. PBM, PBZ, and Macro domains) [
9
]. Such proteins include XRCC1, a scaffolding protein for other DNA repair proteins [
10
], Ligase III, which can reseal a DNA strand break [
11
], the ATP-dependent chromatin remodeler ALC1 (CHD1L [
12
]) and Ku70/Ku80 [
13
] and APLF [
14
], which are required for nonhomologous end-joining (NHEJ). AutoPARylation results in the release of PARP1/2 from DNA presumably due to repulsion from the negatively charged PAR chains, thereby inactivating enzymatic activity. As such, the average dwell time of PARP1/2 at sites of DNA damage is rather transient and is associated with a short burst of activity. Additional important components in this response to DNA damage are the enzymes that remove the PAR chains including poly-ADPR glycohydrolase (PARG) that hydrolyzes the ribose-ribose bond in the PAR chains, MacroD1, MacroD2, and TARG1 that hydrolyze a mono-ADPR from Glu/Asp residues, and ARH3 that hydrolyzes a mono-ADPR from Ser residues [
15
,
16
].
PARP1/2 have been of significant interest as viable drug targets ever since the seminal discovery of the synthetic lethality between inhibitors of PARP (PARPi) and the BRCA-/- phenotype found in some breast, ovarian, and prostate cancers [
17
,
18
]. Simply stated, treatment of cells with PARPi leads to PARP1/2 binding to damaged DNA. The unrepaired damage caused by transcription-replication conflicts [
19
] and/or the unrepaired PARP1/2-bound DNA [
20
] eventually lead to double strand breaks that are not repaired in the BRCA-/- cancer cells. As a result, PARPi selectively kill cancerous cells while leaving most normal cells where BRCA is present relatively unscathed. There are currently four approved PARPi in clinical use (olaparib, talazoparib, rucaparib, and niraparib; reviewed in [
21
–
24
]). While these first generation PARPi are an improvement over nonspecific chemotherapeutics, there is a need for PARPi with increased specificity, due to dose-limiting toxicities and eventual resistance to PARPi. Recent reports from AstraZeneca of PARPi that are selective for PARP1 relative to PARP2 demonstrate increased patient tolerability, and this is a possible game-changer in the treatment of BRCA-/- cancers [
25
].
Although ADP-ribosylation of nuclear proteins including histones [
26
] and the enzyme responsible for the majority of this activity (PARP1) [
27
] have been known since 1963 and 1968, respectively, it took more than 50 years for Ivan Ahel’s group to serendipitously identify histone PARylation factor 1 (HPF1) as the key mediator that promotes PARP1-mediated histone PARylation [
28
]. Surprisingly, the transPARylation of histones facilitated by HPF1 is concomitant with a switch from autoPARylation on glutamate and aspartate (Glu/Asp) side chains, to auto- and transPARylation on serine (Ser) side chains (reviewed in [
29
–
33
]). Such a dramatic switch in substrate specificity, conferred by a protein that does not have any catalytic activity is quite rare in enzymology (see Structures of HPF1 with PARP1 and PARP2 below). The discovery is especially intriguing for an enzyme that is as well studied as PARP1. In this review, we consider the implications of the HPF1-mediated change in catalytic mechanism, active site architecture, and change in substrate specificity, with particular emphasis on the role of HPF1 in the response to DNA damage, and the implications of serine versus glutamate/aspartate PARylation. We discuss the potential role of HPF1 in upending the development of next-generation PARPi and conclude by posing several burning open questions in the PARP1/2 field in relation to HPF1.
PARylation of histones
Histones are the basic components of nucleosomes that organize the entire genome in all eukaryotes. The four linker histones H2A, H2B, H3, and H4 form an octameric core that wraps 150 bp of DNA in two tight superhelical turns. This particle is further stabilized by the linker histone H1, which also promotes the close packing of nucleosomes into compacted higher order structures [
34
]. The four core histones as well as linker histone have long “histone tails” that are highly positively charged, and these serve as the sites of a plethora of post-translational modifications. Literally every known post-translational modification has been found on histones, including MARylation and PARylation [
35
].
The discovery of HPF1 solved a major puzzle that had long persisted in the PARP field. The earliest reports of ADP-ribosylation in 1963 indicated that this modification occurred predominantly on histones isolated from cells and nuclear extracts [
26
]. Much of the early work of histone PARylation focused on linker histone H1 and identified chain lengths of 7–8 ADPRs/histone [
36
]. However, once PARP1 was cloned and purified, there were no reliable reports of
in vitro
histone PARylation using either purified free histones or nucleosome substrates. In our own lab, we invested quite some effort trying to PARylate histones using purified reagents, and we know of other laboratories with similar unpublished futile efforts. Thus, the original report by the Ahel laboratory that demonstrated transPARylation of histones, but only in the presence of HPF1, was groundbreaking [
28
]. The story of the serendipitous discovery of HPF1 is quite interesting and worth briefly summarizing. The Ahel laboratory was actively hunting for poly(ADP-ribose)-binding zinc finger (PBZ) domain-containing proteins that could play novel roles in the response to DNA damage through their recruitment to PAR chains. By sequence analysis, they identified CG1218, a protein harboring a PBZ domain in
Drosophila melanogaster
[
37
]. The closest human homolog of CG1218 was the previously uncharacterized protein C4orf27, which ironically did not contain a PBZ domain and correspondingly also did not bind PAR. However, C4orf27 was recruited to sites of DNA damage, and this recruitment was dependent on PARP1. Using knock-out studies, they then showed that loss of C4orf27 led to increased sensitivity to DNA damaging agents and PARPi, increased autoPARylation, and loss of histone PARylation. The rest, as they say, is history.
The HPF1 amino acid sequence is found in essentially all
Metazoa
(animals) including primitive organisms such as sponges, and experimental organisms such as
D. melanogas
ter and
Caenorhabditis elegans
[
38
]. Importantly, the HPF1-dependence of histone PARylation by both PARP1 and PARP2 has been extremely well validated by laboratories around the world, both
in vitro
and in cells [
7
,
12
,
30
,
38
–
46
]. Looking in more detail at the mechanism of histone PARylation in the context of the nucleosomal substrate, the Deindl laboratory has shown preferential initiation on the histone H2B that is more proximal to the double strand DNA break at the end of an asymmetrical nucleosome [
47
]. This result suggests that ADPR is likely to be added primarily on one side, not both sides, of a damaged nucleosome. Their hypothesis was validated by their cryo-EM structure of the chromatin remodeler ALC1 bound to a PARylated nucleosome [
47
]. The Lavrik laboratory has compared HPF1-stimulated histone PARylation by PARP1 and PARP2 in the context of nucleosome substrates that incorporate different models for DNA damage [
48
]. They have found that the most significant stimulation of histone PARylation by HPF1
in vitro
occurs using PARP2 (not PARP1), and with a nucleosome with a one-nucleotide gap opposite the end of the free DNA, imitating an intermediate in base excision repair. Their results emphasize that both PARP1 and PARP2 require HPF1 for histone PARylation and suggest an important role for PARP2 in DNA damage repair in the context of chromatin [
42
,
43
].
(Mis)Identification of sites of PARylation
From the earliest reports of the existence of protein PARylation, there has been quite a bit of uncertainty regarding the linkage between ADPR and its target proteins. At the time, Glu/Asp residues [
49
] and phospho-Ser residues [
50
,
51
] were originally suggested targets for modification based on isolation of modified histones and hydrolytic analysis of their amino acids. The classic early experiments to identify which amino acids are subject to PARylation relied heavily on chemical sensitivity of the linkage to cleavage by various treatments. The best chemical probe for evaluating the Glu/Asp linkage is hydroxylamine (NH
2
OH), which specifically cleaves ADPR attached to these residues and does not cleave other possible linkages (Fig.
1B
). Early investigations using this method revealed targeting of different types of amino acids on isolated histones that appeared to vary depending on sample preparation [
50
–
55
]. Given that NAD
+
is not cell-permeable, experiments using radiolabeled NAD
+
were more commonly performed in isolated cell nuclei (
in vitro
) than in intact cells (
in vivo
). Interestingly,
in vitro
experiments yielded longer chains that were more susceptible to cleavage by hydroxylamine (i.e. modification of Glu/Asp residues) than
in vivo
experiments that yielded shorter chains with some resistance to hydroxylamine (i.e. modification of Ser residues; see Fig.
1B
). In light of the discovery of HPF1, we can now reinterpret these results to suggest that isolation of nuclei disrupted the PARP1–HPF1 interaction, thus yielding more Glu/Asp linkages and longer chains rather than the shorter and more prevalent Ser-linked chains observed with HPF1 (see also The Many Activities of PARP1 ± HPF1 below).
Developments in mass spectroscopy approaches represented a major advance for identifying sites of PARylation compared to chemical sensitivity of partially purified protein mixtures. Mass spectroscopy tolerates the use of endogenous NAD
+
(i.e. without radiolabels) in intact cells under varying conditions (i.e. following DNA damage) and localizes specific modification sites within a unique protein sequence. Original efforts to identify PARylation sites by mass spectrometry primarily yielded modification of Glu/Asp residues [
56
–
60
] although there were many experimental hurdles including minimizing long PAR chains to a “scar” either by hydroxylamine treatment or treatment with PARG, enrichment of PARylated proteins from the proteome, and the chemical lability of the ADPR-linkage to ionizing techniques in the mass spectrometer [
61
]. At the time of Ahel’s discovery of HPF1, there was a concomitant flurry of improved procedures in sample preparation (e.g. enrichment using macro domains) and mass spectrometry (e.g. more gentle ionization to preserve the linkage) [
62
]. These new methods were able to identify thousands of Ser-modified sites in hundreds of proteins [
63
–
68
]. Additionally, it was demonstrated that PARylation of serines was the predominant PARylation event observed after DNA damage [
16
,
63
,
64
,
69
,
70
]. Many serine histone PARylation sites are found in a “KS” motif, wherein a Lys residue precedes the site of modification, and canonical histone marks on these Lys residues (e.g. acetylation) reduce the efficiency of PARylation on the adjacent Ser residues [
62
,
71
]. Recently, these advances in methods have allowed for the identification of even more histone modifications including ADPr-Ub, aka “MARUbylation,” the HPF1-mediated attachment of a single ADPR to a Ser residue followed by the addition of a ubiquitin to the ADPR [
72
,
73
]. Emphasizing the presumed importance of PARylation of Ser residues in the DNA damage response among animals, HPF1 from
Drosophila
functions much like human HPF1 in mediating transPARylation of Ser residues (29% sequence identity) [
38
].
Importantly, the role of HPF1 in mediating the observed PARylation of Ser residues has been validated extensively both
in vitro
and
in vivo
. In vitro reconstitution experiments demonstrated HPF1-dependent transPARylation on Ser residues in free histones H3 and H1, as well as for the more physiological substrate of intact nucleosomes [
7
,
66
,
71
]. Using free histone H3, the primary Ser modification sites are Ser10 and Ser28. Using intact mononucleosomes, the primary Ser modification sites are Ser6 on histone H2B and Ser10 and Ser28 on histone H3. In all of these studies, the autoPARylation of PARP1 in the presence of HPF1 is also directed at Ser residues, with the primary sites of modification being Ser499, Ser 507, and Ser519 [
45
]. HPF1
KO
cells prepared independently by three different groups consistently demonstrate the loss of PARylation of Ser residues with increased modification of Glu/Asp residues, particularly on Asp387, Glu488, and Glu491 of PARP1 [
43
,
62
,
63
]. Although autoPARylation is very abundant on PARP1 in both wild-type and HPF1
KO
cells, this switch in specificity from Ser residues to Glu/Asp residues upon loss of HPF1 applies to all modified proteins. Additionally, it has been shown that complementation of HPF1
KO
cells with functionally inactive HPF1 does not restore PARylation of Ser residues [
74
].
At this point in time, the PARP1 community now (mostly) agrees that PARylation of Ser residues is the primary site of both autoPARylation and transPARylation in human cells, using both untreated cells and cells treated with DNA damaging agents like hydrogen peroxide (H
2
O
2
) or methyl methane sulfate (MMS). The reason for the parenthetical “mostly” is that the recent re-discovery of the lability of Glu/Asp modifications and approaches to prevent this in the workup has led to renewed efforts to identify these modifications, finding ample MARylation and PARylation sites on Glu/Asp residues throughout the proteome even in wild-type cells that do contain HPF1 [
75
–
77
]. It is unclear whether Glu/Asp modifications on nonhistone proteins require any specific or general co-proteins like HPF1 to facilitate their transPARylation in favor of the intrinsic autoPARylation of PARP1/2. Two studies that have explicitly addressed the dependence of PARylation on HPF1 for nonhistone proteins definitively showed that PARP1 can modify these proteins on Glu/Asp residues in the absence of HPF1, further confirming that the PARylome is varied and complex [
78
,
79
]. Moving forward in the field, it is important that all investigations of transPARylation of proteins address the potential role of HPF1 and possibly other, yet unidentified factors, as well as the lability/residue identity of these linkages. If transPARylation without participation from HPF1 occurs on Ser residues, is there an as-yet-unknown protein that mimics the role of HPF1? And does transPARylation without HPF1 on non-Ser residues require an HPF1-like protein? Obviously, the book is far from closed on this, and we still have a lot to uncover in this field.
Structures of HPF1 with PARP1 and PARP2
HPF1 folds into a three-dimensional structure with two tightly associated domains that have no known structural homologs [
74
], a rarity in this day and age, where the Protein Data Bank contains >235 000 structures (Fig.
3A
). The less-well conserved N-terminal domain has as yet no known function, whereas the more highly conserved C-terminal domain contains the interaction sites for PARP1/2, including the “acidic corner” that contains the crucial amino acids Asp283 and Glu284 (see below). The four published structures of HPF1 alone show essentially identical conformations (pairwise global RMSDs < 1 Å) despite the differing origins of the proteins (e.g. human versus sea urchin with <50% sequence identity) and different crystallization conditions (Table
1
).

HPF1 forms only a weak complex with PARP1 or PARP2, and the interface is highly dependent on the DNA-mediated activated state of PARP1/2. Upon binding DNA, both PARP1 and PARP2 undergo significant conformational changes, with one of the most important element being the disordering of the HD-helical subdomain (see Fig.
2A
) [
80
–
82
]. This disordering allows for access of NAD
+
(required for enzymatic activity) and other small molecules (i.e. PARPi) to the active site [
83
]. Importantly, the HD-helical subdomain of PARP1/2 also interferes with HPF1 binding and therefore PARP1/2 and HPF1 do not form a complex unless PARP1/2 are bound to damaged DNA. This explains why the first structures of PARP1/2 with HPF1 were with shorter protein constructs that lacked the HD-helical subdomain (and thus do not require DNA). PARP1 is a difficult protein to work with, often visualized as protein domains connected by flexible linkers, much like beads-on-a-string (Fig.
2A
). As such, most of the structural information we have on how HPF1 interacts with PARP1/2 comes from structures with the shorter and more stable PARP2 (Fig.
2A
and Table
1
). The region of contact between PARP1/2 and HPF1 is through the C-terminal part of the catalytic domain of PARP1/2, which is highly conserved. Also, the PARP1
cat
– HPF1 (PDB #6m3i) and PARP2
cat
– HPF1 (PDB #6tx3) complexes align with an RMSD of 0.4 Å and the contacts between HPF1 and PARP1 were confirmed in part by NMR analyses [
74
]. Thus, the descriptions provided below apply equally to both the PARP1–HPF1 and PARP2–HPF1 complexes.
The PARP1/2–HPF1 interface consists of ∼30 residues from each protein and buries ~1300 Å
2
(Fig.
3
). The long helix (α9) forms the basis for the interaction with PARP1/2 and there are many crucial points of contact across the protein–protein interface that are conserved between PARP1 and PARP2 but not found in other PARPs (Fig.
3B
). Also, the two C-terminal residues of PARP1 (Leu1013 and Trp1014) and PARP2 (Leu569 and Trp570) make van der Waals contacts with Phe268 of HPF1 (Fig.
3B
). The importance of these interfacial residues has been confirmed by mutagenesis and serve as the basis for
in vitro
and cell-based experiments validating the importance of the interaction between PARP1/2 and HPF1 [
74
]. Most significantly, Glu284 of HPF1 was identified as the catalytic residue that facilitates the PARylation of Ser residues and the transPARylation of histones (Fig.
3B
) [
7
,
40
,
74
]. The E284A mutant binds more tightly to PARP1 than wild-type HPF1 [
7
,
40
], does not promote the modification of Ser residues nor the transPARylation of histones, instead allowing for the autoPARylation of Glu/Asp residues on PARP1 primarily on Asp387, Glu488, and Glu491 [
7
,
74
]. Mutation of Arg239 in HPF1, which interacts with and stabilizes Glu284, also disrupted PARylation of Ser residues, oddly causing previously unseen PARylation of Glu235 and Glu240 on HPF1 [
40
]. Mutations of Asp283 or Phe280 in HPF1 prevent complex formation between PARP1 and HPF1 and thus also disrupt transPARylation and modification of Ser residues [
7
,
74
].
The most striking discovery in the structure of the PARP–HPF1 complex was that HPF1 contributes residues to form a shared active site with PARP1/2 [
40
,
74
]. Specifically, Glu284 of PARP1 sits only 4.6 Å away from the ribose of NAD
+
(as modeled using the nonhydrolyzable analog; Fig.
3B
). Also, the region near the active site of PARP1 is transformed from electropositive to electronegative by the binding of HPF1 due to the many acidic residues contributed by HPF1. Importantly, this shared active site explains the dramatic change in amino acids targeted for PARylation in that Glu284 serves as the catalytic base responsible for deprotonating Ser residues, and the electronegative surface serves as a docking site for the Lys-Ser motif that is the preferred substrate in the histone tails. The need for a catalytic residue for deprotonation of Ser residues was conceived prior to any PARP1–HPF1 structure and served as motivation for the independent discovery of Glu284 through classical enzymology in our lab (Fig.
1C
). In agreement with the role of Glu284 as a catalytic base, PARylation of Tyr residues has also been shown to depend on HPF1 [
71
,
84
].
The formation of an active site using shared residues from two different proteins is a relatively rare phenomenon in enzymatic catalysis, perhaps because of the difficult evolutionary path to this solution. One prominent example where a shared active site has evolved independently multiple times is the so-called Arg-finger that is contributed by GTPase-activating proteins (GAPs) for hydrolysis of GTP by various GTPases [
85
]. For these GTPases, hydrolysis of GTP to GDP and the concomitant conformational changes that affect downstream signaling pathways are accelerated by 2–3 orders of magnitude by the Arg finger located on the GAP which positions the γ-phosphate of GTP to stabilize its oxygens, thereby making it a better leaving group.
The structural information gained by high resolution X-ray crystallography with truncated catalytic domains of PARP1/2 was validated at lower resolution for more physiologically relevant complexes of full length PARP2 bound to HPF1 in the presence of nucleosomes using cryo-electron microscopy, although even here not all regions of the protein were sufficiently ordered to be resolved (Table
1
) [
86
]. Specifically, PARP2 was seen to bind HPF1 only if the HD-helical subdomain underwent a significant rearrangement compared to the unactivated form of PARP2, consistent with the crystal structures that were obtained only when the HD subdomains were removed from the catalytic domains of PARP1/2. Additionally, the bridging of two DNA ends that is typically observed for PARP2 [
87
,
88
] is also compatible with HPF1 binding [
86
]. The Halic laboratory was also able to identify alternate conformations for the PARP2–HPF1–nucleosome complex that appear to allow for entry and exit of NAD
+
as well as the tails of histone H3, the targets of PARylation [
86
]. These types of conformational changes appear to be important for promoting successive rounds of PARylation.
The many activities of PARP1 ± HPF
Not only does the presence of HPF1 alter the primary sites of PARylation (Ser residues on histones and PARP1 versus Glu/Asp residues on PARP1), but there is also an HPF1-mediated change in chain elongation activity that has been validated both
in vitro
and
in vivo. In vitro
, PARP1 by itself assembles long chains of PAR typically consisting of 200–300 residues with branching approximately once every 20 ADPRs (Fig.
4A
) [
7
,
89
]. In contrast, the addition of HPF1 to an
in vitro
PARylation reactions yields much shorter chains (<15 residues) (Fig.
4B
) [
7
,
28
,
66
,
90
,
91
]. From a structural perspective, shorter PAR chains in the presence of HPF1 are easy to understand as HPF1 blocks a part of the acceptor site needed for chain elongation and branching [
40
,
74
]. Specifically, the interaction of Asp283 of HPF1 with His826 of PARP1 disrupts PAR chain elongation as His826 is known to bind to the second phosphate of the ADPR acceptor (Fig.
2B
) [
92
]. The formation of shorter chain lengths as a result of HPF1 addition to a PARylation reaction has also been observed for PARP2 [
88
]. Mechanistically, the basis for much shorter PAR chains in the presence of HPF1 is due to a dramatic increase in hydrolysis of NAD
+
, i.e. the addition of ADPR to water instead of to an amino acid on a protein (“treadmilling”) [
7
,
90
]. It is worth noting that the presence of HPF1 in solution does not completely block PAR chain elongation (i.e. it is not generating only MAR). The presence of HPF1 increases the speed of the initiation reaction (on Ser residues) after which PARP1 still functions as a polymerase when HPF1 is not bound, an event that occurs quite readily given the weak interaction between these two proteins [
7
,
91
,
93
].

However, because HPF1 does not bind to PARP1 until PARP1 is bound to DNA, the two proteins arrive separately and there is the possibility of some Glu/Asp PARylation of PARP1 occurring prior to the arrival of HPF1 (bottom branch). Additionally, the weak affinity and lower abundance of HPF1 compared to PARP1 may allow repeated initiation on Glu/Asp residues (dotted arrow in bottom branch). Either way, PARylation of histones and PARP1 on Ser residues (and/or Glu/Asp residues) after the departure of HPF1 leads to PARP1 dissociation from DNA, relaxation of chromatin, and the recruitment of DNA repair factors (pink). In cells, after some time, the action of PARG yields a wave of MARylation wherein the chromatin remains relaxed and leads potentially to the recruitment of different DNA repair proteins (yellow). PARP1 itself is returned to its unmodified form by the action of PARG, TARG1 (terminal ADP-ribose glycohydrolase 1), and ARH3, the Ser-specific glycohydrolase.

Recently, the Pascal laboratory has shown that PARP1 can synthesize free PAR chains that are not attached to PARP1 or any other protein [

HPF1 knockdown, deletion, or overexpression
The conservation of HPF1 in Metazoa suggests that HPF1 might be an essential protein like many other DNA repair proteins (e.g. XRCC1) [
97
], with the caveat that neither PARP1 nor PARP2 by themselves are essential proteins [
98

99

parp1-/-parp2-/-
mouse displays embryonic lethality [

hpf1

hpf1
by morpholinos in zebrafish (
Danio rerio
) affected early development in a manner reminiscent of treatment with DNA-damaging insecticides [
101
]. Similarly, in human cells, removal of
hpf1
by CRISPR–Cas results in increased sensitivity of
HPF
KO

PARP1

hpf1-/-

45

17

18

102
]. Interestingly, BRCAness upon deletion of
hpf1
has been independently discovered in genome-wide screens for BRCAness [
103

104

BRCA1

The deletion of HPF1 abrogates the PARylation of histones in cells and is detrimental to chromatin relaxation at sites of DNA damage [
41
] even though autoPARylation of PARP1 occurs at higher levels than in the presence of HPF1 [
28
]. In HPF1
KO
cells there is the same amount of ADPR at sites of DNA damage as in wild-type cells, but PARP1 generates fewer albeit longer PAR chains than in the wild-type controls with HPF1 (Fig.
4A
) [
28

41
]. The absence of histone PARylation in HPF1
KO
cells leads to a defect in chromatin relaxation. Whereas histone PARylation is required to open up the chromatin, histone MARylation is sufficient to maintain this open state, and it is the removal of these histone marks by the ARH3 hydrolase that allows chromatin to recondense [
72
]. Complementation of
HPF
KO
cells with the E284A point mutant, which binds to PARP1 more tightly than wild-type HPF1 but does not promote histone modifications, does not restore chromatin relaxation [
32
]. Additionally, depletion of HPF1 in
Xenopus laevis
egg extracts, significantly reduces PARylation of histone H3 [
42
]. Thus, the presence of HPF1 in cells leads to shorter PAR chain lengths and diminished histone PARylation. Whether the increased production of ADPR in the presence of HPF1 observed
in vitro
(“treadmilling”) also occurs in cells and, if so, whether increased levels of free ADPR have an as yet undefined signaling or second messenger role remains to be resolved.
Surprisingly, despite the absence of histone PARylation and chromatin relaxation in HPF1
KO
cells, there is some uncertainty whether the absence of HPF1 affects downstream DNA repair pathways. The Ahel and Huet laboratories report slower recruitment of DNA repair proteins that bind to mono-ADPR (i.e. MacroD1) or that require access to DNA (i.e. ZNF384, CHD4, and CHD7) [
28
,
41
]. This is consistent with the idea that HPF1-dependent histone PARylation rather than PARP1 autoPARylation is required for DNA repair by homologous recombination (HR) and NHEJ. Using
Xenopus laevis
egg extracts, the Nakanishi laboratory has shown that depletion of HPF1 blocks the backup pathway of Okazaki fragment ligation that is mediated by Lig3–XRCC1 in the absence of Lig1 [
42
]. The Fan laboratory demonstrated that HPF1 is important for single-strand break repair in response to DNA damage caused by oxidative stress in cells undergoing active transcription [
46
]. Also, HPF1-dependent MARylation is important for telomere homeostasis [
45
]. In contrast, the Hanzlikova laboratory reports normal recruitment of XRCC1 and normal repair of single strand breaks following treatment with H
2
O
2
[
43

KO

They conclude that the robust autoPARylation of PARP1 observed at sites of DNA damage is sufficient for recruiting single-strand break repair factors and restoring the integrity of the damaged DNA. Are these discrepancies regarding the “essentiality” of HPF1 due to differing model cell lines or due to emphasis on different aspects of DNA repair (i.e. SSBs versus DSBs versus Okazaki fragment ligation)?There are two other interesting observations related to deletion or knockdown of HPF1 from the Ahel laboratory. First, hyperPARylated PARP1 that arises due to the absence of HPF1 in in HPF1
KO
cells remains localized at sites of DNA damage for a longer time than the less PARylated PARP1 in wild-type cells [
28
,
45
]. This is a perplexing result because autoPARylation is thought to cause the dissociation of PARP1 from DNA [
105
] and the longer PAR chains that are generated in the absence of HPF1 should lead to increased dissociation of PARP1 from DNA. Is it possible that the PARylation of histones (i.e. making chromatin more negatively charged), not autoPARylation, or repair mediated specifically by PARylation of Ser residues is the primary driver for the dissociation of PARP1 from DNA in cells? Or does hyperPARylated PARP1 become ensconced in liquid-liquid phase separation? [
106
] Second, knockdown of HPF1 has compounding effects towards genomic instability in cells lacking TARG1, the glycohydrolase that removes the ADPRs linked to Glu and Asp residues [
107
]. Thus, there appears to be some toxic effect of Glu/Asp PARylation for which the presence of either HPF1 (i.e. diversion of modification to Ser residues) or TARG1 (i.e. removal of modifications from Glu/Asp residues) is protective. Given that addition of the first ADPR is the rate-determining step in PAR chain formation [
96
], it is possible that leaving a residual MARylation on Glu/Asp residues leads to unwarranted PAR chain extension HPF1 overexpression, though rarely studied, has been observed in colorectal cancer cells that also aberrantly overexpress ANP32B, a member of the acidic leucine-rich nuclear phosphoprotein 32 family that is also a histone chaperone [
108
]. Overexpression of HPF1 in turn was mediated by binding of ANP32B to the promoter of
hpf1
and could be downregulated by silencing of
anp32B
. Additionally, overexpression of ANP32B, and thus HPF1, was found to decrease the efficacy of the PARPi AG-14361. The abundance of HPF1 has been proposed to play a role in the aging of tendon stem/progenitor cells (TSPCs) where increased levels (∼2-fold) of both HPF1 (and PARP1) are detected in aged rat TSPCs compared to young TSCPs [
109

Saccharomyces cerevisiae
, despite having the same name, is unrelated to histone PARylation factor; yeast also do not have any PARPs [
110
]. Purposeful overexpression of HPF1 in model human cells yields PAR chains that are shorter with even more mono-ADP-ribosylation than the controls with normal levels of HPF1 [
41
,
95
]. Further investigating HPF1 levels in other cancer scenarios in comparison to normal cells may help elucidate the importance of HPF1 in certain clinical settings.
Recruitment of HPF1 to sites of DNA damage
Because of the demonstrated importance of the recruitment of PARP1 to sites of DNA damage and the role of HPF1 in mediating histone PARylation, several groups have investigated when in relation to other DNA damage repair proteins HPF1 arrives at sites of DNA damage. The classic experiment for observing recruitment is by quantitating the accumulation of fluorescently-labeled protein (i.e GFP-tagged) at sites of DNA damage induced by laser irradiation [
111
]. The Ahel laboratory originally demonstrated that HPF1 is recruited to sites of DNA damage and that this requires a physical interaction with PARP1 via its C-terminal region (i.e. Leu1013 and Trp1014; see Fig.
3B
), but does not require the catalytic activity of PARP1 [
28
]. The Huet laboratory demonstrated a similar dependence of HPF1-recruitment on PARP1 (but not PARP2). They also demonstrated that HPF1 accumulates just as fast as PARP1 but appears to dissipate more slowly than PARP1, which begs the questions of what it is doing there without PARP1, and how it stays there without its own DNA-binding domain? [
41
] In contrast, we showed a slower recruitment of HPF1 compared to PARP1, indicating that they do not travel together. This apparent discrepancy might be explained by different analysis methods of essentially the same experiments [
112
,
113
]. Emphasizing the requirement for a PARP1–HPF1 interaction for HPF1 recruitment, the Huet laboratory also demonstrated that mutation of the two most C-terminal residues of PARP1 (LW to AA) abrogated the recruitment of HPF1 to sites of DNA damage [
41
]. Slowing the release of PARP1 from sites of DNA damage, either by using PARylation mutants of PARP1 (i.e. E988K) or by addition of PARPi, causes longer retention of HPF1, consistent with a PARP1–HPF1 complex existing at sites of DNA damage [
41
]. In contrast to the direct interaction with PARP1, the Fan laboratory has found that recruitment of HPF1 to chromatin after oxidative damage depends instead on a direct interaction with Cockayne syndrome group B (CSB), a member of the Swi2/Snf2 family of ATP-dependent chromatin remodelers [
46
]. They demonstrated that HPF1 co-immunoprecipitated with CSB, but only after induction of oxidative stress. CSB itself is recruited to sites of oxidative damage in cells undergoing active transcription through its PAR-binding domain, and thus its association with DNA damage sites, and the consequential association of HPF1, is dependent on the activity of PARP1/2, albeit not a direct interaction with PARP1. As such, as with many things related to PARP and HPF1, the picture is far from clear and requires further experiments. A tentative mechanism is that HPF1 does accumulate at sites of DNA damage and that this recruitment is dependent on either the activity or a direct physical interaction with PARP1/2; however, there could be context-specific variations.
HPF1 abundance
PARP1 is the most abundant nuclear protein after histones [
114
]. In fact, there is sufficient PARP1 to occupy on average 1 in 20 nucleosomes, consistent with the role of PARP1 in chromatin compaction and transcription [
5
,
6
]. HPF1, on the other hand, is present at concentrations on average 30-fold lower than HPF1 (range 10–100-fold), depending on the cell or tissue type [
114
,
115
]. Despite the much lower concentration of HPF1 compared to PARP1, and its relatively weak affinity for PARP1 (∼1 µM), HPF1 can fulfill its role as a modifier of PARP1 (and PARP2) activity through a mechanism known as “hit-and-run” [
96
]. In this mechanism, HPF1 binds and releases from PARP1 (which has to be bound to damaged DNA) rapidly, increasing the rate of initiation of modification on Ser residues by 40-fold to outcompete the slower initiation of modification on Glu/Asp residues that occurs in the absence of HPF1. Importantly, this hit-and-run mechanism is consistent with the observation of dominant modification at Ser residues in cells despite the much higher concentration of PARP1 than HPF1.
Lack of HPF1 in plants
Despite the prevalence of HPF1 in all animals and even though essentially all plants have PARP1 and/or PARP2 homologs, there are no apparent HPF1 homologs in plants. For example,
Arabidopsis thaliana
genome encodes three PARPs, wherein AtPARP1 and AtPARP2 correspond to hPARP1 and hPARP2 [
116
], respectively, each with 38% sequence identity. AtPARP1 is responsible for most of the PARylation activity in cells [
117
], as is also seen for hPARP1. But
A. thaliana
, like all other plants, does not have an HPF1 homolog. Additionally, PARP1/2 in all plants lack at least two of the most C-terminal amino acids found in hPARP1/2 (Leu1013–Trp1014 or Leu569–Trp570, respectively) that are known to form an essential contact with HPF1 (see Fig.
3B
) [
86
]. There is only one report that has explicitly studied the PARylation of plant histones, demonstrating that this modification was hydroxylamine-sensitive, suggesting modification of Glu/Asp residues [
50
]. Is it possible that the robust response to DNA damage in plants is not dependent on PARylation of Ser residues but rather relies on PARylation of Glu/Asp residues? Given the continued uncertainty of the role of different PARylation sites in humans and the relevance of HPF1 to the DNA repair process, this question is interesting and should be interrogated with more modern methods.
Considerations for drug discovery
The discovery of HPF1 and its contributions to a shared active site with PARP1/2 opens the possibility of developing next generation inhibitors. Recall that all existing PARPi were developed prior to the discovery of HPF1. Consistent with this lack of consideration for HPF1, we and others have demonstrated only modest sensitivity of existing PARPi to the presence of HPF1, either
in vitro
or
in vivo
[
118
,
119
]. Because of issues with patient tolerability, specifically regarding dose-limiting hematological toxicities, recent advances in the PARPi field have focused on generating specificity towards PARP1 while sparing PARP2 [
120
–
122
].
Conceptually, increasing the specificity of PARPi by targeting just the PARP1–HPF1 complex instead of the entire much larger pool of PARP1 makes a lot of sense. PARP1 is >30 times more abundant than HPF1 and has many roles in the nucleus that are unrelated to the DNA-damage response, for example in shaping chromatin architecture and in regulating transcription [
6
,
123
]. The response to DNA damage and the effectiveness of PARPi, in contrast, appears to depend on HPF1-mediated PARylation [
45
,
63
]. Also, in wild-type cells it is the HPF1-mediated PARylation of Ser499, Ser507, and Ser519 that mediates release of PARP1 from sites of DNA damage [
45
]. Blocking these serine PARylations on PARP1 should lead to increased retention (trapping) of PARP1 at damage sites, widely considered the molecular basis for the efficacy of PARPi. Thus, inhibiting the PARP1–HPF1 complex preferentially over the isolated PARP1 active site provides a more relevant and less abundant target than binding to all the cellular PARP1. Using a lower dose by targeting only the PARP1–HPF1 complex may mitigate side effects arising from PARPi binding any of the other 17 PARPs that do not bind HPF1, or any other NAD
+
-utilizing enzymes. A lower dose may also lengthen the time to resistance or reduce dose-limiting toxicity, which is currently the largest limitation of PARPi treatment. Additionally, the larger shared active site between PARP1–HPF1 provides opportunities for generating novel chemical matter that can contact both PARP1 and HPF1 and expand beyond the typical nicotinamide mimics that form the basis for all existing PARPi.
In a completely alternative approach, one could attempt to disrupt HPF1-mediated PARylation. with the goal of reducing histone PARylation and thereby inhibiting DNA repair [
124
]. The knockout studies of HPF1 that demonstrate the potentiation of the efficacy of PARPi provide a proof-of-concept that inhibition of PARP1 in the absence of HPF1 using currently approved PARPi is beneficial [
45
]. A mechanistic approach towards this goal would be to disrupt the PARP1–HPF1 interaction using protein-protein interaction inhibitors that target the interface between these two proteins.
Toward the goal of developing novel inhibitors of PARP1 that account for the existence and relevance of HPF1, two different high-throughput screening methods have been recently described. One method, which is amenable for finding inhibitors of PARP1 that are either more or less potent in the presence of HPF1 monitors the dissociation of a fluorescently labeled DNA by fluorescence polarization following PARylation of PARP1 [
125
]. Another method, which can be used for finding inhibitors of the PARP1–HPF1 protein–protein interaction, monitors the FRET signal of individually labeled proteins [
124
]. As of now, this is early days and neither of these methods have yielded compounds of the potency and specificity required for testing in cellular models. As such, it is unclear which approach may be more favorable.
We have shown that the key to potency of existing PARPi is their slow dissociation from PARP1, more specifically the PARP1–HPF1 complex [
119
,
126
]. However, the benefits of HPF1 to potency for existing PARPi are minimal (∼5-fold), and as noted, existing PARPi were not optimized for the PARP1–HPF1, but rather for PARP1 alone. The Lavrik laboratory has shown modest effects of some compounds (1.3–1.9-fold) in the opposite direction, wherein the inhibition of PARP1 by aminomethylmorpholino nucleosides in the presence of HPF1 is weakened [
127
]. The PARPi field is currently missing a good set of tool compounds that have greater specificity either towards PARP1 alone (i.e. blocking HPF1 binding) or toward the PARP1–HPF1 complex (i.e. only binding to the complex). Such tool compounds would be valuable for deconvoluting the role of HPF1 under multiple different cellular conditions such as active replication, damage by different agents, etc. Additionally, although much progress has been made in developing antibody reagents that are specific to MAR or PAR modifications [
95
], the community could also benefit from reagents that are specific to the linkage (e.g. through Ser or Glu/Asp residues).
Open questions
For these open questions, please refer in part to Fig.
4
, but let your imagination expand beyond the shown possibilities therein, using the questions in the following text for stimulation.
Question #1 (
branch point in
Fig.
4B
): In what order do components of the repair machinery arrive at (and leave from) sites of DNA damage, and does HPF1 change the order of these events? Do PARP1 and HPF1 arrive essentially simultaneously, or does PARP1, being much more abundant, arrive first? Recall that PARP1 arrives at sites of DNA damage very early, and that HPF1 does not bind to PARP1 unless the HD-helices have been distorted, an event that does not occur until PARP1 binds to damaged DNA. Thus, PARP1 and HPF1 do not travel as a complex, and possibly PARP1 has time to initiate autoPARylation on Glu/Asp before being joined by HPF1.
Question # 2 (both branches in Fig.
4B
): How does HPF1 leave PARP1 at sites of DNA damage to allow for PAR chain extension? Is departure of HPF1 driven just by the weak affinity between these two proteins, or do MAR modifications on Ser and/or Glu/Asp residues serve as a trigger? What effect do MAR and/or PAR modifications on PARP1 have on HPF1 binding and on the ensuing activity of the PARP1–HPF1 complex? Also, given the much higher levels of PARP1 compared to HPF1, does every PARP1 at sites of DNA damage have the opportunity to be bound to HPF1 to trigger PARylation on Ser residues or is there a stochastic mixture of PARP1 bearing modifications on only Ser residues, only Glu/Asp residues, or both?
Question #3 (bottom branch in Fig.
4B
, curved dotted arrow): In the hit-and-run scenario, what does PARP1 do once HPF1 has left the scene? Does it initiate autoPARylation on remaining Asp/Glu? Does it acquire another HPF1? Or does it only elongate existing chains on histones and itself? PARP1 removal from DNA damage sites is a critical step in the DNA repair process since PARP1 itself only provides the signal for recruitment of the machinery but must get out of the way for DNA damage repair to proceed. If PARP1 arrives first and modifies Glu/Asp residues, does the dissociation process begin? Is transPARylation of histones and/or PARP1 (on Ser residues) catalyzed in the presence of HPF1 sufficient to drive PARP1 removal from damaged nucleosomes or does PARP1 removal depend on PARylation of Glu/Asp residues?
Question #4: Is the mechanism of DNA damage repair as initiated by PARP1 ± HPF1 inherently a sloppy or a stochastic process? This question is directly tied to the role of HPF1 in DNA repair. Recall, hundreds of proteins have been found to be modified on thousands of sites, both in the presence and absence of HPF1. Are each of these modifications individually consequential or merely a by-product of an emergency response wherein PARP1 will PARylate something/anything, on Glu/Asp residues or on Ser residues, at or near the site of the DNA damage. Does such “splashing” of ADPR paint then mediate recruitment of DNA repair factors; and close counts, because once you are near the site of DNA damage, diffusion in the area is fast? Or is actual specific modification on histone tails (on Ser residues) what makes DNA repair so effective? There is a clear need for more research using the combined efforts and ingenuity from the vibrant and collaborative PARP community.
