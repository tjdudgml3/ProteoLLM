Before you begin
Blastoids
are in vitro-derived 3D structures that can resemble key architectural and lineage features of the natural blastocyst, offering a scalable and ethically accessible model for studying early mammalian development.
Researching
phosphorylation
in blastoid cells is essential because this dynamic post-translational modification regulates nearly all aspects of embryogenesis, including:
cell polarization
and compaction (via proteins such as Par/aPKC complexes
2
),
lineage specification
(e.g., through phosphorylation-mediated activation of transcription factors like YAP
3
),
and signal transduction
in pathways such as Hippo, Wnt, and TGF-β, which direct cell fate decisions.
4
Quantifying phosphorylation events provides functional insight into signaling dynamics that underpin successful blastoid formation and offers a readout of developmental fidelity compared to in vivo embryos.
Innovation
This protocol advanced the current methodology by establishing the first integrated and reproducible workflow for quantitative phosphoproteomic analysis of EPSC-derived blastoids. Unlike existing generalized phosphoproteomic methods, this approach is specifically optimized for the unique challenges of blastoid models, including complex culture procedures and the dynamic signaling events governing embryonic patterning. Key innovations include precise temporal sampling aligned with critical developmental milestones (Day 3 and Day 5) and an integrated workflow combining efficient IMAC-based phosphopeptide enrichment with high-pH fractionation to maximize depth. By providing a robust framework that directly links phosphorylation signaling to blastoid development, this protocol enables systematic investigation of embryogenesis in an accessible and scalable model system, overcoming technological and ethical limitations of traditional embryo-based studies.
Prepare MEF plate
Timing: 1.5 weeks
1.
Sacrifice a timed-pregnant mouse (typically at E12.5–E14.5) following institutional ethical guidelines.
Note:
Before E12.5, the embryos are too small and the dissection is difficult. After E14.5, the fibroblasts have a significantly shorter
in vitro
lifespan before reaching senescence, so they are inefficient and costly for routine lab use. During E12.5-E14.5, the organs are easy to remove, and the embryos are large enough to have a high yield while fibroblasts still have high proliferative capacity and minimal senescence.
2.
Sterilize the abdomen with ethanol.
3.
Isolate the uterus.
4.
Transfer embryos to a sterile dish with PBS.
5.
Decapitate and eviscerate embryos to exclude organs/neural tissue, retaining the torso.
6.
Finely chop torsos into small fragments using sterile scissors or scalpels.
7.
Incubate fragments in trypsin-EDTA (15–30 min at 37°C) to dissociate cells, with gentle agitation.
8.
Add MEF medium (DMEM + 10% FBS + 1x NEAA + 1x GlutaMAX) to inhibit enzymatic activity.
9.
Centrifuge (300 ×
g
, 5 min).
a.
Discard the supernatant.
b.
Resuspend in fresh medium before the next step.
10.
Seed cells in culture 100 mm dishes and incubate at 37°C with 5% CO
2
.
11.
Replace the medium after 24 h to eliminate non-adherent cells/tissue remnants.
12.
Grow MEFs until passage 4.
a.
Refresh the medium every 48 h.
b.
Passage at 80% confluency.
Note:
Healthy MEFs should have a classic bipolar or tripolar “spindle-like” shape. Microscopic inspection for confluence, morphology, and contamination is the absolute minimum before passaging. In addition, the Trypan Blue exclusion assay should be performed regularly.
13.
Treat MEF cells with 10 μg/mL mitomycin C.
14.
Detach MEFs from the plate with TrypLE (1 mL/10 cm
2
area).
Optional:
Freeze cells at 6 million cells per mL in 10% DMSO in FBS.
Note:
It is recommended to generate enough MEFs at once and store frozen cells for future use.
15.
Cover the bottom of 6-well tissue culture dishes with sterile 0.1% gelatin solution.
a.
Incubate at 37% for at least 1 h.
b.
Remove the gelatin solution before the next step.
16.
Thaw MEF vials and seed them on a 0.1% gelatin-coated plate in MEF medium at about 3.3 × 10
4
/cm
2
.
a.
Use MEF plates within 24–48 h.
The protocol below provides step-wise details that are based on the method section of Dr. Hongkui Deng’s research article (“Derivation of Pluripotent Stem Cells with In Vivo Embryonic and Extraembryonic Potency” by Yang, Liu, Xu, Wang, and Wu et al).
5
Generate and culture mEPSC
Timing: 2–3 weeks
17.
Seed blastocysts.
a.
Harvest mouse blastocysts at E3.5.
i.
Cut at the uterine bifurcation to separate the uterine horns.
ii.
Hold the cervix with forceps.
iii.
Insert a blunt needle into the cervical opening.
iv.
Inject pre-warmed (37°C) 0.5 mL M2 medium + 3% BSA.
v.
Expel blastocysts into a 35 mm cell culture dish.
b.
Transfer harvested blastocysts to MEF plates in N2B27-LCDM medium.
Note:
Avoid overcrowding by plate ≤10 blastocysts/well of a 24-well plate.
c.
Incubate at 37°C and 5% CO
2
.
i.
Change medium every 24 h.
18.
Monitor outgrowth.
a.
Check for attached blastocyst outgrowths under a microscope.
Note:
Minimize vibration/shaking throughout incubation to avoid detaching colonies.
b.
Blastocysts’ outgrowth usually happens on Day 4 after seeding.
Note:
Outgrowth should appear as intact dome-shaped colonies.
19.
Obtain and maintain mEPS cells.
a.
Dissect outgrowths into small clumps (3–5 cells/clump) using a sterile needle or pipette tip.
Note:
Minimize time outside the incubator.
b.
Transfer clumps to fresh MEF plates with N2B27-LCDM medium.
c.
Passage cells every 2–3 days by repeating step 4a in this section.
Note:
Cells should be passaged to at least P5 for blastoid formation experiments. Early passages may contain a heterogeneous mix of fully reprogrammed EPS cells, partially reprogrammed cells, and differentiated cells. Allowing cells to stabilize through early passages is critical for reproducibility.
d.
(Optional but recommended) Freeze multiple vials at P5-P8 to establish low-passage stocks.
The protocol below applies to embryo-derived EPSCs and ES/iPS-converted EPSCs. The procedure for generating EPS-blastoids is modified from the protocol by Li et al.
6
Generate EPS blastoids
Timing: 2 weeks
20.
Culture EPSCs.
a.
Maintain EPSCs on mitomycin C-treated MEFs in N2B27-LCDM medium.
Note:
Optimal passages of EPSCs are P5-P15. High passages may have karyotype instability, reduced competency, or even senescence.
b.
Replace medium daily.
c.
Before beginning a blastoid experiment, conform EPSCs are in a healthy state.
i.
>90% of colonies should be smooth-edged and dome-shaped.
Optional:
Perform live-cell stains or alkaline phosphatase stains to confirm EPSCs are in a healthy and undifferentiated state.
21.
Dissociate EPSC colonies.
a.
Treat EPSC colonies with TrypLE Express Enzyme to dissociate into single cells.
b.
Transfer cells to a 0.1% gelatin-coated 6-well plate.
i.
Incubate at 37°C for 1 h.
Note:
This step is to exclude MEF from EPSCs, because MEF can prevent EPSCs from aggregating and impede the generation of blastoids. Check the plate under a microscope to ensure that most MEFs, which are larger in size, are depleted from EPSCs. As we tested with a strain of GFP-expressing iPSCs, gelatin-coated plates can deplete about 50% of MEF carryover, reducing the percentage of MEF carryover to under 5% of single cells (
Figure 1
).

c.
Collect the cell suspension.
d.
Filter the cell suspension through a 40 μm cell strainer to eliminate cell aggregates.
e.
Following filtration, assess cell viability using Trypan Blue exclusion.
i.
Only suspensions with >85% viability should be used for subsequent experiments.
Note:
Confirm the absence of large cell aggregates via phase-contrast microscopy.
f.
Count cells with a cell counter.
22.
Prepare AggreWell Plate.
a.
Set up AggreWell 400 per
the manufacturer’s instructions
.
23.
Aggregate EPSCs.
a.
Resuspend about 6000 cells in supplemented EPS blastoid seeding medium.
b.
Seed into one well of a 24-well AggreWell plate (5 cells/microwell).
c.
Gently shake the plate in straight lines and spin down at 200 ×
g
for 2 min.
d.
Designate this seeding day as Day 0.
24.
Culture Blastoids.
a.
On Day 1, observe the plate under the microscope to confirm that cells form a compact aggregate, in which individual cells cannot be distinguished under a phase-contrast microscope.
b.
Remove the medium slowly without aspirating cell aggregates.
c.
Replace with EPS blastoid induction medium.
d.
Check the culture daily to examine blastoid formation.
Note:
Look for the formation of a defined, large, single fluid-filled cavity and the appearance of a distinct inner cell mass (ICM)-like structure.
25.
Harvest EPSC blastoids.
a.
Day 3 and Day 5 are the important timepoints to harvest EPSC blastoids (
Figure 2
).
Note:
On Day 3, there should be a “phase-bright” small cavity inside the smooth spherical ball under phase-contrast microscopy. And on Day 5, the blastoid should be a near-perfect morphological mimic of a blastocyst with a large and dominant blastocoel cavity and a discernible inner cell mass (ICM)-like structure (a dense cluster of cells at one pole). Definitive confirmation requires immunofluorescence staining of CDX2 for the outer trophectoderm-like layer and OCT4 for the inner ICM-like cluster.
Figure 2
Timeline to harvest blastoids (“Generate EPS blastoids”)
Passage isolated mEPSCs for at least five generations to achieve homogeneity and full reprogramming. At this point, cryopreserve the mEPSCs for future use. Initiate the blastoid experiment by seeding mEPSCs in AggreWell, designated as Day 0. By Day 3, use phase-contrast microscopy to assess if cell clusters have formed a small cavity, and harvest early blastoid samples to evaluate embryo development. By Day 5, successful blastoid development is indicated by a distinct blastocoel and a dense cell cluster at one pole, resembling an ICM-like structure.
b.
Manually collect EPS aggregates/blastoids using a mouth pipette.
Note:
For manual control without a mouth pipette, use a P200 pipettor with wide-bore tips for blastoids collection, but this method is less precise.

Step-by-step method details
Proteomics sample preparation for TMT labeling
Timing: 2 days
This section describes the procedure to process blastoids for quantitative proteomics. In this procedure, blastoids from Day 3 (polarization and compaction) and Day 5 (lineage specification) are collected, and a 6-plex TMT kit is used to quantitatively assess the relative abundance of peptides.
1.
Collect samples and lyse cells.
a.
Collect EPS-blastoids on
Day 3
and
Day 5
(three biological replicates per group).
Note:
Steps 1 to 3 need to be performed consecutively each day, which will cost about 20 h on each day (including overnight incubation).
b.
Prepare each sample by adding 3 × 10
4
blastoids to 100 μL lysis buffer at 4°C.
c.
Sonicate samples 3x on ice.
d.
Centrifuge at 12000 ×
g
, 4°C for 10 min in a 24 × 2 mL tube quick-lock rotor.
e.
Collect 80 μL supernatant and quantify protein with 10 μL supernatant using a BCA kit.
2.
Digest proteins.
a.
Add 0.8 μL DTT from a 0.5 M stock to a final concentration of 5 mM and incubate at 56°C for 30 min.
b.
Add 9 μL 140 mM iodoacetamide to a final concentration of 14 mM and incubate at RT for 15 min in darkness.
c.
Quench unreacted iodoacetamide by adding 0.9 μL 0.5 M DTT to an additional 5 mM and incubating for 15 min at room temperature in the dark.
d.
Dilute samples with 272 μL 100 mM NH
4
HCO
3
to reduce the urea <2 M.
e.
Add 4 μg trypsin (1:50 trypsin: protein) and incubate overnight at 37°C.
f.
Add another 2 μg trypsin (1:100 ratio) and incubate at 37°C for 4 h.
3.
Cleanup peptides.
a.
Desalt peptides using a Strata X C18 SPE column.
Note:
The desalting step costs approximately 30 min per sample. To save time, use a 96-well plate format and perform desalting samples in parallel.
b.
Vacuum-dry peptides for about 1.5 h.
Pause point:
Dried peptides can be stored at −20°C for 1–2 weeks or −80°C for at least a month.
4.
Label TMTs.
a.
Reconstitute peptides in 100 μL 0.5 M TEAB (the peptide concentration is estimated to be 6 mg/mL).
b.
Dilute each sample to 100 μg peptides in 100 μL 0.5 M TEAB.
c.
Label peptides with TMT6 reagents following
the manufacturer’s protocol
.
i.
Day 3 replicates: TMT
6
-126, 127, 128.
ii.
Day 5 replicates: TMT
6
-129, 130, 131.
d.
Incubate at RT for 2 h.
e.
Pool all TMT-labeled samples.
Enrich phosphorylated peptides
Timing: 4 h
This section further processes the TMT-labeled peptides with IMAC to selectively retain phosphopeptides and exclude non-phosphorylated peptides as much as possible.
5.
Prepare samples.
a.
Dissolve TMT-labeled peptides in the enrichment buffer.
b.
Centrifuge at 12000 ×
g
for 5 min to pellet insoluble debris.
c.
Transfer 90 μL supernatant to a new tube.
6.
Prepare immobilized metal ion chromatography (IMAC) material (
Figure 3
.1).
a.
Pre-wash IMAC beads with enrichment buffer.
Figure 3
Schematics for IMAC bead handling (step 8)
Start with gently shaking a tube containing IMAC Fe-NTA beads in the enrichment buffer. Add digested protein samples, and phosphopeptides will bind to the Fe-NTA complex. Separate the beads and liquid by applying a magnetic field. Then wash the mixture by sequentially adding wash buffer and enrichment buffer for three rounds and applying magnetic fields as needed. Finally, use ice-cold 10% ammonia water to elute phosphopeptides from IMAC Fe-NTA beads.
7.
Bind phosphopeptides (
Figure 3
.2).
a.
Incubate the peptide solution with pre-washed IMAC beads on a rotating mixer at room temperature for 1 h.
b.
Over the incubation period, the initially cloudy suspension of beads will gradually clear upon successful binding.
Note:
Gently shake during incubation to prevent bead damage.
8.
Wash IMAC-peptide complexes
7
(
Figure 3
.3).
a.
Wash IMAC-peptide complex with 10 bead volumes of enrichment buffer for 1 min.
b.
Wash IMAC-peptide complex with 10 bead volumes of wash buffer for 1 min.
c.
Repeat a-b 2 times.
Note:
Using a magnetic tube rack to rapidly collect beads on the side of the tube, and pipette away from the magnetic pellet to prevent bead loss.
9.
Elute phosphopeptides (
Figure 3
.4).
a.
Elute bound phosphopeptides with ice-cold 10% ammonia water (pH ∼11).
b.
Collect and combine all eluate fractions.
Note:
To prevent degradation, elute quickly and in a cold environment.
10.
Concentrate samples.
a.
Dry combined eluates by vacuum centrifugation for 2 h.
Pause point:
Samples can be stored at −80°C for a week.
Fractionate peptides by reversed-phase HPLC
Timing: 4 h
This section aims to reduce sample complexity for downstream LC-MS/MS analysis. After IMAC enrichment, the hydrophilic phosphopeptide will elute earlier on the reversed-phase HPLC, resulting in a chromatogram with higher peak intensity in the early elution.
11.
Prepare the HPLC system.
a.
Equilibrate Agilent 300 Extend C18 column (5 μm, 4.6 × 250 mm) with starting buffer (pH 9.0).
12.
Load and separate peptides.
a.
Dissolve dried peptides in 0.1% NH
4
OH.
b.
Inject the sample onto the column.
c.
Separate peptides:
i.
Linearly increase the gradient from 8% to 32% acetonitrile (ACN) in 0.1 M ammonium bicarbonate (NH
4
HCO
3
) adjusted to pH 9 with ammonium hydroxide (NH
4
OH) over a period of 60 min. Set the flow rate to 1 mL/min.
d.
Collect 60 fractions.
13.
Pool fractions (
Figure 4
).
a.
Combine the 60 fractions into 4 pooled fractions (e.g., fraction 1 = 1 + 5 + 9 +…+57; fraction 2 = 2 + 6 + 10 + … +58, etc.).
Figure 4
Illustration for HPLC fraction strategy (step 13)
Phosphopeptides from the enrichment step are loaded onto an equilibrated C18 column. An acetonitrile gradient is increased from 8% to 32% over the course of 60 min (e.g., set 8% acetonitrile as solvent A and 58% acetonitrile as solvent B, and increase the percentage of solvent B from 0 to 48% while both are adjusted to pH 9). The flow rate is set to 1 mL/min, and one 1 mL fraction is collected every minute from the HPLC eluate. Non-sequentially combine the fractions as illustrated into 6 pooled fractions for further processing.
Note:
This step helps to maximize phosphopeptide diversity in each fraction by pooling fractions non-consecutively.
14.
Concentrate.
a.
Dry pooled fractions by vacuum centrifugation for 2 h.
Pause point:
Samples can be stored at −80°C for a week.
Perform LC-MS/MS runs
Timing: 1.5 days
This section acquires proteomics data on LC-MS/MS with parameters specified for Q Exactive plus.
15.
Desalt peptides.
a.
Desalt peptides using C18 ZipTips:
i.
Wet tip with 50% ACN/0.1% FA.
ii.
Equilibrate with 0.1% FA.
iii.
Dissolve dried samples with 0.1% FA.
iv.
Bind peptides by pipetting samples through tips.
v.
Wash with 0.1% FA.
vi.
Elute with 80% ACN/0.1% FA.
vii.
Dry samples by vacuum centrifugation for 1 h.
16.
Set up the LC gradient (
Figure 5

Expected outcomes
This protocol yields the identification of thousands of phosphopeptides and can map them onto 2000+ proteins (
Figure 6
). Among the phosphorylated sites, there are around a thousand hyperphosphorylated sites and hypophosphorylated sites at a similar magnitude. About 24% (5039/21318) of identified peptides do not have a TMT label, slightly limiting the resolving power of this quantitative approach. However, at the protein level, this limitation attenuates as only 3% (97/3168) of proteins do not have TMT labels, and the identification of phosphorylated sites is the same across replicates. In addition, less than 0.5% of identified peptides do not have the same status of TMT labels between replicates, suggesting good reproducibility of this approach. Most phosphosites are serine (>90%) while tyrosine contributes a small fraction (<1%), agreeing with eukaryotic phosphorylation precedence. This result confirms active phosphorylation activity in mouse blastoids, with an example showing changes in the phosphorylation status of PDHA1 from Day 3 to Day 5 (
Figure 7
). During the rapid and precise events of early embryogenesis, post-translational regulation—particularly through phosphorylation—serves as a critical mechanism to swiftly control protein activity without requiring new gene transcription. This process acts as a master switch, coordinating essential developmental programs. For instance, phosphorylation directly activates key signaling pathways like MAPK and PI3K/AKT, which govern fundamental cell behaviors including proliferation, differentiation, and survival.
11
It also establishes cellular architecture, as seen when the phosphorylation of polarity complexes (Par3/Par6/aPKC) dictates apical-basal polarity and forms tight junctions to create distinct tissue layers.
2
Furthermore, phosphorylation meticulously drives the cell cycle by activating and deactivating cyclins and cyclin-dependent kinases, ensuring timely cell divisions.
12
Finally, it regulates gene expression at the nuclear level by modifying transcription factors to recruit them to DNA or by altering histones to open chromatin, allowing specific genes to be read.
13
Thus, through these multifaceted roles, phosphorylation provides the dynamic and immediate control necessary to orchestrate the complex dance of early development.

Limitations
EPSC-derived blastoids may not fully replicate
in vivo
embryo phosphorylation dynamics: for example, a significant hyperphosphorylation of glycolysis is observed in blastoids but not in embryos, potentially due to the artificial media component and high glucose content in the
in vitro
environment.
1
,
14
Future exploration of the phosphoproteome of alternative cell models should be encouraged to examine which system best simulates
in vivo
phosphorylation events. These alternative cell models can be “assembloids” with trophoblast stem cells and extraembryonic endoderm cells or even human naïve pluripotent stem cells-derived blastoid models.
15
,
16
Multi-omics analysis, especially metabolomics analysis, enabled by the large-scale production of blastoids, can also aid the interpretation of embryonic developmental events.
17
In addition, spliceosome is increasingly phosphorylated from 2-cell stages to blastocysts
in vivo
, but the phosphorylation of spliceosome is less significant
in vitro
, potentially due to the lack of a maternal molecular landscape that is rich with maternal RNA inputs.
1
,
14
The protocol relies on an
in vitro
system lacking maternal tissue interactions, which might change the post-translational modifications of relevant pathways. Introduction of maternal tissues, such as a 3D co-culture system with endometrial organoid assemblies,
18
,
19
might better emulate
in vivo
phosphorylation environments. Yet, the rest of the protocol can be adapted to examine phosphoproteomics of
in vivo
embryos or more complex
in vitro
systems suggested above, or the data can be compared to existing phosphoproteomics data for cross-validation.
14
On a brief note, although
in vivo
data did not observe significant phosphorylation of glycolysis, RNA stabilization is predominantly regulated by phosphorylation and thus supports the observation in blastoids.
Troubleshooting
Problem 1
Low mEPS viability (
before you begin
, step 19b).
Potential solution
Include 10 μM ROCK inhibitor (Y-27632) in the N2B27-LDCM media.
Problem 2
Protein digestion is incomplete (Step 2).
Potential solution
F
irstly, check if trypsin activity is at its optimum. One possible caveat with the current protocol is the protease inhibitor cocktail in the lysis buffer. We intend to overcome the inhibitor activity by two steps of trypsin digestion. If the problem persists, an alternative solution is to use sodium deoxycholate (SDC) 4% (wt/vol) in 100 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.5) as a lysis buffer at 4°C and quickly heat-treat the lysate for 5 min at 95°C to stabilize the proteome according to Humphrey et al.
20
However, our current method uses inhibitors and keeps samples at low temperatures because heat treatment might still reduce the maximum number of peptides detected.
21
Problem 3
Inconsistent sample input (Step 1b).
Potential solution
Normalize by protein content instead. After lysis/sonication (Step 1c), measure protein concentration (BCA, Step 1e) before proceeding. Use equal protein amounts (e.g., 50 μg) for digestion. Blastoid size/lysis efficiency may vary; protein normalization ensures comparable input.
Problem 4
Suboptimal trypsin digestion (Step 2e/f).
Potential solution
Use enzyme-to-protein mass ratio (1:50) based on BCA-quantified protein, not fixed μg. Run SDS-PAGE on 5% of lysate pre-/post-digestion to confirm complete cleavage.
Problem 5
Biased IMAC enrichment of multi-phosphorylation over mono-phosphorylation (Step 8).
Potential solution
Combine IMAC with TiO
2
enrichment in a sequential or parallel workflow. TiO
2
captures monophosphorylated peptides more efficiently, improving the coverage of tyrosine sites. Validate with phosphopeptide standards to ensure balanced representation.
Problem 6
Limited fractionation depth on low-abundance phosphopeptides (Step 13).
Potential solution
Increase fractionation to 12–24 pools (non-adjacent concatenation) if instrument time is available.
Problem 7
Omitting biologically important phosphosites with ambiguous fragmentation (Step 18).
Potential solution
Cross-validate sites using complementary tools like
Mascot Delta Score
or
PTMProphet
to rescue borderline probability sites (0.6–0.75). Prioritize low-probability sites in enriched pathways (e.g., glycolysis/Hippo) for manual MS/MS spectrum validation.
