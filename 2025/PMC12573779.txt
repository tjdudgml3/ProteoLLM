INTRODUCTION
Alzheimer's disease (AD) is characterized by the accumulation of neurotoxic protein aggregates, notably amyloid beta (Aβ) plaques and tau neurofibrillary tangles. With the advent of tau‐selective positron emission tomography (tau PET) imaging, it is possible to visualize tau pathology in vivo.
1
,
2
Tau PET not only tracks the spatial progression of tangles along the Braak stages, but also serves as a more proximate indicator of neuronal dysfunction and clinical symptoms than amyloid burden.
3
,
4
Despite this advance, the molecular underpinnings responsible for elevated tau PET signals remain incompletely understood. Moreover, beyond the classical amyloid and tau‐centric view, AD pathology encompasses a broad array of interconnected biological processes (BPs). Emerging evidence has implicated neuroinflammation, lipid and glucose metabolism, proteostasis, and other systemic alterations in the progression of AD.
5
,
6
,
7
However, their individual contributions and interactions in relation to tau pathology are complex and remain to be elucidated.
The advent of high‐dimensional omics technologies has provided an opportunity to interrogate these complex molecular changes.
8
,
9
These approaches have underscored that AD is a multi‐factorial disease; therefore, an integrative systems biology strategy is well suited to capture the heterogeneous molecular landscape underlying tau pathology.
10
,
11
,
12
In parallel, neuroimaging‐based analyses have increasingly been incorporated.
13
,
14
However, prior single‐omics studies, while informative, have provided fragmented views of the molecular landscape underlying tau accumulation. Proteomic analyses alone captured protein‐level changes but missed upstream transcriptional regulation and pathway connectivity. Similarly, transcriptomic studies revealed gene expression alterations but could not account for post‐translational modifications and protein–protein interactions (PPIs) that drive cellular dysfunction.
8
,
9
Tau PET imaging studies have predominantly focused on regional burden quantification and clinical correlations, with limited integration of molecular data to elucidate underlying BPs,
3
,
4
which left substantial gaps in understanding how molecular networks orchestrate the complex pathophysiology reflected in tau PET signals. Furthermore, existing molecular studies have largely relied on individual biomarker approaches rather than systems‐level network analysis. Individual proteins or genes rarely drive complex phenotypes in isolation. Instead, they function cooperatively within molecular networks to modulate complex phenotypic transitions.
Thus, we aimed to integrate tau PET imaging with multi‐omics network modules to provide a systems‐level framework connecting in vivo neuroimaging biomarkers with their molecular processes in AD tau accumulation (Figure
1
).

METHOD
2.1
Study cohort
Data were obtained from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) database.
15
All participants with complete tau PET data and baseline data were included. Additionally, a subset of these participants also had cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) proteomics available.
According to ADNI diagnostic criteria, participants were classified as cognitively normal (CN) if they exhibited no significant cognitive deficits or functional impairments affecting activities of daily living. CN participants typically demonstrated a Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) score between 24 and 30, a Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) of 0, and a Memory Box score of 0. Participants with cognitive impairment (CI), encompassing both mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and dementia, were characterized by an MMSE score of 24 to 30, a CDR of 0.5, and a Memory Box score of ≥ 0.5. In this group, global cognitive function and daily activities remained sufficiently intact to preclude a clinical diagnosis of dementia at the screening assessment. Participants with an MMSE score between 20 and 26 and a CDR of ≥ 0.5, meeting the National Institute of Neurological and Communicative Disorders and Stroke/Alzheimer's Disease and Related Disorders Association criteria for probable AD, were also included in the CI cohort.
Demographic variables included age, sex, and years of education. Apolipoprotein E (
APOE
) ε4 carrier status was determined from genotyping. Cognitive and executive functions were quantified by Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), Alzheimer's Disease Assessment Scale 13‐item Cognitive Subscale (ADAS13), Trail Making Test Part B scores (TRABSCOR), and functional autonomy captured by Functional Activities Questionnaire (FAQ).
2.2
AD tau PET imaging biomarker measurement
Tau PET data were processed using the University of California, Berkeley PET analysis pipeline. The dataset comprised
18
F‐flortaucipir (FTP, AV1451) scans acquired prior to September 1, 2022 (8 mm isotropic resolution), as well as
18
F‐MK6240, and
18
F‐PI2620 scans acquired thereafter (harmonized to 6 mm full width at half maximum). Standardized uptake value ratios (SUVRs) were intensity normalized to inferior cerebellar gray matter, with additional eroded subcortical white matter normalization available for longitudinal analyses. All images underwent rigorous quality control.
16
Tau SUVR values from 60 distinct regions were included. For each region, SUVR differences between two groups were assessed by linear regressions, adjusting for age, sex, years of education, and
APOE
ε4 genotype. Regions showing significant differences after Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction were preserved for further analysis.
To identify tau PET imaging predictors of CI, we constructed a classification model by the Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM) algorithm. The model input comprised SUVRs from significantly different regions. A 5‐fold cross‐validation approach was used to optimize LightGBM hyperparameters.
17
Model performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). The tau PET imaging predictor was defined as regional SUVR features whose cumulative gain ranked within the top 80% of the total model gain.
RESEARCH IN CONTEXT
Systematic Review
: We reviewed prior studies that investigated relationships between tau and molecular alterations in Alzheimer's disease (AD). Although individual omics datasets have separately identified tau‐correlated changes in lipid metabolism, immune signaling, and synaptic function, there remains a lack of comprehensive frameworks that map multi‐omic biological modules onto in vivo tau positron emission tomography (PET) imaging burden in cognitively characterized human populations.
Interpretation
: Our findings demonstrated that biologically interpretable omics‐derived modules showed close correspondence with regional tau PET intensity in individuals with AD. Modules enriched in lipid metabolism, mitochondrial bioenergetics, microglial activation, and synaptic function exhibited distinct regional associations with tau accumulation, and several of these modules correlated with cognitive decline metrics. These results indicated that the anatomical patterning of tau pathology reflected underlying molecular vulnerabilities and was shaped by conserved multi‐omic biology.
Future Directions
: Future studies should assess the temporal trajectories of omics–tau relationships in longitudinal cohorts, to determine whether early molecular signatures could predict regional vulnerability to tau aggregation. In parallel, experimental validation of key driver genes and pathways might clarify their causal roles in tau propagation.
Last, we assessed the clinical relevance of regional tau burden by examining associations between tau PET imaging predictor SUVR values and neuropsychological performances. Separate linear regression models were fit for each cognitive outcome, with SUVR values as predictors. All models adjusted for age, sex, years of education, and
APOE
ε4 status. Because the number of participants with both diffusion tensor imaging and Aβ data was limited, we conducted a multivariate linear regression in which Aβ was included as an additional covariate as sensitivity analysis.
Key tau PET imaging biomarkers were defined as regional SUVR measures that (1) exhibited significant elevation in CI compared to CN participants after FDR correction, (2) demonstrated high feature importance in distinguishing CI from CN within the LightGBM classification framework, and (3) showed robust associations with cognitive and functional outcomes.
2.3
Multi‐omics overlapping differential signatures
Differential expression analysis comparing CN and CI groups was first conducted on CSF proteomic data obtained from the ADNI. Expression values were log2‐transformed. Group‐level differences were assessed using two‐sample
t
tests, with effect sizes expressed as log2 fold change.
To enhance cross‐dataset consistency and mitigate potential variability intrinsic to proteomic profiling, we incorporated bulk peripheral blood transcriptomic datasets of
GSE63063
and
GSE97760
.
18
,
19
Differential gene expression analysis was performed by the limma package.
20
For each omics layer, molecules with nominal
p
< 0.01 were considered significant. We defined overlapping differential signatures as those proteins whose corresponding gene symbols were also differentially expressed (
p
< 0.01) in at least one of the two transcriptomic datasets.
2.4
Integration with tau PET imaging biomarkers
We applied two‐way orthogonal partial least squares (O2PLS) modeling to capture the shared covariation between the CSF proteome and tau burden.
21
This approach enabled us to model the proteomic–tau relationships while filtering out variance in each data modality unrelated to the other. We ranked proteins by the absolute magnitude of their joint‐component loading and selected the top 50 proteins as the key tau‐associated proteomic signatures. We also constructed a joint association score matrix between all proteomic and tau variables. We highlighted the top 50 protein–tau pairs with the greatest absolute joint scores, which indicated the highest covariance associations of a protein abundance with tau PET uptake in a particular brain region.
2.5
Network‐based module identification and functional enrichment analysis
We constructed a PPI network of the top 50 O2PLS‐derived proteins using the STRING database. To further resolve functional substructures within the PPI network, we used Louvain community detection.
22
Functional enrichment analyses across Gene Ontology (GO) categories including BP, cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF) as well as Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways were performed with clusterProfiler package.
23
2.6
Gene set variation analysis
Based on previously defined PPI modules, we applied gene set variation analysis (GSVA) to quantify the activity of each protein module in individual subjects.
24
Module scores were computed using the GSVA R package with a Gaussian kernel, producing continuous, normalized enrichment scores for each sample and module.
2.7
Association of module activity with tau PET burden and cognitive performance
To investigate the relationship between protein module activity and disease markers, multivariate linear regression models were constructed with module scores as independent variables and key tau PET SUVRs from previously identified tau PET imaging biomarkers or cognitive scores (ADAS13, MMSE) as dependent variables. Covariates included age, sex, and
APOE
ε4 genotype to adjust for known confounders.
2.8
Cell type–specific enrichment patterns
To investigate whether the tau‐associated molecular modules exhibited altered expression patterns in specific neural cell populations in AD and independently validate the biological activity of the modules, we additionally analyzed the single‐nucleus RNA‐sequencing (snRNA‐seq) dataset (
GSE147528
). We quantified the activity of each module across major cell types using AUCell analysis.
25
Cell type–specific comparisons of module activity between AD and control groups were performed using Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests, with FDR correction applied. All analyses were performed in R 4.3.2.

3.2
Differences in tau PET uptake between CI and CN
Among 60 regions, 48 exhibited significantly different SUVR values in CI relative to CN. The largest between‐group differences were observed in medial temporal and limbic regions, including the amygdala, entorhinal cortex, and hippocampus (Table
S1

3.3
LightGBM‐based identification of 16 tau PET imaging predictors
A LightGBM classification model trained on SUVR features achieved robust performance in discriminating CI from CN, yielding an AUC of 0.91 in 5‐fold cross‐validation (Figure
2A
). The top‐ranking tau PET imaging predictors included 16 regions (Figure
2B
). To address potential dataset specificity of the selected regions of interest (ROIs), we performed a parallel analysis using the meta‐temporal ROI. With the same LightGBM framework, the temporal meta‐ROI alone achieved a 5‐fold cross‐validation AUC of 0.72, indicating that while tau burden in canonical temporal regions carried discriminative information, the full model captured additional spatial heterogeneity of tau burden and provided superior discrimination of cognitive status.

3.4
Significant associations between 15 tau PET imaging biomarkers and clinical measures
Among 16 top‐ranking imaging predictors, 15 regions demonstrated significant associations with at least three out of five cognitive and functional outcomes assessed. These regions included the amygdala, entorhinal cortex, hippocampus, inferior lateral ventricle, frontal pole, parahippocampal gyrus, caudal anterior cingulate, temporal pole, lateral ventricle, inferior temporal cortex, superior temporal cortex, caudal middle frontal gyrus, fusiform gyrus, lateral orbitofrontal cortex, and cuneus (Figure
2C
). Multivariate regression analyses revealed higher SUVR values were associated with lower scores on the MMSE and MoCA, indicating worse global cognitive function. Similarly, increased tau burden in these regions was correlated with higher scores on the CDR Sum of Boxes, ADAS13 and FAQ, reflecting greater cognitive impairment severity. Thus, these 15 regions were designated as tau PET imaging biomarkers (Table
S2a
in supporting information). In the sensitivity analysis restricted to the subset of 271 individuals with available Aβ data, higher SUVR values remained significantly associated with worse global cognition and greater impairment severity, thereby reinforcing the robustness of the observed associations (Table
S2b
).

DISCUSSION
This study highlighted the value of positioning tau PET not only as a marker of disease severity but also as an imaging anchor that could be mechanistically contextualized by omics‐derived molecular networks. We identified 15 tau‐associated imaging biomarkers. The network‐based module analysis further delineated four major molecular modules whose expression patterns closely reflected tau PET pathology in AD.
4.1
Biological interpretation of module associations
Module 1 was the largest network module, enriched in energy metabolism, protein homeostasis, and cytoskeletal regulation. Higher Module 1 activity correlated with elevated tau PET SUVR in regions spanning the temporal, frontal, and limbic lobes, among others. Elevated Module 1 scores were linked to worse cognitive performance. Impaired glucose metabolism is a well‐recognized feature of AD and is often interpreted as a neurodegenerative marker. The strong association of Module 1 with tau PET burden implied that regions with high tau accumulation also experience pronounced metabolic stress, which might exacerbate neurodegeneration.
26
,
27
Additionally, the cytoskeletal components of Module 1 aligned with the known role of tau in maintaining microtubule integrity.
28
,
29
As tau becomes hyperphosphorylated and dissociates from microtubules, it causes microtubule destabilization and axonal transport failure, leading to neurofibrillary tangle formation and neuronal dysfunction. Our results reinforced this paradigm, linking tau PET‐detectable pathology to concurrent disruptions in cellular bioenergetics and cytoskeletal maintenance.
The prominence of LDHA in Module 1 also warranted acknowledgment of potential astrocytic contributions. Emerging evidence indicated that astrocytic glycolysis became dysregulated in AD, with alterations in lactate metabolism potentially disrupting the astrocyte–neuron lactate shuttle that was critical for synaptic function and neuronal energy homeostasis.
30
,
31
The significant elevation of Module 1 activity in astrocytes observed in single‐nucleus validation suggested that the LDHA‐related metabolic signature might reflect astrocytic dysfunction alongside neuronal metabolic stress. Recent studies implicated astrocytic lactate production in modulating tau phosphorylation through metabolic crosstalk mechanisms.
32
,
33
The observed association between Module 1 activity and tau PET burden might therefore underscore the potential contribution of neuron–astrocyte metabolic interactions to tau‐related neurodegeneration.
Module 3 activity was broadly positively associated with tau PET burden across wide swaths of the cortex and limbic regions (including temporal, frontal, cingulate, and hippocampal areas) and concurrently associated with worse cognition. Module 3 was characterized by pathways related to cell adhesion and nutrient sensing. Dysregulation of cell adhesion molecules could reflect loss of synaptic connectivity or altered glial–neuronal interactions in regions with heavy tau burden.
34
,
35
Nutrient‐sensing pathways, including those governed by insulin signaling and the mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR), have been increasingly implicated in AD progression and tau aggregation.
36
,
37
mTOR in particular is a master regulator of metabolic homeostasis and protein synthesis, and its hyperactivation has been linked to enhanced tau phosphorylation and reduced autophagic clearance of protein aggregates in AD.
38
,
39
The prominent association of Module 3 with tau PET suggested that aberrant intercellular signaling and metabolic regulatory mechanisms might facilitate tau accumulation and cognitive decline. The single‐nucleus validation reinforced the biological plausibility of Module 3 and illuminated its cellular contexts.
By contrast, weaker associations observed for Modules 2 and 4 with tau PET likely reflected distinct pathophysiological processes that operated independently of tau tangle accumulation or cognitive decline. Module 2, enriched for synaptic modulation and vesicular dynamics pathways, demonstrated no significant correlation with regional tau burden despite showing positive associations with preserved cognitive function, suggesting that Module 2 might represent compensatory or resilience mechanisms that support cognitive maintenance despite ongoing pathological changes. Module 4, enriched for lipid metabolism and immune activation pathways, exhibited selective regional associations. These processes might constitute responses that helped maintain neural function in the face of accumulating pathology, operating through tau‐independent mechanisms rather than processes directly driving cognitive decline.

4.2
Translational implications and clinical utility
The identified molecular modules presented several translational opportunities for biomarker development and therapeutic intervention. The robust associations of Modules 1 and 3 with both regional tau burden and cognitive outcomes suggested they could serve as molecular readouts of disease severity and progression. The continuous nature of GSVA‐derived module scores provided quantitative measures that could be incorporated into clinical trial designs as exploratory endpoints. These scores could potentially detect treatment responses earlier than conventional cognitive assessments. Additionally, the module framework could guide patient stratification in clinical trials, identifying individuals with specific molecular signatures who may be more likely to respond to targeted interventions. Thus, future longitudinal studies should evaluate whether baseline Module 1 and 3 activity scores can predict subsequent tau accumulation and cognitive decline, potentially offering earlier detection capabilities. From a therapeutic perspective, the modular framework of Modules 1 and 3 also provided mechanistic targets for intervention strategies.
4.3
Limitations and future directions
This study had several limitations. First, tau PET tracers within the ADNI dataset reflected real‐world imaging heterogeneity but inevitably introduced some inter‐tracer variability. While all images were harmonized to a common spatial resolution and normalized using a uniform reference region to minimize systematic bias, tracer‐specific analyses or harmonization onto a universal uptake scale could not be performed due to dataset constraints. Second, tau PET tracers exhibited some degree of off‐target binding particularly in regions such as the choroid plexus or basal ganglia, resulting in potential spillover effects into neighboring structures like the lateral ventricles. Elevated signal in periventricular spaces should be interpreted cautiously, as it might reflect partial volume effects or atrophy‐related tracer dilution. Novel tau tracers with improved specificity are expected to substantially mitigate these issues in future studies. Third, the cross‐sectional design might restrict the ability to determine causal relationships between module activity and tau pathology. Finally, blood transcriptomics might not fully capture brain‐specific molecular alterations due to blood–brain barrier constraints and heterogeneity across platforms, tissues, and cohorts. However, blood transcriptomic data were not used as a surrogate for brain pathophysiology but as a secondary validation layer to identify systemic signals. By requiring molecular consistency across CSF proteomic, blood transcriptomic, and snRNA‐seq platforms, we prioritized robust alterations manifesting across compartments.
Future multi‐modal longitudinal designs integrating omics, PET imaging, and clinical trajectories represent the most critical next step for advancing this research. Next, independent cohorts including populations with different genetic backgrounds and disease severities are essential for generalizability. Finally, experimental model validation is another direction for establishing mechanistic causality, which could determine whether changes in module activity directly influence tau pathology or represent downstream consequences.

CONCLUSION
In conclusion, this study integrated modular structures obtained from omics data with tau PET imaging in AD, providing novel insights into the BPs accompanying tau pathology, encompassing neuronal metabolism, cytoskeletal regulation, and intercellular signaling.
