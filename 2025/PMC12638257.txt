Main
Myelination is an ensheathment wherein oligodendrocytes wrap around the axons of neurons, forming an insulative layer that enhances the speed and efficiency of neural communication
1
. The oligodendrocytes consist of OPCs and mature oligodendrocytes (mOLs), the latter of which are differentiated from the former and are responsible for forming the myelin sheath
2
,
3
. Although most OPCs differentiate into mOLs for myelination of neurons during development, OPCs are also present and evenly distributed throughout the adult brain
4
,
5
. The adult OPCs play a particularly important role in the remyelination of neurons with damaged myelin sheath (demyelinated neurons) associated with viral infections (for example, cephalomeningitis)
6
, ischaemia (for example, stroke)
7
, mental stress (for example, depression and anxiety)
8
,
9
or ageing
10
,
11
. In response to demyelination, OPCs migrate to the site of injury, proliferate and differentiate into mOLs to re-envelop exposed axons
12
. Thus, remyelination is critical for maintaining normal neurological functions in the adult brain
12
.
It is well known that remyelination is one of the few regenerative processes in the central nervous system that depends on the ability of native OPCs to proliferate and differentiate into mOLs
13
,
14
. Deficiency in OPCs has been linked to various demyelination diseases, such as multiple sclerosis, a chronic autoimmune disease affecting the central nervous system, which is characterized by inflammation, demyelination, gliosis and neuronal loss
15
,
16
. Recent studies have suggested that dysregulated metabolic homeostasis, such as imbalances between anabolism and catabolism, may underlie the cause of multiple sclerosis
17
.
AMPK is a crucial regulator of metabolic homeostasis, responding to fluctuations in energy and nutrient availability
18
–
20
. Recent studies have shown that AMPK is a master sensor of glucose; it is activated via a specialized glucose-sensing pathway during fasting in an AMP-independent mechanism operating on the surface of the lysosome
21
–
23
. In this pathway, the decrease of glucose, and consequently the glycolytic intermediate fructose-1,6-bisphosphate (FBP), is directly sensed by aldolase associated with the lysosomal proton pump v-ATPase. This leads to inhibition of endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-localized transient receptor potential V (TRPV) channels, converting low cellular glucose/FBP to low local Ca
2+
signal at the ER–lysosome contact site
22
,
24
. TRPV in low Ca
2+
then interacts with v-ATPase, causing a reconfiguration and inhibition of the aldolase–v-ATPase complex
24
. As a result, AXIN utilizes v-ATPase and its associated Ragulator as docking sites to tether the upstream kinase liver kinase B1 (LKB1) to AMPK at the lysosome, where LKB1 activates AMPK by phosphorylating Thr172 of AMPKα
21
. Once activated, AMPK phosphorylates multiple targets, thereby inhibiting anabolic processes and stimulating catabolic activities to minimize ATP consumption and promote ATP production to maintain metabolic homeostasis
19
,
25
. However, heterogeneous populations of cells within a specific tissue, such as the brain, may have an unsynchronized need for anabolic and catabolic activities. The regulation of AMPK in these diverse cells may respond differently to changes in glucose levels. However, the underlying mechanisms underpinning such spatiotemporal regulation of AMPK remain obscure.
Here, we examined how different neuronal cells respond to changes in glucose levels. We isolated the main types of cerebral cortex cells and tested their ability to respond to glucose levels for AMPK activation. We found that AMPK is not activated in the specific cell type of OPCs, unlike the other neuronal cells tested. We further show that ALDOC in OPCs is acetylated at Lys14 (K14). OPCs expressing ALDOC-Lys14Arg (ALDOC-K14R), with lysine altered to arginine to mimic the deacetylated state of ALDOC, restore activation of AMPK in low glucose. Moreover, in the ALDOC-K14R-expressing OPCs, proliferation of OPCs was reduced, together with a decrease in the differentiation of OPCs into mOLs. In mice, we also found that the OPC-specific expression of ALDOC-K14R activated AMPK in demyelinated lesions induced by cuprizone, lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC) and experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE), where glucose and FBP levels are low. This activation led to defects in myelin regeneration. Conversely, OPC-specific knockout of
AMPKα1
and
AMPKα2
in the background of OPC-specific expression of ALDOC-K14R reversed the defects of remyelination, indicating that ALDOC-K14R exerts a negative impact on myelination through AMPK. Furthermore, pharmacologically induced activation of AMPK by the TRPV antagonist AMG-9810 (mimicking inhibition following glucose starvation) or the expression of the constitutively active PRKAG1 (AMPKγ1)-p.Asp317Ala (D317A) mutant (yielding constitutively active heterotrimeric AMPK)
26
inhibited proliferation of OPCs and their differentiation into mOLs. Thus, our study reveals that K14 acetylation of ALDOC in OPCs prevents AMPK activation in response to low glucose. This ensures the proliferation and differentiation of OPCs for normal myelination during development and for remyelination in neuronal disorders.
Results
AMPK in OPCs is not activated in low glucose
We isolated the four main types of brain cells—neurons, astrocytes, microglia and OPCs—from the cerebral cortex of rats of postnatal days 0–1 (P0–P1). OPCs were differentiated into mOLs, and AMPK activity in the different cell types was measured following incubation in media containing different concentrations of glucose. We found that in neurons, AMPK activity increased as early as 0.5 h when glucose decreased to 10 mM, as evidenced by the phosphorylation of Thr172 on AMPKα (p-AMPKα) and Ser79 on acetyl-CoA carboxylases (p-ACC; Fig.
1a
and Extended Data Fig.
1a,f
). AMPK in microglia was also activated within 3 h of low glucose, when glucose fell to 1.75 mM (Fig.
1c
and Extended Data Fig.
1b,g
), while in astrocytes, the activation of AMPK required a glucose-free environment (0 mM glucose; Fig.
1e
and Extended Data Fig.
1c,h
). Surprisingly, we observed that in OPCs, there was no change in p-AMPKα or p-ACC following incubation in the glucose-free medium for up to 24 h (Fig.
1g
and Extended Data Fig.
1d,i
). However, in mOLs differentiated from OPCs, AMPK was effectively activated as early as 3 h in medium containing 1.75 mM glucose (Fig.
1i
and Extended Data Fig.
1e,j
). In parallel with AMPK activity, we then determined the levels of adenine nucleotides and FBP following glucose removal, which modulate the AMP-dependent
27
and the lysosomal AMP-independent activation of AMPK
22
, respectively. FBP levels decreased within 3 h of glucose removal in all the five cell types (Fig.
1b,d,f,h,j
and Supplementary Fig.
1
). Of note, except for neurons, no cell types showed any increase in the ratios of AMP:ATP and ADP:ATP (Extended Data Fig.
1k–o
). In the absence of glucose in the media, the neurons displayed shrinkage and detachment from the culture dish after 2 h of starvation, indicative of an energy deficiency. The above results reveal that AMPK is refractory to activation in response to low glucose in OPCs. We hypothesized that this might be due to a blockade of the lysosomal AMPK pathway.

TRPV cation channels in OPCs remain active in low glucose
We next investigated why the lysosomal AMPK pathway is blocked in OPCs with reduced levels of FBP (Fig.
1g,h
). To determine changes occurring to the components of the lysosomal AMPK pathway, we found that v-ATPase was not suppressed in low glucose (Fig.
2a
), as demonstrated by the LysoSensor Green dye signal, which decreases with an increase in lysosomal pH
28
. We also found that metformin is able to inhibit v-ATPase and activate AMPK in OPCs (Fig.
2b–e
; note that metformin at 100 µM used in these cells did not elevate the ratio of AMP:ATP or ADP:ATP, as shown in Fig.
2c
). As metformin binds to PEN2, which then binds to the ATP6AP1 subunit of v-ATPase, inhibiting it to trigger the AMPK-activating route downstream of TRPV
29
,
30
, these results suggest that the step of TRPV inhibition might be blocked, such that v-ATPase cannot be inhibited in OPCs in low glucose. Indeed, we observed that the activity of TRPV2, which was expressed at the highest levels among the TRPV family members in OPCs (Extended Data Fig.
2a
), did not show reduced activity in low glucose (Fig.
2f
; evidenced by the fluorescence from the indicator TRPV2-GCaMP6s, which reflects the Ca
2+
-releasing activity of TRPV2)
24
,
31
. Conversely, pharmacological inhibition of TRPV by the antagonist AMG-9810 (refs.
24
,
32
) led to the inhibition of v-ATPase and activation of AMPK in the OPCs (Fig.
2g,i
; validated in Fig.
2h
). These results indicate that following removal of glucose, aldolase does not inhibit TRPV in OPCs, thus blocking low-glucose-induced AMPK activation (summarized in Extended Data Fig.
2d
). Interestingly, after differentiation from OPCs, mOLs showed a significant reduction of the activities of v-ATPase and TRPV2 in low glucose (Extended Data Fig.
2b,c
), and led to AMPK activation (Fig.
1i
and Extended Data Fig.
1e,j
).

Acetylation of ALDOC at K14 prevents low-glucose-induced AMPK activation in OPCs
We then examined why aldolase failed to inhibit TRPV in OPCs. Mass spectrometry was performed to look for post-translational modification(s) of ALDOC, which we found to be the most highly expressed isozyme of aldolase in OPCs (Extended Data Fig.
2a
), as reported previously for the brain
33
,
34
. As shown in Extended Data Fig.
3a
, a total of 87 modified sites were identified, including acetylated lysine residues (18 sites), methylated lysine (19 sites) and arginine (19 sites), and phosphorylated serine (12 sites), threonine (13 sites) and tyrosine (6 sites). We then individually mutated these sites, and found that ectopic expression of ALDOC-K14R with the K14 mutated to arginine (R) led to activation of AMPK in OPCs incubated in media lacking glucose (Fig.
3a
and Extended Data Fig.
3b
). Importantly, the ALDOC-K14R mutant has a similar
K
m
for FBP to that of the wild-type ALDOC (Fig.
3b,c
). We also found that the expression of ALDOC-K14R rendered OPCs responsive to low glucose, resulting in the inhibition of TRPV2 and v-ATPase in these cells following glucose removal (Fig.
3d–g
). We also generated an antibody that specifically reacts with acetylated ALDOC-K14 (AcK14-ALDOC; validated in Extended Data Fig.
3c
). Using this antibody, we observed high levels of AcK14-ALDOC when endogenous ALDOC was immunoprecipitated from OPCs (Fig.
3h
). In comparison, K14 acetylation of ALDOC was barely detected in MEFs, HEK293T cells and mOLs (Fig.
3h
), in all of which AMPK activation by low glucose occurs (Fig.
1i
and refs.
21
,
35
). Taken together, these results suggest that K14 acetylation of ALDOC contributes to the resistance to the induction by low glucose of AMPK activation in OPCs.

We also explored the mechanism underlying the high levels of ALDOC-K14 acetylation in OPCs. We found that, through quantitative protein mass spectrometry, the protein levels of SIRT2, a deacetylase of the sirtuin family, were low in OPCs compared to other sirtuins or deacetylases of the histone deacetylase (HDAC) family. SIRT2 was increased by eightfold in mOLs (Fig.
4a
and Extended Data Fig.
4a
). We also found that levels of NAD
+
, which serves as a co-substrate for sirtuins
36
, were lower in OPCs compared to mOLs (Extended Data Fig.
4b
). As for acetyltransferases that catalyse the acetylation, they were found to be similar at the protein level between OPCs and mOLs (Extended Data Fig.
4a
). Surprisingly, we also found lower levels of acetyl-CoA, a substrate for acetyltransferases
37
,
38
, in OPCs (Extended Data Fig.
4c
). Thus, the reduced SIRT2 levels might account for less deacetylation, and hence the increased levels of acetylated ALDOC-K14. Importantly, when SIRT2 was ectopically expressed in OPCs at levels comparable to those in mOLs (Extended Data Fig.
4d
), we observed a significant decrease in the acetylation of ALDOC-K14 (Fig.
4b
). In addition, ectopic expression of SIRT2 restored the ability of low glucose to lead to AMPK activation, TRPV2 inhibition and v-ATPase inhibition in the OPCs (Fig.
4c–g
). Expression of a dominant-negative form of SIRT2, SIRT2-His150Tyr (SIRT2-H150Y)
39
, did not lead to increased AMPK activity in low glucose (Fig.
4c–g
). Moreover, inhibiting SIRT2 with its specific inhibitor, SirReal2 (ref.
40
), significantly increased the acetylation of ALDOC-K14 in mOLs and prevented AMPK activation in response to low glucose (Fig.
4h
). Therefore, SIRT2 functions as a gatekeeper for both ALDOC-K14 acetylation and AMPK activation.

Interestingly, the control of ALDOC acetylation by SIRT2 appears to be somewhat restricted in OPCs and mOLs. In some cancer lines tested (such as A549, H1299, IMR-90, WI-38 and MCF 10A), low SIRT2 protein compared to mOLs still showed little AMPK activation in low glucose, with ALDOC-K14 acetylation almost undetectable (Extended Data Fig.
5a–c
). It is possible that other factors may be involved in AMPK activation with regards to deacetylation-related AMPK activation, as seen in the case of A549 cells that lack active LKB1 (refs.
41
,
42
). Similarly, depletion of SIRT2 or pharmacological inhibition of SIRT2 did not impair low-glucose-induced AMPK activation or ALDOC-K14 acetylation in mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) and HEK293T cells (Extended Data Fig.
5d–i
). In other neural cell types, such as neurons, microglia and astrocytes, AMPK responded to low glucose despite minimal SIRT2 expression (Extended Data Fig.
5b,i–l
). Thus, the regulation of AMPK activation by ALDOC-K14 acetylation/deacetylation depends on cell types.
Activation of AMPK in OPCs inhibits myelination

6a
; labelled with BrdU and Olig2, that is, the percentage of BrdU
+
/Olig2
+

)

Discussion
In this study, we have uncovered a mechanism underlying a cell-type-specific regulation of AMPK activation in response to low glucose, through acetylation of the sensor of glucose/FBP. We have demonstrated that the blockade of low-glucose-induced AMPK activation ensures the proliferation of and differentiation of OPCs into mOLs. Delineation of how acetylation on ALDOC blunts the response to low glucose shows that Ac-ALDOC-K14 can no longer inhibit the cation channel TRPV. This is in contrast to the other systemic cells such as muscular cells and hepatocytes, in which FBP-unoccupied aldolases inhibit TRPVs to cause inhibition of v-ATPase to trigger the activation of the lysosomal AMPK
21
,
24
. It is also important to note that low glucose OPCs do not show increased AMP levels, which excludes the canonical activation mode of AMPK as well
27
,
52
. On the other hand, forced activation of AMPK by expression of the nonacetylated-mimetic ALDOC-K14R mutant that can respond to low glucose to inhibit TRPV, by pharmacological inhibition of TRPV, or by expression of the constitutively active mutant AMPKγ1-D317A, causes severe defects in OPC proliferation and differentiation, both in vitro and in vivo. Consequently, the aberrant activation of AMPK disrupts myelination during development and impairs remyelination following the demyelinating damage (Extended Data Fig.
10f
).
It is important to note that AMPK activation through the glucose-sensing pathway is accompanied with the inhibition of mammalian/mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1)
21
,
53
–
55
. Hence, the blockade of the glucose-sensing pathway also avoids inhibition of mTORC1 to ensure the high degree of anabolism in cells such as OPCs, as OPCs, when differentiating into mOLs, need to synthesize large amounts of lipids, such as fatty acids, phospholipids and cholesterol, to generate the myelin sheath membrane
56
–
60
. The myelin sheath also contains large quantities of proteins, including MBP and MAG
43
,
44
. It has been estimated that once OPCs differentiate into mOLs, they produce three times their own membrane each day during the myelinating process
61
. Importantly, mTORC1 has been shown to be required for promoting the translation of MBP and the synthesis of cholesterol and phospholipids via SREBP1 (refs.
62
,
63
). Inhibition of mTORC1, S6 kinases (S6Ks) or SREBP1 has been shown to cause severe defects in myelination
64
–
66
. If the glucose-sensing pathway is unchecked to allow activation of AMPK in OPCs, the biosynthesis of lipids and proteins will be hampered. Indeed, we observed that OPCs treated with AMG-9810 (Extended Data Fig.
6c
), expression of AMPKγ1-D317A (Fig.
5c
) or with low glucose after expressing ALDOC-K14R (Extended Data Fig.
9e
), all activated AMPK, and showed strong inhibition of S6K phosphorylation, as well as its substrate S6, as a result of mTORC1 inhibition. In addition, direct inhibition of mTORC1 in OPCs through rapamycin significantly reduced OPC proliferation (measured by the percentage of BrdU
+
/Olig2
+
cells) and differentiation into mOLs (identified by the relative area of MBP
+
cells and the levels of MAG protein in these cells; Extended Data Fig.
6d–f
), consistent with those seen in mice where AMPK is activated in OPCs (Fig.
5d–h
). Therefore, blocking AMPK activation in OPCs may act to ensure high anabolic metabolism in the developing brain or in the lesion of adult brain. Furthermore, we examined ULK1 activation by determining its phosphorylation at the Ser555 site, which is catalysed by AMPK
67
,
68
, and noted an increase in ULK1 phosphorylation (Fig.
5c
and Extended Data Figs.
6c
and
9e
). Overall, we have demonstrated that controlled regulation of AMPK is critical for maintaining the functions of OPCs, which may also lend itself to explaining the mechanism underlying neurological diseases associated with myelination and remyelination.
Limitations
While we have demonstrated that blocking AMPK is critical for OPC function under low glucose/FBP conditions relevant to acute demyelination lesions, several limitations should be considered:
Gaps in clinical settings: Direct measurements of glucose and FBP levels in brain lesions from individuals with demyelinating disorders (for example, naturally occurring multiple sclerosis) are absent due to the inherent challenges of obtaining such human samples. This limits direct validation of the proposed metabolic microenvironment in human pathology.
Disease heterogeneity: The observed low-glucose triggered AMPK activation in demyelination models (cuprizone, LPC and EAE) may not fully represent all demyelination contexts, particularly chronic or age-associated forms where alternative mechanisms might dominate.
Cellular complexity: The potential contribution of other glial cells (for example, astrocytes and microglia) present in demyelinated lesions to AMPK-mediated inhibition of remyelination requires further investigation, as our study focused primarily on OPCs and mOLs.

RT–PCR
RT–PCR was performed as described previously
80
,
90

Random primer mix, enzyme mix and 5× RT buffer (all from the ReverTra Ace qPCR RT kit) were then added to the RNA solution, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 15 min, and then at 98 °C for 5 min on a thermocycler. Generation of antibody targeting AcK14-ALDOC
Rabbit polyclonal antibody against acetylated ALDOC-K14 (AcK14-ALDOC, 1:1,000 dilution for immunoblotting) was raised following the protocol as described previously
73
. The peptide (Ac-K)ELSDIALRIVAC of human ALDOC conjugated to the KLH immunogen (linked to the cysteine residue) was used. Rabbits were then biweekly immunized with 300 µg of the KLH-conjugated peptide antigen, which was pre-incubated with 1.5 mg manganese adjuvant (kindly provided by Z. Jiang from Peking University
91
) for 5 min and then mixed with PBS to a total volume of 1.5 ml, for four times, followed by collecting antiserum. The AcLys14 antibody was then purified from the antiserum using the peptide (Ac-K)ELSDIALRIVAC conjugated to the SulfoLink coupling resin/column supplied in the SulfoLink Immobilization Kit. To prepare the column, 1 mg of the peptide was first dissolved with 2 ml of coupling buffer, followed by the addition of 0.1 ml of TCEP (25 mM stock concentration), and then incubated at room temperature for 30 min. The mixture was then incubated with SulfoLink resin in a column, which was pre-calibrated by 2 ml of coupling buffer two times on a rotator at room temperature for 15 min, followed by incubating at room temperature for 30 min without rotating. The excess peptide was then removed, and the resin was washed with 2 ml of wash solution for three times, followed by 2 ml of coupling buffer two times. The nonspecific-binding sites on the resin were then blocked by incubating with 2 ml of cysteine solution (by dissolving 15.8 mg of
L
-cysteine-HCl in 2 ml of coupling buffer to make a concentration of 50 mM cysteine) on a rotator for 15 min at room temperature, followed by incubation for another 30 min without rotating at room temperature. After removing the cysteine solution, the resin was washed with 6 ml of binding/wash buffer, followed by incubating with 2 ml of antiserum mixed with 0.2 ml of binding/wash buffer for 2 h on a rotator. The resin was then washed with 1 ml of binding/wash buffer for five times, and the antibody was eluted with 2 ml of elution buffer. The eluent was then mixed with 100 µl of neutralization buffer. Antibody was validated for immunoblotting as shown in Extended Data Fig.
