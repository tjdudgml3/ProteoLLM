Introduction
Tumor necrosis factor (TNF) is a key proinflammatory cytokine that plays a crucial role in maintaining tissue homeostasis through the regulation of inflammation
1
–
3
. This inflammation can be beneficial during infections, as it provides cell-extrinsic signals that facilitate appropriate immune responses
4
,
5
. However, it can also be highly detrimental by inducing cell death and contributing to the pathogenesis of various inflammatory diseases
6
,
7
. The sensing of TNFα by its receptor TNFR1 normally does not result in cell death; instead, multiple cell death checkpoints or brakes determine whether TNFα induces transcriptional responses and promotes cell survival or triggers cell death
8
,
9
. Receptor interacting protein kinase 1 (RIPK1) is a key signaling node downstream of TNFR1
10
, which normally mediates TNFα-induced transcriptional response and promotes cell survival via its scaffolding function. However, in conditions when the protective checkpoints are blocked, RIPK1 induces apoptosis and/or necroptosis through its kinase activity
11
–
13
. In cells stimulated by TNFα, RIPK1, together with other proteins, is rapidly recruited to TNFR1 to form the TNFR1 signaling complex called complex I
14
,
15
. Upon its kinase activation in complex I, RIPK1 promotes the formation of a secondary cytosolic complex termed complex II encompassing RIPK1, FADD, and caspase-8, which facilitates the activation of caspase-8 and initiates apoptosis
16
. In conditions when caspase-8 is inhibited, the kinase activity of RIPK1 promotes the assembly of another complex known as necrosome comprising RIPK1, RIPK3, and MLKL, which promotes RIPK3 kinase-dependent activation of MLKL and induces necroptosis
17
,
18
.
Recent studies have identified several cell death checkpoints that protect against RIPK1-mediated cell death, which involve complex post-translational modifications such as ubiquitination, phosphorylation, and caspase-mediated cleavage
19
,
20
. For example, several kinases, such as MK2, TBK1, TAK1, and IKKα/β, prevent RIPK1 kinase activation and the subsequent cell death and inflammation by phosphorylating RIPK1
15
,
21
–
23
. Notably, complete depletion of each these genes in mice causes embryonic lethality
22
,
24
,
25
. These lethalities are associated with excessive cell death pathways, in many cases, demonstrated to be driven by TNFα and can be rescued by inactivation of RIPK1 kinase
10
. Consequently, clinical trials have progressed with RIPK1 kinase inhibitors for treating several human inflammatory and neurodegenerative conditions that are associated with TNF-induced cell death
12
,
26
. Thus, the identification of new cell death checkpoints in the TNF pathway may widen the spectrum of patients with pathologies that may benefit from anti- or pro-cell death therapies.
Metabolism is a fundamental biological process indispensable for all organisms, as it maintains cellular homeostasis and all cellular activities
27
–
29
. Metabolic enzymes play a crucial role in driving various metabolic activities. The diverse functions of metabolic enzymes facilitate the integration of various signals, enabling the precise transduction of external stimuli into cellular fate determinations
30
. In addition to their metabolic functions, metabolic enzymes have been linked to various cellular signaling pathways via their non-canonical functions, such as directly regulating the post-translational modifications of target proteins and indirectly regulating protein functions with metabolites
29
,
31
. Our previous research demonstrated that the key cytosolic enzyme of the uronic acid pathway, UDP-glucose 6-dehydrogenase (UGDH), can inhibit TNF-induced cell death through its metabolic function, which is dependent of its metabolic substrate UDP-glucose
4
. Nevertheless, whether metabolic enzymes regulate TNF-induced cell death through their non-metabolic functions remains unexplored.
Hexokinases (HKs), essential enzymes in glucose metabolism, catalyze the phosphorylation of imported glucose to yield glucose-6-phosphate (G-6-P)
32
,
33
. This product is subsequently metabolized through various pathways, including glycolysis, glycogenesis, pentose phosphate pathway, and hexosamine biosynthesis pathway
34
. These metabolic processes underscore the critical roles of HKs in ATP generation, anabolic biosynthesis, glucose storage, enrichment of the NADH pool, and protein glycosylation
35
. In mammals, there are five known isoforms of HKs including HK1, HK2, HK3, HK4, and the hexokinase domain-containing protein-1 (HKDC1)
36
. The hexokinase isoforms HK1-3, characterized by their high affinity for glucose, are composed of two structurally similar 50-kDa N- and C-terminal lobes
37
,
38
. HK2 is unique in that it possesses two functional catalytic lobes, while the N-terminal lobes of HK1 and HK3 lack the ability to phosphorylate glucose
32
. HK2 serves a critical role in the metabolic process of gluconeogenesis
32
,
34
. Notably, the complete deletion of HK2 in mice is embryonical lethal
39
,
40
, suggesting that HK2 may have a specific function in protecting against cell death.
In this study, we identified a glucose kinase-independent function of HK2 in regulating TNFα-induced RIPK1 kinase-dependent cell death. We demonstrate that HK2 inhibits RIPK1 kinase activity through constitutive phosphorylation at serine 32 (pS32). This phosphorylation serves as a physiological brake that directly inhibits RIPK1 kinase activity in response to TNF, and prevents TNFα-induced RIPK1 kinase-dependent cell death. We further show that HK2 is elevated in various pathological conditions including live ischemia reperfusion (I/R) injury and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), which is mediated by the transcriptional factor HMGA1. We found that HK2 deficiency aggravates liver I/R injury in a RIPK1 kinase-dependent manner, while overexpression of HK2 protects against liver I/R injury. Furthermore, HK2 deficiency or RIPK1 S32A mutation enhances TNFα-induced cell death in HCC cells and synergistically improves the efficacy of anti-PD1 therapy in an HCC model. Thus, we identified a cell death checkpoint that depends on the non-metabolic function of HK2 in TNFα-induced RIPK1-mediated cell death, revealing a potential therapeutic avenue for RIPK1-related diseases through HK2 non-metabolic functions.

HK2 inhibits RIPK1 activation via its glucose kinase-independent activity
HK2 is well-known for its glucose kinase activity in glucose metabolism by catalyzing the transition of glucose to G-6-P. We next examined whether HK2 regulates TNFα-induced cell death via its metabolic function. To this end, we first investigated whether the catalytic product G-6-P directly modulates TNFα-induced cell death. However, free G-6-P is difficult to pass through the cell membrane due to its negative charge, we employed the well-established liposome technology to encapsulate G-6-P (G-6-P-liposome), thereby facilitating its cellular uptake (Supplementary Fig.
3a
). We found that G-6-P administration had no effect on cell death and the levels of p-S166 RIPK1 in HK2-deficient MEFs stimulated with TNFα alone or TNFα/CHX (Supplementary Fig.
3b–e
). At the same time, we investigated whether the D-galactose, a hexose sugar metabolized in vivo to generate G-6-P, and pyruvate, a downstream metabolite of HK2, directly modulates TNFα-induced cell death. We found that D-galactose or sodium pyruvate administration had no effect on cell death and the levels of p-S166 RIPK1 in HK2-deficient MEFs stimulated with TNFα alone or TNFα/CHX (Fig.
2a–d
, Supplementary Fig.
3f–i
). To confirm that TNFα-induced cell death is independent of G-6-P, we next performed knockout experiments targeting HK1, an isoenzyme of HK2
51
. HK1 is ubiquitously expressed
32
, knockout of HK1 in MEFs led to significantly reduced levels of G-6-P (Supplementary Fig.
3j
). However, the cell death induced by TNFα alone or TNFα/CHX was not affected by HK1 deficiency (Supplementary Fig.
3k, l
). Furthermore, no RIPK1 activation was observed in HK1-deficient cells stimulated with TNFα alone or TNFα/CHX (Supplementary Fig.
3m, n
). Thus, the metabolic activity of HK2 is not involved in regulating TNFα-induced RIPK1 activation and cell death.

HK2 normally binds to mitochondria outer membrane to function as a metabolic enzyme
32
. We next examined whether this subcellular localization of HK2 is required for its regulation of TNFα-induced cell death. To this end, we generated reconstituted cell line expressing WT HK2 or a truncation mutant of HK2 lacking the N-terminal 15 amino acids (ΔN15), which is essential for its association with mitochondria outer membrane
51
. Notably, HK2-ΔN15-expressing cells showed similar sensitivity to cell death induced by TNFα alone or TNFα/CHX to that of HK2-WT-expressing cells (Supplementary Fig.
4b, c
). The levels of p-S166 RIPK1 and cleaved caspases were also not affected by the ΔN15 truncation in HK2 (Supplementary Fig.
4d, e
). Thus, mitochondria association is dispensable for HK2’s regulatory role in inhibiting TNFα-induced cell death.
HK2 uses its protein kinase function to inhibit RIPK1
Previous study demonstrated that HK2 can function as a protein kinase
52
. We hypothesized that HK2 uses its protein kinase function to prevent TNFα-induced RIPK1 activation and cell death. To verify the conjecture, we transfected the glucose kinase and protein kinase -dead mutant (S340A/S788A) as well as D209A/D657A mutant, which retains protein kinase function but is glucose kinase-deficient, into HK2-KO MEFs
52
,
53
. Concurrently, we used the HK2 metabolic activity assay to assess the S340A/S788A mutants and D209A/D657A mutant effect on HK2 metabolic function. Both mutants significantly inhibited HK2 metabolic activity (Supplementary Fig.
4a
). Notably, S340A/S788A mutant failed to inhibit cell death, RIPK1 and caspases activation induced by TNFα alone or TNFα/CHX in HK2-KO MEFs, but D209A/D657A mutant protect HK2-KO MEFs from TNFα alone or TNFα/CHX treatment (Supplementary Fig.
4b–e
). Furthermore, HK2-KO MEFs exhibited similar sensitivity to necroptosis induced by TNFα/CHX/zVAD to that of HK2-KO MEFs (Fig.
2e
), the activation of RIPK1, RIPK3, and MLKL HK2-KO MEFs was also no different from that of HK2-KO MEFs stimulated with TNFα/CHX/zVAD (Fig.
2f
). Thus, the protein kinase activity of HK2 is required for its inhibitory function on TNFα-induced RIPK1-mediated cell death.
Next, we characterized the interaction between RIPK1 and HK2. We found that ectopically expressed RIPK1 was co-immunoprecipitated with HK2 (Fig.
2g
). In addition, the endogenous interaction between RIPK1 and HK2 were also observed in unstimulated conditions (Fig.
2h
), suggesting that HK2 may constitutively phosphorylate RIPK1. Interestingly, this interaction was enhanced after deletion of the N-terminal 15 amino acids of HK2 (HK2-ΔN15) (Fig.
2g
), which is possibly attributed to the reduced binding of HK2 to mitochondria following the ΔN15 deletion. Consistent with the enhanced binding, HK2-deficient MEFs expressing HK2-ΔN15 mutant displayed reduced sensitivity to TNFα/CHX/zVAD-induced necroptosis and reduced RIPK1 activation compared to cells expressing HK2-WT (Fig.
2e, f
).
These findings collectively suggest that HK2 suppresses TNFα-induced RIPK1-mediated cell death through its protein kinase function.
HK2 directly phosphorylates RIPK1 at S32 through its C lobe
To examine whether HK2 phosphorylates RIPK1, we next performed mass spectrometry analysis of phosphorylated RIPK1 to determine the relevant residue(s) that can be phosphorylated by HK2. We found that the phosphorylations of S32, S304 and S566 of RIPK1 were significantly increased by HK2 (Fig.
2i, j
). In addition, S32, but not others, is evolutionarily conserved in humans, mice and rats (Fig.
2k
, Supplementary Fig.
4f
). To investigate RIPK1 S32 phosphorylation, we generated a phospho-specific antibody targeting p-S32 of RIPK1. In an in vitro kinase assay, the recombinant HK2 protein was observed to phosphorylate RIPK1, which was purified from HK2 deficient HEK293T cells, specifically at the S32. This phosphorylation event was abolished by the S32A mutation in RIPK1 (Fig.
2l
). Interestingly, RIPK1 kinase activation, as marked by its autophosphorylation of S166, was suppressed in the presence of HK2 protein, suggesting that HK2-mediated phosphorylation direct inhibits RIPK1 kinase activation (Fig.
2l
). Notably, the purified HK1 was unable to phosphorylate RIPK1 at serine 32 and the activation of RIPK1 kinase (Supplementary Fig.
4g
). In contrast, RIPK1 S32A mutation alone had no effect on the kinase activity of RIPK1. However, the inhibitory effect of HK2 on RIPK1 kinase was compromised when RIPK1 S32 was mutated (Fig.
2l
), suggesting that HK2 relies on its phosphorylation of RIPK1-S32 to directly inhibit RIPK1 kinase activation.
HK2 comprises two highly similar 50-kDa lobes: the N-terminal lobe (residues 17–450) and the C-terminal lobe (residues 465–917), each contributing uniquely to catalysis and regulation
52
. We then performed co-immunoprecipitation assay using HK2 truncated constructs with the deletion of either N lobe or C lobe (Supplementary Fig.
4h
). We found that the interaction of RIPK1 and HK2 depends on the C lobe of HK2 (Supplementary Fig.
4i
). Furthermore, the C lobe, but not the N lobe, was capable of phosphorylating RIPK1 at S32 in vitro (Fig.
2m
). We performed a comprehensive sequence alignment between the N-terminal (black, Supplementary Fig.
4j
) and C-terminal (red) domains of HK2, with sequence disparities highlighted in yellow. This analysis revealed divergence in their amino acid compositions. Specifically, the N-terminus is enriched in hydrophobic residues (e.g., Val, Ile, Leu, green in the Supplementary Fig.
4j
) that form a stabilizing hydrophobic core. In contrast, the C-terminus contains a higher proportion of polar residues (e.g., Asp, Glu, Lys, blue in the Supplementary Fig.
4j
). This sequence differentiation likely enables the N- and C-terminal domains to coordinate direct phosphate group transfer from ATP to target protein side chains. Thus, we speculate that these differences in sequence and biochemical properties do not affect N- and C-terminal domains binding with glucose or its metabolism activity, but may lead to significant differences in their binding to or phosphorylation of RIPK1. Since the C lobe of HK1, HK2 and HK3 have some similarities, we tested if they also can phosphorylate RIPK1 at S32. As detected by co-immunoprecipitation assay and in vitro kinase assay, the results demonstrated that only the C-terminus of HK2 interacts with RIPK1 and facilitating its phosphorylation at the S32 (Supplementary Fig.
4k, l
).
Next, we investigated S32 phosphorylation of endogenous RIPK1 in TNFα-stimulated cells. We observed that p-S32 RIPK1 was detected constitutively in WT cells but not in RIPK1-deficient cells, and TNFα stimulation had minor effect on its levels (Fig.
2n
). However, in HK2-deficient cells, the levels of p-S32 RIPK1 was significantly decreased regardless of TNFα treatment (Fig.
2o
), suggesting that RIPK1 is constitutively phosphorylated by HK2 and unaffected by TNFα stimulation. As a contrast, we did not observe any effect of RIPK1 kinase-dead mutation on p-S32 RIPK1 levels (Fig.
2p
). Thus, RIPK1 is constitutively phosphorylated by HK2.
To understand the mechanism by which S32 phosphorylation inhibits RIPK1 kinase activation, we next analyzed the protein structure of RIPK1. RIPK1 retains all amino acids necessary for ATP binding and hydrolysis typical of kinases, including the catalytic triad residues Lys45, Glu63, and Asp156, as well as key residues in the P-loop (residues 24–31) which is responsible for ATP binding and the catalytic loop (residues 136–143)
54
. Of note, S32 located within the kinase domain (residues 1–294) and adjacent to the P-loop (Fig.
2q
). Specifically, S32 site is positioned adjacent to the hydrogen bond formed between lysine 30 (K30) and glutamic acid 19 (E19) (Fig.
2r
). We hypothesized that pS32 may interfere with the hydrogen bond between K30 and E19 via electrostatic repulsion, thereby altering the P-loop structure and subsequently inhibiting RIPK1 kinase activity. Supporting this hypothesis, in vitro kinase assays demonstrated that the mutants RIPK1 K30A, RIPK1 E19A, and RIPK1 K30A/E19A significantly inhibited RIPK1 autophosphorylation (Fig.
2s
). To further elucidate the role of hydrogen bonding between K30 and E19 in the activation of RIPK1, we introduced mutations at S32, substituting it with amino acids which has a larger side chain, including phenylalanine, arginine, and tyrosine, to disrupt the hydrogen bonding between K30 and E19. The results indicated that these mutations significantly inhibited the activation of RIPK1, cleaved caspases and cell death induced by TNFα/5z7 (Supplementary Fig.
4m, n
). The in vitro kinase assay showed that despite the inability of these HK2 mutants to phosphorylate the RIPK1 S32 site, they are able to effectively inhibit RIPK1 activation (Supplementary Fig.
4o
). This suggests that disrupting the hydrogen bond between K30 and E19 indeed impairs the function of RIPK1.
S32 phosphorylation of RIPK1 prevents its activation and TNFα-induced cell death
To investigate the function of S32 phosphorylation in regulating RIPK1 activity and cell death, we reconstituted RIPK1-deficient MEFs with WT RIPK1, S32A mutant, and the phospho-mimetic S32E mutant. The results revealed that S32A mutation in RIPK1 significantly increased the sensitivity of MEFs to death induced by TNFα alone and resulted in RIPK1 kinase activation, which can be blocked by RIPK1 kinase inhibitor Nec-1s (Fig.
3a, b
). RIPK1 S32A mutation can activate RIPK1 and convert TNFα/CHX -induced RIA to RDA (Fig.
3c, d
). RIPK1 S32A mutation also sensitized cells to TNFα/5z7-induced RDA and TNFα/CHX/ZVAD-induced necroptosis. In contrast, RIPK1 S32E mutation largely reduced the levels of TNFα-induced cell death and RIPK1 activation compared to WT RIPK1 (Fig.
3e–h
). Notably, while HK2 knockdown sensitized cells to TNFα alone-induced death and increased the activation of RIPK1 in WT RIPK1-reconstituted cells, HK2 knockdown did not induce cell death and RIPK1 activation in RIPK1 S32E-reconstituted cells treated with TNFα alone (Fig.
3i, j
), suggesting that HK2 inhibits RIPK1 activation and cell death in a manner dependent on S32 phosphorylation. Taken together, these findings indicate that HK2 inhibits TNFα-induced RIPK1-mediated cell death by phosphorylating RIPK1 at S32.

To investigate the mechanism underlying the upregulation of HK2 mRNA levels, we utilized the bioinformatics tool JASPAR to analyze the promoter region of HK2 for potential transcription factors. The predicted transcription factors were then intersected with differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from livers subjected to I/R injury and those from sham operations, as well as between primary hepatocytes before and after in-vitro culture. Through this approach, we identified three potential transcription factors for HK2, including SOX5, HMGA1, and PPARA (Fig.
4g
). Among these transcription factors, only HMGA1 was increased in liver I/R injury, hepatocytes after culture and HCC compared with the corresponding controls (Fig.
4h
, Supplementary Fig.
5d–f
). We thus focused on HMGA1. Consistently, we found that HMGA1 protein levels were significantly elevated in all of these three conditions (Fig.
4i
), which suggests that HMGA1 might activate the expression of HK2. In line with this notion, knockdown of HMGA1 resulted in a concomitant reduction in both mRNA and protein levels of HK2 in primary hepatocytes and HCC cell line Huh7 (Fig.
4j–m
). To establish a direct association between HMGA1 and HK2 transcription, we introduced mutations into the HMGA1 binding sites within the HK2 promoter and subsequently conducted a luciferase reporter assay to evaluate HMGA1’s specific binding capability to the HK2 promoter. Our results demonstrated that the activity of the wild-type HK2 promoter was significantly upregulated upon HMGA1 overexpression, which was abolished following mutation of the HMGA1 binding sites (Fig.
4n
). Consistently, ChIP-qPCR assays revealed that HMGA1 binding to the HK2 promoter in primary hepatocytes was markedly enhanced after in-vitro culture compared to that of pre-culture (Fig.
4o
). Moreover, the knockdown of HMGA1 increased the sensitivity of primary hepatocytes to apoptosis induced by TNFα alone or TNFα/CHX, and facilitated the activation of RIPK1 in these cells, which was effectively reversed by Nec-1s (Supplementary Fig.
5g–j
). Importantly, overexpression of HK2 reversed the increased sensitivity of HMGA1-deficient cells to TNFα-induced apoptosis and RIPK1 activation (Fig.
4p–s
). Thus, these findings demonstrate the transcription factor HMGA1 promotes the amplification of HK2 in pathological conditions.

Discussion
The Complex Ⅰ serves as a pivotal checkpoint within the TNFα signaling pathway, determining the activation status of RIPK1 kinase. Central to this regulatory mechanism are various post-translational modifications of RIPK1. Key regulators of RIPK1, including A20, LUBAC, NEMO, TAK1, and IKKα/β, are swiftly recruited to the TNF-RSC, where they directly influence RIPK1 activation by modulating its ubiquitination and phosphorylation states. Nonetheless, it remains unclear whether metabolic kinases can phosphorylate RIPK1 through their non-metabolic activity. In this study, we identified HK2 as a critical regulatory checkpoint in TNFα-induced RDA. Our findings demonstrate that HK2 deficiency enhances RDA and inflammation. Notably, HK2 functions as a protein kinase, phosphorylating RIPK1 at S32. Phosphorylation of RIPK1 S32 disrupts the hydrogen bond between K30 and E19, thereby altering the P-loop structure and subsequently inhibiting RIPK1 kinase activity. Our findings indicate that this non-canonical function of HK2 mitigates hepatocyte apoptosis and inflammation by preventing RIPK1 activation (Fig.
7f
). In a liver I/R model, the hepatocyte-specific deletion of HK2 exacerbates liver damage by promoting hepatocyte apoptosis, a condition that is mitigated by the
Ripk1
D138N
mutation. Concurrently, our findings indicate that T cells within tumor tissues predominantly eradicate tumor cells through the secretion of TNFα. Inhibition of HK2 in HCC cells not only increases the sensitivity of these cells to TNFα-induced RDA and enhances the therapeutic efficacy of immunotherapy, but also reconfigures the immunosuppressive microenvironment of tumors. Reintroduced S32A mutant of RIPK1 into
Ripk1
KO MEFs activated RIPK1 and promoted TNFα-induced apoptosis. In an HCC model, defective RIPK1 S32 phosphorylation in HCC cells results in enhanced efficacy of immunotherapy for HCC, and inhibits tumor growth.
RIPK1 serves as the principal downstream effector in TNFα signaling. Within this signaling cascade, two distinct apoptotic pathways are recognized: a RIPK1 kinase-independent apoptotic pathway (RIA), which is inhibited by nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) and cellular FLICE-like inhibitory protein (cFLIP), and a RIPK1 kinase-dependent apoptotic pathway (RDA), which is suppressed by mechanisms independent of NF-κB. RIPK1 inhibition protects retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) in preclinical glaucoma models. Genetic inactivation of RIPK1 improves RGC survival and preserves retinal function in the preclinical glaucoma models of optic nerve crush (ONC) and IRI, which indicate that RIPK1 plays an important role in modulating neuroinflammation and that inhibiting RIPK1 activity may provide a neuroprotective therapy for glaucoma
66
. Meanwhile, the kinase activity of RIPK1 is critically implicated in the pathogenesis of liver diseases, such as liver I/R and HCC, primarily through its regulation of caspase-dependent hepatocyte apoptosis induced by TNFα. The hepatocyte-specific deletion of the endogenous inhibitor of RIPK1 kinase, such as TAK1- which performs inhibitory phosphorylation on RIPK1- results in spontaneous hepatocyte apoptosis, steatohepatitis, fibrosis, and HCC
67
,
68
. Our study demonstrated that inactivation of RIPK1 kinase through the introduction of the
Ripk1
D138N
mutation in hepatocellular HK2-deficient mice significantly ameliorated liver pathology by preventing hepatocyte apoptosis and subsequently reducing the development of HCC. In this study, we identify HK2 as an endogenous inhibitor of RIPK1 in hepatocytes. The loss of HK2 converts RIA into RDA due to unrestrained RIPK1 activity in TNFα stimulated hepatocytes. In contrast to hepatocellular HK2-deficient mice, mice without hepatocyte-specific loss of HK2 exhibit a normal phenotype and do not present with liver pathology under unstressed conditions.
HK2 is the initial key enzyme in glycolysis, responsible for converting glucose into the metabolic intermediate G-6-P. Recent studies indicate that the aberrant upregulation of HK2 is pivotal in the dysregulation of glycolysis, particularly in instances of unregulated glycolytic activity. The role of HK2 has been extensively characterized in various pathological processes, including liver I/R, HCC, and diabetes, where it is often overexpressed and associated with disease progression
69
. HK2-deficient photoreceptors are more susceptible to acute nutrient deprivation in the experimental retinal detachment model, suggest that HK2 expression is critical for preserving photoreceptors during acute nutrient stress and aging. However, retinal glucose metabolism is largely unchanged after HK2 deletion, suggesting that the non-enzymatic role of HK2 is important for maintaining photoreceptor health
70
. These findings indicate that HK2 is crucial in modulating cell death during disease progression, potentially through mechanisms independent of its metabolic functions. Given that RIPK1 is also integral to the progression of these diseases, we propose the hypothesis that HK2 may regulate RIPK1 via its non-metabolic functions, thereby influencing the progression of cell death. While our current findings strongly support HMGA1 as a key transcriptional regulator of HK2, we acknowledge that the transcriptomic data contain numerous DEGs whose potential contributions cannot be completely excluded. However, other candidates lack comparable predictive evidence and experimental validation, making them less likely to exert predominant effects independently. Therefore, we conclude that HMGA1-mediated regulation of HK2 represents the most plausible mechanism based on current evidence, while remaining open to the possibility that additional factors may contribute to this regulatory network. Future studies will further investigate these potential interactions. In this study, we demonstrate a novel function of HK2 as a suppressor of TNFα-induced hepatocyte apoptosis. Additionally, we show that HK2 inhibits TNFα-induced hepatocyte apoptosis through its protein kinase activity, specifically by directly phosphorylating RIPK1 at S32, thereby inhibiting RIPK1 activation. Thus, our study builds a direct link between HK2 and RIPK1 kinase regulation.
Methods
Mouse ischemia-reperfusion (I/R) model
The mouse 70% warm ischemia-reperfusion (I/R) model was employed using male mice at 24 weeks of age. Prior to surgery, mice were anesthetized with 50 mg/kg sodium pentobarbital to ensure the absence of toe-clamping pain reflex and corneal reflex. The abdominal skin was then sterilized using a 75% ethanol and betadine solution, repeated three times. The surgical procedure involved a midline incision of the abdomen, layer by layer, during which the left and middle branches of the portal vein were identified, isolated, and ligated to induce partial ischemia of the liver for 1 hour. Reperfusion of the liver was visually confirmed upon releasing the ligations of the left and middle branches of the portal vein. Six hours after reperfusion, mice were re-anesthetized for blood collection from the orbital vein. Following blood sampling, the liver was perfused with saline through the portal vein, and the left and middle lobes were resected for subsequent analysis.

Pre-reperfusion liver samples were harvested from the left hepatic lobe after cold storage before transplantation, whereas post-reperfusion samples were collected from the same lobe following approximately 2 hours of portal reperfusion before abdominal closure. Samples were either formalin-fixed for pathological analysis or frozen in liquid nitrogen for immunoblotting analysis. All donor livers were obtained via the China Organ Transplant Response System during the period from August 2022 to October 2023. Analysis of cytotoxicity and viability
To assess the cytotoxicity under different treatment conditions, primary hepatocytes were treated with SytoxGreen (Invitrogen) at a final concentration of 5 mM in the culture medium following various treatments. Statistics
The data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). All experiments were repeated at least three times. Statistical analysis was performed using the software GraphPad Prism 8.0 (v8.4.1). The Shapiro-Wilk test is used to determine if a sample follows a normal distribution or not. For normally distributed data, unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests were used for comparing two groups, and one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s tests was employed for multiple group comparisons with a specified control. For data involving more than one variable, two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni’s post hoc tests was applied for multiple group comparisons. Nonparametric analyses were conducted for non-normally distributed data, using the Mann-Whitney test for two-group comparisons or the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunnett’s test for multiple comparisons. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, denoted as (*), P < 0.01 as (**), P < 0.001 as (***), and non-significant results as (n.s.).
