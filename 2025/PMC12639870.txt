Introduction
Brucellosis caused by the genus
Brucella
is a worldwide spread bacterial zoonosis affecting animal and human health, characterized by abortion and infertility in animals and chronic debilitating disease in humans [
1
,
2
].
Brucella
spp. are Gram-negative bacteria classified into 12 species based on their preferred hosts. Among them,
B. melitensis
(goats and sheep),
B. abortus
(cattle), and
B. suis
(swine) are the most pathogenic to humans [
3
]. Brucellosis remains the most prevalent bacterial zoonosis worldwide, with an estimated 2.1 million new human cases annually [
4
], underscoring its public health significance. Currently, live attenuated vaccines such as Rev.1, S19, and RB51 are used to control brucellosis in animals [
5
]. However, these vaccines have several limitations, including residual virulence that can lead to human infection and reproductive complications in pregnant animals. Notably, no vaccine is approved for human use against brucellosis [
6
].
Outer membrane vesicles (OMVs), naturally secreted by Gram-negative bacteria, have emerged as promising vaccine candidates due to their immunogenicity and safety [
7
–
12
]. These non-replicating, spherical nanoparticles mimic the outer membrane of the parental bacterium, containing components such as lipopolysaccharides (LPS), outer membrane proteins (OMPs), and peptidoglycan (PG) [
7
,
8
,
13
]. OMV-based vaccines have already shown clinical success, as exemplified by
Neisseria meningitidis
OMVs, which provide effective protection against serogroup B meningococcal infections in humans [
8
]. Similarly, OMVs from various
Brucella
species, including
B. abortus
,
B. melitensis
, and
B. suis
, have been found to contain immunostimulatory components and can modulate host immune responses [
13
–
15
]. Despite their immunostimulatory potential, OMVs derived from wild-type
B. abortus
elicit only modest immune responses. Nonetheless, animal studies demonstrate that OMVs from wild-type strains can confer protective immunity comparable to that of the RB51 vaccine [
16
].
As facultative intracellular pathogens,
Brucella
spp. evade host immunity through several strategies [
17
] : (a) noncanonical LPS, a poor activator of Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), is one of the most critical features of
Brucella
spp [
18
,
19
]; (b) the flagella of
B. abortus
is a poor inducer of TLR5-mediated inflammatory responses due to its amino acid sequence that is not recognized by TLR5 [
20
,
21
]; (c) a
Brucella
type IV secretion system (T4SS) effector called Btp1 down-modulated maturation of infected dendritic cells (DCs) by interfering with signaling via TLR2 [
22
] and also inhibited signaling by TLR4 and TLR6 [
23
].
In our previous study, we designed a recombinant enteric
Yersinia pseudotuberculosis
PB1 + strain to modify its LPS and tailor an Asd + plasmid pSMV13 for high synthesis of the
Yersinia pestis
LcrV antigen, which generated the OMVs with a significant reduction in the TLR4 stimulation and effective protection for an experimental mice model against pneumonic plague infection [
9
]. Further, a new
Y. pseudotuberculosis
mutant strain carrying the
lpxE
insertion and
pmrF-J
deletion is constructed and shown to exclusively produce monophosphoryl lipid A having adjuvant properties, which also generated the OMVs with lower activation of TLR4 stimulation, substantially reduced reactogenicity and complete protection to mice against a high-dose of respiratory
Y. pestis
challenge [
24
]. Additionally,
Pseudomonas aeruginosa
PA103 were eliminated multiple virulence factors to generate a safe and effective OMVs vaccine [
11
].
In this study, we performed an in-depth characterization of OMVs derived from wild-type
B. abortus
. We found that these OMVs enhance both the invasion and intracellular survival of
B. abortus
in macrophages ex vivo and promote its pathogenicity in mice by modulating macrophage polarization in vivo. However, OMVs derived from the wild-type strain elicited only a weak innate immune response and showed limited immunogenicity. To address this, we aimed to enhance the antigenicity and immunogenicity of
B. abortus
OMVs. To this end, a genetically modified
B. abortus
strain was engineered (
Brucella abortus
∆
eipB
∆
per
∆
wadC
) to remodel its noncanonical LPS (Scheme
1
). Immunization of mice with recombinant OMVs was expected to induce a more robust
Brucella
-specific humoral response and T-cell response compared to OMVs derived from the wild-type
B. abortus
strain. These enhanced immune responses are anticipated to correlate with improved bacterial clearance, reduced tissue damage, and increased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, relative to standard immunization with wild-type OMVs.

OMV isolation and analysis
OMVs were isolated from
B. abortus
2308 and its derivatives as described previously [
9
–
11
]. A brief procedure and analysis are included in Supplementary file 1.
Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)
For transmission electron microscopy of
B. abortus
2308 and its derivatives or its purified OMVs, bacteria were cultured or OMVs were isolated as above. The detailed procedures for TEM observation were conducted as described in Supplementary file 1.
OMVs proteomics and lipidomics analysis
OMVs from
B. abortus
2308 or Bru-M3 were isolated as above, and the proteins and lipids in OMVs were analyzed by LC-MS/MS analysis. The detailed procedures for LC-MS/MS analysis were conducted as described in Supplementary file 1.
Determination of co-incubation with OMVs on macrophage cytotoxicity ex vivo
RAW 264.7 macrophages were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 10
4
cells per well and cultured for 12 h. Then cells were co-incubated with PBS (control) or 2 µg/ml OMVs from
B. abortus
2308 or its derivatives, and the cell supernatants were collected at 4, 10 and 24 h post co-incubation. Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) in the cell supernatants were analyzed using a CytoTox 96 nonradioactive cytotoxicity assay (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Supplementary File 1).
Effect of co-incubation with OMVs on macrophage molecular adaptations by proteomics analysis
RAW 264.7 macrophages were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 10
6
cells per well and cultured for 12 h. Then cells were co-incubated with PBS (control) or 2 µg/ml OMVs from
B. abortus
2308. After 10 h post co-incubation, the cells were collected to analyze the proteins using LC-MS/MS analysis. The detailed procedures for LC-MS/MS analysis were conducted as described in Supplementary file 1.
Determination of co-incubation with OMVs on innate immunity activation
To determine stimulatory activity of OMVs from
B. abortus
2308 or its derivatives via TLR4, RAW-Dual™ cells (InvivoGen, CA, USA) were seeded at a density of 5 × 10
4
cells per well in 96-well tissue culture plates and were co-incubated with PBS (control) or 2 µg/ml OMVs from
B. abortus
2308 or its derivatives. After 4, 10 and 24 h post co-incubation, relative NF-κB activity was determined by measuring the embryonic alkaline phosphatase (SEAP) activity that accumulated the culture media according to the manufacturer’s instructions. SEAP activity was evaluated using a spectrophotometer at 620–655 nm.
Determination of co-infection with OMVs on
B. abortus
pathogenicity in vivo
To explore the role of OMVs from
B. abortus
2308 in
B. abortus
pathogenicity in vivo, six-week-old female BALB/c mice (
n
= 6 per group) were i.p. challenged with 2 × 10⁵ CFU of
B. abortus
2308 mixed with PBS (Bru&PBS, as control) or 2 × 10⁵ CFU of
B. abortus
2308 mixed with OMVs from 2 to 10 µg
B. abortus
2308 (Bru&OMVs). For determination of the bacterial burden after Bru&PBS or Bru&OMVs infection, animals were euthanized, and the serum, spleens and livers were collected at 4 weeks post infection. The weight of spleens was measured, and spleens and livers were respectively homogenized in ice cold PBS (pH 7.4) using a high-throughput tissue grinder at power 70 W for 4 min. Serial dilutions of each organ homogenate were plated on tryptic soy agar (TSA), and each count was confirmed with duplicate plates with three dilutions to determine the bacterial titer per gram of tissue. Histopathology of spleens and livers were analyzed by H&E staining (see Supplementary file 1).
Polarization of murine peritoneal macrophages
Six-week-old female BALB/c mice (
n
= 3 per group) were i.p. challenged with PBS, OMVs, Bru&PBS, or Bru&OMVs. After day 4 post-challenge, mice were euthanized, and murine peritoneal macrophages were collected and polarization status of macrophages was determined by the flow cytometric analysis (see Supplementary File 1).
Evaluation of protective immunity of OMVs
To evaluate the protective immunity of OMVs from
B. abortus
2308 or its derivatives, six-week-old female BALB/c mice (
n
= 6 per group) were s.c or i.n. immunized with OMVs from
B. abortus
2308 or its derivatives. Vaccination with PBS was used as an experimental control. Booster vaccination was then performed 3 weeks after the initial vaccination. Blood was collected via the submandibular veins to harvest serum for antibody analysis at 2 or 5 weeks after the initial vaccination. The weight of all immunized animals was monitored over a 6-weeks period. At 6 weeks after the initial vaccination, mice were challenged i.p. with
B. abortus
2308 in 100 µL of PBS. For determination of the bacterial burden after
B. abortus
2308 infection, animals were euthanized, and the serum, spleens and livers were collected at 4 weeks post infection. The weight of spleens was measured, and spleens and livers were respectively homogenized in ice cold PBS (pH 7.4) using a high-throughput tissue grinder at power 70 W for 4 min. Serial dilutions of each organ homogenate were plated on TSA, and each count was confirmed with duplicate plates with three dilutions to determine the bacterial titer per gram of tissue. Histopathology of spleens and livers were analyzed by H&E staining (see Supplementary file 1).
Antibody and cytokine analysis
Each mouse was bled by submandibular vein puncture. The collected sera were stored at − 20 °C until further use. An enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was used to assay the titers of antibodies (IgG, IgG1, IgG2a, IgM) against
B. abortus
2308 in the serum (see Supplementary File 1). Cytokines IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-9, IL-13, IL-17 A, IL-18, IL-22, IL-23, and IL-27 levels in the serum were analyzed as described in Supplementary file 1.
Measurement of T cell responses in the spleen
The activation and cytokine production of T cells in the spleen were evaluated. Six-week-old female BALB/c mice (
n
= 5 per group) were immunized and challenged as above. Then spleens were obtained aseptically from euthanized mice after 4 weeks post challenge and were dissociated with 70-mm strainers to obtain single cells. The-individual cell populations derived from the lysis of red blood cells (RBC) were seeded in 12-well cell culture plates. The T-cell populations and their corresponding cytokines was determined by the flow cytometric analysis (see Supplementary file 1).

Second-level GO classification revealed that the proteins were primarily involved in molecular functions such as structural components of the ribosome and ATP or DNA binding, and biological processes including translation and proteolysis (Fig. S1A; Supplementary File 1). KEGG pathway analysis indicated that OMV

To further characterize OMV
Bru

The lipid profile was dominated by polar lipids and glycerophospholipids, with phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) and phosphatidylcholine (PC) as the most abundant species, along with notable levels of phosphatidylglycerol (PG), cardiolipin (CL), and phosphatidylserine (PS) (Fig.

To evaluate potential cytotoxicity of OMV

OMVs enhance
B. abortus
pathogenicity in mice via modulation of macrophage polarization
OMVs from
B. abortus
have been shown to facilitate bacterial survival by modulating host immune responses, promoting chronic infection in both phagocytic and non-phagocytic cells [
25
]. Definitely, OMV
Bru
pre-treatment significantly enhanced bacterial uptake and the intracellular survival of
B. abortus
ex vivo. To investigate the contribution of OMV
Bru
to
B. abortus
pathogenicity in vivo, a murine infection model was designed (Fig.
2
A). The intraperitoneal route is frequently used to establish a persistent
Brucella
infection in the mouse model [
26
]. Hence, mice were intraperitoneally injected with
B. abortus
mixed with either 2 µg/mL OMV
Bru
(Bru&OMV
Bru
) or PBS (Bru&PBS). At 4 weeks p.i., mice infected with Bru&OMV
Bru
exhibited significantly increased spleen weights, indicating enhanced splenomegaly (Fig.
2
B). Bacterial burdens in both spleens and livers were significantly higher in the Bru&OMV
Bru
group compared to Bru&PBS (Figs.
2
C-D). A similar enhancement in pathogenicity was observed with a higher OMV
Bru
dose (10 µg/mL) (Figs. S3A-D; Supplementary File 1), indicating the dose-independent effect of OMV
Bru
to virulence.
Histopathological analysis revealed that both Bru&PBS and Bru&OMV
Bru
groups exhibited disorganized spleen architecture with neutrophil infiltration (yellow arrows), accumulation of epithelioid macrophages, and reduced white pulp lymphocytes (Fig.
2
E). Notably, lymphocyte hyperplasia in the marginal zone (green arrows) was more pronounced in the Bru&OMV
Bru
group (Fig.
2
E). In the liver, parenchymal microgranulomas (black arrows) were observed in both infected groups, with an apprently higher frequency in the Bru&OMV
Bru
group (Fig.
2
F).
Serum cytokine levels further supported these findings. Compared to sham controls, Bru&PBS infection significantly elevated IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-4, and IL-6, but not IL-13 (Figs.
2
G-K). There was no significant differences in the levels of IL-13. In contrast, all five cytokines were significantly upregulated in Bru&OMV
Bru
-infected mice, with particularly strong increases in IFN-γ and TNF-α. Similar cytokine profiles were observed with the 10 µg/mL OMV
Bru
dose (Figs. S3E-I; Supplementary File 1), suggesting a consistent role for OMV
Bru
in exacerbating host inflammation and possibly macrophage polarization.
Macrophages differentiate into either pro-inflammatory M1-like (CD86
+
) or anti-inflammatory M2-like (CD206
+
) phenotypes depending on environmental cues [
27
]. To determine whether OMV
Bru
affects macrophage polarization in vivo, peritoneal macrophages from mice infected with PBS, Bru&PBS, OMV
Bru
, or Bru&OMV
Bru
at day 4 p.i. via flow cytometry were analyzed. Compared to PBS controls, Bru&PBS infection increased CD206
+
M2-like macrophages with no significant change in CD86
+
cells (Figs.
2
L-M). In contrast, Bru&OMV
Bru
infection resulted in a significant decrease in CD206
+
cells and a corresponding increase in CD86
+
cells, indicating a shift toward a pro-inflammatory M1-like phenotype. OMV
Bru
alone did not alter the proportions of CD86
+
or CD206
+
cells compared to PBS controls (Figs.
2
J-K).
These findings demonstrate that OMVs facilitates
B. abortus
pathogenicity by promoting pro-inflammatory macrophage polarization (M1-like macrophages).

The lipopolysaccharide (LPS) of
Brucella
consists of three domains: the O antigen (the immunodominant region), the core oligosaccharide, and lipid A. The
per
gene encodes perosamine synthetase, which is essential for O-antigen biosynthesis; loss of this antigen attenuates
Brucella
virulence [
33
]. The
wadC
gene encodes a core glycosyltransferase critical for LPS core structure; its deletion also results in
Brucella
attenuation and induces proinflammatory responses [
34
]. To remodel the LPS structure, sequential deletions of
per
and
wadC
were performed in the Bru-M1 background, generating Bru-M2 and Bru-M3 strains, respectively (Table
1
).
The immunostimulatory activities of OMVs from Bru, Bru-M1, Bru-M2, and Bru-M3 were evaluated using RAW-Dual™ cells by detecting secreted embryonic alkaline phosphatase (SEAP) activity. OMVs from all strains (OMV
Bru
, OMV
Bru−M1
, OMV
Bru−M2
, OMV
Bru−M3
) significantly activated both NF-κB and IRF signaling pathways, indicating strong stimulation of innate immune responses (Fig.
3
B). Among them, OMV
Bru−M3
exhibited the highest stimulatory activity, suggesting that OMVs derived from Bru-M3 are more effective in activating innate immunity against
Brucella
infection.
To assess cytotoxicity, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release from OMV-treated cells was measured. OMV
Bru−M1
and OMV
Bru−M2
induced LDH release levels comparable to those induced by OMV
Bru
at 4, 10, and 24 h post-incubation (Fig.
3
C). In contrast, OMV
Bru−M3
induced significantly lower LDH release at 24 h, indicating that
wadC
deletion reduced OMVs cytotoxicity. Additionally, OMV
Bru−M3
also showed the high cellular viability of macrophages (Fig. S1I; Supplementary File 1).
Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was used to analyze the morphology of the bacterial strains and their OMVs. TEM images (Fig.
3

3

Bru−M1
, OMV
Bru−M2

Bru−M3
had significantly smaller average diameters (58.51 nm, 53.25 nm, and 58.60 nm, respectively) compared to OMV
Bru
(87.10 nm), indicating that the genetic modifications influenced OMVs size (Fig.
3

Bru−M3
was conducted. A total of 1,050 unique proteins were identified in OMV

3

Discussion
Outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) are biologically active nanoparticles naturally secreted by Gram-negative bacteria, including
Brucella
spp., and have gained attention as promising vaccine platforms against bacterial pathogens. The first identification of
Brucella
-derived vesicles by Gamazo and Moriyon in 1987 revealed the release of membranous structures enriched in lipopolysaccharides (LPS), proteins, and phospholipids [
14
]. Subsequent studies confirmed the presence of OMVs in several
Brucella
species [
13
,
16
,
37
]– [
38
]. However, native
Brucella
OMVs exhibit limited immunogenicity (Figs.
4
C and
5
C, Fig. S9C) and low production yields (Fig.
3
A), and their roles in
Brucella
pathogenesis remain incompletely understood. This study aimed to characterize
Brucella
OMVs and genetically remodel the bacterium to enhance OMVs yield and immunogenicity.
Genetic modification of bacterial genome has emerged as a successful strategy to engineer OMVs with improved yield and altered functionality. Disruption of membrane integrity genes, such as
tolA
,
tolB
,
tolR
, and
pal
, has increased OMVs yield in
E. coli
,
Shigella
,
Y. pseudotuberculosis
, and
Helicobacter pylori
[
9
,
28
–
31
]. However, deletion of these genes in
B. abortus
was unsuccessful, likely due to their essential role in
Brucella
viability. Interestingly, we found that disruption of
eipB
, a periplasmic protein critical for envelope integrity and virulence [
32
], significantly increased OMVs production, approximately two-fold compared to wild-type
B. abortus
(Fig.
3
A). This supports the idea that weakening envelope stability promotes OMVs release, possibly by loosening the interaction between the inner and outer membranes.
The poor immunogenicity of wild-type
Brucella
OMVs is likely attributable to their unique LPS, which is characterized by low endotoxicity and minimal activation of host immune responses [
18
]– [
19
]. Since LPS is a major structural and immunological component of OMVs, we hypothesized that remodeling the noncanonical
Brucella
LPS could enhance OMVs immunogenicity. Disruption of genes involved in O-antigen and core oligosaccharide biosynthesis resulted in OMVs with greater innate immune stimulatory capacity (Fig.
3
B) and improved humoral (Fig.
6
) and Th1 cellular responses (Fig.
7
), as evidenced by elevated cytokine production (Figs.
4
G and
5
G, Fig. S9G). These findings confirm that remodeling LPS enhances OMVs immunogenicity and potentiates TLR signaling, facilitating robust adaptive immune priming against brucellosis. Lipid A, a component of LPS, also contributes to
Brucella
’s evasion of innate immunity due to its atypical long-chain fatty acids [
19
]. However, the specific genes responsible for this lipid A structure in
B. abortus
remain unidentified and warrant further investigation. Apart from LPS, numerous
Brucella
outer membrane proteins (Omp) and periplasmic proteins have been evaluated as protective antigens that efficiently induce anti-
Brucella
immune responses in mouse models, including Omp2b, Omp16, Omp19, Omp31, P39, Cu-Zn superoxide dismutase, lumazine synthase, the ribosomal protein L7/L12, and nucleoside diphosphate [
39
]. Similarly, some specific components within OMVs from the remodeled strain (OMV
Bru−M3
) were identified in this study, including OmpA, OmpW, Omp2a, Omp2b, Omp10, Omp19, Omp28, Omp31, ribosomal proteins, Cu-Zn superoxide dismutase, nucleoside diphosphate (Supplementary File 2). We speculated that these specific components also contribute to enhance immune stimulation. Furthermore, in contrast to OMV
Bru
, a detailed comparative analysis indicated that OMV
Bru−M3
enclosed in Omp2a, nucleoside diphosphate, and more ribosomal proteins, specifically including L11, uS12, uL14, bS21, uL22, and bL32 (Supplementary File 2). These compositional differences in OMVs may be connected with the improved performance of OMV
Bru−M3
, and the potential auxiliary roles of these proteins in protection merit further investigation.
Bacterial OMVs are derived from the cell envelope of Gram-negative bacteria which consists of two membranes, the outer membrane, the cytoplasmic membrane, with a network of peptidoglycan and the periplasmic space in between [
40
]. Our results revealed that both wild-type and remodeled OMVs contain proteins derived from the outer membrane, periplasm, and inner membrane (Figs.
1
A-B and
3
D), consistent with previous reports [
16
]. Interestingly, OMV
Bru−M3
were smaller and enriched in periplasmic and inner membrane proteins (Figs.
3
G-H), underlining that alteration in membrane integrity and LPS structure influence vesicle composition. Lipidomic profiling confirmed that polar lipids–phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), phosphatidylcholine (PC), and phosphatidylglycerol (PG)--predominate in OMVs (Figs.
1
C and
3
I), supporting their origin from the
Brucella
envelope. Some of these lipids are known to participate in host immune modulation [
41
]. Similarly,
Helicobacter pylori
OMVs modulate cell proliferation [
42
]. Functionally, OMV
Bru
promoted macrophage proliferation (Figs. S1E-H), an effect amplified by OMV
Bru−M3
(Fig.
1
I). Remarkably, this was accompanied by reduced cytotoxicity (Figs.
1
D and
3
C), suggesting that OMVs-mediated modulation of macrophages may favor bacterial survival. Supporting this, pre-incubation with OMV
Bru
and OMV
Bru−M3
increased
B. abortus
internalization and persistence in macrophages ex vivo (Fig.
S2
).
In vivo, co-infection with OMVs enhanced
B. abortus
pathogenicity, as evidenced by increased bacterial burden, organ pathology, and proinflammatory cytokine production (Figs.
2
A-F). Macrophages are important immune cells that resist pathogen invasion, and host macrophages in a ground state of M0 can be driven to either M1 or M2 states of activation after exposure to pathogen invasion [
43
]. Co-infection with OMVs assisted
Brucella
to induce more proinflammatory M1-like macrophages and less anti-inflammatory M2-like macrophages (Figs.
2
L-M). It is reported that OMVs robustly stimulate M1 macrophages to activate macrophage phagocytosis [
44
]. Mechanistically, OMVs skewed macrophage polarization toward the proinflammatory M1 phenotype, which may enhance phagocytic uptake of
Brucella
and facilitate its systemic dissemination. Further, our proteomic and innate immunity profiling revealed that
Brucella
OMVs modulated the immune signaling pathways in macrophages (Figs.
1
E-J). We speculated that there are specific components in
Brucella
OMVs that activate the host cell signaling pathways and surface receptors related to M1 macrophage polarization, such as NF-κB signaling pathway, MAPK signaling pathway, JAK-STAT signaling pathway, inflammasome signaling pathway. However, further research is needed to determine which components play a key role.
It is reported that subcutaneous (s.c.) vaccination of OMVs provided effective protection against brucellosis [
35
]. Moreover, intranasal (i.n.) mucosal vaccination has the unique advantage of efficiently eliciting mucosal immune responses, which are particularly important for protection against respiratory and mucosa-associated pathogens [
45
]. Vaccination with OMV
Bru−M3
, delivered via s.c. (Fig.
4
) or i.n. (Fig.
5
) routes, conferred better effective protection against
B. abortus
infection. Intramuscular (i.m.) administration remains the most common route for licensed vaccines, and numerous clinical trials have shown that subcutaneous (s.c.) administration induces comparable adaptive immune responses to i.m. delivery [
46
]. In this study, we also assessed the protective efficacy of OMV
Bru−M3
bionanoparticles delivered via the i.m. route against brucellosis in mice, confirming that i.m. vaccination with OMV
Bru−M3
elicits comparable protective immunity to s.c. delivery (Fig. S9; Table S2; Supplementary File 1). Moreover, i.n. immunization required lower doses and achieved superior bacterial clearance, emphasizing the potential of mucosal OMVs vaccination. Notably, it was observed that mice immunized with OMV
Bru−M3
exhibited transient weight loss (Figs.
4
B and
5
B, Fig. S9B). We speculated that more abundant components of proteins and lipids in the OMV
Bru−M3
contained some stimulating factors that elicited a transient inflammatory response in mice, resulting in a mild reactogenicity. Additionally, the
Brucella
challenge was performed via the intraperitoneal route in this study, as it results in a rapid systemic distribution of
Brucella
spp., significant splenomegaly and high bacterial loads in the spleen and liver [
26
]. Under natural conditions,
Brucella
usually breaches the mucosal barrier to establish an infection [
47
]. However, once
Brucella
establishes an infection, both mucosal and intraperitoneal infection models lead to a systemic infection. In this study, OMV
Bru−M3
immunization via different routes elicited the systemic immune responses (Figs.
6
and
7
). Thus, we speculated that the protection offered by OMV
Bru−M3
immunization against mucosal or aerosol challenge would be comparable to that against intraperitoneal challenge. Specially, mucosal vaccination could boost efficacy by stimulating both local mucosal and systemic immune responses [
48
]. Therefore, i.n. mucosal vaccination with OMV
Bru−M3
probably exhibits stronger protective immunity against natural respiratory mucosa infection of
Brucella
than against an intraperitoneal one.
Protective efficacy correlated with elevated serum levels of IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-6 (Figs.
4
G and
5
G, Fig. S9G) and increased Th1 cytokine production by splenic CD4⁺ T cells (Fig.
7
, Fig. S12), underscoring the importance of Th1 responses in controlling
Brucella
infection [
49
–
52
]. Although OMV
Bru−M3
also induced higher serum IL-17 A following i.m. (Figs. S9G) or s.c. (Figs.
4
G) administration, this was not observed after i.n. delivery or in splenic CD4⁺ T cells (Fig. S8; Supplementary File 1), suggesting that Th17 responses are not essential for protection. Elevated levels of IL-4, IL-1β, and IL-18 in OMV
Bru−M3
-immunized mice were also detected post-challenge, indicating potential auxiliary roles in protection that merit further exploration.
Humoral immunity contributed to protection, as OMV
Bru−M3
induced strong anti-
Brucella
IgG and IgM responses (Fig.
6
, Fig. S11), and IgG2a/IgG1 ratios suggested a Th2-biased antibody profile. Given the reported protective roles of IgM in intracellular infections [
53
,
54
], its role in anti-
Brucella
immunity should be further investigated. Consistent with previous studies [
52
], CD8⁺ T cell responses were modest and likely played a limited role in protection (Fig.
7
, Fig. S12).
Conclusion
In summary,
Brucella
OMVs promote bacterial invasion and dissemination by modulating macrophage function in an experimental mice model. By remodeling the
B. abortus
strain to enhance OMVs yield and immunogenicity, we generated a vaccine candidate (OMV
Bru−M3
) that induces robust Th1-biased cellular and humoral responses and confers better effective protection against
Brucella
infection in mice. These findings support the potential of genetically engineered OMVs as a novel platform for brucellosis vaccination, particularly via mucosal routes. Further investigation into lipid A biosynthesis and the roles of non-Th1 cytokines may reveal additional mechanisms underlying OMVs-mediated protection.
