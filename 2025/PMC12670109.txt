1
Introduction
Red pepper (
Capsicum annuum
L.) is a globally vital condiment crop. China is the dominant producer for it with an annual yield exceeding 60 milliontons, accounting for 46 % of global supply. The processing of these peppers generates significant quantities of seeds (30–60 % of fruit dry weight) that remain largely underutilized despite their considerable nutritional value (
Wang, Ma et al., 2021
). These pepper seeds harbor 13.8–28.3 % (
w
/w) nutritionally complete protein yet exhibit a variety of functional features and negligible antinutritional factors (
Deng et al., 2025
). These characteristics position pepper seed protein as a promising alternative protein supplement and food additive, thereby demanding urgent development and application strategies (
Cvetković et al., 2022
). Understanding the fundamental traits is of great significance for further development and application of these proteins.
Current studies on pepper seed proteins have primarily focused on the total proteins' isolation and extraction or the modification of these proteins' physicochemical properties through various processing techniques. For instance, high hydrostatic pressure (HHP) has been shown to positively influence the structure and physicochemical properties of the isolated total proteins of Yidu-Red pepper seeds, thereby enhancing its functional performance (
Deng et al., 2025
). Similarly, ultrasonication-assisted extraction can induce significant structural reconfiguration of the red pepper seed total proteins, leading to improved functionality (
Manzoor et al., 2025
). Notably, a synergistic effect was observed when ultrasonication was combined with enzymatic hydrolysis. This combined approach yielded bell pepper seed protein hydrolysates with lower molecular weight and significantly higher antioxidant activity compared to the untreated protein (
Li et al., 2018
). Although these methods have provided valuable insights into the characteristics of pepper seed proteins, they often overlook the unique functional characteristics of different protein components within the total protein. Given the diversity of Chinese pepper species (over 30 cultivars as of 2022), as pointed out by
Jin et al. (2022)
, the types and quantities of proteins isolated from red pepper seeds can vary significantly depending on the variety and the extraction method used. These differences directly lead to differences in the functions and biological activities of the obtained protein isolates. Therefore, these limitations highlight the urgent need for more targeted research, which should focus on characterizing specific protein components from specific sources of peppers (such as clearly defined cultivars and origins) (
Deng et al., 2025
;
Wang, Ma, et al., 2021
).
This study focused on the main seed protein fractions from two widely cultivated and consumed pepper species in China, Erjingtiao and Zidantou. Erjingtiao is frequently employed in Ciba pepper paste formulations and serves as a key seasoning in hot pot bases due to its favorable flavor profile. While Zidantou exhibits an exceptionally high seed-to-pericarp ratio (113.6 %) (
Yuan et al., 2022
). Therefore, this study aimed to isolate and characterize the main protein fractions from Erjingtiao and Zidantou red pepper seeds, and to evaluate their antioxidant, functional, and bioactive peptide properties. Specifically, this work sought to compare the antioxidant activities and taste characterizations of the extracted protein fractions from the Erjingtiao and Zidantou pepper seeds to select the superior protein fraction, followed by comprehensive characterization of its structural and functional attributes. The protein sequences were determined through mass spectrometry-based proteomics to establish fundamental structural information. Furthermore, we employed an integrated approach combining bioinformatic analysis with experimental validation to explore enzymatic modification strategies for enhancing bioactivity and flavor characteristics. Additionally, the identification of bioactive peptides in the resulting hydrolysates was conducted to fully assess the potential of pepper seed proteins as functional food ingredients with improved nutritional value and technological properties.

Materials and methods
2.1
Materials and reagents
Red peppers were purchased from Sichuan Chuanjiao Seed Industry Technology Co., Ltd. (Sichuan, China). The 1,1-diphenyl-2-picryl-hydrazyl (DPPH) radical was purchased from Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The 2,2′-azino-bis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS), potassium persulfate, glutathione (GSH), trichloroacetic acid (TCA) and phenanthroline were supplied by Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). 8-Anilino-1-naphthalenesulfonic Acid (ANS) was purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) gel preparation kit, rainbow 180 broad-spectrum protein marker, 4 × protein loading buffer Tris and SDS were obtained from Solarbio Life Sciences Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Papain and trypsin were purchased from Dibai Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All other chemicals not otherwise specified were of analytical grade.
2.2
Preparation of red pepper seed protein
Defatting of red pepper seeds was performed following
Manzoor et al. (2025)
. Protein isolation was conducted using an optimized ultrasonication-assisted alkaline extraction and isoelectric precipitation method. Defatted pepper seed powder (1:30,
w
/
v
) was homogenized in distilled water, and adjusted pH to 9.5 using 0.5 M NaOH/HCl solution. The suspension underwent ultrasonication (300 W, 30 °C, 15 min) followed by magnetic stirring (500 rpm, 2 h). After centrifugation (6000 ×
g
, 20 min, 25 °C), the supernatant was acidified to pH 4.0 and incubated at 4 °C for 30 min. The precipitate was collected by centrifugation (5000 ×
g
, 20 min, 4 °C), washed three times with distilled water, and resuspended in distilled water (1:3
w
/
v
, pH 7.0). Final supernatants containing Erjingtiao red pepper seed protein (
E
-RPSP) and Zidantou red pepper seed protein (
Z
-RPSP) were obtained after centrifugation (5000 ×
g
, 20 min, 4 °C). The red pepper seed protein was collected and freeze-dried to powder for subsequent experiments.
2.3
Determination of non-reducing and reducing SDS-PAGE
Protein samples (2 mg/mL) were prepared in 4× reducing or non-reducing Laemmli buffer. Separation was performed using a discontinuous buffer system with 12 % resolving gel (pH 8.8) and 3 % stacking gel (pH 6.8). For reducing conditions, samples were denatured at 100 °C for 3 min followed by rapid cooling. Aliquots (20 μL) of samples were electrophoresed at constant voltage (80 V through stacking gel; 100 V through resolving gel). Gels were stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250 for 2 h, destained until clear background was achieved, and analyzed using ImageJ software.

2.5
Electronic tongue analysis and sensory evaluation
Taste profiles of protein were analyzed using a C-tongue system (cTongue，Bosin，Shanghai，China) featuring a six-electrode array (S1: W, S2: Au, S3: Ti, S4: Pt, S5: Pd, S6: Ag). After 20 min of system preheating, the homogenized samples (1 mg/mL, 30 mL) were analyzed with 300× signal amplification (range: −2.5 to 2.5) for 5 min (six replicates per sample). Principal component analysis was performed with calibration using standard taste solutions: 0.25 mg/mL MSG (umami), 0.70 mg/mL NaCl (salty), 7.19 mg/mL sucrose (sweet), 0.96 mg/mL citric acid (sour), and 0.03 mg/mL quinine sulfate (bitter). The method of sensory evaluation is as shown in the sensory evaluation method of supplementary material.
2.6
Determination of circular dichroism (CD) spectrum
The secondary structure of protein was analyzed by far-ultraviolet CD spectrum (Chirascan, Applied Photophysics, England). Briefly, protein solutions (0.5 mg/mL, phosphate buffer) were adjusted to pH 4.0, 6.0, 8.0, and 10.0 using 0.1 M HCl or NaOH and equilibrated at 25 °C for 30 min prior to measurement. The CD spectra (185–260 nm) of the
Z
-RPSP solutions were obtained using a quartz cuvette of 1 mm path length, a scanning speed of 100 nm/min, and a reaction time of 0.50 s. The obtained spectra were processed using the instrument software (Pro-data), and the secondary structure composition was calculated using free online CD deconvolution software (CDNN).
2.7
Determination of intrinsic fluorescence spectrum
The tertiary structure of protein (0.5 mg/mL, phosphate buffer) was analyzed using spectrofluorophotometer (RF-6000, Shimazu, Japan). Protein solutions were adjusted to pH 4.0–10.0 using 0.1 M HCl or NaOH, and then equilibrated at 25 °C for 30 min prior for measurement. The scanning speed is 600 nm/min, the scanning range is 300–450 nm, and the excitation wavelength is 280 nm.
2.8
Determination of surface hydrophobicity
Protein solutions (0.5 mg/mL) were prepared and adjusted to pH values of 4.0–10.0. Four milliliters of protein solution was mixed with 20 μL of 8.0 mM ANS solution and vortexed thoroughly. Fluorescence measurements were conducted with excitation light at 390 nm and emission scanning from 410 to 650 nm. The net fluorescence intensity was calculated by subtracting the blank (water) signal from sample readings.

2.14
Least gelation concentration (LGC)
LGC of protein was tested according to
Zhang, Liu et al. (2025)
. The protein solutions (80–180 mg/mL) were homogenized by magnetic stirring (500 rpm, 30 min, 25 °C). After degassing (2000 ×g, 2 min), aliquots (5 mL) of protein solutions were heated in a 100 °C water bath for 30 min, followed by rapid cooling in ice-water and storage at 4 °C for 12 h. LGC was determined as the lowest protein concentration that maintained structural integrity upon tube inversion.
2.15
Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) sequencing
The target zona alba (48 kDa) gel slice was precisely cut and sent to Shanghai Fuda Detection Technology Group Co., Ltd., then LC-MS/MS detection and PEAKS software screening were used for sequencing.
2.16
Computer simulation and bioinformatics analysis
UniProt database (
https://www.uniprot.org/
) was used to search for the amino acid sequence of target protein. BIOPEP-UWM (

) was used to evaluate the quality of predicted tertiary structure of the protein.
2.17
Enzymatic hydrolysis of protein
Protein solution (50 mg/mL) was incubated with trypsin (pH 8.0, 37 °C) and papain (pH 6.5, 50 °C) separately at an enzyme activity of 6000 U/g for 6 h. The reactions were terminated by heating at 85 °C for 15 min, followed by immediate cooling in an ice bath. The mixtures were then centrifuged at 9000 ×
g
for 20 min at 4 °C to obtain the enzymatic hydrolyzates. The antioxidant activities (including ABTS Radical Scavenging Assay and OH Radical Scavenging Assay) and taste profiles of enzymatic hydrolyzates were measured following the methods of 2.5.2, 2.5.3 and 2.6. Besides, the peptides in enzymatic hydrolyzates were identified by Shanghai Fuda Detection Technology Group Co., Ltd. using LC-MS/MS method. The analysis of biological activity of identified peptides was carried out following the methods of 2.17.
2.18
Statistical analysis
The functional properties and antioxidant properties of the protein were measured in triplicate. The results are presented as the mean (
n
= 3) ± SD. Multigroup comparison of the means was performed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by post-hoc contrasts using Duncan's multiple range test. The statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS 27.0 and GraphPad Prism 10.0 software. Statistical significance for all tests was set at
P
< 0.05.

3.1
Non-reducing and reducing SDS-PAGE
Non-reducing SDS-PAGE was performed to analyze the protein profiles of defatted powders from Erjingtiao and Zidantou red pepper seeds. As shown in
Fig. 1
A, two pepper seed samples both exhibited five main protein bands (180, 110, 85, 48 and 30 kDa), and the molecular weight of main protein fraction was close to 48 kDa.
Wang, Ma, et al. (2021)
observed distinct electrophoretic banding patterns in Yidu-red pepper seeds by SDS-PAGE, and the result primarily shows main proteins at 50, 35, 20, 15, and 5 kDa, respectively.
Li et al. (2018)
reported SDS-PAGE profiles (43.0, 35.2, 33.5, 24.3, 20.0, and 13.7 kDa) of bell pepper seed proteins. These indicated the protein profiles of pepper seeds are different because of different species. Despite these differences, a conserved protein band at approximately 45 kDa was consistently detected across all studies. Then, the main protein fractions of Erjingtiao (E) and Zidantou (Z) red pepper seeds were purified and characterized by non-reducing and reducing SDS-PAGE. SDS-PAGE photos (

B and

Taste profiles of

E

Zhang, Yu, et al., 2025

3.4.1
CD and endogenous fluorescence spectrums
The effects of pH on the secondary and tertiary structures of Z-RPSP were explored by CD and endogenous fluorescence spectrums. CD spectrum of Z-RPSP exhibited a distinct double-bottom structure (
Fig. 4
A), indicating that Z-RPSP is typical of an α + β protein. The α-helix, β-sheet, β-turn and random coil ratios of Z-RPSP at pH 4, 6, 8 and 10 were evaluated by spectral analyses and Applied Photophysics Computer software as well as shown in
Table 1
. CD spectrum analysis results of
Fig. 4
A and
Table 1
revealed significant pH-dependent variations in the secondary structure of
Z
-RPSP. Under neutral to alkaline conditions (pH 6–10), increased α-helix content (from 27.5 % to 29.5 %) with decreased β-sheet (from 19.9 % to 19.6 %) and random coil (from 36.2 % to 34.1 %) contents were observed. These indicated the enhanced structural ordering through strengthened intramolecular hydrogen bonding, resulting in greater molecular rigidity. In contrast, acidic conditions (pH 4) promoted α-helix destabilization (reduced to 20.8 %) with increased β-sheet (24.2 %) and random coil (39.4 %) formation, suggesting protein unfolding and structural flexibility due to compromised hydrogen-bonding networks (
Li et al., 2020
).

3.4.2
Surface hydrophobicity and SH content
The surface hydrophobicity of
Z
-RPSP is positively correlated with the fluorescence intensity of ANS bound to it, and the maximum fluorescence intensities of Z-RPSP at pH 4, 6, 8, 10 were 18,403,477.8, 16294249.3, 6071976.6, 4713383.2 a.u., respectively (
Fig. 5
A). The maximum fluorescence intensity, corresponding to peak surface hydrophobicity of
Z
-RPSP, occurred at pH 4.0. Interestingly, an inverse correlation was observed between surface hydrophobicity and α-helix content (
Fig. 4
A). This finding is consistent with reports on β-conglycinin, in which a higher α-helix content correlates with lower hydrophobicity, suggesting that pH-induced structural unfolding exposes buried hydrophobic domains (
Tian et al., 2020
). Comparable pH-dependent hydrophobicity trends have been documented in soybean protein variants (
Zhu et al., 2020
) and β-caseins from bovine and donkey milk (
Zhang et al., 2024
). These indicated that pH is a critical determinant of surface hydrophobicity of
Z
-RPSP through its influence on molecular conformation.

3.4.3
EAI and ESI
EAI and ESI of
Z
-RPSP at different pH were presented in
Fig. 5
C. EAI of Z-RPSP at pH 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12 were 24.8, 22.0, 30.7, 37.9, 73.1, 83.4 m
2
/g, respectively. ESI of
Z

Sharafodin & Soltanizadeh, 2022
). However,
Z
-RPSP showed lower EAI at pH 4 (acidic conditions) despite its α-helix content was reduced (
Fig. 4

Fig. 5

Z

This apparent discrepancy highlights the multifactorial regulation of EAI, which depends not only on secondary structure and hydrophobicity but also on additional physicochemical parameters, including pH-dependent surface charge and solubility (
Ge et al., 2021
). Our results revealed the U-shaped pH-dependence for EAI of
Z
-RPSP. This pH-dependent behavior is consistent with observations in other plant proteins including legume, and rice bran (
Tang et al., 2023
;
Zhao et al., 2012
).
3.4.4
FC and FS
FC and FS represent critical functional properties that characterize a protein's ability to form and stabilize air-water interfacial films (
Ma et al., 2022
).
Fig. 5
D shows the FC and FS of
Z
-RPSP at different pH. FC of Z-RPSP increased from 25.0 % to 48.3 % with pH from 2 to 12, and FS of
Z
-RPSP at pH 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12 were 84.0 %, 91.9 %, 93.3 %, 89.0 %, 92.7 %, 89.9 %, respectively. FC of
Z
-RPSP exhibited significant pH dependence, which gradually increased from 25.0 % to 48.3 % with increasing pH (2 to 12). In contrast, under the pH conditions of all tests, FS of
Z
-RPSP always maintained a high level (>84 %), but the overall curve change was consistent with FC. This pH-dependent foaming behavior mirrors that observed in milk proteins. Overall, FC increases with the increase of pH value, and comparable FC is reported under similar conditions (
Zhao et al., 2022
). Besides, at pH 4.0,
Z
-RPSP exhibited maximum foam stability (FS). This can be attributed to the formation of a denser protein adsorption layer at the air-water interface with minimized intermolecular electrostatic repulsion within the pH range of 4.0–4.5. Consequently, the foam generated under acidic conditions demonstrated enhanced stability compared to that formed at neutral pH (
Lajnaf et al., 2018
).
3.4.5
WHC and OHC
WHC and OHC of Z-RPSP at different temperatures were measured and shown in
Fig. 5
E and
Fig. 5

OHC of Z-RPSP increased first from 1.8 to 2.0 g/g with temperature from 30 to 40 °C, and then decreased from 1.7 to 1.2 g/g with temperature from 50 to 80 °C. WHC of Z-RPSP initially increased with temperature, reaching maximum values at 50 °C–60 °C, and then decreased at higher temperatures. In this study, the OHC (2.04 g/g) of Z-RPSP at 40 °C was higher than the maximum OHC (approximately 1.6 g/g) of the pepper seed protein reported by
Manzoor et al. (2025)
. This is similar to the WHC of soy protein, which is higher at temperatures from 40 °C to 70 °C (
Ma et al., 2022
), and the observed increase in water-holding capacity (WHC) of
Z
-RPSP may be attributed to the exposure of hydrophilic groups on the protein structure, enhancing its interaction with water molecules through hydrogen bonding and polar interactions (
Silva et al., 2023
). The OHC of
Z
-RPSP showed a comparable trend, peaking at 40 °C (2.0 g/g), and this value is closed to that for brewer spent grain protein at the same temperature (about 2.8 g/g). Compared to WHC, OHC is less affected by temperature changes because that it is mostly attributed to the physical entrapment of exposing hydrophilic or lipophilic groups of oil (
Silva et al., 2023
).

3.6
Antioxidant activities and taste characterization of enzymatic hydrolysates of Z-RPSP
In this study, enzymatic hydrolysis of Z-RPSP was preliminarily explored. The first step was to select the appropriate protease through virtual enzymatic hydrolysis of Z-RPSP (based on protein A0A2G2YPI4), and the selecting indicator was the total number of predicted Z-RPSP-hydrolyzed peptides with function activities. Trypsin and papain were chosen for actual hydrolysis experiments due to their ability to generate abundant peptide fragments with diverse biological activities.
Z
-RPSP hydrolysates by trypsin and papain were subsequently evaluated for their antioxidant capacity and flavor characteristics.
Therefore, virtual enzymatic hydrolysis of Z-RPSP based on the protein (A0A2G2YPI4) was performed. The quantity and functional activities of the predicted peptides after hydrolysis of Z-RPSP by papain, trypsin, subtilisin, proteinase K, chymotrypsin and chymase were displayed in Table A.2. Total number of predicted Z-RPSP-hydrolyzed peptides with functional activities by papain and trypsin were 156 and 78, reaching the first and second among the selected hydrolytic enzymes, respectively. Therefore, in the actual enzymatic hydrolysis experiment, trypsin and papain were selected for subsequent experiments.
Antioxidant activities of enzymatic hydrolysates of
Z
-RPSP (P-Z-RPSP and T-Z-RPSP) were preliminarily studied by measuring the ABTS and OH radical scavenging rates. As shown in

Fig. 7

Z

Z

Krunić & Rakin, 2022

Wang, Li, et al., 2022
). Research results in this study further validated that enzymatic modification is a potent strategy for improving bioactivities of

-RPSP.

3.7
Peptides analysis of T-
Z
-RPSP
Peptides in T-Z-RPSP were identified by LC-MS/MS and Peaks studio software. A total of 7396 peptides were found and 1446 peptides based on confidence level (>50) and peak area were then selected. Next, these peptides were further analyzed by PeptideRanker, ToxinPred and BIOPEP-UWM databases to screen the peptides with high safety, function and biological activities. As shown in Table A.3, 76 umami peptides, 50 salty peptides, 100 bitter peptides, 8 enhancing-flavor peptides, 5 enhancing-saltiness peptides, 15 antioxidant peptides, and 5 anti-inflammatory peptides were identified from T-Z-RPSP. The obtained functional peptides might be the main cause of the changes in the antioxidant activity and flavor characteristics of Z-RPSP hydrolysate. Although bitter peptides were numerically predominant in the hydrolysate, their sensory impact may be mitigated by the balanced interplay of various non-volatile compounds rather than individual components (
Chen et al., 2019
). Besides, some peptides demonstrated multiple functions, such as peptides TAPAHPPYFEMIK and FDYPPYGLNAPHTNPR, which have dual antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities. These results provide a foundation for the further research on the enzymatic processing of Z-RPSP and the protein-derived active peptides.
4
Conclusion
In this study, the main protein fractions of
E
-RPSP and Z-RPSP with high purity were obtained. Z-RPSP and E-RPSP showed similar bitter taste, but Z-RPSP exhibited superior antioxidant capacity in terms of DPPH, ABTS and OH radical scavenging activity as well as Fe
2+
chelating activity. Significant pH-dependent variations in the secondary and tertiary structure of Z-RPSP were observed. Acidic conditions promoted α-helix destabilization with increased β-sheet, random coil formation and sulfhydryl groups as well as the exposure of hydrophobic domains of Z-RPSP. Moreover, Z-RPSP has good performances in the functional characteristics, including emulsifying activity, foaming ability, WHC and OHC, and gel-forming properties. Enzymatic hydrolysis technics of Z-RPSP was preliminarily investigated by combining computer simulation (based on LC-MS/MS sequencing results) and the measurements of antioxidant capacity and flavor characteristics after enzyme hydrolysis. Antioxidant capacity and flavor (bitter to saline taste) notably enhanced after trypsin hydrolysis. Various bioactive peptides were identified from Z-RPSP hydrolysate with potential high activities. Z-RPSP has good antioxidant capacity and functional properties, as well as can be hydrolyzed by enzymes to produce bioactive peptides. These demonstrated that Z-RPSP possesses significant potential as a sustainable plant protein resource for applications in the food processing industry.
In the future research, we will conduct in-depth mechanism studies on functional characteristics such as emulsification of Z-RPSP and seek technical means to enhance these functional properties. We will explore methods to expand the extraction process and optimize bioavailability, produce flavor and antioxidant peptides based on the main protein fraction from red pepper seeds. Additionally, will verify the protein fraction's health benefits through mouse experiments to fully evaluate its commercial application potential in the plant-based food system and nutritional supplements.
