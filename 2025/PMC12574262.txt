Introduction
Diacylglycerol kinases (DGKs) are essential lipid-metabolizing enzymes that phosphorylate diacylglycerol (DAG) to generate phosphatidic acid (PA), functioning as key nodes in both intracellular signaling cascades and membrane lipid homeostasis [
1
]. DGKs modulate cellular processes through two primary mechanisms: by consuming DAG to suppress DAG-mediated signaling pathways such as protein kinase C (PKC) and RasGRP, and by producing PA to activate downstream cascades involving mTOR, Raf, and phosphodiesterases (PDEs) [
2
–
5
]. Through this dual action, DGKs act as metabolic integrators, coordinating responses related to growth, proliferation, immune activation, and metabolic reprogramming.
To date, ten DGK isoforms have been identified in mammals, being categorized into five subtypes on the basis of structural motifs. These isoforms exhibit marked differences in tissue distribution, substrate affinity, subcellular localization, and regulatory mechanisms, indicating a high degree of functional specificity [
6
]. Such structural and regulatory diversity underlies the isoform-specific physiological roles of DGKs. Recent studies have revealed that individual DGK isoforms serve as key modulators of processes including adipogenesis, insulin signaling, immune function, and inflammation–metabolism interactions, across various disease contexts such as cancer, metabolic syndrome, and cardiovascular conditions [
5
,
7
–
10
].
Despite a growing understanding of DGK biology, the isoform-specific roles of DGKs in metabolic diseases and cancer, the upstream and downstream regulatory networks, and their causal relationships with disease progression remain incompletely understood. In particular, the contributions of DGKs to tumor metabolic adaptation, immune evasion, and inflammation-coupled metabolic dysregulation are not yet fully elucidated. Moreover, the current lack of highly selective and clinically viable DGK inhibitors hinders their translational potential as therapeutic targets.
This review provides a comprehensive overview of DGK structural features, catalytic mechanisms, and biological functions, with a particular focus on their roles in lipid signaling, immunometabolism, and tumor progression. We also highlight current progress in the development of DGK-targeted therapies, identify key technical and translational challenges, and propose future directions for exploiting DGKs as therapeutic nodes in metabolic and neoplastic diseases.
Structural characteristics and functional classification of DGKs
The functional diversity of mammalian DGKs stems from their structural heterogeneity. The ten known isoforms (α, β, γ, δ, ε, ζ, η, θ, ι, and κ) are classified into five types (types I–V) on the basis of distinct modular domain arrangements and dynamic subcellular localizations. This classification reflects an evolutionarily conserved structure–function coupling mechanism that enables precise spatiotemporal regulation of DAG and PA metabolism (Fig.
1
A) [
11
]. In this section, we systematically outline the structural domains of DGK isoforms and their functional implications, providing a theoretical basis for understanding subtype-specific physiological and pathological roles (Fig.
1
B).

Structural classification
Type I DGKs (α, β, and γ)
Type I DGKs—DGKα, DGKβ, and DGKγ—were the first isoforms to be identified, characterized by highly conserved structural features. They are notably responsive to intracellular calcium fluctuations and rapidly transduce DAG-related signals. Structurally, they share a conserved architecture consisting of an N-terminal recoverin homology domain (RVH), a calcium-binding EF-hand domain, two tandem C1 domains, and a canonical catalytic domain (kinase domain). These domains cooperatively modulate the enzyme’s activation status, membrane association, and substrate specificity [
14
,
15
].
A defining feature of type I DGKs is the EF-hand domain, which contains a pair of α-helices forming a canonical calcium-binding “hand” structure. This motif senses intracellular Ca
2+
levels and undergoes conformational shifts upon calcium binding, thereby promoting enzyme translocation from the cytosol to DAG-enriched membranes and activating catalytic activity [
16
]. The C1 domains facilitate DAG recognition and membrane anchoring, albeit with lower affinity compared with PKC isoforms, yet sufficient for targeting DGKs to signaling hotspots [
17
]. The catalytic domain, shared among all DGK isoforms, consists of a catalytic DAGKc subdomain and an accessory DAGKa subdomain and constitutes the enzymatic core for DAG phosphorylation [
18
].
Type II DGKs (δ, η, and κ)
DGKδ, DGKη, and DGKκ represent Ttype II isoforms, distinguished by their unique N-terminal pleckstrin homology (PH) domain. This domain facilitates membrane targeting through interactions with phosphoinositide-rich regions [
19
]. Following the PH domain, these isoforms contain two tandem C1 domains similar to those found in PKCs and a sterile alpha motif (SAM) domain at the C-terminus. The SAM domain mediates homo- or hetero-oligomerization, thereby influencing localization and enzymatic function [
20
]. The catalytic domain resembles that of type I DGKs. Notably, DGKκ lacks a SAM domain but harbors a distinctive C-terminal motif that may interact with type I PDZ domains, potentially influencing protein–protein interactions [
21
].
Type III DGKε
DGKε is the most structurally minimal member of the DGK family. It contains only two C1 domains, a catalytic domain, and a recently identified ER-targeting transmembrane domain [
22
]. DGKε uniquely exhibits substrate selectivity for 1-stearoyl-2-arachidonoyl-
sn
-glycerol [
23
]. The presence of this transmembrane domain accounts for its stable association with the endoplasmic reticulum membrane, distinguishing it from other DGK isoforms. Lacking PH domains, regulatory modules, nuclear localization signals, or protein interaction motifs, DGKε serves as an ideal model for dissecting the core catalytic mechanisms of DGKs owing to its structural simplicity.
AlphaFold predictions for human DGKε (AF-
P52429
) highlight a high-confidence catalytic core with flexible interdomain linkers. This arrangement positions the kinase near arachidonate-enriched DAG pools and suggests putative C1-catalytic interfaces that could mediate allosteric regulation [
22
]. These predictions align with experimental data showing that the N-terminal hydrophobic stretch is necessary and sufficient for ER targeting and membrane anchoring of DGKε.
Type IV DGKs (ζ and ι)
DGKζ and DGKι are distinguished by their complex and multifunctional domain architecture. In addition to tandem C1 domains and the conserved catalytic core, they harbor a myristoylated alanine-rich C kinase substrate (MARCKS) homology domain. This domain, enriched in positively charged residues, interacts with acidic phospholipids and is implicated in nucleocytoplasmic shuttling and membrane association [
24
]. Furthermore, both isoforms contain a PDZ domain and ankyrin repeats (ANK), which facilitate specific protein–protein interactions, contributing to their regulatory versatility [
25
].
Type V DGKθ
DGKθ exhibits a unique domain configuration, comprising three atypical C1 domains (as opposed to the two found in most isoforms), a PH domain, and a proline/glycine-rich region (PGR) at the N-terminus, which likely serves as a docking site for SH3 domain-containing proteins [
26
]. Intriguingly, the PH domain of DGKθ contains a Ras-association (RA) motif, suggesting a potential interaction with RhoA, linking lipid signaling to small GTPase-mediated pathways [
27
].
Enzymatic properties of DGKs
Catalytic mechanism
The catalytic reaction of DGKs represents a typical ATP- and metal ion-dependent phosphorylation process [
28
]. The enzymatic activity of DGKs critically depends on a highly conserved catalytic domain that contains distinct binding sites for ATP and DAG. These sites enable precise spatial recognition and substrate positioning, ensuring efficient transfer of the phosphate group [
29
]. ATP binding generally requires Mg
2+
as cofactors. In the form of a MgATP complex, Mg
2+
stabilizes the active site by coordinating with water molecules and conserved residues, thereby enhancing enzymatic efficiency [
30
,
31
].
Enzyme activation
DGK activation is a complex and subtype-specific process regulated by diverse signals, including phosphorylation, calcium influx, and lipid signaling (Fig.
2
). For instance, DGKα is tightly regulated by intracellular Ca
2+
levels, sensed via its EF-hand domain. Upon elevation of calcium levels, DGKα undergoes activation and membrane translocation, resulting in enhanced catalytic activity [
15
]. DGKζ is activated through phosphorylation downstream of the extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) signaling pathway, whereas DGKθ responds directly to phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate [PI(4,5)P
2
], which enhances its ability to phosphorylate DAG [
1
]. Protein kinase C (PKC)-mediated phosphorylation is another key regulatory mechanism. For example, PKCγ directly interacts with DGKγ and phosphorylates Ser-776 and Ser-779, significantly enhancing its activity [
32
].

Subcellular localization
Precise subcellular localization is essential for DGKs to execute their context-specific biological functions. Most DGK isoforms are cytosolic or peripheral enzymes that can be dynamically recruited to membrane interfaces to phosphorylate membrane-associated DAG substrates, whereas DGKε is constitutively anchored to the ER membrane via its N-terminal hydrophobic helix [
33
,
34
]. Each DGK isoform utilizes distinct structural motifs to associate with subcellular compartments such as the plasma membrane, endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, and nucleus. The following sections summarize isoform-specific localization patterns and their functional implications.
Plasma membrane localization
Several DGK isoforms translocate to the plasma membrane in response to extracellular stimuli to modulate local DAG signaling. DGKα is a classic example of a stimulus-dependent translocating enzyme. It resides predominantly in the cytosol under basal conditions, but upon T cell receptor engagement, the EF-hand domain senses elevated Ca
2+
and rapidly directs DGKα to the plasma membrane, where it converts DAG to PA [
35
–
37
]. Similarly, DGKζ translocates from the cytosol to the membrane in response to PKC activation to regulate local DAG pools [
38
]. The DGKη2 splice variant is constitutively membrane-associated and plays a role in neuronal signaling and the pathophysiology of neuropsychiatric disorders. TPA (12-
O
-tetradecanoylphorbol-13-acetate) stimulation can induce DGKδ1 translocation from the cytosol to the membrane [
39
]. Notably, DGKκ is constitutively localized to the plasma membrane and remains unaffected by TPA stimulation [
40
]. DGKθ also translocates to the plasma membrane following G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) activation or PKC stimulation [
41
]. Under high-glucose conditions, DGKδ1 rapidly localizes to the plasma membrane via its PH and C1 domains in a PI3K-dependent manner [
20
]. GPCR activation can also promote DGKζ translocation via mechanisms involving its MARCKS-like domain and PDZ-binding motif [
29
,
42
]. Moreover, DGKη translocates to the plasma membrane upon osmotic stress via its PH domain, which binds PIP2 [
19
].
Cytosolic localization
Under resting conditions, many DGK isoforms are predominantly cytosolic, exhibiting isoform-specific intracellular distribution patterns. For example, DGKα, DGKδ, DGKζ, and DGKθ are widely distributed in the cytoplasm [
41
,
43
–
45
].
Localization to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and Golgi apparatus
DGKγ is enriched in intracellular membrane systems, including the ER and Golgi apparatus [
46
]. Similarly, DGKδ is widely localized to both the ER and Golgi, where it is targeted to Golgi membranes via its SAM and PH domains. This localization enables DGKδ to regulate vesicular transport from the ER to the Golgi, contributing to critical cellular processes such as primary cilium assembly [
47
,
48
]. DGKε is predominantly associated with the ER membrane and exhibits substrate specificity for DAG species containing arachidonic acid chains. This ER-associated localization is essential for maintaining lipid homeostasis and signal transduction. A small fraction of DGKε has also been observed in the Golgi apparatus [
34
,
49
].
Nuclear localization
Certain DGK isoforms, such as DGKα, ζ, and ι, can translocate to the nucleus under specific conditions, suggesting potential roles in transcriptional regulation and apoptosis [
50
]. Among them, DGKζ is the most extensively studied nuclear-localized isoform. It contains a nuclear localization signal (NLS), and its nuclear import relies on cooperative interactions between the NLS and C-terminal regions, including ankyrin repeat motifs [
51
]. Additionally, DGKζ modulates p53 subcellular distribution through direct interaction, thereby influencing p53-dependent apoptotic pathways [
52
]. DGKγ has also been reported to localize to the nucleus, where it may regulate nuclear lipid signaling [
53
]. In specific cell types, such as K562 leukemia cells, DGKα exhibits nuclear localization and has been implicated in cell cycle regulation [
54
].
Other subcellular localizations
Beyond the classical organelles, members of the DGK family also function in other subcellular structures, notably within the cytoskeletal system. DGKζ is the most clearly defined cytoskeleton-associated isoform, interacting with actin and actin-regulatory proteins to participate in cytoskeletal remodeling [
55
]. DGKβ colocalizes with actin filaments, indicating a potential role in cytoskeletal regulation as well [
56
]. Moreover, endosomes—heterogeneous vesicles formed via endocytosis—also serve as functional sites for DGK activity. Both DGKδ and DGKη have been detected on endosomal membranes [
21
,
55
].

Regulatory networks of DGKs
Signal transduction along the DAG–PA axis
As pivotal enzymes at the intersection of signal transduction and metabolic regulation, DGKs act as pivotal hubs linking DAG and PA metabolism (Fig.
3
). In the classical phospholipase C (PLC) signaling pathway, membrane receptor activation—such as by G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) or receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs)—induces the hydrolysis of PIP2 into DAG and inositol trisphosphate (IP3) [
57
]. DAG then serves as a second messenger to activate downstream pathways involving protein kinase C (PKC), Ras guanine nucleotide-releasing proteins (RasGRPs), and protein kinase D (PKD), thereby regulating cell proliferation, differentiation, and immune responses [
58
–
61
]. PKC and PKD are serine/threonine kinases involved in diverse receptor-mediated signaling cascades, while RasGRPs promote GTP loading of Ras and trigger its activation [
62
].

However, excessive DAG accumulation can lead to sustained aberrant signaling. DGKs attenuate this by phosphorylating DAG into PA, thus curbing DAG-mediated proinflammatory and oncogenic effects [
27
]. Importantly, the PA produced by DGKs is not merely a biosynthetic intermediate but also functions as a signaling lipid, activating mTORC1/2, phosphatidylinositol 4-phosphate 5-kinase (PI4P5K), and SHP-1 phosphatase to coordinate cell growth, vesicular trafficking, and metabolic reprogramming [
63
–
66
]. In addition, PA serves as a docking site for proteins with PA-binding domains, thereby spatially recruiting signaling molecules to membranes and promoting the assembly of signaling complexes [
67
]. For example, PA recruits Sos to the plasma membrane, thereby amplifying Ras–ERK signaling, while also stabilizing mTORC1 at lysosomal membranes to promote nutrient sensing [
68
,
69
].
Of note, PI(4,5)P
2
itself has a unique fatty acid signature, being enriched in 1-stearoyl-2-arachidonoyl (18:0/20:4) species [
70
]. This molecular composition is preserved through the PI cycle, such that DGK-derived PA must be efficiently channeled into CDP-DAG and phosphatidylinositol (PI) synthesis to regenerate 18:0/20:4-PI(4,5)P
2
[
71
]. Distinct DGK isoforms contribute differentially to this process: DGKε preferentially phosphorylates 18:0/20:4-DAG and thereby plays a critical role in sustaining the fidelity of the PI cycle, whereas other isoforms such as DGKδ and DGKζ provide bulk PA for de novo PI synthesis under conditions of high signaling demand [
12
,
72
,
73
]. Thus, DGKs not only terminate DAG signaling and generate PA as a second messenger, but also integrate into the PI cycle to ensure the maintenance of lipid species essential for receptor-mediated signaling.
Notably, the interconversion between DAG and PA is not unidirectional; phosphatidic acid phosphatases (PAPs) can dephosphorylate PA back into DAG, forming a feedback regulatory loop with DGKs [
74
]. Moreover, DAG can be produced by PLC through the hydrolysis of PI(4,5)P
2
, whereas PLD hydrolyzes phosphatidylcholine (PC) to generate PA, which can subsequently be converted into DAG by PAP [
1
]. This dynamic DAG–PA–PI cycle provides a highly flexible mechanism for fine-tuning receptor signaling and maintaining phosphoinositide homeostasis [
11
].
Functional roles of DGKs in cells
In recent years, significant progress has been made in elucidating the diverse cellular functions of DGKs. For instance, in T cells, DGKα rapidly depletes plasma DAG following TCR stimulation, thereby preventing sustained activation of the RasGRP1–Ras–ERK pathway, while simultaneously generating PA to promote mTORC1 assembly—together maintaining a balanced immune activation threshold [
75
,
76
]. Genetic deletion of DGKα or DGKζ in T cells results in hyperresponsiveness and increased IL-2 production, underscoring their role as negative regulators of TCR signaling [
77
].
In microglia, DGKβ suppresses oxidative burst during Fcγ receptor-mediated phagocytosis by inhibiting PKCβ activity [
78
]. This function protects neural tissue from excessive ROS damage during immune responses, suggesting DGKβ as a potential target in neuroinflammation and neurodegenerative disorders.
In cancer cells, DGKζ-generated PA activates mTORC1 signaling, enhancing lipid synthesis and cell growth [
79
]. Functional studies also show that loss of DGKε impairs PA-mediated phosphoinositide resynthesis, indicating a critical role in phosphoinositide turnover [
80
]. DGKδ regulates clathrin-mediated endocytosis [
81
]. More specifically, DGKδ-derived PA recruits AP-2 adaptor proteins to endocytic pits, thereby promoting vesicle formation and receptor internalization [
81
]. This function links DGKδ to growth factor receptor trafficking and signaling duration.
DGKθ has been identified as a downstream effector of RhoA. Its activity is negatively regulated by RhoA, and inhibition of DGKθ by RhoA leads to DAG accumulation and enhanced PKC signaling in response to extracellular cues [
82
]. Moreover, in hepatocytes, DGKθ integrates with bile acid signaling to modulate Akt activation and glucose homeostasis [
83
].
Interactions between DGKs and transcription factors
PPARs
Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPARs) are nuclear receptors central to lipid metabolism regulation and consist of three subtypes: PPARα, PPARγ, and PPARδ [
84
]. Among these, interactions between DGKs and PPARα or PPARγ have been extensively studied, revealing their roles in metabolic control, anti-inflammatory responses, and cardioprotection.
PPARα is a key transcription factor involved in fatty acid oxidation and mitochondrial function, with high expression in the liver, heart, and kidneys [
85
]. Recent studies have shown that the natural compound rutaecarpine activates the HNF1B–DGKθ–PPARα axis, thereby suppressing apolipoprotein C3 (APOC3) expression. This promotes lipid catabolism and reduces hepatic lipid accumulation, highlighting DGKθ as a critical upstream regulator of PPARα [
86
].
PPARγ is primarily expressed in adipose tissue, hematopoietic cells, and the colon [
87
]. PPARγ agonists, such as thiazolidinediones (TZDs) and 15d-prostaglandin J2 (15d-PGJ2), enhance DGKα activity via receptor-dependent mechanisms. The resulting conversion of DAG to PA suppresses the DAG–PKC signaling pathway. This mechanism effectively attenuates high-glucose-induced inflammatory responses in endothelial cells, including reduced adhesion molecule expression and monocyte adhesion, suggesting that the PPARγ–DGKα axis underpins PPARγ’s vascular anti-inflammatory effects [
88
].
In cardiomyocytes, both PPARγ and PPARα ligands—such as troglitazone and fenofibrate—activate DGKζ, thereby inhibiting the DAG-PKCε signaling cascade and preventing endothelin-1 (ET-1)-induced cardiac hypertrophy. Further studies reveal that DGKζ activation impedes ET-1-mediated nuclear export and phosphorylation of HDAC5, retaining HDAC5 within the nucleus and ultimately suppressing hypertrophic gene expression (e.g., BNP, β-MHC). These findings elucidate the cardioprotective role of the PPAR–DGKζ axis but also, for the first time, identify a nuclear function of DGKζ in epigenetic regulation via spatial control of HDAC5 during cardiac hypertrophy [
89
,
90
].
SREBP-1
Sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1 (SREBP-1) is a key transcription factor regulating fatty acid synthesis and lipogenesis [
91
]. Studies have shown that DGKζ regulates mTORC1 and lipogenic metabolism in cancer cells via SREBP-1 [
92
]. In adrenocortical cells, cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) signaling promotes the formation of a transcriptional complex between SF1 and SREBP1 and recruits it to the DGKθ promoter, thereby synergistically activating DGKθ transcription. This coupling links lipid metabolic control (via SREBP1) with steroidogenesis (via SF1), identifying DGKθ as a downstream effector of the cAMP-SF1/SREBP1 axis [
93
]. Knockout of DGKθ reduces DAG-to-PA conversion in hepatocytes, resulting in abnormal activation of the DAG-PKCε signaling pathway, which upregulates the expression of SREBP-1c and its downstream lipogenic genes (e.g., FAS, PPARγ) while suppressing genes involved in fatty acid oxidation (e.g., CPT1a). This ultimately promotes hepatic steatosis and insulin resistance [
94
].
FOXO
Forkhead box O (FOXO) proteins are a family of transcription factors in mammals, including FOXO1, FOXO3, FOXO4, and FOXO6. They play crucial roles in cellular responses to various stressors [
95
]. The DGKα gene contains binding sites for FOXO transcription factors [
96
]. Under conditions such as growth factor deprivation, FOXO factors bind directly to the DGKα promoter to upregulate its expression, enhancing DAG metabolism and inhibiting mitogenic signaling. This reveals DGKα as a key effector in FOXO-mediated cellular homeostasis and antitumor responses [
97
].
DGKζ inhibits FOXO transcriptional activity through a kinase-independent mechanism that depends on its nuclear localization. By doing so, it blocks the ubiquitin–proteasome system (UPS)-mediated protein degradation, promoting muscle hypertrophy during mechanical overload and mitigating muscle atrophy during denervation or fasting [
98
]. A recent study further showed that
Polygonatum cyrtonema
polysaccharides (PCP) upregulate DGKζ, and inhibit FOXO activity and its downstream target Atrogin1, thereby suppressing UPS-mediated protein degradation and alleviating chemotherapy (gemcitabine/cisplatin)-induced muscle wasting [
99
].
FXR
Farnesoid X receptor (FXR) is a bile acid sensor that regulates lipid metabolism, cholesterol efflux, and gut–liver axis homeostasis [
100
]. Bile acids activate FXR, which binds directly to the DGKθ promoter and enhances its expression, promoting the conversion of DAG to PA. This subsequently activates the mTORC2–Akt–FOXO1 signaling axis, inhibits hepatic gluconeogenesis, and improves glucose homeostasis, identifying DGKθ as a key effector in FXR-mediated metabolic regulation [
83
].
HIF-1
Hypoxia inducible factor 1 (HIF-1) is an oxygen-regulated transcriptional activator. DGK catalyzes the conversion of DAG to PA, which promotes hypoxia-dependent stabilization and transcriptional activity of the HIF-1α subunit (particularly through domains 530–582 and 775–826). This drives the expression of genes involved in hypoxic adaptation, identifying the DGK–PA signaling axis as a critical upstream mechanism for oxygen sensing and HIF-1 activation [
101
].

Impact of DGKs on diseases
DGKs regulate lipid signaling and are involved in the pathogenesis of multiple diseases. Isoform-specific functions confer both protective and pathogenic roles, making DGKs potential therapeutic targets.
Cancer
DGKs contribute to tumorigenesis both directly—by enhancing oncogenic signaling in tumor cells—and indirectly—by limiting antitumor immunity in the tumor microenvironment (TME).
Oncogenic and tumor-suppressive isoforms
DGKα is the best-characterized oncogenic isoform, promoting tumor proliferation, angiogenesis, and survival through pathways such as PKCζ–NF-κB in melanoma, Raf–MEK–ERK in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), PDE4A1–mTOR in glioblastoma, and cyclin D3 (CCND3) in lung cancer [
102
,
103
]. Additionally, DGKα facilitates migration in bladder cancer [
104
] and predicts recurrence in intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma [
105
]. It also promotes angiogenesis via HIF-1α activation and reduces ROS accumulation by inhibiting NOX4 through PA generation [
106
–
108
].
In contrast, DGKγ acts as a tumor suppressor in colorectal cancer by inhibiting Rac1-mediated invasion [
109
]. DGKη enhances tumor progression in NSCLC and gastric cancer via MAPK activation [
110
,
111
]. DGKζ promotes EMT in breast and cervical cancer but suppresses ERK3 in lung cancer [
112
–
115
]. DGKι may modulate tumorigenesis through epigenetic mechanisms [
116
].
Immunomodulatory functions in the TME
In T cells, DGKα and DGKζ act as negative regulators of T cell receptor (TCR) signaling by dampening the PLCγ–DAG–Ras–ERK cascade, thereby driving T cells into a hyporesponsive state [
113
,
117
,
118
]. Pharmacologic inhibition of DGKα enhances T cell cytotoxicity and synergizes with programmed death protein-1 (PD-1) blockade [
119
], while dual DGKα/ζ inhibitors restore TCR signaling and promote tumor regression [
120
,
121
].
Beyond T cells, DGKs regulate multiple immune components within the tumor microenvironment. DGKα-deficient macrophages exhibit heightened responses to lipopolysaccharide (LPS), IL-4, and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), indicating a regulatory role in macrophage polarization and activation [
122
]. In vascular endothelial cells, DGKγ is upregulated under hypoxic conditions, contributing to immune evasion and angiogenesis through the ZEB2–transforming growth factor beta-1 (TGF-β1) signaling axis [
123
].
Metabolic disorders
DGKs play isoform-specific roles in insulin sensitivity, glucose homeostasis, and lipid metabolism under conditions such as obesity and high-fat diet (HFD) exposure.
DAG–PKC signaling and insulin resistance
Increased intracellular DAG levels activate PKC isoforms, notably PKCε and PKCθ, leading to inhibitory phosphorylation of insulin receptor substrates and disruption of insulin signaling [
124
]. DGKδ, highly expressed in skeletal muscle, limits DAG accumulation and suppresses PKCε activity. Its deficiency leads to insulin resistance, impaired AMPK signaling, and metabolic dysregulation [
125
,
126
]. Overexpression of DGKδ confers protection against HFD-induced obesity by promoting metabolic adaptation in skeletal muscle and adipose tissue [
127
].
DGKε modulates tissue lipid composition via selective phosphorylation of polyunsaturated DAGs. In early HFD feeding, DGKε deficiency promotes adipocyte hypertrophy and inflammation through the TNF-α–PKCθ axis [
128
]. However, at later stages, it enhances fatty acid oxidation and mitochondrial function, resulting in improved glucose tolerance [
129
].
DGKζ-deficient mice display improved insulin signaling and energy metabolism, characterized by increased fatty acid oxidation and energy expenditure [
130
]. DGKα also exhibits protective roles by ameliorating diabetic nephropathy through vitamin E-dependent activation, preventing podocyte injury via DAG reduction [
131
].
Regulation of insulin receptor and lipid homeostasis
DGKδ interacts with β-arrestins to fine-tune insulin receptor signaling [
132
]. DGKε contributes to phosphoinositide recycling and energy metabolism [
133
]. Meanwhile, DGKζ deficiency enhances insulin signaling, improves insulin sensitivity, and promotes fatty acid oxidation and energy metabolism. [
134
].
Cardiovascular diseases
DGKs modulate cardiac and vascular homeostasis by regulating DAG–PKC signaling, thus influencing hypertrophy, fibrosis, and vascular function.
Cardiac remodeling and stress responses
In cardiomyocytes, excessive DAG activates PKC isoforms, promoting hypertrophy and apoptosis. DGKζ, the predominant cardiac isoform, attenuates Gq-coupled receptor agonist-induced hypertrophy, improves ventricular remodeling, and preserves cardiac function post-myocardial infarction and in diabetic cardiomyopathy models [
135
,
136
]. It also modulates Beclin1-dependent autophagy, contributing to hypertrophic regulation [
137
], and inhibits ET-1–PKC signaling to prevent pathological remodeling [
138
].
DGKε reduces DAG levels and PKC activation, thereby protecting against pressure overload-induced hypertrophy and fibrosis, improving survival and cardiac function through regulation of TRPC6 expression [
139
].
Additionally, DGKζ may alleviate doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity via cooperation with Hsp70 and E6AP, promoting p53 ubiquitination and degradation [
140
]. In contrast, DGKα exacerbates ischemia/reperfusion injury by inhibiting cardioprotective signaling via PKCε, ERK1/2, and p70S6K [
141
].
Vascular regulation and angiogenesis
In vascular smooth muscle cells, DGKε stabilizes actin stress fibers [
142
], and its deficiency impairs VEGFA signaling in endothelial cells, leading to thrombotic microangiopathy [
143
]. DGKγ, upregulated by IL-1β via p38 MAPK, is implicated in inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) protein transport in endothelial cells [
144
]. DGKα promotes VEGF-A165-induced proliferation and migration, highlighting its role in angiogenesis [
145
]. By modulating the DAG–PA axis, DGKs help reduce vascular hyperreactivity and thrombosis risk [
146
].
Neurological disorders
DGKs modulate synaptic function, neurodevelopment, excitability, and psychiatric pathophysiology. DGKα and DGKζ are involved in immune-mediated neuroinflammation. In experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE), they suppress DAG–PKCδ signaling, alleviating disease progression in models of multiple sclerosis [
147
].
DGKβ, enriched in the cortex and hippocampus, regulates dendritic spine morphology, synaptic plasticity, and cognitive function. Its deficiency leads to impaired learning and schizophrenia-like behaviors [
148
–
150
].
DGKγ supports neuronal morphology and motor coordination; its loss impairs dendritic development and induces motor deficits [
151
,
152
]. DGKη, identified via GWAS as a susceptibility gene for bipolar disorder, influences mood regulation, with knockout models showing mania-like phenotypes [
153
,
154
].
DGKκ is downregulated in fragile X syndrome (FXS), contributing to aberrant lipid signaling and spine morphology [
155
,
156
]. DGKε is involved in epilepsy and neurodegeneration. It regulates seizure susceptibility via long-term potentiation (LTP) modulation and may alleviate mutant huntingtin toxicity in Huntington’s disease [
157
–

DGKζ interacts with PSD-95 to regulate spine density and synaptic plasticity; its deletion increases seizure susceptibility [

162

163
]. DGKα and DGKζ nuclear localization in retinal neurons suggests roles in visual signal integration [

Therapeutic potential of DGKs
With the increasing recognition of DGKs as key modulators of lipid signaling, inflammation, and cellular metabolism, therapeutic targeting of DGK isoforms has attracted growing interest across fields such as oncology, immunotherapy, and metabolic diseases. Current efforts have largely focused on the development of small-molecule inhibitors, particularly against DGKα and DGKζ, with several candidates demonstrating promising preclinical efficacy and even progressing into early-phase clinical trials. These compounds aim to rebalance aberrant DAG–PA signaling by either restoring DAG-mediated immune activation or suppressing oncogenic PA-dependent pathways. Unlike the substantial progress made in DGK inhibitor development, the exploration of isoform-specific agonists is still in its early stages. Furthermore, combination strategies involving DGK inhibitors and immune checkpoint blockade have shown synergistic potential in tumor models, suggesting that DGK modulation may serve as a powerful adjuvant to existing immunotherapies. Collectively, these developments reflect a shift from purely mechanistic investigation toward translational applications, yet also highlight the ongoing need for isoform-selective tools, tissue-targeted delivery systems, and a deeper understanding of DGK context-specific roles to fully harness their therapeutic potential.
Inhibitors
Research on isoform-selective DGK inhibitors is advancing rapidly, particularly for DGKα and DGKζ, where significant breakthroughs have been made. These inhibitors show potential not only in anticancer therapy but also in immune modulation and metabolic disease treatment. Below, we summarize the mechanism of action, potency (50% inhibition concentration, IC
50
), selectivity, and biological effects of key DGK inhibitors.
R59022
R59022
is one of the earliest and most classic DGK inhibitors developed for functional research [
165
]. Initially thought to primarily inhibit DGKα, it has been widely used to investigate DGK roles in immune regulation, lipid metabolism, and signal transduction [
166
]. It inhibits the 80-kDa DGK isoform with an IC
50
of 10 μM [
167
]. Although its precise binding mode remains undefined, current evidence indicates that
R59022
interferes with DGK activity by altering the intracellular DAG:PA balance rather than directly competing at the ATP pocket, thereby providing indirect mechanistic insights. This explains its ability to suppress airway smooth muscle contraction [
168
]. It alleviates inflammation mediated by conjugated linoleic acid in human adipocytes [
169
] and enhances the oxidative burst response of neutrophils via PKC activation [
170
]. However,
R59022
faces several limitations, such as low selectivity, poor membrane permeability, metabolic instability, and off-target effects, which restrict its clinical applicability [
171
,
172
]. Therefore,
R59022
is more suitable as a pharmacological tool in basic research, laying the groundwork for the development of highly selective and drug-like DGK inhibitors.
R59949
R59949
, a structural analog of
R59022
and member of the indolylmaleimide class, is a dual inhibitor of DGKα and DGKζ, widely used in studies of DGKζ function [
173
]. It exhibits a submicromolar potency with an IC₅₀ of 0.3 μM in platelet and erythrocyte DGK assays [
174
]. It elevates intracellular DAG, activates PKC signaling, and regulates cellular processes such as activation, differentiation, and apoptosis [
175
,
176
]. It also reduces inducible nitric oxide (NO) production by inhibiting
l
-arginine transport across membranes [
177
]. In fibrosis-related research,
R59949
has been shown to upregulate α-SMA expression and promote PKCδ and Smad2 phosphorylation, suggesting a potential role in TGF-β signaling modulation [
178
]. Despite its wide use,
R59949
shares pharmacological limitations with
R59022
, such as low selectivity, poor bioavailability, and metabolic instability. Like
R59022
, it is better suited for in vitro mechanistic studies rather than as a clinical drug candidate.
Ritanserin
Initially developed as a 5-HT 2A/2C receptor antagonist for the treatment of depression and anxiety disorders [
179
], ritanserin was later identified as a selective inhibitor of DGKα, expanding its potential applications to oncology and metabolic diseases [
119
]. Structural and biochemical studies have demonstrated that ritanserin binds to the C1 domain of DGKα—an allosteric site distinct from the ATP pocket—thereby stabilizing an inactive enzyme conformation and inhibiting its catalytic activity [
28
,
180
]. With an IC
50
of approximately 27.4 μM, it exhibits modest selectivity for DGKα over other isoforms such as DGKβ, γ, and ζ [
171
]. In T cells, ritanserin enhances TCR signaling via ERK and PKC activation, contributing to the functional restoration of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes and potentially augmenting the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors such as anti-PD-1 therapies [
119
,
181
]. In acute myeloid leukemia (AML), ritanserin disrupts the PLD-SphK1 axis by inhibiting DGKα, subsequently suppressing the Janus kinase–signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK–STAT) and MAPK pathways, and exerting antitumor effects [
182
]. Additionally, it has demonstrated immunoregulatory potential in X-linked lymphoproliferative disease type 1 (XLP-1) by restoring restimulation-induced cell death (RICD) in SAP-deficient lymphocytes [
174
]. Although clinical trial data supporting its use as an anticancer agent are currently lacking, ritanserin represents a promising case of drug repurposing and serves as a valuable pharmacological tool in the development of more selective DGKα inhibitors.
CU-3
CU-3 is a small-molecule compound that exhibits high selectivity for DGKα. It acts by competitively inhibiting ATP binding to the catalytic domain of DGKα, rather than its regulatory domains [
172
]. It demonstrates IC₅₀ values of 0.6 μM in biochemical assays [
172
]. In vitro studies have shown that CU-3 induces apoptosis in hepatocellular carcinoma (HepG2) and cervical cancer (HeLa) cells, and enhances IL-2 production in Jurkat T cells, indicating dual antitumor and immunostimulatory activities [
172
,
183
].
JNJ-3790339
To improve the selectivity and efficacy of DGKα inhibitors, structural modifications of ritanserin led to the identification of JNJ-3790339. It exhibits IC₅₀ values of 9.6 μM for DGKα [
180
]. This compound displays cytotoxic effects comparable to ritanserin in glioblastoma and melanoma cells and elicits more robust T cell activation, underscoring its potential as a DGKα-selective inhibitor [
180
].
AMB639752
AMB639752 was initially identified via virtual screening among 127 candidate compounds and exhibits high selectivity for DGKα, outperforming conventional inhibitors such as
R59022
and
R59949
. Unlike certain DGK inhibitors (e.g., ritanserin), AMB639752 does not affect serotonin receptors, indicating greater target specificity and reduced off-target risks [
184
]. It shows IC₅₀ values of 6.9 μM against DGKα [
185
]. Based on its structure, several analogs were synthesized, among which compounds 11 and 20 demonstrated enhanced DGKα inhibition while maintaining low affinity for serotonin receptors. These compounds were shown to restore apoptosis and reduce cancer cell migration in cellular models of X-linked lymphoproliferative disease, suggesting their potential to prevent metastasis [
185
].
ASP1570
ASP1570 is the first DGKζ-selective inhibitor to advance into clinical evaluation, marking a significant step toward the translational application of DGKζ-targeted therapies. It demonstrates IC₅₀ values of 4.7 nM (human DGKζ) and 3.0 nM (mouse DGKζ) [
186
]. It competitively binds the ATP-binding site of DGKζ, effectively inhibiting the conversion of DAG to PA and thereby enhancing DAG-mediated signaling downstream of the TCR [
187
]. ASP1570 demonstrates high isoform selectivity in various in vitro models, with minimal off-target effects on DGKα and other isoforms. In tumor immunology, ASP1570 has been shown to reverse T cell exhaustion within the tumor microenvironment, promote antigen-specific CD8
+
T cell infiltration, and enhance cytotoxic activity against solid tumors. Notably, it exhibits synergistic antitumor efficacy when combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors such as anti-PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies [
187
]. ASP1570 also enhances interferon-γ (IFN-γ) production and degranulation in activated NK cells in vitro and promotes NK cell-mediated tumor clearance in vivo [
188
]. Moreover, it restores T cell functionality suppressed by a variety of inhibitory signals including TGF-β, prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), adenosine, PD-1, cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4), and T cell immunoreceptor with Ig and ITIM domains (TIGIT), and has been shown to suppress tumor growth in two syngeneic murine models [
187
]. Phase I clinical trials have demonstrated favorable oral bioavailability and tolerability, and phase II studies are currently underway to evaluate its therapeutic efficacy in solid tumor immunotherapy [
189
,
190
].
BAY2965501
BAY2965501 is a highly selective, orally bioavailable DGKζ inhibitor developed by Bayer, designed to enhance T cell-mediated anti-tumor immune responses. It exhibits an IC₅₀ of 76.09 nM (0.076 μM) against DGKζ [
191
]. It is currently being evaluated in a first-in-human clinical trial for patients with advanced solid tumors (
NCT05614102
). Preliminary studies suggest that BAY2965501, in combination with anti-PD-L1 antibodies, reduces tumor growth more effectively than anti-PD-L1 monotherapy [
192
]. As the first DGKζ inhibitor to enter clinical trials, BAY2965501 represents a novel immunotherapeutic strategy that may offer new treatment options for a range of refractory cancers.
BMS-502 and BMS-684
BMS-502 is a dual inhibitor of DGKα and DGKζ, identified through phenotypic screening. It promotes cytokine release and T cell proliferation in both human and murine cells, with reported IC₅₀ values of 0.0046 μM for DGKα and 0.0021 μM for DGKζ [
193

193

Compound A
Compound A is a potent and selective inhibitor of type I DGKs, including DGKα, DGKβ, and DGKγ, with IC₅₀ values of 0.04 μM, 0.02 μM, and 0.01 μM [
194
]. It induces apoptosis in A375 melanoma cells, reduces cell viability, and enhances IL-2 production by T cells, indicating its potential for augmenting anticancer immune responses [

Stemphone
Stemphone is a natural compound isolated from the fungus
Drechslera saccharin

Agonists
To date, the majority of DGK-targeted interventions have focused on inhibitor development, particularly in the context of cancer immunotherapy, where notable progress has been made. In contrast, the exploration of DGK agonists remains limited and is still in the early phases of screening and mechanistic studies.
KU-8
KU-8 is a compound identified through high-throughput screening, characterized by its unique dual activity. It activates DGKα and DGKθ by approximately 20% while exhibiting strong inhibitory activity against DGKκ [
198
]. This dual regulatory capability makes KU-8 a valuable tool for studying DGK isoform functions and developing isoform-specific therapeutic strategies.
EGCG
Epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), a major green tea polyphenol, has been identified as a functional activator of DGKα. In diabetic nephropathy models, EGCG ameliorates albuminuria through DGKα-dependent mechanisms, including c-Src–mediated membrane translocation and interaction with the 67-kDa laminin receptor [
199
]. This activation stabilizes podocyte focal adhesions under high-glucose conditions, preserving glomerular integrity. Notably, methylated EGCG with higher bioavailability showed enhanced therapeutic efficacy.
α-Tocopherol
α-Tocopherol (αToc), long regarded as the principal antioxidant in biological membranes, has recently been shown to activate DGKα through a nonantioxidant mechanism [
200
,
201
]. Recent studies identified the 67-kDa laminin receptor (67LR) as a membrane receptor for αToc, where αToc binds a specific hydrophobic pocket distinct from the EGCG-binding site [
200
]. This interaction promotes DGKα activation and improves diabetic nephropathy in vivo, revealing a novel signaling pathway for αToc beyond its classical antioxidant role [
202
].
Combination strategies: synergistic therapeutic applications of DGK modulation
Accumulating evidence from preclinical tumor models supports the concept that DGK inhibition can serve as a powerful adjunct to existing therapies by amplifying immune responses and sensitizing tumors to treatment. Rather than acting as a standalone intervention, DGK-targeted agents appear most effective when integrated into combination regimens that engage parallel or complementary pathways.
One of the most well-substantiated strategies involves the co-administration of DGKα or DGKζ inhibitors with immune checkpoint blockade (ICB). In multiple murine models, pharmacological inhibition of DGKα—either through small molecules such as ritanserin or DGKα inhibitor—potentiated anti-PD-1 or anti-PD-L1 therapy, resulting in enhanced CD8
+
T cell infiltration, increased granzyme B and IFN-γ production, and durable tumor regression [
203
]. Similar findings were reported with dual DGKα/ζ inhibitors, which amplified TCR signaling even under low-affinity antigen stimulation and improved tumor control when combined with PD-1 blockade [
120
]. Importantly, these synergistic effects are not merely additive; they reflect a convergence on immune reactivation. While checkpoint inhibitors relieve extrinsic T cell suppression, DGK inhibitors act intracellularly to lower the activation threshold of effector T cells. This dual mechanism offers a rationale for using DGK modulation as a sensitizing strategy, particularly in tumors characterized by low neoantigen burden or T cell dysfunction.
Although clinical translation is still in its early stages, the favorable immune potentiation profile of DGK inhibitors has prompted the development of isoform-selective compounds such as BAY2965501, which are now entering early-phase trials [
186
]. Further, the use of nanocarrier systems or antibody-directed delivery may enhance the tissue specificity of DGK inhibitors, reducing systemic toxicity in combination regimens.
Challenges and translational pathways
Barriers to clinical translation
Despite the emerging role of DGKs as critical regulators and promising therapeutic targets in various major diseases, their clinical translation remains relatively limited. Two primary obstacles are currently recognized. First, owing to the high structural conservation of the catalytic domains across the ten DGK isoforms, existing small-molecule inhibitors generally lack sufficient isoform selectivity, increasing the risk of off-target effects and limiting precision application in specific diseases. Second, the widespread tissue expression of DGKs raises concerns about systemic interventions disrupting nontarget tissues, thereby increasing the risk of adverse effects. Currently, mature tissue-specific delivery systems are still lacking.
Additionally, functional evaluation of DGKs remains reliant on traditional methods such as radiolabeling and thin-layer chromatography (TLC), which are inadequate for modern high-throughput and dynamic drug screening requirements. On the clinical validation front, apart from the DGKζ inhibitors ASP1570 and BAY2965501 entering phase I trials, most candidate compounds remain at the cellular or animal research stage, with limited clinical evidence to support their use.
Opportunities for technological innovation
To address these bottlenecks, researchers are pursuing breakthroughs through structural analysis, drug screening, and delivery optimization. On one hand, cryo-electron microscopy (Cryo-EM) and artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted modeling are accelerating the resolution of DGK isoform structures, providing a solid foundation for designing highly selective ligands. Meanwhile, high-throughput activity assays based on ATP consumption and chemiluminescence are improving screening efficiency and accuracy.
On the other hand, advances in tissue-targeted delivery technologies—such as engineered adeno-associated virus (AAV) capsids, lipid nanoparticles (LNPs), and targeting peptide systems—hold promise for achieving precise delivery of DGK modulators to T cells, the liver, or tumor microenvironments, thereby reducing systemic toxicity. Overall, DGK-targeted drug development is currently transitioning from target validation to therapeutic strategy optimization. Future efforts should focus on integrating mechanistic research with pharmacological evaluation to accelerate precision medicine applications for metabolic diseases, malignancies, and immune dysregulation disorders.
Reframing DGKs as contextual metabolic switches
Beyond their canonical enzymatic role in regulating the balance between DAG and PA, DGKs are increasingly recognized as dynamic integrators of cellular context. Rather than acting solely as metabolic “valves,” DGKs may operate as context-sensitive metabolic switches, adjusting cellular signaling landscapes in response to pathophysiological stressors such as inflammation, hypoxia, or nutrient excess. This reconceptualization positions DGKs not only as regulators of lipid second messengers but also as transducers of metabolic state into transcriptional and epigenetic outcomes.
A notable example lies in DGKζ, which translocates to the nucleus under defined stimuli and engages with chromatin-associated proteins such as HDAC5 and FOXO transcription factors. These interactions imply that certain DGK isoforms contribute to gene–environment crosstalk, potentially influencing chromatin accessibility and the persistence of stress-induced transcriptional programs—mechanisms that may underlie metabolic memory in chronic diseases.
In parallel, DGKα and DGKθ display bifunctional roles in immune and metabolic compartments, modulating both mTOR and Ras–ERK signaling depending on tissue type and activation state. This duality highlights their position as bimodal regulatory nodes that interlink metabolic adaptation with immune responsiveness.
To facilitate therapeutic translation, we propose a functional taxonomy of DGK isoforms into disease-relevant modules: tumor-permissive (e.g., DGKα and DGKη), immunosuppressive (e.g., DGKζ), and metabolic-sensing (e.g., DGKε and DGKθ) subtypes. Each group presents distinct therapeutic opportunities and challenges, depending on their expression pattern, cellular localization, and network connectivity.
Moving forward, high-resolution approaches such as single-cell transcriptomics, spatial proteomics, and mass-spectrometry-based lipidomics will be essential for delineating the spatiotemporal activity of DGKs in situ. These integrative tools will not only enhance our mechanistic understanding but also inform the design of precision therapies targeting DGKs in context-specific disease niches.
Conclusions
DGKs play a pivotal role in integrating metabolism and signaling by regulating the DAG–PA axis. Isoform-specific structures and subcellular localization endow DGKs with highly diverse functions. Recent studies have confirmed their critical roles in metabolic disorders, tumor progression, immune regulation, as well as cardiovascular and neurological diseases, thus forming a potential “lipid signaling–pathophysiological regulation–targeted intervention” axis. While DGKα and ζ contribute to tumorigenesis and immune suppression, DGKε and θ demonstrate therapeutic potential in lipid modulation, insulin sensitivity, and cardioprotection.
Although DGKζ inhibitors such as ASP1570 have advanced into clinical trials, most other compounds remain in the preclinical stage. The development of targeted interventions is still hindered by low isoform selectivity, high systemic toxicity, and inadequate pharmacodynamic evaluation. Future studies should leverage cryo-EM, AI-guided screening, and tissue-specific delivery platforms to bridge the gap between mechanistic insights and clinical application. As key regulatory nodes at the intersection of metabolism, immunity, and cancer, DGKs offer exciting opportunities for developing novel therapeutic strategies and precision medicine solutions for a broad spectrum of refractory diseases.
Importantly, DGKs should be redefined not merely as enzymatic modulators of DAG–PA balance but as context-dependent metabolic switches that integrate external cues with intracellular signaling, transcriptional regulation, and metabolic reprogramming. This reconceptualization offers a framework for rationally designing DGK-targeted interventions tailored to disease-specific microenvironments. Overall, continued mechanistic exploration combined with translational innovation holds promise for leveraging DGKs as strategic nodes in precision therapies for cancer, metabolic disorders, immune dysfunction, and beyond.
