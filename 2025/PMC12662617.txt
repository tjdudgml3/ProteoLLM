Introduction
Noncommunicable diseases
Noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) are still the most important public health concerns across the globe, causing death as well as morbidity. The key factors for the transition away from a largely communicable diseases scenario include changing demographics environmental and economic changes.[
1
]
NCDs are those which do not have a short-term onset, have long-term consequences for health, and therefore need ongoing care and treatment. Cancers, diabetes, cardiovascular disease (CVD), as well as chronic lung diseases, are among these ailments. CVD, cancers, diabetes, as well as chronic lung diseases are among these ailments.[
2
]
NCDs are becoming increasingly common. NCDs claimed the lives of 17.9 million people in 2021, accounting for 60% of all deaths. The majority of these fatalities occur in poor and middle-income nations, with 16 million people under the age of 70 dying. NCD deaths are anticipated to climb by an additional 17% in the next 10 years.[
1
]
In 2019, CVDs took the lives of 17.9 million people in the globe, representing 32% of all deaths. Eighty-five percent of the deaths were caused by heart disease and strokes.[
1
] CVDs, which were formerly identified with developed nations, are now growing or fast expanding in poor countries. Indeed, developing nations accounted for 86% of DALYs caused by CVDs in 1998, and CVDs were responsible for a third of worldwide mortality in 1999, with 78% occurring in low- and middle-income nations. As a result of lifestyle changes brought about by economic development and urbanization in emerging nations undergoing socioeconomic transition, CVDs are expected to become the major cause of death in developing countries by 2010.[
1
] Patients with renal impairment are at a much-increased risk of developing acute heart failure (HF). It has been shown that heart disorders account for 43.6% of all fatalities in chronic kidney disease (CKD) patients. Patients with CKD are predicted to have a 10–20 times higher risk of dying from different cardiac illnesses than those in comparable categories in the general population.[
3
]
Cardiovascular diseases definition and etiology
The circulatory system consists of the heart as well as its blood vessels. Anomalies of the endocarditis, conduction system, and rheumatic heart disease are just a few of the potential problems that might arise in the heart. Atherosclerosis of the heart, which is sometimes called aortic atherosclerosis, is a kind of cardiac disease that affects the heart and blood vessels in the legs and arms. Coronary artery disease is also called as coronary heart disease (also known as heart disease).[
4
] Regardless of the fact that CVD can be caused by a variety of etiologies, such as emboli in a patient with atrial fibrillation causing ischemic stroke or rheumatic fever starting to cause valvular CVD, addressing atherosclerosis risk factors is crucial because it is a common denominator in the pathophysiology of cardiovascular events.[
5
]
Cardiorenal syndrome definition and etiology
Cardiorenal syndrome (CRS) is defined as HF that leads to renal impairment and vice versa. CRS is a poor prognostic sign because it increases morbidity and mortality in individuals who have HF as well as CKD or acute kidney injury (AKI) at the same time.[
6
]
Heart and function of kidney are inextricably linked, and interaction between the two organs takes place through a number of bidirectional channels. The severity of the failing organ might trigger a variety of complicated metabolic, cell-mediated, and humoral processes that impact other organs, resulting in high treatment costs and much increased morbidity and death.[
7
] As a framework for investigating the pathophysiology of both cardiac and renal dysfunction, this condition is known as CRS. Both CRS type 1 and CRS type 3 are particularly important in cardiorenal crosstalk in high-acuity medical institutions, where they are often encountered in the coronary care unit and critical care unit of a hospital; CRS type 1 tends to occur in these areas.[
8
]
Crosstalk between organs
The intricate biologic interaction and response among distant organs facilitated by molecular, cellular, neuronal, paracrine, and endocrine processes is known as organ crosstalk. This crosstalk helps the human body maintain homeostasis and optimum functioning in its natural condition. During disease situations, however, this crosstalk may transmit the sick organ’s effect across to the other organs, causing structural and functional failure. Heart and kidney function are inextricably linked, and communication between the two organs takes place through a number of bidirectional channels.[

] CRS type 1 is distinguished by a fast deterioration of cardiac function that leads to severe renal damage. This kind of CRS is defined by increases in serum creatinine (sCr) of 25%–50% from baseline in the range of 0.1–0.5 mg/dl.[

Biomarkers
The National Institutes of Health defines a biomarker as a “characteristic that is objectively measured and assessed as an indication of normal biochemical functions, pathogenic processes, or pharmacological reactions to a therapeutic intervention,” according to their definition. This wide definition encompasses data acquired from analyzing a person’s transcriptome, genome, metabolome, proteome, subclinical illness indicators, as well as metabolic end products.[
10
]
Acute-phase reagents like serum amyloid A and P element, coagulation-related proteins like plasminogen activator-inhibitor-1 as well as D-dimer, proinflammatory cytokines like interleukin (IL)-6 and IL-18, phospholipases like type II secreted phospholipase A2, as well as oxidative stress markers like type II secretory phospholipase A2. BNP and NT proBNP are cardiac stress markers, although not cardiac-specific. Both BNP and N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) have similar clinical usefulness and may be used to rule out acute HF. These indicators help to stratify risk in patients before they leave the hospital.[
11
]
The National Institute of Clinical Biochem Laboratory Medicine Practices Guidelines council has chosen a selection of indicators that seem to be the most potential for future research due to the enormous number of developing candidate blood biomarkers in the domain of primary prevention of CVD: lipoprotein subtypes, particle density, h-sensitive C-reactive protein (CRP), lipoprotein-associated phospholipase A2, high fibrinogen, as well as the colocalization of leukocytes with lipoproteins. These are only a few of the many prospective options, and even for the majority of these markers, their clinical value in all areas of CVD and type 2 diabetes (T2D) has yet to be completely demonstrated.[
12
]
Genomics
It is possible to genotype 2.5 million single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in persons with and without a disease of interest to identify SNPs that are associated with an underlying health issue. This method reflects an unbiased scan of genetic variants and disease risk, as opposed to candidate biomarker-based investigations.[
13
]
Transcriptomics
The transcriptome is the biological level closest to an individual’s phenotype. Gene expression is a quantitative feature that is both highly heritable and influenced by environmental factors. Despite the fact that protein-coding genes account for just 25% of the human genome, early data suggest that the bulk of the genome produces RNA transcripts. Messenger RNAs and microRNAs have been studied extensively as possible biomarkers for a variety of illnesses, including cancer, cardiometabolic, autoimmune, and inflammatory disorders.[
14
]
Proteomics
Proteomics studies try to quantify the whole collection of proteins in biological samples such as urine, plasma/serum cerebrospinal fluid, and saliva, at the same time. Because proteins as well as its biological functions are significant drivers of the range of phenotypes that might appear from a common set of genes, proteomics may outperform genomics. Because of their complexity, serum and plasma stand out among the sample types of interest. Proteomics-related techniques based on samples of serum or plasma face a significant hurdle as a result of this.[
15
]
Metabolomics
The metabolome (defined as all small-molecule metabolites detected in or created in an organism) has the least number of biomarkers compared to the genome, transcriptome, and proteome. Lipids, sugars, nucleotides, amino acids, organic acids, and a variety of other low-molecular-weight molecules are among them. The most often used techniques are nuclear magnetic resonance and several kinds of mass spectrometry.[
16
]
Transcriptomics studies to discover novel T2D and CVD prediction biomarkers might benefit greatly from biopsy samples taken from liver, muscle, cells, adipose tissue, myocardium, as well as the vascular system.[
12
]
Significance of blood plasma-based biomarkers
A biomarker for a CVD should, in theory, improve the clinician’s capacity to manage the patient effectively. Several studies have suggested a range of protein biomarkers for CVD, despite the fact that proteomics methods can detect and quantify hundreds of proteins. Only a few plasma or serum biomarkers have been effectively integrated into cardiology practise, with the majority of these being diagnostic markers. For example, the myocardium-specific structural proteins troponin I and troponin T are used to diagnose myocardial damage,[
17
] as well as B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP), a hormone secreted from the ventricles during myocardial stress and used to diagnose and treat acute congestive HF. They provide the foundation for myocardial infarction and myocardial stress diagnosis. Furthermore, when assessed using high-sensitivity assays, these markers have been shown to have the potential to be risk predictors. Indeed, multiple investigations have shown that there is a risk gradient correlating to an increased risk of an adverse cardiac event with higher troponin levels within the reference level 6, 7, and 8. However, none of the commonly utilized CVD protein biomarkers were discovered in proteomics discovery trials at first.[

Cardiorenal disease
For a long time, CRS was thought to be a chronic or acute renal disfunction caused by basic alterations in heart event. A consensus description of CRS has recently been proposed,[

Classification
Chan
et al

The major failure of organ, whether heart (CRSs) or kidney (renocardiac syndromes), and duration of disease (chronic or acute) are used to divide the clinical categorization into four subcategories (CRS 1-4). CRSs type 5 refers to a systemic condition (for example, sepsis) that causes cardiorenal dysfunction.[

Cardiorenal syndrome type 1 (acute cardiorenal) and cardiorenal syndrome type 3 (renocardiac syndrome)
Acute CRS (CRS type 1): Acute cardiac failure, also known as acute decompensated HF (ADHF), occurs as a result of both nonischemic as well as ischemic cardiac events. AKI affects 25%–40% patients of ADHF who are admitted to the hospital. Even though current knowledge of the pathophysiology of type 1 CRS has improved over the past decade, the illness’s mortality and morbidity remain high, and timely disease detection remains a clinical requirement[
21
]
Acute renocardiac syndrome (CRS type 3): About 15% of patients of AKI die from heart disease. AKI is associated with left ventricular dysfunction, cardiac fibrosis, and coronary artery disease (CAD) in several patients. There is a scarcity of evidence on the incidence of events of acute cardiac after an AKI episode. The pathophysiologic pathways that underlie type 3 CRS are unclear[
22
]
Cardiorenal syndrome type 2 (chronic cardiorenal) and cardiorenal syndrome type 4 (renocardiac syndrome)
Chronic CRS (CRS type 2): CKD develops in around 45%–60% of chronic HF (CHF) patients. Type 2 CRS is characterized by cyanotic nephropathy in patients with congenital cardiac disease. However, CKD and CHF often overlap; it is tough to predict which of 2 chronic illnesses has formed[
23
]
Chronic renocardiac syndrome (CRS type 4): Myocardial calcification, atherosclerotic coronary disease, coronary calcification, acute coronary syndrome, left ventricular systolic and cardiac fibrosis and hypertrophy, diastolic dysfunction as well as sudden death are all linked to the late CKD stages. As the prevalence of CKD rises, type 4 CRS is now becoming a serious health issue with significant mortality and morbidity. CKD patients have a 15–20 time’s greater risk of cardiac death as compared to healthy individuals.[
24
]
Heart–kidney crosstalk
CRS type 1 is most often associated with acute decompensate HF and occurs in the aftermath of ischemic (myocardial infarction and cardiac surgery) or nonischemic (valve malfunction, pulmonary embolism, and aortic dissection) cardiac occurrences.[
25
] Any of these clinical disorders might decrease kidney function, which is linked to longer hospital stays, higher morbidity, and higher mortality. Reduced baseline renal function, diabetes, and past bouts of HF are all risk factors for this condition.[
26
] Renal arterial underfilling as well as increased venous congestion are predicted consequences of acute HF (AHF), especially in the setting of systolic dysfunction and reduced cardiac output.[
27
] In the context of AHF, reduced kidney perfusion overactivates the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone pathway, encouraging water and salt retention, contributing to systemic and kidney hypertension, as well as endothelial and glomerular damage (Cruz DN 2013). In addition, profibrotic and proinflammatory characteristics of angiotensin II and aldosterone lead to kidney injury, which is particularly prominent in CRS type 2.[
23
]
Hyponatremia is a common complication of AHF and is an independent predictor of death and decompensation hospitalization. Hyponatremia caused by HF is largely hypervolemic and a sign of congestion, that is a major contributor to AKI. Hyponatremia in these individuals may be used as an indicator of overall neurohumoral activation, which includes nonosmotic vasopressin production and increased levels of plasma renin, aldosterone, angiotensin II, and catecholamines. By disrupting systemic and renal hemodynamics, certain routinely recommended medicines for the treatment of AHF may also contribute to the development of AKI.[

Significance
Although there has been substantial progress in biomarker development for the diagnosis and prognosis of CRS in the last decade, more work is needed before novel biomarkers can be translated into therapeutic application. A clinically relevant biomarker is one that can be evaluated quickly and cheaply and may give data in adding up to medical evaluation to impact medical decisions. BNP, creatinine, NT-proBNP, as well as cTns, have all been shown to be useful in detecting cardiac and renal problems. Newer AKI biomarkers, such as NGAL, KIM-1, and cystatin C, need more well-constructed, appropriately operated experimental trials which focus on the test technique. In the general population, levels of NT-proBNP and BNP have been proven to predict mortality and cardiac outcomes. Values of NT-proBNP and BNP may be used in the future to examine patients with valvular heart disease, chemotherapy patients at risk of drug-induced cardiotoxicity, preoperative examinations, and screening of asymptomatic persons for heart disease, such as preparticipation screening of sports.[

MicroRNAs for cardiorenal syndrome: The future is now
CRS was important in the gradual acceptance of microRNAs (miRNAs) in clinical practice. Genetic code encodes noncoding RNA molecules called microRNAs, which may vary in length between 18 and 25 nucleotides and therefore have no effect on protein synthesis. The UTR (3’-untranslated region) or CDS (coding sequence) of gene expression is where they affect posttranscriptional activity. MicroRNAs have been shown to have a positive or negative role in processes involving atherosclerosis as well as arterial remodeling, including monocyte activation, endothelial dysfunction and migration in the arterial wall, and plaque formation.[

Genes involved in cardiorenal disease
Epigenetic changes are thought to occur when CVD and CKD progress, according to new studies. In a utero environment programming has a major impact on mitochondrial dysfunction, smoking, nephron numbers, and hypertension, among other factors. Most of these alterations are directly implicated in the establishment of the five forms of CRS although the impact of methylation, RNA interference, and histone modification on CRS remains unknown. There have been some new discoveries in the field of cardiorenal physiopathology. During renal damage, histone H3 epigenetics are heavily acetylated, dimethylated, and phosphorylated. Conclusion
Significantly contributing to morbidity are CVD disorders, which are also on the rise and resulting in additional problems such as kidney failure (AKI or CKD), diabetes type 2, obesity, thyroid, and other issues. Someone’s age, gender, ethnicity, genetic composition, and other sub-phenotypes, as well as any self-induced clinical difficulties, form the basis of a customized diagnostic and therapeutic plan. Unbiased methods provide the necessary information to build a strategy for finding novel biomarkers with practical applications. It is possible to identify potential biomarkers for predicting risks or to employ them as preventative signatures for the early detection of problems involving several organs. Algorithm modeling can also help validate biomarkers for (a) novel therapy targets, (b) medical treatments, and (c) provide accurate guidance for precision medicine decisions concerning CVD patients and renal complications’ risk prediction. As of right now, no customized algorithm combining studies on cardiovascular and renal biomarkers has been created for the at-risk or sick population.
Because of the multiple organ dysfunction, the treatment is further complicated by the organ’s crosstalk between the kidney and the heart. Both CRD and non-CRD patients experience complicated, multifaceted pathophysiology. Patients experience a range of secondary clinical consequences over time, including blood pressure fluctuations, dehydration, strokes, kidney failure, and bone deterioration.
