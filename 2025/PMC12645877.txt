1.
Introduction
Cancer is a global health concern. According to the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), there were around 20 million new cases of cancer in 2022, and it is predicted to reach 35 million (a 77% increase) by 2050 [
1
]. Cancer is one of the top three mortality causes in 177 countries at age 30–70 years [
2
]. It has overtaken cardiovascular disease as the number one cause of death in a high-income country [
2
,
3
]. It is estimated that 1 in 5 individuals develop cancer in their lifetime, and 1 in 9 men and 1 in 12 women die from it [
1
]. With this growing burden, a cancer control strategy is crucial.
Given the complexity of the disease, different modalities have been developed for cancer treatment. Chemotherapy was created in the early 1900s and has been used as the most common cancer treatment until now [
4
]. In 1960, surgery and radiotherapy were the primary treatments, especially for solid cancers. Later on, the combination of different therapies was utilized to overcome relapse and micrometastases [
4
,
5
]. The advancement of molecular biology and genomic technology led to improved targeted chemotherapy for specific molecular targets in 1990 [
5
]. Since then, targeted or personalized therapy has been developed and has shown promising results in various cancers.
Related to the development of targeted therapy, cancer research has evolved around two main topics: identifying the tumor (1) molecular profiling, and (2) molecular target [
6
]. Identifying the mutation profile of a particular cancer is the first step in discovering a target and subsequent therapy. The molecular profile or target can be cancer genomic, epigenetic, or microenvironmental. This review systematically examines the role of hydrogel platforms in the initial stages of molecular biomarker profiling, which is used for cancer diagnosis.
Hydrogel, a polymer multinetwork, has been utilized for various purposes in biomedicine. In the past 15 years, hydrogel, in its bulk form or integrated into other devices, has been developed to capture cancer-related biomarkers from body fluids and tissues [
7–39
]. These innovations are related to the superior properties of hydrogels. The main property of hydrogels is that they can swell and retain water without easily dissolving [
40
]. It is because of the hydrogel’s hydrophilic functional groups, such as hydroxide and carboxylic acid groups, which allow rapid water absorption and expansion multiple times its weight [
41
]. When in contact with a solution such as tissue-derived fluid from a liquid biopsy, the molecule adheres to the hydrogel surface and gradually diffuses through the network meshes. This exposure also allows the enlargement of the network meshes and transportation of more/larger molecules [
40
].
Besides its swelling ability, the characteristics of hydrogels can be tailored according to the intended use. Hydrogels can be fabricated in a wide range of sizes, including macrogels, microgels, and nanogels [
42
]. The ability to precisely control the size of hydrogels enables their integration into various device platforms or molecules, including biological probes. This ability facilitates the selective capture of specific biomarkers, or for other diagnostic purposes, such as imaging [
42–44
]. Another distinctive advantage of hydrogels is their ability to respond to various external stimuli, enabling on-demand control of their functional properties, which is not typically possessed by other bioreceptors, such as antibodies [
43
,
45
]. These properties might be useful since tumor tissues and their microenvironment differ significantly in pH, temperature, reactive oxygen species, and enzyme expression compared with healthy tissues [
42
].
In terms of functionality, the application of hydrogels in biomarker capture is projected for liquid biopsy. Until now, tissue biopsy is still a standard for cancer diagnosis, with the aid of imaging techniques such as Computed Tomography or Positron Emission Tomography [
46
]. Compared to tissue biopsies, liquid biopsies, such as blood, urine, or saliva, are a less invasive method and more accessible for certain types of cancer. For cancer patients, a noninvasive method offers greater comfort, which may enhance patient compliance with early detection protocols and increase the feasibility of ongoing disease monitoring [
47
]. Therefore, this review aims to evaluate the current performance of hydrogels as diagnostic devices for detecting cancer biomarkers. The objective of this review is to systematically search the literature, compile relevant data, and analyze emerging trends in the application of hydrogels as diagnostic devices for cancer biomarkers.
2.
Methods
2.1.
Search strategy
The systematic review follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The research question is:
What is the effectiveness of hydrogels as diagnostic tools for detecting cancer biomarkers?
The research question was established based on population criteria: research articles with cancer subjects, exposure: hydrogel used as a direct biomarker sampling tool for cancer detection or diagnosis, and outcome: detection of cancer biomarker.
The search was conducted across five databases: PubMed, Scopus, Embase, MEDLINE, and EBSCO (Dentistry & Oral Sciences) until 31
st
October 2024. Search terminologies used were

Supplementary Table S1

2.2.
Articles selection and data extraction
The search results were managed by Covidence. Duplicates were automatically removed by Covidence and manually removed by the reviewers. Two reviewers independently screened based on the inclusion criteria: (1) Hydrogel as a direct biomarker sampling tool for diagnosing or detecting cancer biomarkers. In this context, ‘direct’ refers to the hydrogel being in immediate contact with the sample, whereby the contained biomarkers are captured intrinsically by the hydrogel matrix itself, rather than using an attached biosensor. For example, a hydrogel microneedle that penetrates the skin of mice in a cancer model [
25
]; (2) All types of research, such as
in vitro, in vivo
, preclinical, and/or clinical research, looking at cancer biomarkers; (3) Written in English or Indonesian.
Articles about hydrogel used as a biomarker sampling method for cancer prognosis or treatment, and hydrogel used as an indirect biomarker detection (i.e., combination with receptors or antibodies to capture biomarkers), systematic reviews, literature reviews, conference abstracts, conference posters, conference presentations, books, book chapters, editorials, and letters to the editor were excluded from the analysis. There were no restrictions on publication year.
The first study selection was executed by screening the title and abstract. In cases of doubt, the article was retained for further consideration in the subsequent selection phase. The second study selection was achieved by reading the full article. After the second selection, the included article underwent a qualification assessment using ToxRtool. In case of a disagreement between two reviewers regarding a qualification assessment, a third reviewer was invited, and the majority of the results were used. The reliable studies were continued for data extraction. Extracted variable including hydrogel’s material and characteristics, research type (
in vitro/in vivo
), cancer sites and types, observed sample, biomarker captured, detection methods, and publication’s year and country. The protocol for this systematic review is registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024600282).
3.
Results
As described in the PRISMA flow diagram (
Figure 1
), 3111 journal articles were retrieved from five databases, with 1010 duplicates detected by both automatic and manual screening. A total of 2101 articles were screened, of which 177 remained for full article reading. Following this, 104 articles were excluded because of following reason: Ineligible outcome or not measuring cancer-related biomarker (7 articles); excluded indication or not for diagnosis (24 articles); non-eligible intervention or not directly capturing biomarker/cancer cell (40 articles); ineligible types of materials or is not hydrogel (56 articles); out-of-scope literatures, including systematic review, literature review, letter to editors, and conference abstract (17 articles). The remaining 33 articles were included for quality assessment with ToxRTool.

Hydrogel and detected biomarker
Various types and forms of hydrogel were used in the included studies, including bulk hydrogel (36.36%), cell/molecular imprinted hydrogel (21.21%), hydrogel layer/coating (15.15%), nanogel/nanoparticle (12.12%), and hydrogel as a part device or hydrogel microneedle (8.08%). Other polymers used include gelatin, chondroitin sulfate, and chitosan. Some studies also used mixtures of polymers, such as PEG, acrylamide, acrylate, cellulose, polyvinyl alcohol, and tripeptide. 4.
Discussion
With advancements in molecular analysis and precision oncology strategy, liquid biopsy is now being explored as a less invasive alternative to surgical biopsy [
47
,
49
]. Additionally, liquid biopsy has demonstrated the ability to sensitively detect cancer biomarkers such as CTCs and circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), significantly enhancing current cancer diagnosis and early detection, progression monitoring, providing prognostic information, and supporting therapy decisions [
49–51
]. To improve the efficiency of liquid biopsies, researchers are investigating standardized approaches, such as the incorporation of specialized sampling devices. Hydrogel has been used for transporting molecules, especially drugs [
52
]. Following the same principle, hydrogels have been studied as devices to capture body fluids and their contents. According to studies included in the current review, hydrogels have been developed as detection devices for cancer-related biomarkers within the past 15 years. This recent innovation has highlighted the potential of hydrogels as a diagnostic alternative. However, a gap remains in the current stages of research and development.
From the 33 included studies, only one, out of the two
in vivo
studies, reported the biomarkers captured in a mouse model. This study is from Park et al., which utilized a hydrogel microneedle to collect exosomes from the back skin of mice with colorectal and skin cancer models [
25
]. Although a study has demonstrated the potential of using hydrogel application on cancer tissue, the remaining 31 studies have focused on developing specific devices and establishing proof of concept through
in vitro
research. These findings illustrate that research on hydrogels as devices for cancer biomarker detection is still in its early stages, even when patient samples are employed. Meanwhile, in clinical settings, hydrogels are mostly used for drug delivery, tissue regeneration or scaffolds [
53
], but there is no known ongoing clinical trial for cancer diagnostics. Future studies could aim to develop the device’s application in
in vivo
cancer models that more closely resemble the clinical characteristics of cancer.
This systematic review classified hydrogel materials according to the primary polymer used in their formulation. For formulations containing multiple polymers, the classification was based on the polymer present in the largest proportion. If two or more polymers were present in equal amounts or were both considered primary components, the hydrogel was classified as a mixture of those polymers, as described in
Table 2
. Characterization of hydrogels used in each study was also described (
Table 1
). One of the characteristics is the hydrogel’s source. Some hydrogels, such as DNA and alginate, are classified as semi-synthetic, despite their natural origin. This is because the fabrication of natural hydrogels in all included studies was combined with synthetic materials, such as derivatives of synthetic polymers or crosslinkers.
Acrylamide emerged as the most frequently used primary polymer, being investigated in 12 studies. It has also been shown to capture most biomarkers (
Table 2
). To form the gel, acrylamide monomers bind with bis-acrylamide crosslinkers and ammonium persulfate (APS) and/or N,N,N’,N’-tetramethylethylenediamine (TEMED) as initiators/catalysts, with a method called free radical polymerization [
13
,
31
,
32
]. This method allows for tailoring the properties of hydrogels by adjusting the concentration of the crosslinker or initiators/catalysts. Increasing the crosslinker will result in higher cross-linking, shorter polymer chains, increase polymer strength, but lower the swelling ability. Meanwhile, decreasing the initiators will slow down the polymerization, increasing the length of the polymer chain, producing a hydrogel with high porosity and higher swelling [
54
,
55
].
Acrylamide-based hydrogel has a high absorption capacity, which is up to 48.39 g water/g gel, and up to 10.87 × 10
−4
cm
2
s
−1
diffusion rate [
56
]. For cancer biomarker detections, acrylamide was utilized in different hydrogel forms, including nanoparticles [
13
,
15
], film [
19
], coating [
21
,
27
], molecular/cell-imprinted hydrogel [
20
,
22
,
23
], and bulk hydrogel [
31–33
]. Acrylamide-based hydrogel has been shown to have effective retention capabilities for a significant variety of proteins [
31
,
32
]. Studies that fabricated acrylamide as nanoparticles incorporated size exclusion, enabling only low-molecular-weight proteins and peptides to penetrate and be entrapped by the hydrogels [
13
,
15
]. Acrylamide hydrogels are also engineered as cell-imprinted hydrogels and selective-binding hydrogels to capture specific cells and proteins [
19
,
20
]. Another approach to the acrylamide hydrogel device is the incorporation of enzymes that digest protein from the tissue region of interest by the hydrogel disc, followed by the extraction of captured proteins [
31
,
32
].
Following acrylamide, hydrogel formulations containing PEG were reported in 7 studies. In comparison, DNA and alginate were reported in 4 studies. CTCs were identified as the most frequently captured biomarkers across three hydrogel materials. In the current study, PEG hydrogels were primarily fabricated as conventional hydrogels as encapsulation matrices. However, Aktan et al. developed a hydrogel by combining PEG and methacrylate, resulting in a temperature-responsive material, and LeValley et al. created photo-responsive hydrogels from PEG diacrylate. Both studies demonstrated the ability to capture and release CTCs on demand [
7
,
18
].
PEG-based hydrogels can be fabricated through various methods of polymerization and co-polymerized with multiple materials, which enables PEG to be precisely engineered with tailored structures and functions while maintaining robust stability [
7
,
18
,
29
]. Hence, different PEG-based hydrogels have considerable variation in properties. For example, a study on polyethylene glycol diacrylate (PEGDA) hydrogel reported up to a 9.4 swelling ratio, which decreased with lower crosslinking [
57
]. Meanwhile, a hydrogel from 8-arm PEG has volumetric swelling ratios >1.5 [
58
]. Additionally, the molecular weight of PEG also influences its properties. Low molecular weight PEG (700 Da) showed a 2–2.3 mesh size, which allowed the diffusion of protein and nutrients, such as glucose and insulin [
57
]. A study by Wu et al. reported that higher molecular weight PEGs (such as PEG 4700 and PEG 20000) have longer polymer chains, which display a greater tendency to interact with proteins. This effect is likely attributed to the presence of hydrophobic CH
2
–CH
2
—groups within the PEG backbone, which promote hydrophobic interactions with protein surfaces [
59
].
DNA-based hydrogels included in this review are mainly fabricated into stimuli-responsive hydrogels, including magnetic [
11
] and biological stimuli [
10
,
30
,
36
] to selectively capture targeted components. DNA is fabricated into a hydrogel with chemical crosslinkers, enzyme-catalyzers, or a self-spinning method. Self-spinning DNA is reported to have a swelling ratio of over 600% [
60
]. For chemically crosslinking DNA, the swelling behavior and pore size of DNA hydrogels depend on the concentration of crosslinkers during fabrication [
61
]. One superior attribute of DNA as a polymer is its biocompatibility and biodegradation, because nucleotides are naturally safe compounds [
61
,
62
]. Because of the biocompatibility and capability to be designed to smart-responsive hydrogels, DNA has developed into an implantable biosensing system and tissue engineering [
61
]. This review observed further application of DNA hydrogel for cancer detection. A study by Chen et al. modified the surface DNA hydrogel to mimic the structure and molecular binding properties of CTCs, enabling the selective and static capture of live CTCs with real-time fluorescence detection [
10
]. Another study by Ye et al. incorporated aptamers that specifically bind to epithelial cell adhesion molecule on the CTCs’ surface, leading to the formation of a hydrogel matrix encapsulating CTCs [
36
]. These studies demonstrated that DNA hydrogels possess bioresponsiveness and biocompatibility, which are essential properties for further
in vivo
applications.
Another polymer found in the included studies is alginate. In addition to biocompatibility and adaptability, alginate exhibits high swelling and absorption capacities because of its polysaccharide composition [
63
]. A study by Varaprasad et al. described that the alginate-acrylamide hydrogel with the highest alginate composition has the highest swelling (109.3 g/g) and diffusion coefficient (3.5116 cm
2
s
−1
) [
64
]. Alginate can be fabricated into a bulk hydrogel in the form of hydrogel spheres/droplets [
17
,
35
], cell-imprinted hydrogel [
16
], and hydrogel microneedles [
25
]. In a study by Gao et al., alginate was engineered into a cell-imprinted hydrogel, which demonstrated excellent biocompatibility by supporting cell adhesion and proliferation [
16
]. Relatedly, Wei et al. also fabricated microdroplets of alginate hydrogel to encapsulate targeted cells while preserving their integrity [
35
]. In addition to being utilized in the only included
in vivo
studies, the biocompatibility of alginate hydrogel is advantageous for real-time monitoring and advanced cellular analysis.
Due to the wide application of hydrogels for cancer biomarker detection devices, this review focused on the direct capture of biomarkers. This means the molecule should be in contact with the hydrogel matrix and move inside it. There are several examples of hydrogel being used as a detection device, but as a base for bioreceptors that bind to the target biomarkers [
65
,
66
], which are then excluded. CTCs are the biomarkers most frequently observed in the included studies. CTCs are cells that detach from the primary tumor and enter the bloodstream to different parts of the body [
51
]. CTCs possess a mutational profile closely resembling the primary tumor, reflecting its heterogeneity. However, CTCs are more commonly detected in advanced stages of cancer rather than in early stages. As a result, their presence is primarily used as a prognostic or metastatic biomarker, rather than for early cancer detection [
47
,
51
]. Therefore, further studies are required to identify reliable biomarkers for early cancer detection, such as circulating tumor DNA or a specific protein marker [
51
].
There is a wide variation in the methods and results of biomarker detections from the review. Most results were observed at the cellular level using imaging, such as fluorescent microscopy and electron scanning microscopes. Other studies used a variety of assays, such as PCR, ELISA, mass spectrometry, and liquid chromatography, to observe RNA, miRNA, and proteins. Three studies conducted sequencing analyses to identify mutations in the target genes [
9
,
29
,
35
]. Because of this variation, it is challenging to statistically compare the accuracy of detection using meta-analysis. However, the comparison is possible with more published research within a similar hydrogel device or methodology. Further studies could aim to compare specific methods for detecting certain biomarkers to build information on prospective hydrogel materials as cancer diagnostic tools.
While identifying detectable biomarkers, most studies do not quantify their concentrations, reporting only presence or absence. Clinically, these biomarkers are frequently observed at very low concentrations. This low quantity could raise concerns about sampling strategy and analytical challenge, as it can fall below the detection limits of even the most advanced assays. While hydrogel-based sampling methods can enhance the detection of these biomarkers by pre-concentrating them from complex biological samples, there is no standardized diagnostic performance. Therefore, further research and validation are needed to establish standardized protocols and ensure consistent diagnostic accuracy in hydrogel-based sampling.
5.
Conclusion
Hydrogels, whether detached or integrated, have demonstrated a promising platform for detecting cancer biomarkers. The classification of hydrogels in relation to various types of biomarkers has been discussed in this review. This advancement offers potential alternatives for a noninvasive diagnosis method and establishes the foundation for improved identification and monitoring in oncology. However, the current preliminary stage of the work and the insufficiency of standardized diagnostic performance highlight the need for further optimization and validation of the application in the translational stage. Establishing reliable and reproducible hydrogel-based diagnostics is clinically relevant, as it could improve early cancer detection, enable prompt and appropriate intervention, and ultimately enhance patient outcomes.
6.
Expert opinion
This systematic review highlights the growing development of hydrogel-based devices for cancer biomarker detection and sample collection. With the rise of molecular profiling and big data analytics, hydrogels offer a bridge between the real clinical world, noninvasive sampling, and laboratory precision diagnostics. Their tunable mesh size, affinity chemistries, and stimuli-responsive properties enable pre-concentration and protection of scarce biomarkers, positioning them as a credible next-generation platform for liquid biopsy.
Most current studies remain at the proof-of-concept stage, with limited
in vivo
data and a predominant focus on CTCs rather than earlier and low-abundance biomarkers such as ctDNA, exosomal RNA, or protein signatures. Translating this promise into clinical utility will require standardized performance metrics, benchmarking against current diagnostic methods, and reproducible large-scale manufacture.
Despite these challenges, the substantial progress of the past decade underscores the strong scientific rationale and translational potential of hydrogel-based diagnostics. With continued optimization, validation, and clinical evaluation, these hydrogel systems could enable minimally invasive, repeatable, and accessible cancer diagnostics that align closely with the vision of personalized therapy and precision oncology. Integrating AI-driven data analysis and design platforms will further accelerate material innovation and diagnostic development, aiding the progress toward clinical adoption of hydrogel-based diagnostic profiling systems.
