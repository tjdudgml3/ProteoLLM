During embryonic development, lens precursor cells undergo specialized differentiation to achieve the transparency and refractive power of lens fiber cells.
1
Pathogenic variants in crystallin genes, transcription factors that regulate gene expression in the lens, and cytoskeletal proteins disrupt this differentiation and result in congenital cataract, an opacity in the lens.
2
Despite identification of more than 50 genes involved in non-syndromic pediatric cataract, a causative variant is identified in only about half of families with hereditary cataract.
2
,
3
Genetic analysis of affected families through exome and genome sequencing continues to identify novel factors involved in this process.
Periaxin (
PRX
) has two alternate transcripts corresponding to different protein isoforms: transcript variant 1 (
PRXa
; 5.2 kb), which encodes S-periaxin (S-PRX; 147 amino acids [aa]; 16 kD), and transcript variant 2 (
PRXb
; 4.6 kb), which encodes L-periaxin (L-PRX; 1461 aa; 147 kD). The transcripts utilize the same promoter and share five identical 5′ exons but differ in their 3′ sequence:
PRXb
contains one additional intron, a 649-bp intron 6 which separates exons 6 and 7, but
PRXa
retains this intron, producing a longer alternative exon (6a). The retained intronic sequence introduces a stop codon after a short coding sequence (20 aa); thus,
PRXa
encodes a truncated version of the protein (S-PRX). Consequently, S-PRX and L-PRX share the initial 127 aa, including the PDZ (PSD95/DLG1/ZO-1) domain and nuclear export signal, but they have different C-terminal regions. Although no specific domain is present in the final 20-aa region unique to S-PRX, the 1334-aa unique sequence of L-PRX contains a nuclear localization signal, 55 repeat motifs forming the long repeat domains, and an acidic domain. Within cell-culture models, S-PRX was primarily seen in the cytoplasm, and L-PRX was found in the cell membrane, cytoplasm, and nuclei.
4
,
5
In 20-day-old murine sciatic nerve sections, L-PRX was concentrated in the periaxonal and abaxonal myelin lamellae and plasma membrane of the Schwann cell, whereas S-PRX was distributed throughout the cytoplasm as well as in the nucleus of the Schwann cell.
6
Recessive variants in
PRX
are well recognized as a cause of neurological phenotypes, including Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease type 4F and Dejerine–Sottas neuropathy.
7
,
8
Pathogenic alleles associated with neurological symptoms are primarily truncating variants,
9
which likely act through a loss-of-function mechanism. The
PRX
−/−
mouse (c.611_3440del), lacking both L-PRX and S-PRX, similarly demonstrates a demyelinating neuropathy.
10
Although initially thought to be specific to Schwann cells, expression of
PRXb
(L-PRX) has since been identified in other tissues, including mammalian adult lens fibers, embryonic murine lenses, and human adult brain endothelium cells; however, the presence of S-PRX has not been evaluated in these tissues.
5
,
11
,
12
Screening of families affected with congenital cataract identified a unique class of
PRX
variants in four families, revealing a novel phenotype for this gene.

Discussion
Although cataract is not a recognized feature associated with
PRX
disruption
,
L-PRX was identified as a member of the cortex adherens junction containing ezrin, periplakin, periaxin, and desmoyokin (EPPD complex) important in lens fiber tissue in mammalian adult lenses.
11
Within the mouse eye, L-PRX is expressed primarily in lens fibers, where it interacts with other proteins involved in membrane organization; L-PRX expression was detectable in the lens pit at embryonic day 10.5 (E10.5), and the concentration increased during lens maturation.
12
L-PRX was localized to the fiber cell plasma membrane in the outer cortex of developing lenses and later demonstrated plaque-like accumulation at the vertices of the mature fiber cells. In
PRX
−/−
mice at 6 to 9 months, eyes were normally sized with no visible anomalies; furthermore, dissected lenses were indistinguishable from age-matched wild-type lenses, with no alteration in lens transparency. Histological analysis using an antibody specific to L-PRX confirmed lack of expression in the knock-out (KO) lenses but no notable differences in fiber cell organization, differentiation, or migration; however, upon sectioning, KO lenses were noted to be softer and more easily deformed than normal lenses.
12
Additionally, a homozygous missense variant in L-PRX, p.Arg167Cys, was identified as one of three candidate variants to explain the dominant adult-onset cataract phenotype in the Emory mouse model,
25
but its role in the phenotype is still unclear. Finally, proteomics analysis in adult human lenses identified the highest concentration for PRX in the differentiating zone of the lens and the lowest concentration in the inner cortex using a peptide sequence within the PDZ domain common to both PRX proteins.
26
No studies have evaluated the presence of S-PRX specifically in the developing or adult vertebrate lens.
Previously reported
PRX
variants primarily result in recessive neurological phenotypes, with the majority of alleles being truncating variants affecting the final exon of
PRXb
(encoding L-PRX) and thus expected to produce a truncated L-PRX protein with no effect on S-PRX. A handful of truncating alleles were reported earlier in the protein. These alleles all occur more than 50 nucleotides upstream of the final intron and thus would be expected to be subject to nonsense mediated decay, resulting in a lack of both transcript isoforms and encoded proteins, L-PRX and S-PRX, and thus a complete loss of function. No previously reported neurological alleles affect the intron 6 canonical splice sites.
9
Only one neurological allele was reported to cause cataract (see above), and no other neurological alleles were identified in the region with a predicted effect on intron 6 splicing.
In contrast to the neurological alleles, all cataract alleles reported here are predicted to affect splicing of intron 6, the final intron unique to
PRXb
(encoding L-PRX). This clustering of cataract alleles in the region surrounding intron 6 is notable and suggests specificity for this phenotype. Results from minigene assays demonstrated abnormal splicing with various levels of intron inclusion detected for all variants identified in this study and use of alternate splice sites for three of the four. Intron inclusion is predicted to result in a switch to the
PRXa
transcript that encodes S-PRX, whereas usage of alternate splice sites is expected to produce new transcripts encoding abnormal L-PRX proteins: an in-frame deletion within the nuclear localization signal of L-PRX, c.383_391del p.(Asn128_Gln130del); an in-frame deletion of the last four codons of exon 6 of PRXb, L-PRX c.370_381del p.(Val124_Leu127del); and skipping or out-of-frame truncation of the penultimate exon producing an out-of-frame c-terminal end of the L-PRX protein and premature termination, p.(Gly62Glufs*23) and p.(Tyr77Thrfs*8), respectively. Thus, these splicing changes are expected to increase the level of S-PRX in all tissues that typically express L-PRX, in addition to introducing abnormal L-PRX variants carrying in-frame deletions or C-terminal truncations and decreasing the level of normal L-PRX. Because partial or complete loss of L-PRX alone does not result in cataracts in humans or mice, we hypothesize that the lens defects are caused by the expression of excess S-PRX and/or in-frame L-PRX alterations, acting through gain-of-function or dominant-negative effects involving macromolecular complexes formed by periaxin, with or without an additional contribution from L-PRX deficiency. The function of S-PRX is largely unknown, with one study reporting a possible role in mRNA splicing.
27
It is currently unknown whether S-PRX is normally present in the developing or adult lens, as all immunofluorescence analyses have used an antibody recognizing the C-terminal region specific to L-PRX,
11
,
12
and mass spectrometry utilized a peptide sequence common to both proteins.
26
S-PRX lacks C-terminal domains (nuclear localization signal, repeat domain, and an acidic domain) but retains PDZ, the N-terminal protein interaction domain implicated in the assembly of supramolecular complexes. Both S-PRX and L-PRX are able to homodimerize as well as heterodimerize via their PDZ domains
28
,
29
; heterodimerization with S-PRX inhibits L-PRX homodimerization,
4
which is important to its scaffolding functions.
30
In addition to this, the overexpressed S-PRX may bind various other PDZ proteins present in the lens, interfering with their normal function/localization; multiple members of the PDZ superfamily were shown to have critical roles in lens development.
31
The erroneous protein products containing a three-amino-acid deletion within the nuclear localization signal of L-PRX may similarly bind normal L-PRX or other PDZ proteins present in the lens and affect their regular distribution/function, leading to aberrant development and loss of lens transparency. Because these variants also affect the amount of functional L-PRX produced, it is likely that they would also result in recessive neurological phenotypes if present in a homozygous/compound heterozygous state.
In summary, we present strong genetic evidence that dominant congenital cataract is caused by a specific class of variants affecting the final intron of
PRX
and leading to splicing aberrations resulting in a replacement of wild-type L-PRX with S-PRX and mutant L-PRX. Because loss of L-PRX in the lens is not currently associated with a cataract phenotype, we propose that the expression of these alternate transcripts/proteins exerts a toxic effect on lens development. The exact mechanism of this action requires further study. Individuals with neurological phenotypes associated with a complete loss of L-PRX and their carrier parents should be evaluated for a possible increased risk of earlier onset of age-related cataract, to fully clarify the possible contribution of PRX isoforms to lens development and maintenance.
