Introduction
Elucidating interactions between eukaryotic microbes and the viruses that infect them is critical for understanding microbial ecology on a global scale. The past twenty years has seen rapid growth in our knowledge of such viruses, in particular the nucleocytoplasmic large DNA viruses (NCLDVs). Often referred to as ‘giant’ viruses due to their atypically large virions (>300 nm in diameter) and genomes (0.3 to >2 megabase pairs) (e.g. refs.
1
–
3
), NCLDVs belong to the phylum
Nucleocytoviricota
4
and have been shown to influence the biology and ecology of diverse algae and protists. For example, NCLDVs impact the persistence of harmful algal blooms by pelagophyte algae
5
,
6
. In Arctic green algae, co-infecting lytic prasinoviruses compete with one another in a temperature-dependent manner, an observation with implications for polar food webs in changing oceans
7
. The nuclear genomes of diverse eukaryotic taxa bear witness to past and present interactions with viruses. These genetic ‘footprints’ range from viral detritus in species of
Acanthamoeba
8
,
9
to giant endogenous viral elements (GEVEs) in green algae
10
, brown algae
11
and fungi
12
, to domesticated mobile genetic elements such as classical retrotransposons and the recently discovered polintons
13
. The existence of stable, long-term associations between microbial eukaryotic hosts and viruses in nature remains an open question.
The 2023 discovery of mirusviruses underscores the power of metagenomics as an engine of exploration. These dsDNA viruses were inferred from metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) pieced together from
Tara
Oceans data
14
,
15
. Mirusvirus genes involved in DNA replication and gene expression share sequence similarity with viruses in the
Nucleocytoviricota
. In stark contrast, mirusvirus genes encoding structural proteins (including the distinctive HK97-fold major capsid protein (MCP)) are related to viruses in the
Duplodnaviria
, which includes tailed bacteriophages and herpesviruses. Mirusviruses are predicted to be abundant and active in ocean surface waters from pole to pole
14
and mirusvirus genetic signatures have recently been detected in freshwater samples
16
. However, because of their MAG-based discovery, the physical nature of mirusviruses and the range of hosts they infect is unclear (beyond the fact that they include cells <5 μm in size). Mirusvirus-like MCP genes have been found in the genome of the phagotrophic green alga
Cymbomonas
17
, and surveys of genomic data have identified mirusvirus-like sequences in diverse protists from across the eukaryotic tree of life
18
. Given this apparently broad distribution, elucidating the biology of mirusviruses is essential to understanding the evolution of microbial eukaryotes and their pivotal ecological roles in aquatic ecosystems.
Mirusvirus genomic elements were recently shown to be a prominent feature of thraustochytrids
18
,
19
, protists well known as aquatic decomposers and biotechnological workhorses used to produce polyunsaturated fatty acids for nutritional supplements
20
. The genome of the marine thraustochytrid
Aurantiochytrium limacinum
contains two ~300 kilobase-pair (kbp) mirusvirus-like elements
19
. The first is a circular episome initially dubbed CE1, and the second is integrated into a sub-telomeric region of chromosome 15 (LE-Chr15), reminiscent of the episomal and integrated versions of herpesviral genomes in metazoans
21
,
22
. Here we show that
A. limacinum
produces mirusvirus particles under starvation and when grown in the rich media used for routine culturing. Starvation induces a dramatic shift in mirusvirus protein expression from the episomal genome and triggers changes in host cell biology. Our results suggest that mirusviruses persistently infect
A. limacinum
and other thraustochytrids, and that dsDNA viruses can have long-term, intimate, hitherto unrecognized associations with protists in nature. Investigation of such interactions in laboratory cultures used for research and biotechnological applications promises new insights into their ecological and evolutionary impact.
Results and discussion
Mirusvirus genes are expressed and encode functionally diverse proteins
Initial characterization of the
A. limacinum
CE1 and LE-Chr15 mirusvirus elements, herein referred to as AurliV-1 and AurliV-2, respectively (for
Aurantiochytrium limacinum
viral elements 1 and 2), showed them to be extremely divergent from one another and from other known viral genomes, including
Tara
Oceans-derived MAGs
14
,
19
. Seventy-five % (128/170) of the AurliV-1 ORFs in the original Joint Genome Institute (JGI) short-read assembly had no hits in standard protein-based BLAST searches, and only 48 of the original 152 JGI-predicted AurliV-2 proteins retrieved an AurliV-1 homolog as a significant hit. To facilitate more detailed functional analysis of
A. limacinum
AurliV-1 and AurliV-2, we revisited the ORFs of both mirusvirus genomes based on new gap-free, nanopore-based, consensus sequences. Figure
1a
shows a physical map of AurliV-1 with an expanded set of 218 ORFs cross-referenced against JGI gene models (when present). One hundred and seventy-three of these ORFs (~78%) were found to have significant functional annotations using BlastP, InterPro, and/or HMMER searches (Supplementary Data
1
). For AurliV-2 (Fig.
1b
), 215 ORFs (79%) had such annotations (Supplementary Data
2
). All predicted proteins were analyzed using Chai-1 (and in some cases AlphaFold (Version 3) to inform downstream structure- / function-based investigations (see below). Combined with the fact that neither AurliV-1 or AurliV-2 show obvious signs of genome erosion
19
, the putative viral functions of many of these proteins suggest that both mirusvirus genomic elements represent current and possibly active infections of their hosts and could yield viral particles.
A. limacinum
could thus serve as an experimental system with which to explore mirusvirus-host interactions.

Transcriptomic data generated in support of the original
A. limacinum
genome sequencing project show that many predicted AurliV-1 genes and some AurliV-2 genes are transcribed when cells are grown in rich liquid medium (790 By+)
19
. Here we performed an independent RNA-seq experiment that yielded similar results, confirming gene expression from both mirusvirus elements when
A. limacinum
is grown on agar plates containing 790 By+ or mGPY media (Supplementary Fig.
1
). AurliV-1 expression was significantly greater than AurliV-2 in both media and was comparable to that of the host genome (Supplementary Fig.
1a
). All AurliV-1 genes in the JGI gene catalog showed evidence of expression, 49 of which had read counts above 500 arbitrary units (A.U.). In contrast, only 10 genes from AurliV-2 had read counts above 500 A.U. and some were not expressed at all. Furthermore, whereas genes for the AurliV-1 MCP, triplex proteins 1 and 2, and portal protein were found to be expressed in both 790 By+ and mGPY (MCP (i.e., Aurli1_48830) is the 13
th
most highly expressed gene, Supplementary Fig.
1b
), transcripts for the AurliV-2 homologs of these key mirusviral structural proteins were negligible or completely absent. The expression of AurliV-2 may be suppressed under the conditions examined.
Of the top 10 most highly expressed genes from AurliV-1, five could not be annotated. Remote homology modeling using Phyre2, HHPred, and AlphaFold3/FoldSeek
23
–
26
failed to provide statistically significant functional predictions for the top two most expressed genes (Supplementary Data
1
; Supplementary Fig.
1b
; both were detected by proteomics (below)). Of the five with confident annotations, none had potential virus-associated functions, with the exception of arylsulfatase Aurli1_41229 and protein kinase Aurli1_48848 (see below). In contrast, all but two of the top 10 most highly expressed AurliV-2 genes could be assigned virus-associated functions, most likely involved in manipulating host signal cascades, such as serine/threonine protein kinase Aurli1_142988. Viral protein kinases are a key feature of large DNA viruses such as herpesviruses, poxviruses, and baculoviruses, and are considered an important aspect of their evolutionary success
27
. The 5
th
most abundant AurliV-2 transcript, Aurli1_13274 (Supplementary Fig.
S1c
), was annotated as a RING-type E3 ubiquitin transferase. Some viruses, such as herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1), encode RING-type E3 ubiquitin ligases, which they use to evade and manipulate host immune systems
28
. Overall, the functional ambiguity of many of the most highly expressed AurliV-1 and AurliV-2 genes underscores the novelty and divergence of mirusvirus proteins.
Aurantiochytrium limacinum
produces mirusvirus particles
Under transmission electron microscopy (TEM), electron-dense virus-like particles 130–150 nm in diameter could readily be seen in the nucleus and cytoplasm of cells subjected to starvation (artificial seawater, ASW) (Fig.
2
). The nuclear capsids, ~90 nm in diameter, were generally hexagonal and of variable electron density, consistent with the presence of different stages of virus maturation. The nuclei of virus-producing cells (Fig.
2b
, d, e) were ultrastructurally distinct from those lacking obvious viral particles (Fig.
2a, c
); the former typically contained more peripherally located nucleoli and sometimes contained fibrous structures (Fig.
2b, e
, Supplementary Fig.
2d
, g). Unusual nucleolus-like structures with demarcations resembling procapsids were also observed (Supplementary Fig.
2b, c, e
, f), as well as mature capsids very close to – and possibly in the process of budding from – the nuclear membrane (Supplementary Fig.
2h, i
). Cytoplasmic virus particles appear to acquire a tegument- or envelope-like structure in discrete, electron dense regions of the cytoplasm, and accumulate in large numbers between the plasma membrane and the cell wall under starved conditions (Fig.
3a, f
, g, Supplementary Fig.
3a–f
). The presence of viral particles in extracellular material was also confirmed by TEM and negative staining of ultracentrifuged supernatant, where mature virions could be observed embedded within, and tethered to, layered organic material presumably derived from the
A. limacinum
cell wall (Fig.
4
, Supplementary Fig.
4
).

Collectively, these observations are remarkably like those made in the early 1970s by Kazama and Schornstein, who described “herpes-type” viruses associated with a thraustochytrid (at the time considered to be a fungus) grown under starvation conditions
29
,
30
. These authors studied a strain identified as
Thraustochytrium
sp., which is today considered to be a distinct genus within the same larger monophyletic clade to which
Aurantiochytrium
belongs. Kazama and Schornstein thus appear to have been the first to observe mirusviruses, at the time assumed to be herpesviruses (phylum
Peploviricota
), and long before the discovery of many members of the
Nucleocytoviricota
.
Unexpectedly, in addition to the starvation conditions studied by Kazama and Schornstein, we also observed virions in control cells grown in rich media (Fig.
2f
, Supplementary Fig.
2
, and Supplementary Fig.
3g–i
). It is thus possible that virion production is associated not only with nutritional status but also specific cell types. The thraustochytrid life cycle is complex and dynamic – in
Aurantiochytrium
viewed under scanning electron microscopy (SEM), ovoid vegetative sporangia, limaciform (‘slug’) cells, and flagellated zoospores (produced from zoosporangia) can all be seen depending on culture conditions (Supplementary Fig.
5
). Importantly, we observed viral particles in various cell types as well as embedded in the cell wall of a putative zoospore (Supplementary Fig.
2a
, j, Supplementary Fig.
4d
, e). This suggests a complex relationship between the virus and the life cycle of its host. Future work should explore the extent to which viral replication is linked to specific host cell types or states.
Nuclear capsids and extracellular virions contain mirusvirus DNA and proteins
We used quantitative PCR (qPCR) and droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) to determine whether the extracellular virions observed by TEM contain AurliV-1 and/or AurliV-2 DNA. Substrate for these experiments was prepared by (i) centrifugation of cultured biomass grown under fed (control) and starved conditions at low speed to sediment cells, (ii) passing the supernatant through a 0.45 μm filter to remove remaining cell-sized debris (and possibly clumped virions), and (iii) DNase treatment to remove contaminating DNA not protected inside viral particles. qPCR and ddPCR primers targeted unique regions of the AurliV-1 and AurliV-2 mirusvirus DNA polymerase genes as well as two non-viral genes on chromosome 15, i.e.,
Ubco
(encoding a putative ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme) and
LeuA
(which encodes an alpha-isopropylmalate synthase) (Supplementary Table
1
).
Ubco
and
LeuA
are not part of the endogenized AurliV-2 region of chromosome 15 and thus serve as a control for host gDNA contamination. The qPCR results suggest that both mirusvirus genomic elements are encapsidated, and that the AurliV-1 DNA polymerase gene is approximately three orders of magnitude (10
3
) more abundant than that of AurliV-2 (Supplementary Fig.
6a
).
We next used qPCR to examine whether the inferred relative abundance of AurliV-1- and AurliV-2-derived virions varied across treatments, again using the DNA polymerase gene as a molecular marker for mirusvirus. The relative abundance of the DNA polymerase gene in the fed and starved samples, with and without DNase treatment, was normalized against a spiked-in luciferase-containing plasmid (to control for DNA extraction variation). The results demonstrate that levels of AurliV-1 and AurliV-2 DNA polymerase genes remained relatively consistent at the 24 h timepoint (Supplementary Fig.
6b
). These results suggest that a larger number of extracellular virions contain DNA from the AurliV-1 episome compared to AurliV-2. Unexpectedly, the fed (control) and starved cells did not differ significantly in terms of extracellular mirusvirus abundance. A more sensitive and robust ddPCR experiment confirmed the presence of AurliV-1 and V-2 DNA polymerase genes in both fed (790 By+) and starved (artificial seawater, ASW) conditions. As with qPCR, extracellular AurliV-1 DNA was found to be much more abundant than AurliV-2 (e.g., under fed conditions at 72 h, ~10
5
copies of AurliV-1 DNA per μL of supernatant compared to <10
2
copies per μL of AurliV-2; Fig.
5a, b
). AurliV-2 DNA was less abundant than that of AurliV-1 under both conditions. The amount of host genomic DNA contamination, as measured using primers that target
LeuA
, was minimal in all samples, and DNase treatment removed contamination from unprotected DNA.

Finally, proteomics was used to positively identify AurliV-1- and AurliV-2-encoded proteins in whole-cell pellets, nuclear-enriched pellets, and concentrated supernatants. From a total of 6,101 identified proteins in the whole-cell fraction, 67 were AurliV-1-encoded, as opposed to only nine from genes on the chromosomally integrated AurliV-2 mirusviral element (Fig.
6a
, Supplementary Data
1
and
2
). Some AurliV-1-derived proteins were detected in all three fractions (e.g., MCP, triplex proteins, a protein kinase, and a peptidase), others in two of the three fractions, and eight were found only in the supernatant (Fig.
6b
, Supplementary Data
1
). The supernatant fraction was dominated by MCP followed by triplex 2 and 1 proteins (Fig.
6b
), consistent with the herpesvirus capsid hexon
31
, and an AlphaFold3 structural prediction of the AurliV-1 MCP supports the existence of a hexameric structure in vivo (Fig.
6c–f
). Notably, AurliV-2 proteins were not detected in the supernatant fraction as seen for AurliV-1 proteins. Given our ddPCR results (Fig.
5
), it is conceivable that AurliV-2 DNA is co-packaged at a low level with the more abundant AurliV-1 in capsids composed entirely of proteins encoded by AurliV-1.

We also compared the proteomes of
A. limacinum
cultures grown in triplicate under control (fed) conditions (790 By+ medium) to those of cultures subjected to starvation (i.e., grown in ASW). Of the 67 detected AurliV-1-derived proteins, 45 were found to be differentially expressed under starvation conditions (Fig.
6a
). Most striking among those enriched in starved cells were MCP, triplex proteins 1 and 2, a putative chromosome partitioning protein (ParA), and two distinct arylsulfatase proteins (Fig.
6g
; see below). AurliV-1 protein abundances were consistently higher in the starvation treatment compared to control cells, supporting the hypothesis that nutrient stress triggers an increase in production of mirusvirus particles from the AurliV-1 episomal element. The exception among the proteins shown in Fig.
6g
is kinase ORF729, which is not differentially enriched between the two conditions and may not be a component of viral particles. Two of nine AurliV-2-encoded proteins were found to be differentially expressed, in this case depleted in ASW relative to standard 790 By+ medium. Neither of these AurliV-2 proteins could be assigned a predicted function.
Persistent mirusvirus infection in
Aurantiochytrium limacinum
and other thraustochytrids
We have shown that
A. limacinum
cells grown in replete media and under starvation conditions express AurliV-1 and AurliV-2 genes and produce virions. Our data suggest that
A. limacinum
AurliV-1 is the predominant mirusvirus genomic element yielding mature virions under starvation and ‘standard’ growth conditions. It is nevertheless possible that AurliV-2 is active to a much lower extent and/or under different conditions. To explore the possibility that the host is limiting the expression of the endogenized AurliV-2 epigenetically, we performed a genome-wide screen for 5-methylcytosine in the Oxford Nanopore sequence data generated herein. We found no obvious differential methylation patterns between AurliV-2 and AurliV-1 under standard growth conditions, and in fact no clear differences in methylation levels between these two mirusvirus genomes and the rest of the
A. limacinum
nuclear genome (Supplementary Fig.
7
). Viral DNAs recently integrated into the nuclear genomes of the amoebozoan
Acanthamoeba
8
and
Amoebidium
(a single-celled relative of animals) are silenced by heavy methylation, a mechanism suggested to enable controlled coexistence of viral DNA in eukaryotic nuclear genomes
32
. In terms of base composition, the AurliV-1 episome has the same GC content as the
A. limacinum
nuclear genome (45.0%), whereas the endogenized AurliV-2 is slightly lower at 41.6%
19
. This is consistent with the hypothesis that AurliV-2 became associated with
A. limacinum
more recently than AurliV-1, and yet is somehow under tighter host control, perhaps due to its chromosomal location.
Why have mirusviruses gone largely unnoticed in
A. limacinum
and other thraustochytrids? In control (fed) cells, only ~3% of 397 cell sections observed under TEM contained at least one obvious virus-like particle, and only a single cell was observed with >5 such particles (Fig.
2f
). Under starvation conditions, the number of observed virus-producing cells were similarly low: ~3% and ~4% of intact, starved cells in TEM sections were found to have at least one viral particle at 19 h (
n
= 693) and 26 h (
n
= 514), respectively, and between 1% and 1.5% of the sampled population had >5 obvious viral particles (Fig.
2f
). Consideration of TEM sections alone undoubtedly underestimates the number of viral particles per cell, but given the large number of cells examined (~400–700 cells), these data are consistent with the hypothesis that in culture a relatively small number of
A. limacinum
cells produce mirusvirus virions in substantial quantities at any given time. This represents an interesting parallel to the recent discovery of an endogenous giant (NCLDV-type) viral element in lab strains and natural isolates of the green alga
Chlamydomonas
, which produce mature virions in late-stage growth in only a small number (up to 3%) of cells even during peak viral activity
33
.
While the endogenous AurliV-2 genomic element is present in all cells, it is formally possible that AurliV-1, whose genome is represented in the original
A. limacinum
JGI assembly at ~9X higher coverage than AurliV-2
19
, is present in only a subset of cultured cells but as a high-copy episome. The high coverage of AurliV-1 was confirmed here by re-sequencing and assembly of the
A. limacinum
genome using the latest nanopore sequencing chemistry. The same ~9:1 AurliV-1:AurliV-2 depth of coverage ratio was again recovered, showing that the stoichiometry of the two mirusvirus genomic elements is stable across different sequencing platforms (454 pyrosequencing and nanopore sequencing) and between different laboratory cultures grown years apart. At present, the diversity of
A. limacinum
cell types in culture is a barrier to further investigation of mirusvirus genome biology at the population level, and long-term synchronization of specific cell types is presently not possible. Vegetative cells grow and divide within sporangia, and even zoospores can exist as clusters (Supplementary Fig.
5
)
34
. Assuming these technical challenges can be addressed, application of single cell ‘omics approaches to the study of
A. limacinum
should be very informative. Interestingly, putative circular mirusviral genomes similar in size to AurliV-1 were recently inferred from draft genomic data for two other thraustochytrids assigned to
Schizochytrium
spp. and the chlorophyte green alga
Ostreobium quekettii
18
. This suggests that episomal mirusvirus genomes may be common in nature and may impact the growth and production of strains used commercially to produce fatty acids
20
.
Mirusvirus appears to represent a persistent infection of
A. limacinum
, and perhaps other thraustochytrids, similar to, but distinct from the phenomenon of latency in animal-infecting herpesviruses
22
; mirusviruses replicate constitutively in cells grown under standard conditions and become a more prominent, if sporadic, feature of cells subjected to starvation. How mature virions are dispersed – and how (and if) they infect naïve hosts – is unclear. In virus-containing starved cells (but less so in fed cells), viral particles were commonly seen accumulating in large numbers in the space between the plasma membrane and cell wall (Fig.
3a, f
, Supplementary Fig.
3
), and only rarely in isolation between cell wall layers and in the extracellular milieu (Fig.
4a, b
, Supplementary Fig.
4
). A potential clue to virion escape comes from the AurliV-1 genome, which encodes three distinct arylsulfatases (Supplementary Data
1
), one of which contains a proline- and threonine-rich, BLLF1-type / herpesvirus-like outer envelope glycoprotein extension on its amino-terminus (Supplementary Fig.
8
). While this BLLF1 domain-containing arylsulfatase was not detected in our proteomic data, the other two (which lack obvious extensions) were enriched under starvation (Fig.
6g
). Three AurliV-1-encoded alpha-galactosidase-like proteins also have BLLF1 domains, but on their carboxy-termini (Supplementary Fig.
9
).
Bioinformatic analysis revealed complex evolutionary histories for both sets of enzymes. Genes encoding arylsulfatase and arylsulfatase-like proteins also reside within AurliV-2 and several other host nuclear chromosomes (1, 2, and 11), as well as in the thraustochytrid
Hondaea fermentalgiana
; these proteins belong to a monophyletic clade and appear to be related to bacterial alkaline phosphatases (Supplementary Fig.
8
). In the case of the AurliV-1 alpha-galactosidases, homologs with and without obvious glycoprotein domains can be found on
A. limacinum
chromosome 6, in
H. fermentalgiana
and other thraustochytrids, and in select fungi and protists (Supplementary Fig.
9
, Supplementary Information File
1
). Regardless of the origins of these AurliV-1-encoded proteins, given that thraustochytrid cell walls are composed of sulphated polysaccharides rich in galactose, xylose, mannose, and other sugars
35
, surface-localized arylsulfatase and alpha-galactosidase activities could serve to degrade the
A. limacinum
cell wall, thus allowing virions to be released from sporangia. They could also facilitate mirusviral infection by digesting cell walls from the outside in. The AurliV-1 genome is predicted to encode a variety of other carbohydrate-degrading enzymes as well, including a putative alpha-amylase and a pectinesterase (Supplementary Data
1
). There is clearly much to learn about mirusvirus-host ‘cross-talk’ at the biochemical and cellular level.
The persistent and productive nature of mirusvirus ‘infection’ in
A. limacinum
suggests a stable, asynchronous virus-host relationship, one with significant implications for understanding viral evolution and ecology in marine environments. The relative impact of the AurliV-1 and AurliV-2 genomic elements on host cell biology is unclear. As recently shown for
Chlamydomonas
spp.
33
, concerted exploration of thraustochytrid strain diversity in nature should provide insight into when and how mirusviruses first became woven into their biology, and the extent to which other viruses play a role. In that vein, it is important to note that although not identified in the
A. limacinum
strain examined herein, lytic single-stranded RNA viruses have been shown to infect the thraustochytrid
Schizochytrium
sp. strain NIBH N1-27
36
,
37
and a lytic large DNA virus (SmDNAV) infects
Sicyoidochytrium minutum
38

Our work on
A. limacinum
has revealed intriguing parallels between the biology of mirusviruses and herpesviruses. In addition to the presence of episomal and sub-telomerically-integrated viral genomes in both mirusviruses and latent herpesviruses

,
21

Elucidating the mechanisms by which mirusvirus capsids are packaged in the nucleus, acquire a lipid envelope, proceed to the cell surface, and exit the cell is an important task for the future. Equally important will be functional characterization of the mirusvirus-encoded proteins that underpin the viral ‘lifecycle’ and, in the case of

RNA-seq and whole genome re-sequencing
For RNA-Seq, sample preparation was performed as described by Horowitz et al.
39

A. limacinum

After three and four days of growth, agar plates were scraped with a plate-spreader, collected in a 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube, and frozen. Library preparation, poly(A) selection, and Illumina HiSeq sequencing were performed by GENEWIZ LLC (NJ). Each sample yielded 812,500 (median) reads from each end. Cutadapt typically removed 8.6% (median) of total bases and trimming by fastQC typically left 712,500 147 base reads from each end.
For long read nanopore sequencing,
A. limacinum
was cultured for three days in 50 mL 790 By+ medium. Genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted using a DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit Quick-Start Protocol (QIAGEN, #69506) with an overnight Proteinase K digestion. Agarose gel electrophoresis (1%) was used to visually assess and confirm the integrity of high molecular weight (20+ kb) DNA. The DNA quantity was assessed using a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer with the dsDNA broad range assay kit. A nanopore (PromethION, Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT)) sequencing library was prepared using the ONT ligation sequencing kit (SQK-LSK114) and ~1 μg of gDNA. A total of 350 ng of prepared library was loaded on a single PromethION R0.4.1 chemistry flow cell (FLO-PRO114M) and sequenced via Oxford Nanopore Technology’s MinKNOW software (v24.06.15). Basecalling was performed with Dorado (v0.8.0) using super accurate model v5.0.0, 400 bps and trimming adaptors. Middle adaptors were removed by Porechop ab initio (v0.5.0) and data filtered using Filtlong (v0.2.1) for 20.6 Gb of reads above q20 and longer than 1 kb. The resulting data were assembled with Canu v2.2 and improved by Medaka (v1.8.0). The long-read assembly was polished using Pilon (v1.22) with Illumina short-read sequence data generated using genomic DNA from cultures grown in liquid 790 By+ medium. The Illumina HiSeq 1000 sequencing (2 × 150 bp) performed by GENEWIZ LLC (NJ) generated 16.5 Gb of paired-end reads available at the NCBI database as BioProject PRJNA1242385. The Illumina reads were trimmed with Trimmomatic (v0.39) to trim 15 bases from the start of read, to trim bases below q20 at either end, and to cut the read when the sliding window average quality dropped below q25. The cleaned reads were then assessed with FastQC (v0.12.1) before being used for Pilon polishing. The AurliV-1 and AurliV-2 sequences were manually checked for sequence errors and submitted to NCBI (
PX058987
(AurliV-1) and

Sample preparation for data-independent acquisition (DIA) analysis
Our protocol was adapted from Hughes et al.
42
. Protein was extracted from cell pellets using 150 μL of a solution of 20 mM Tris-Cl pH 7.5, 150 mM KCl, 5 mM MgCl
2
, 0.5% (v/v) NP-40, 0.5% (w/v) sodium deoxycholate, and 0.5X cOmplete protease inhibitor (Sigma). A 10 μL nuclease solution (1 U/μL benzonase) was added to the lysate and incubated at 24 °C for 10 min. After nuclease digestion, 50 μL of a solution of 600 mM TrisCl pH 7.5, 8% (w/v) SDS, and 10 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) was added to the lysate and incubated at 95 °C for 5 min in a heating block. After cooling to 24 °C, lysates were alkylated using 20 mM chloroacetamide (final concentration) and subsequently quenched with DTT. Protein concentration of the resulting lysate was measured using an EZQ assay (Thermo Scientific). Protein cleanup prior to digestion was carried out using an adapted version of the SP3 protocol
43
. Briefly, 10 μL of a 50 mg/mL carboxylate-coated bead stock (Cytiva) was added to 40 μg of protein in 100 μL of solution followed by 400 μL of acetone. After a 5-min incubation at 24 °C, mixtures were centrifuged for 5 min at 5000 ×
g
. Resulting supernatants were discarded and pelleted material was resuspended in 800 μL of 80% ethanol and pelleted again as above. The ethanol supernatant was removed and 1 μg of trypsin/LysC (Promega, V5071) in 100 μL of 100 mM Tris-Cl pH 7.5 with 1 mM CaCl
2
was added. After digestion, the supernatant was collected after centrifugation at 12,000 ×
g
for 2 min and subsequently desalted using C18 HLB SPE (Waters) micro-elution plates. SPE wells were initially conditioned with 100% (v/v) acetonitrile (100 μL) followed by equilibration with 0.1% (v/v) trifluoroacetic acid (TFA, Thermo Scientific) (100 μL). Loaded peptides were rinsed using 4% (v/v) acetonitrile in 0.1% (v/v) formic acid (Thermo Scientific) (200 μL) and eluted using 60% (v/v) acetonitrile in 0.1% (v/v) formic acid (100 μL). All SPE steps were carried out using a vacuum manifold. Eluted peptides were evaporated to dryness in a SpeedVac centrifuge and reconstituted in a solution of 1% dimethylsulfoxide (v/v) in 1% (v/v) formic acid. Reconstituted peptide solutions were quantified on a NanoDrop spectrophotometer using measured signal relative to a standard of known concentration.
Data acquisition (DIA)
Data-independent acquisition analysis was carried out on an Orbitrap Lumos instrument interfaced to a Waters M-Class liquid chromatography (LC) system. For analysis, peptide samples were initially trapped on a pre-column (ACQUITY UPLC M-Class Symmetry C18 Trap, 180 µm × 2 cm, 5 µm beads, Waters) and then separated by an analytical column (nanoEase M/Z Peptide BEH C18, 75 µm × 25 cm, 1.7 µm beads, Waters). Samples were initially trapped for 3 min at a flow rate of 20 µL/min of 1% mobile phase B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile). Gradient separation of trapped peptides started at an initial condition of 3% mobile phase B, ramped to 7% B in 1 min, to 28% B in 25 min, to 80% B in 0.5 min, hold at 80% B for 2 min, ramp to 3% B in 0.5 min, and a final hold at 3% B for 11 min for a total run time of 40 min (mobile phase A = 0.1% formic acid in water, flow rate = 300 nL/min). For analysis on the Orbitrap Lumos, each peptide sample was injected three individual times (40-min acquisition time for each), with each acquisition covering analysis of a separate mass range (injection 1 = 430–550, 2 = 550–690, 3 = 690–930 m/z). Specifically, the Orbitrap Lumos MS was globally set to use a positive ion spray voltage of 2200 V, an ion transfer tube temperature of 275 °C, a default charge state of 3, and an RF Lens setting of 45%. The complete duty cycle of the acquisition method consisted of two MS1 scans and two sets of windowed MS2 scans (order MS1 - MS2 - MS1 - MS2). For the first, low mass range injection, the initial MS1 scan covered a mass range of 425–555 m/z at a resolution of 60,000 with an AGC target of 4e5 (100%) and a max injection time set to ‘Auto’. The following set of DIA MS2 scans covered a precursor range of 430–550 m/z with an isolation window size of 4 m/z (0 m/z overlap) for a total of 30 scan events. Each scan used an HCD energy of 30% and covered a defined mass range of 200–1800 m/z at a resolution of 30,000 with a normalized AGC target of 1000%, and the maximum injection time set to ‘Auto’. Loop control was set to ‘N’ with a value of 30 spectra. The next MS1 scan and following DIA MS2 used the same settings as the previous exception that the MS2 precursor mass range was set to 428–552 m/z to give a 2 m/z stagger with the previous scan windows. For the second, medium mass range injection, the scan settings were the same as for low mass injection with the following changes: (i) MS1 scan range of 545–695 m/z; (ii) DIA-MS2 precursor scan ranges of 550–690 m/z and 548–692 m/z; (iii) Loop control number of spectra = 35. For the third, high mass range injection, the scan settings were the same as the others with the following changes: (i) MS1 scan range of 685–935 m/z; (ii) DIA-MS2 precursor scan ranges of 690–930 m/z and 686–934 m/z; (iii) DIA-MS2 isolation window of 8 m/z; 4. Loop control number of spectra = 30. All scan data were acquired in centroid mode.
Sample preparation for data-dependent acquisition (DDA) analysis
As with DIA, protein was extracted from cell pellets using 150 μL of solution (20 mM Tris-Cl pH 7.5, 150 mM KCl, 5 mM MgCl
2
, 0.5% (v/v) NP-40, 0.5% (w/v) sodium deoxycholate, and 0.5X cOmplete protease inhibitor). DNA/RNA was supported using 10 μL of a nuclease solution (1 U/μL benzonase) added to the solution and incubated at 24 °C for 10 min. After nuclease digestion, 50 μL of a solution of 600 mM TrisCl pH 7.5, 8% (w/v) SDS, and 10 mM DTT was added to the lysate and incubated at 95 °C for 5 min. After cooling to 24 °C, lysates were alkylated using 20 mM chloroacetamide (final concentration) and quenched with DTT. Protein concentration of the lysate was measured using a BCA assay (Thermo Scientific). Protein cleanup prior to digestion was performed using a version of the SP3 protocol
43
. 10 μL of a 50 mg/mL carboxylate-coated bead stock (Cytiva) was added to the protein solution followed by acetone (80% v/v final) to support precipitation
44
. After a 5-min, 24 °C incubation, mixtures were centrifuged for 5 min at 5000 ×
g
. Supernatants were discarded and pelleted material resuspended in 800 μL of 80% ethanol and pelleted again. Ethanol supernatant was removed and 1 μg of trypsin/LysC (Promega, V5071) in 100 μL of 4 M urea was added to each sample and incubated at 30 °C for 4 h with shaking (800 rpm). Samples were then diluted to 1 M urea by addition of 300 μL of 100 mM Tris-Cl pH 7.5 and digested for an additional 16 h under the same conditions. Supernatant was then collected after 2 min centrifugation at 12,000 ×
g
and desalted using Strata-X SPE (Phenomenex, 8M-S100-4GA) micro-elution plates. SPE wells were initially conditioned with 100% (v/v) methanol (100 μL) followed by equilibration with 0.1% (v/v) trifluoroacetic acid (TFA, Thermo Scientific) (100 μL). Loaded peptides were rinsed using 4% (v/v) methanol in 0.1% (v/v) formic acid (Thermo Scientific) (200 μL) and eluted using 60% (v/v) methanol in 0.1% (v/v) formic acid (100 μL). SPE steps were all carried out using a centrifuge (250 ×
g
for 3 min). Eluted peptides were evaporated as above for DIA and reconstituted in 1% dimethylsulfoxide (v/v) in 1% (v/v) formic acid.
Data acquisition (DDA)
As with DIA, DDA-MS analysis was performed on an Orbitrap Lumos connected to an UltiMate 3000 LC. Peptide samples were trapped on a pre-column (75 µm × 3 cm, 1.9 µm C18 Reprosil-Pur Basic beads from Dr. Maisch) and separated by an analytical column (75 µm × 25 cm with an integrated nanospray tip, 1.9 µm C18 Reprosil-Pur Basic beads). Data acquisition utilized a standard DDA scheme. The Orbitrap Lumos was globally set to use a positive ion spray voltage of 2200 V, an ion transfer tube temperature of 275 °C, a default charge state of 2, advanced precursor determination activated, and an RF Lens setting of 45%. MS1 survey scans covered a mass range of 375–1400 m/z at a resolution of 60,000 with an automatic gain control (AGC) target of 4e5 (100%) and the injection time set to ‘Auto’. Precursors for tandem MS/MS (MS2) analysis were selected using monoisotopic precursor selection (‘Peptide’ mode), charge state filtering (2-4z), dynamic exclusion (20 ppm low and high, 60 s duration, exclude isotopes = TRUE, exclude within cycle = TRUE), and an intensity threshold of 2.5e4. MS2 scans were carried out in the Orbitrap at a resolution of 30,000 using a higher-energy collision dissociation setting of 30%, an AGC target of 2e5 (400%), injection time set to ‘Auto’, and an isolation window setting of 1.6 m/z. The total allowable MS2 cycle time was set to 3 s. MS1 data were acquired in profile mode, and MS2 in centroid.
Data analysis
MS data were analyzed against a fasta reference genomic/transcriptomic database generated from (i) transcriptome assemblies stemming from the Marine Microbial Eukaryote Transcriptome Sequencing Project
45
, (ii) JGI-based gene models for
A. limacinum
, and (iii) ORFs equal or greater than 150 nucleotides generated from AurliV-1 and AurliV-2 consensus sequences from the nanopore-derived, Illumina-polished
A. limacinum
genome assembly. DDA-MS raw data were analyzed using FragPipe software (version 22.0) with MSFragger (version 4.1) using the default workflow settings
46
. Specifically, precursor and fragment tolerances of 20 parts-per million (PPM), a fixed modification of 57.02146 at C, and variable modifications of 15.9949 at M and 42.0106 at protein N-termini were used. Matching spectra were rescored using MSBooster and peptide spectral match (PSM) validation utilized Percolator prior to ProteinProphet scoring to support filtering at <1% FDR at the PSM, peptide, and protein levels. Peptides were quantified using the IonQuant package (version 1.10.27) built into FragPipe with default settings. Only protein groups with two or more razer peptides were considered as having been identified by proteomics and used for subsequent analyses.
DIA raw data were de-multiplexed using msconvert (ProteoWizard, version 3.0, peakPicking = ‘vendor’, demultiplex = overlap only) and analyzed using FragPipe software with DIA-NN (version 1.8.2) for quantification. The three injection intensities were summed for each sample. Only protein groups with intensities >0 in at least three of six samples were kept for subsequent analysis. Statistical analysis was performed through the Fragpipe analyst package
47
, where a cutoff of the
p
adjusted value of 0.05 and a log
2
fold change >1 was used to determine differentially expressed proteins.

Bioinformatic analysis of arylsulfatase and alpha-galactosidase proteins
Arylsulfatase and alpha-galactosidase homologs with similar architectures were identified using NCBI’s Conserved Domain Architecture Retrieval Tool (CDART). Additional homologs were identified using BLASTP, TBLASTN, and RPS-BLAST searches against (i) a custom database representing genomic data from multiple protist lineages
