1
Introduction
In the fields of gerontology and anti‐ageing medicine, a distinction is made between chronological age, which is the amount of time elapsed since birth, and biological/physiological age, estimated using the progressive and highly variable rate of damage accumulation in biomolecules and somatic cells. They lead to compromised biological functions, identified as hallmarks of ageing (López‐Otín et al.
2013
). The list of the original nine hallmarks (including those discussed in this paper, such as, e.g., genomic instability, telomere attrition, mitochondrial dysfunction, cellular senescence) has recently been expanded with additional ones (e.g., compromised autophagy, inflammation) (Schmauck‐Medina et al.
2022
). Strikingly, mitochondria are highlighted as the main site of both generation and integration of oxidative stress in the ageing process (Brand
2000
; Sastre et al.
2000
; Lenaz et al.
2002
).
Mitochondria lie at the crossroads of bioenergetics and the oxidative stress theory of ageing. Indeed, they are often referred to as the ‘powerhouse’ of the cell, primarily responsible for producing adenosine triphosphate (ATP), the cell's main source of energy. They are also the main producers of reactive oxygen species (ROS), considered the inevitable by‐products of aerobic metabolism (Harman
1972
; Theurey and Pizzo
2018
; Zhang et al.
2011
), (but see Kervella et al. (
2025
) for an alternative view). In that respect, some old‐established theories of ageing emphasise the importance of energy metabolism and oxidative stress in explaining longevity and senescence (Pearl
1928
; Harman
1956
,
1972
). In particular, ageing is characterised by a progressive decline in mitochondrial activity and response to mitochondrial stress, leading to multiple effects such as decreased energy production (ATP), enhanced ROS production, accumulation of deleterious mutations in mitochondrial DNA, and abnormal accumulation of tricarboxylic acid cycle metabolites (Lima et al.
2022
; Korovesis et al.
2023
). Key features of mitochondrial ageing have served as essential drivers for advancing research on mitochondria in the context of evolutionary biology, particularly with regard to ageing mechanisms like the proposed buffering effect of uncoupling proteins against oxidative stress (Koch et al.
2021
; Criscuolo et al.
2005
).
Evolutionary biologists are not interested in directly mitigating ageing processes or lengthening healthspan. Rather, they seek to explain the origins of the natural variability in ageing rates observed among and within species.
In this context, a growing number of studies have highlighted mitochondria as central players in explaining natural variation in ageing rates through differences in free radical production, both across species and among individuals (Ku et al.
1993
; López‐Torres et al.
2002
). One striking example is the paradoxical longevity of birds, which maintain higher metabolic rates than similarly sized mammals, yet exhibit remarkably long lifespans; a pattern associated with lower mitochondrial free radical production (Barja et al.
1994
).
Specifically, the relationship between mitochondrial respiration and whole‐organism energy expenditure has been proposed as a possible explanation (Mélanie et al.
2019
; Boël et al.
2023
). A higher efficiency in producing cell energy at a low cost in terms of oxidative damage may have co‐evolved with enhanced individual fitness (Salin et al.
2019
; Stier et al.
2014
). To date, the majority of these studies have integrated mitochondria into their explanatory framework by focusing on the connections between mitochondrial respiration (O
2
consumption) and ATP/ROS production, with mitochondrial efficiency (i.e., the rate of ATP produced per O
2
consumed or ROS generated) being a key variable (Salin et al.
2018
; Caldeira da Silva et al.
2008
; Roussel et al.
2015
). In a nutshell, oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) consists of an electron transport chain that generates a protonmotive force between the mitochondrial matrix and the mitochondrial intermembrane space. This protonmotive force is used by ATP synthase to phosphorylate ADP (adenosine diphosphate) into ATP (adenosine triphosphate). Hence, the protonmotive force couples substrate oxidation (i.e., OX) to ATP synthesis (i.e., PHOS). The coupling between these OX and PHOS components remains variable and subject to modulation by internal (e.g., leakage of protons from the inner membrane, i.e., not back into the mitochondrial matrix via ATP synthase (Ricquier and Bouillaud
2000
)) and external factors (such as diet [Salin et al.
2021
]). Thus, measuring mitochondrial efficiency has proved to be of primary importance in shedding light on the mechanisms underlying life‐history trade‐offs in multiple species (Metcalfe and Olsson
2022
; Koch et al.
2017
). However, the implication of mitochondrial bioenergetics in life‐history trade‐offs and ageing cannot be comprehended solely through changes in mitochondrial efficiency (coupling) because what matters is the balance between ATP generation and consumption rate. It should then be recalled that: (1) mitochondria respond to the ATP demand via the dependence of OXPHOS on ADP substrate availability (Chance and Williams
1956
); (2) the ATP/ADP ratio reflects both coupling efficiency and the balance between ATP forming steps and ATP (energy) consuming cellular processes; (3) when ATP consumption exceeds production, the concentration of adenine nucleotides (ATP + ADP + AMP) decreases, with ATP being the most affected (Borregaard and Herlin
1982
). Although the most convincing evidence came from strictly glycolytic cells, this appears to be a general phenomenon. The consequence is that it is not only the absolute amount of ATP produced (which influences turnover speed) (Nicholls
2013
), but also the ATP/ADP ratio (hence energy released by ATP hydrolysis) that dictates how much energy is available for cell maintenance (Nicholls
2013
; Veech et al.
1979
). (4) Mitochondria may affect cell ageing via particular maintenance processes like the fission‐fusion mechanism (Westermann
2010
). For instance, mitophagy, that is, the removal of non‐functional mitochondria and thereby the maintenance of efficient effectors of aerobic metabolism (ATP produced at low ROS‐related cost), is reduced throughout the ageing process in animals (Schmauck‐Medina et al.
2022
). All of these points remain to be explored in most species.
Social insects are a unique research animal model, notably because genetically similar individuals can express contrasted phenotypes associated with different ageing rates (Lucas and Keller
2014
). In comparison to non‐social species, they are notably characterised by immutable sterile castes (extreme altruistic behaviour), leading to a division of labour that defines eusocial species (Heinze and Giehr
2021
). In eusocial Hymenoptera (ants, bees and wasps), at least two distinct female castes coexist, each with specific roles: a single or a few queens, dedicated to reproduction, and workers, which can be either entirely (for eusocial species) or partially sterile and carry out non‐reproductive tasks. In this case, breeding females may live longer than non‐breeding individuals, with ant queens living one or two decades longer than workers, while males die soon after the mating flight (Keller and Genoud
1997
) (see also Korb and Thorne (
2017
); Tasaki, Matsuura, and Iuchi (
2018
) for termites). Thus, the usual life history trade‐off between fecundity and longevity does not seem to apply to social insects (Koch et al.
2021
; Heinze and Giehr
2021
) and a fundamental challenge is to understand how a single genome can produce alternative phenotypes, and particularly alternative ageing phenotypes in monogyne species (i.e., all individuals originating from a single breeder).
Lasius niger
is a monogyne ant species, where the queen and the sterile workers coexist (Keller and Genoud
1997
; Parker
2010
). Within the latter, there is a division of labor based on temporal polyethism, with the specific behaviour of workers depending on their chronological age: the oldest worker individuals are in charge of the riskiest task of leaving the nest in search for food, while the youngest concentrate on brooding and caring for the queen. The lifespan of
L. niger
queens is about 10 times that of workers (Keller
1998
; Kramer et al.
2016
). Thus, despite being genetically very close, the rate of biological ageing is extremely dissimilar within a colony, depending on individual's social and behavioural status.
Mechanistic data on ageing in social insects actually indicate caste specificities. In honeybees, functional decline of brain or motor functions reflects lifespan disparities between workers of different castes, with foragers presenting cues of reduced learning performance for a given chronological age (Münch et al.
2013
; Behrends et al.
2007
). Lipofuscin is a pigment that accumulates in multiple somatic cells with age and has been related to age‐related diseases including brain disorders (Fonseca et al.
2005
; Terman and Brunk
1998
). In social insects, lipofuscin accumulates at a lower rate in tissues of long‐lived worker honeybees than in short‐lived individuals of the same age (Münch et al.
2013
). Therefore, cognitive and more broadly somatic ageing does not appear to be a strict function of chronological age in eusocial insects, and the animal's social role in the hive seems to act as a modulator of biological ageing (Behrends et al.
2007
). One potential factor contributing to their extended longevity is the shielding of queen physiology induced by reproduction per se. It has been observed that mating can actually increase the longevity of
Cardiocondyla obscurior
queen ants, regardless of whether the males have been sterilised or not (Schrempf et al.
2005
). Interestingly, studies involving bees and ants have shown that when the queen is removed, and workers start reproducing, they tend to live longer than their counterparts which do not reproduce in colonies with a queen (Majoe et al.
2021
; Dixon et al.
2014
; Negroni et al.
2021
). Negroni et al. have investigated the transcriptomic regulation of lifespan extension in ant fertile workers of
Temnothorax rugatulus
(Negroni et al.
2021
). They found that the most differentially expressed gene, encoding an isoform of catalase, was downregulated in reproductive individuals. Long‐lived reproducers might therefore be exposed to lower levels of oxidative stress (Negroni et al.
2021
). This situation is mirrored in honey bees, in which the expression level of catalase is also lower in 1‐year‐old queens than in workers (Corona et al.
2005
), and in the ponerine ant subfamily, where antioxidant enzymatic activity is lower in gamergates than in workers (Schneider et al.
2011
).
On the other hand, there is an increase in catalase transcript abundance and activity in termite queens (Tasaki et al.
2017
), and the same observation is made for Cu/Zn‐superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity (Tasaki, Kobayashi, et al.
2018
). It is noteworthy that catalase is also upregulated in fat bodies of old versus young queens of
T. rugatulus
, suggesting a late‐life up‐regulation of anti‐oxidant shielding that may regulate ageing rate (Negroni et al.
2019
). Thus, it seems that taxon‐specific (ants vs. termites) patterns of antioxidant mechanisms may have differently evolved in the regulation of caste‐specific longevity among social insects (Kramer et al.
2021
).
Comparing mitochondrial and metabolic traits between castes of ants with contrasting longevity is likely to bring new insights into the mechanisms underlying their contrasted life spans. Since all females in a given colony inherit their mitochondria from the founding queen, we sought to explore how mitochondria from a unique maternal lineage are actually associated with different longevities in different individuals. We assessed individual resting metabolic rate (RMR), mitochondrial density, efficiency in ATP production and energy charge, as well as different proxies of oxidative status in queens and workers of
L. niger
. In line with the predictions of the oxidative stress theory of ageing, we hypothesized that mitochondrial characteristics would differ among castes, with long‐lived queens more likely to exhibit a bioenergetic profile associated with long lifespan, that is, low ROS production, high antioxidant capacity and therefore less cellular damage for a given level of oxygen consumption and/or ATP production.
2
Materials and Methods
The initial aim of this study was to perform indirect calorimetry, followed by measurements of mitochondrial function through respiration on isolated mitochondria from the same individuals or on permeabilized tissues. This approach would have allowed us to consider measurements of mitochondrial efficiency, ROS production and membrane potential. However, we could not obtain good quality and reproducible bioenergetic signals when adding substrates of the electron transport chain or ATP when using isolated mitochondria from ants. This led us to revise our experimental approach to assessing the overall energy function of cells, along with ratios of mitochondrial enzyme activities to estimate the cell's oxidative stress status.
We chose, on the one hand, to measure catalase activity due to its role in reducing hydrogen peroxide (H
2
O
2
), one of the main cell ROS. Additionally, we measured total glutathione (GSH + GSSG) levels, often considered one of the most essential intracellular antioxidants. We also used transcriptomic results on antioxidant defences, obtained by other teams, to get a more complete picture (Parker et al.
2004
).
As our focus was on mitochondrial bioenergetics, we complemented our functional assays with a targeted analysis of the mitochondrial proteome using previous proteomic data obtained in our team (Quque et al.
2019
), and conducted microscopy analyses to support our findings.
Finally, we measured adenylate and guanylate energy charge (AEC and GEC) to assess the energetic status of the cells. During these analyses, we observed consistently high levels of the nucleoside inosine in queen samples. Since inosine is a key intermediate in nucleotide metabolism, its presence raised the possibility of a caste‐specific reliance on the purine salvage pathway. We therefore reanalyzed our untargeted metabolomic dataset with a targeted approach, screening for other intermediates and degradation products associated with purine metabolism.

The detailed methodology of

Briefly, 4‐year‐old queens were identified by their larger size, and workers were classified based on their social roles: nest‐workers cared for the queens and eggs, while foragers searched for food outside the nest. 2.2
Oxygen Consumption (Indirect Calorimetry)
We measured O
2
consumption in foragers, nest‐workers, and queens using an Oxygraph O
2
k (Oroboros Instrument GmbH, Innsbruck, Austria) and DatLab software. Typically used with stirred liquid suspensions, such as isolated mitochondria, this system was adapted for measurements in a gaseous phase following a protocol that was recently validated by comparison with classic oxygen consumption methodologies (Kervella et al.
2023
). Oxygen consumption rates were assessed in 6 queens (4 years old) and 22 worker pools across two experimental sessions. Measurements were conducted at 20°C, with the initial 15 min excluded to ensure that ants reached a resting state. Since the ants were not restrained and free to move within the chambers, we refer to these measurements as resting metabolic rate (RMR), rather than basal metabolic rate (BMR).
2.3
Body Mass and Resting Metabolic Rate Normalisation
All pools were weighed immediately after oxygen measurements (Mettler Toledo AG285, ±0.01 mg accuracy). Mean body mass values were (mean ± SD): queens 28.19 ± 5.419 mg, foragers pools 4.27 ± 0.53 mg, nest‐workers pools 5.15 ± 1.06 mg. Resting metabolic rate was calculated by dividing the oxygen consumption rate by the pool mass raised to the power of a caste‐specific allometric coefficient (
α
), as previously determined in the same species (1.039 for queens and 0.6492 for workers respectively) (Kervella et al.
2023
). This allowed to normalise the values by taking into account the distinct morphologies between castes. Following mass measurements, the ants were placed in a 15 mL falcon tube with water‐impregnated cotton before being ground for subsequent measurements of enzymatic activities (aconitase, fumarase and citrate synthase) and total glutathione content.
2.4
Adenylate and Guanylate Energy Charge and
ATP
Content
The Adenylate Energy Charge (AEC), proposed by Atkinson in 1967 (Atkinson and Walton
1967
), is used as a practical index of the physiological status and health of the cells, based on the total amount of energy available for the cell. It is given by the following formula: AEC = ([ATP] + 0.5[ADP])/([ATP] + [ADP] + [AMP]). The normal AEC for viable organisms (eukaryotes as well as prokaryotes) is comprised between 0.8 and 0.9 (Atkinson
1968
; Chapman et al.
1971
), that is, ATP producing reactions are in balance with the ATP consuming reactions. When AEC drops to between 0.7 and 0.5, organisms are still in a viable state, but homeostasis is not maintained (De la Fuente et al.
2014
). Very unfavourable conditions are when AEC < 0.5, a status lethal for most cells (Passman et al.
2023
). Guanylate Energy Charge (GEC), analogous to AEC but for guanine nucleotides, is also an indicator for metabolism that reflects the cell's ability to synthesise proteins (Karl et al.
1978
).
Ants from colonies maintained for over a year (with one queen being older) were identified within their colony through successive foraging marking sessions conducted more than 1 week before measurements (to avoid any stress that may lower AEC value). They were caught gingerly and directly ground with a Dounce homogeniser in an ethanol/Hepes 20 mM (7/3;
v
/
v
) solution precooled at −20°C. Glassware and solutions were kept on dry ice. Samples were stored at −80°C a few days before being sent to the SAM facility (TBMCore Bordeaux). Ethanol/Hepes extracts were evaporated using a rotavapor (3 min, 65°C), and the dried sample was resuspended in 500 μL of nuclease/phosphatase‐free water. Insoluble particles were removed by centrifugation (21,000×
g
, 4°C, 1 h), and the supernatant was ultra‐filtrated on nanosep10K Omega (Pall). Metabolites were then separated on an Integrion chromatography station (Thermo Electron) at 0.38 mL/min and 30°C using an AS11‐HC‐4‐μm column (250 × 2 mm, Thermo Electron) and the potassium hydroxide discontinuous gradient as previously described (Pinson et al.
2023
). Peak area quantification was done by UV absorbance at 260 nm for all nucleotides/nucleosides. Metabolite contents were determined using standard curves obtained with pure compounds.
In a previous study (Hammad et al.
2024
), it appeared that when derived from samples prepared for AEC determination, levels in adenine nucleotides were highly variable probably because of differences in yields of extraction between samples that were cancelled by AEC calculation. For this reason, we also measured the ATP content on 3 other whole ants per group, with ATP Bioluminescence Assay Kit CLS II by Roche, Mannheim Germany implying much less preparative steps. Since the pH optimum is between 7.6 and 8, we increased tricine concentration as suggested in the protocol (from 0.04 to 0.1 M here) to avoid acidification of our samples due to formic acid. The pH never dropped off even before dilution preceding luminescence measurement. Here, homogenizations were performed individually into 50 μL buffer for workers, into 200 μL for queens, with polypropylene micropestles. To remove tissue debris, the homogenised samples were centrifuged at 4°C. They were then diluted by 1:200 before reading plates, to stay in the standard range. Results were normalised using sample protein content with the Bradford method (BioRad).
2.5
Preparation for Enzymatic Measurements
The day after the RMR measurement sessions, the ants were homogenised in 300 μL of phosphate buffer (0.1 M, 5 mM EDTA, triton 0.1%, pH 7.3). Homogenates were immediately placed on ice and centrifuged at 8000×
g
at 4°C for 10 min. Resulting supernatants were used for enzymatic measurements. Aconitase/fumarase activities were assayed within 2 h (see below); other tubes were aliquoted and kept at −80°C for the measurement of the citrate synthase activity.
Homogenates from
Locusta migratoria
flight muscles were previously frozen at −80°C and used as an inter‐plate control. Normalisation of enzymatic activities was done using samples' protein content determined by bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (Pierce Company).
2.6
Aconitase/Fumarase Activity Ratio
The aconitase and fumarase activities were measured in 96 well plates allowing measurement at 240 nm (Couplan et al.
2002

The test is measuring the rate of increase in optical density (slopes) expected to measure reaction rates of these enzymes. Firstly, 50 μL of phosphate buffer (100 mM phosphate buffer, EDTA 5 mM) was introduced in each well, followed by 20 μL of the homogenate or 20 μL of the buffer for negative control (1:4 dilution for Queens because optical density [OD] was too high per se). Samples were measured in duplicate. The reading protocol for measurement of 240 nm wavelength was as follows: (1) Shaking (15 s, 2 mm linear); (2) readings every 20 s on 2 min to evaluate the background and stability; (3) pause for initiation of aconitase reaction with addition of isocitrate solution 120 mM (25 μL) with the aid of a multichannel pipette, shaking as before, 8 min of readings each 20 s with 3 s shaking (slope a); (4) pause for initiation of fumarase reaction with addition of malate 250 mM (25 μL), rest of the procedure as before (slope b). Catalase Activity
The measurements of catalase activity were taken from pools of ants segregated in parallel with the RMR/enzymatic experiments, but the colonies used here were young‐of‐the‐year (so were the queens). The enzyme activity was determined using the Oroboros system, based on the following reaction: H

We used a 750 rpm stirrer at 25°C for data recording with a 2‐s interval and background correction (Figure

2.8
Glutathione Content
Total glutathione (GSH + GSSG) content was determined by the glutathione reductase enzymatic method (Rahman et al.
2006
). We used half area plates to concentrate samples (Greiner Bio‐One 675101) with small volumes of reagents. Glutathione standards were prepared from a 3.28 mM GSSG stock solution for final concentrations from 0.048 to 3.78 μM. A working solution of 5 U/mL of glutathione reductase and 2.6 mM of 5,5′‐dithiobis (2‐nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB) was prepared in assay buffer. All samples were deposited in duplicates. The reaction was initiated by mixing 20 μL of the latter working solution with 35 μL of homogenate or standard. We used 1:12 and 1:3 sample dilution for queens and workers, respectively. Finally, 10 μL of 1.2 mM NADPH solution were added, and the increase in absorbance was recorded at 405 nm wavelength (final volume of 65 μL), with a TECAN spectrophotometer (model Infinite M200). Total glutathione concentrations were normalised using sample protein content (see above).
2.9
In Silico Analysis of Proteomic Mitochondrial Dataset
We conducted an in silico reanalysis of proteomic data previously acquired by our group on ant colonies of the same origin (Quque et al.
2019
), but here focusing only on mitochondrial proteins. The data are available online to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE partner repository (Vizcaíno et al.
2016
) with the dataset identifier PXD006779. Among the 1380 proteins previously quantified, 240 proteins were identified as playing a role in mitochondrial function, after manually filtering for names containing ‘mito’ or the names of electron transport chain complexes. Data analysis with DEP v1.22.0 package was conducted focusing on those mitochondrial proteins' expression among queens, nest‐workers, and foragers on the basis of fold changes (calculated based on Limma V3.56.2) as developed in (Zhang et al.
2018
). To ensure proper analysis, a variance‐stabilising transformation using VSN algorithm was applied (Huber et al.
2002
). Missing values that concerned all individuals in the same group, or even a given caste (see Figure
S3A
), were assumed as missing not at random (MNAR) and were attributed the minimal quantitative value in the dataset; other missing data were assumed as missing at random (MAR) and inferred with the
k
NN (
k
nearest neighbours) algorithm (DEP uses VIM package v6.2), which imputes missing values based on proteins with similar expression profiles (Kowarik and Templ
2016
).

3
Statistics
Statistics analyses were conducted using RStudio v4.0.2 (
http://www.R‐project.org/
) and Prism v9.4.0. Normality of model residuals was verified by the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homoscedasticity by Bartlett's test. For each random variable, social group (queen, nest‐worker or forager) was used as a fixed factor. One‐way ANOVA with Tukey's multiple comparisons test was conducted when normality and homoscedasticity were verified (RMR, AEC, ATP/ADP, ATP content, GEC and aconitase/fumarase ratio). In cases of unequal variances, the Welch ANOVA test was employed with Dunnett's T3 to compare all pairs of variables (mass, citrate synthase activity, ATP/AMP ratio, aconitase, fumarase and catalase activities). In conditions where normality distribution was not verified, comparison on ranks was done with the Kruskal–Wallis test (inosine, inosine reported to the mass and total glutathione levels). Similarly, paired comparisons were done when workers were merged or a subcaste was absent, using Welch's
t
‐test in the case of unequal variances (for mitochondrial volume and density); otherwise, Mann–Whitney tests were used for non‐parametric data (qPCR analysis and circularity index). Specific post hoc test
p
‐values are indicated on the figures. Differential enrichment analysis of our mitochondrial protein dataset was then performed by applying empirical Bayes statistics on protein‐wise linear models using limma v3.54.0 (Zhang et al.
2018
). Fold changes of differentially enriched proteins were illustrated using volcano plots.

Discussion
Our comparison of the mitochondrial functioning in
L. niger
castes underlined several key bioenergetics characteristics. First, measurements of oxygen consumption rate and Krebs cycle enzymatic activities (citrate synthase, aconitase and fumarase) indicated a lower mitochondrial activity in queens than in workers. Secondly, mitochondrial volume and mtDNA/nucDNA ratio suggested that mitochondrial content is similar in queens and workers. Thirdly, the adenylate energy charge of queens' mitochondria was higher, indicating a more favourable energy status that supports maintenance mechanisms. Thus, while queens display a lower resting metabolic rate, this does not necessarily reflect decreased mitochondrial ATP production but may instead indicate a reduced overall energy demand despite the cost of reproduction. Fourthly, oxidative stress appeared to be of lower amplitude in queens, as reflected by (1) a similar level of aconitase/fumarase ratio (i.e., a measure of mitochondrial oxidative damages), (2) stable glutathione levels and lower catalase activity, and (3) proteomic profiles suggesting a specific antioxidant protection for key mitochondrial enzymes or enhanced mitochondrial fission/fusion rates (i.e., a proxy of mitochondrial maintenance). Preliminary microscopy results (see Figure
S9
) also showed lower (no detection) lipofuscin accumulation in queens, supporting the idea that queens may experience less oxidative damage at the cellular level compared to workers. It is important to note, furthermore, that our study used laboratory‐reared individuals, which are not exposed to pollutants and pathogens—two major sources of oxidative stress in natural conditions. Under the controlled environment of the laboratory, the levels of oxidative stress measured in workers may then be potentially underestimated in comparison with natural conditions, the difference between queens and workers being therefore probably more pronounced in the wild. It is also important to note that the time of year when measurements were conducted (before diapause) may influence the differences observed between castes. Such seasonal effects could not be controlled in our study and should be investigated in future work. Overall, our data suggest that ant queens may live longer because they combine lower oxygen consumption with a higher ability to ensure mitochondria maintenance, including some specific antioxidant defences.
Measurements of adenine nucleotides highlighted that AEC was higher in queens. ATP hydrolysis is the primary means of supplying energy to processes that prevent fitness decay in the short term (e.g., preservation of cellular ion gradients, active elimination of waste or incoming poisons) and in the long term (e.g., replacement of altered/aged structures requiring protein and lipid synthesis, DNA repair). Adenosine triphosphatases (that convert ATP into ADP, hereafter ATPases) are sensitive to the concentration of their substrate (ATP) and to the ratio between substrate and product (ATP/ADP ratio). Moreover, the higher the ATP/ADP ratio, the greater the energy released by ATP hydrolysis (Nicholls
2013
) (see below). Consequently, as queens exhibit a higher ATP/ADP ratio, this might indicate that their ATP‐consuming effectors, such as ATPases, benefit from a more efficient use of energy compared to workers. Such a situation would particularly favour ATPases when the cell is short on energy, like those involved in protein recycling including mitochondrial proteins (Xu et al.
2011
), and would guarantee better somatic protection/repair of the queen's organism as a whole. Such an improved maintenance capacity may, in the long term, have a significant positive impact on queen fitness by mitigating damage accumulation (ageing). The extreme difference in longevity observed between closely related individuals (queens and worker ants) was expected to reveal previously described biochemical factors associated with the fitness of the organism (e.g., oxidative stress), not directly under the control of inherited genes (as ants of the same nest are close kin) but rather of task specialization (determined by castes). Our results complete this existing picture by showing that higher AEC could be an important additional factor, as its decrease would mechanically reduce global ATP availability but also the net energy gain per molecule of hydrolysed ATP (due to the Gibbs free energy effect) (Nicholls
2013
; Alberty
1994
). Altogether, such a bioenergetic advantage may allow queens to maintain a more youthful condition. Workers' lifestyle calls for a higher energy demand, that is, a higher ATP turnover rate and consequently a higher metabolic rate. As a consequence, for ATP production to match the energy demand, the metabolic rate will rise and/or the AEC will decrease (with an increase in AMP, the lowest energy state of adenylate). As a consequence, the high ATP consumption that sustains the worker lifestyle may have a long‐term deleterious impact on their maintenance capacities of the soma, by reducing the renewal rate of biochemical structures (including mitochondria themselves). Any unbalanced investment in mitochondrial maintenance will inevitably lead to a higher fraction of damaged components and a faster decay of the performance of workers (senescence), ultimately resulting in a shorter lifespan. Whether such an energy ATP‐dependent trade‐off mechanism is effectively modulating the levels of oxidative damage deserves consideration, as it would explain how ageing is dictated by social status (worker or queen) in eusocial insects. Studies carried out on isolated mitochondria do not show that ROS are produced as a direct consequence of increased oxygen consumption; instead, higher mitochondrial proton conductance (or increased uncoupling) tends to reduce ROS production by decreasing the mitochondrial membrane potential (Brand
2000
). In contrast, altered enzymatic function of redox proteins (of the electron transport chain) tends to favour the release of mitochondrial ROS thus implying a higher demand in terms of repair—a situation that becomes increasingly precarious as repair efficiency declines with age. In the following discussion, we will highlight that queen ants may actually have coevolved a bioenergetic status compatible with a higher investment in mitochondrial maintenance.
Black garden ant queens were found to have a lower (based on citrate synthase activity), or at least comparable (based on mitochondrial genome copy numbers or on total mitochondrial volume) mitochondrial density to that of workers. In the literature, mitochondrial density is often described to increase with advancing age to compensate for altered mitochondrial function due to oxidative damage accumulation, for instance as stated in the trophocytes and fat cells of worker bees (Chuang and Hsu
2013
). Previous observations of young and older queen bees (16 months) found no difference in mitochondrial density (Hsu and Lu
2015
), suggesting that the maintenance of their bioenergetic capacity with age is a particular trait of this long‐lived caste. The higher AEC index in ant queens supports the idea that they have a higher capacity for energy production per mitochondrion compared to workers. Additionally, they have significantly higher amounts of inosine, which could suggest increased utilisation of the purine salvage pathway in the biosynthesis of corresponding nucleotides, thereby avoiding the higher energy costs of de novo purine biosynthesis for their cellular functioning (Ipata et al.
2011
; Srinivasan et al.
2021
). This is especially interesting, as in
C. elegans
, activating the purine salvage pathway at the expense of degradation to uric acid has been shown to boost ATP production while reducing mitochondrial oxidative stress (Yoshina et al.
2022
). These effects help preserve mitochondrial function, delay signs of ageing such as muscle deterioration, and ultimately extend lifespan (Yoshina et al.
2022
).
Supporting this interpretation, hypoxanthine and uric acid—degradation products of inosine (Fasullo and Endres
2015
)—were detected in foragers and nest workers but not in queens, as revealed by reanalysis of our metabolomics data (Quque et al.
2021
). This suggests an enhanced purine salvage in the queens. Our proteomic data showed that several enzymes involved in purine metabolism tended to be more abundant in queens: APRT (adenine phosphoribosyltransferase), NT5C (cytosolic 5′‐nucleotidase), and XDH (xanthine dehydrogenase). Note that HGPRT (hypoxanthine‐guanine phosphoribosyltransferase), the enzyme of the purine salvage pathway that catalyzes the conversion of hypoxanthine (product of inosine) to IMP (see Figure
5
), is absent in insects (Petitgas et al.
2024
), raising questions about the mechanism through which inosine might be recycled. Although data in insects remain scarce, metabolic flux analyses in
Drosophila melanogaster
larvae suggested the existence of a putative inosine kinase (Johnson et al.
1980
). Nevertheless, inosine may also have other roles beyond purine recycling, particularly as a stable intermediate or signaling molecule, as suggested by its reported neuroprotective properties (Srinivasan et al.
2021
; Nascimento et al.
2021
). Furthermore, it should be noted that host purine metabolism may also be influenced by the microbiota (Yamauchi et al.
2020
), whose composition and activity may vary between castes in social insects (Otani et al.
2019
; Liberti et al.
2024
).

Although mitochondrial respiratory complexes are central to oxygen consumption, we found only limited differences in ETC subunits between castes, despite higher metabolic rates in foragers. This suggests that metabolic differences are not primarily driven by ETC composition, but may instead involve variation in mitochondrial density, turnover, or substrate routing via transporters and redox shuttles. In line with this idea, our mitochondrial proteomic profiles revealed increased levels of specific proteins in queens, especially those associated with the mitochondrial membrane (see Figure
6
). Voltage‐dependent anion channel 1 (VDAC1) is the most abundant protein in the mitochondrial outer membrane (Camara et al.
2017
), and these two isoformes of the VDAC1 porine were both more abundant in queens. VDAC1 is important for a variety of mitochondrial functions, including diffusion of metabolites (such as ATP, ADP, NADH, pyruvate), nucleotides and ions (such as Ca
2+
) at the outer mitochondrial membrane (Camara et al.
2017
). The higher expression of VDAC1 as well as ADP/ATP translocase supports the idea that ATP/ADP trafficking is a key parameter of queen bioenergetics. One of the most significant differences between queens and workers concerns two isoforms of the oxoglutarate dehydrogenase enzyme (OGDH, or 2‐oxoglutarate dehydrogenase complex component E1, also referred to as KGDH for
α
‐
ketoglutarate
dehydrogenase). OGDH has been shown to maintain mitochondrial fusion and fission events in both
C. elegans
and human cells (Gallo et al.
2011
). A higher content of 2‐oxoglutarate malate carrier (OGC) that transports 2‐oxoglutarate (or α‐ketoglutarate αKG) in the inner membrane of mitochondria is in line with the idea of a higher mitochondrial maintenance process. These proteomic data support our indication of mitochondrial fission/fusion events being more prone to occur, as also suggested by a first observation in electron microscopy and the lower circularity index measured in queens, indicating more elongated mitochondria (not shown in the results, see Supplementary
Methods
and Figures
S6C
and
S9C
). As altered mitochondrial dynamics with senescence can inhibit mitophagy and lead to accumulation of damaged or dysfunctional mitochondria in cells (Srivastava
2017
), higher rates of fission/fusion events, by regularly rejuvenating the mitochondrial pool, should better protect queens from the deleterious effects of ageing on bioenergetics. To further validate these results, additional microscopic observations and markers of mitochondrial dynamics (e.g., RT‐qPCR on DRP1 and MFN1 for fission and fusion for instance) could help clarify whether specific regulations of mitochondrial biogenesis play a role in queen longevity. Note that while DNA sequences for these markers were actually searched in available databases, they were not found for our species and their expression levels using qPCR characterisation remains to be done.

A high metabolic rate and oxygen consumption imply an inevitable associated risk of oxidative damage to biomolecules by ROS, according to the ‘ROS theory of ageing’. This theory complements the rate of living theory and stipulates that the higher the metabolic rate and the associated oxidative cost, the faster the ageing process. Yet, while ROS are deleterious at high concentrations, they play a Janus‐faced role, as they also act as signalling molecules involved in redox regulation and cellular homeostasis. It is thus the balance between ROS production and antioxidant defences that ultimately determines the oxidative stress status (Poljsak et al.
2013
). Contrary to our initial expectations, the aconitase/fumarase ratio was not found to be higher in the long‐lived and less metabolically active queen caste compared to the worker caste. This suggests that queens and workers ended with similar levels of mitochondrial oxidative insults on aconitase, while RMR is higher in workers. The antioxidant defences were found at lower levels in the long‐lived queens, with catalase activity being lower in queens (or non‐significantly different for total glutathione levels). Our observation aligns with previous findings that report reduced SOD (CuZn) transcript levels in ant queens (Parker et al.
2004
), while metabolomic data highlight an enriched glutathione pathway in workers (Quque et al.
2021
). Collectively, these data across enzymatic, transcriptomic, and metabolomic levels suggest that queens may invest less in traditional antioxidant defences, likely due to their lower RMR and unique social and reproductive roles and relatively lower exposure to environmental stressors. Unlike other social insects, such as termites (Tasaki et al.
2017
; Tasaki, Kobayashi, et al.
2018
), the social shielding provided by workers, who bear the brunt of environmental stress (e.g., Cremer et al.
2007
), may allow queens to spare energy through reducing investment in antioxidant defences. In social insects, trophallaxis, that is, the mouth‐to‐mouth exchange of fluids, not only distributes nutrients across the colony but also transfers hormones, peptides, and metabolites (LeBoeuf et al.
2016
; Tasaki et al.
2023
; Peng et al.
2025
). In termites, it has been shown that workers engage in discriminative trophallaxis, delivering distinct fluid compositions to the king and queen versus workers, with caste‐ and role‐dependent metabolite biases, including compounds linked to antioxidants or longevity in the reproductive caste (Tasaki et al.
2023
; Peng et al.
2025
). In the ant
Camponotus floridanus
, nest‐workers have higher levels of oxidative stress‐related proteins (Hakala et al.
2021
). The authors propose a model of distributed metabolism within the colony, akin to a superorganism and consistent with the oxidative shielding hypothesis (Hakala et al.
2021
). Here, nest‐workers could therefore play an active role in the collective management of oxidative stress by producing and potentially transmitting protective molecules via trophallaxis to the queen and larvae.
In our study, older colonies were founded by queens collected in the same location in Brussels, whereas younger colonies were founded from queens collected immediately after swarming in Berlin from the same urban area (a parking lot). No further information was available regarding their early environmental conditions. Although all colonies were maintained under identical laboratory conditions for several weeks to months prior to sampling, we cannot exclude the possibility that earlier environmental factors may have influenced our mitochondrial traits. These concerns are relevant in our insect model, where larval nutrition and social environment are known to exert strong carry‐over effects on adult phenotypes (Xie et al.
2015
; Wang et al.
2016
). Nevertheless, we ensured that colony size was of similar magnitude among the older colonies used, as colony size, as well as nest space and population density (also standardised) is known to influence global metabolic rate (Gillooly et al.
2010
; Waters et al.
2017
; Cao and Dornhaus
2008
).
Chronological age was not controlled in workers. While behavioural role is a major determinant of phenotype in social insects (Quque et al.
2023
), controlling for age would be essential to detect subtle differences. This remains to be tested experimentally in the future, by synchronising worker cohorts via the removal of nearly mature larvae to prevent the emergence of younger individuals.
In conclusion, our findings support a mitochondrial basis for the remarkable longevity of the black garden ant queens, thereby reframing classical ageing theories. Our study indeed demonstrates that queen longevity extends beyond the simple ROS/antioxidant paradigm, relying on a combination of (1) a higher ATP/ADP ratio that may facilitate more efficient energy release per ATP molecule, (2) a lower metabolic rate that reduces baseline oxidative stress, and (3) the allocation of conserved energy towards mitochondrial maintenance, as suggested by proteomics. Notably, our results highlight the purine salvage pathway as a promising mechanism that may play a role in enabling queens to sustain mitochondrial function and minimise ROS production, thereby potentially reducing oxidative stress without heavily investing in traditional antioxidant defences. In addition, properly exploring differences among castes in the rate of lipofuscin accumulation, which has been observed to accumulate in social bees with chronological age (Martin et al.
2021
; König et al.
2017
) will be of importance. Indeed, the inhibition of mitochondrial fission leads to increased lipofuscin formation (König et al.
2017
), making lipofuscin detection a promising proxy of mitochondrial dynamic/dysfunctionality in our model. The balance between reproductive energy demand and somatic maintenance suggests a sophisticated energy allocation strategy in queens, which could be central to their long lifespan. Future studies exploring mitochondrial efficiency, AEC, and purine salvage pathways across eusocial insects—and even other long‐lived organisms—will be invaluable for validating these hypotheses and enhancing our understanding of the bioenergetic adaptations that support extreme longevity. This perspective opens a fascinating avenue for exploring how selective pressures on reproduction and survival have shaped ageing trajectories via mitochondrial regulation, in relation to sociality.
