In higher eukaryotes, mitochondria are indispensable organelles for cellular metabolism and signaling. Mitochondria house a multi-copy and circular genome, known as mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA). The relatively small (16,569 bp) genome encodes essential protein subunits of the oxidative phosphorylation system, as well as a suite of tRNAs and rRNAs, critical for mitochondrial function (
1
,
2
). Beyond its gene-encoding role, mtDNA can also act as a damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) molecule upon release into the cytoplasm, triggering immune and inflammatory responses (
3
,
4
). Consequently, mtDNA defects are implicated in a spectrum of pathologies, including mitochondrial disorders, neurodegenerative diseases, and aging (
5
,
6
).
mtDNA exists in multiple copies and exhibits heteroplasmy, a state of coexistence of normal and defective mtDNA molecules harboring mutations and deletions. While mitochondria can maintain regular function below a certain threshold of mtDNA heteroplasmy, mtDNA is inherently susceptible to oxidative damage and environmental insults (
7
–
9
), leading to the formation of covalent DNA adducts or lesions. Due to its redundancy and limited repair mechanisms, mtDNA accumulates DNA lesions at higher levels than nuclear DNA (nDNA) upon exposure to genotoxic compounds (
9
). Intriguingly, the increased lesion burden often does not translate to elevated mutation rates (
10
,
11
), but rather a rapid mtDNA copy number decline (
9
), suggesting active degradation and release of damaged mtDNA molecules (
2
,
12
). Indeed, protein factors, such as DNA polymerase γ (
13
,
14
), mitochondrial genome maintenance exonuclease 1 (MGME1) (
13
,
15
,
16
), and mitochondrial transcription factor A (TFAM) (
17
,
18
), have been shown to be involved in mtDNA degradation with different types of mtDNA damage. Furthermore, both native and damaged mtDNA can be extruded from mitochondria, triggering innate immune and inflammatory responses via pattern recognition receptors (
3
,
4
) in the cytoplasm. mtDNA containing certain DNA lesions, such as 8-oxo-7,8-dihydro-2’-deoxyguanosine (8-oxodG), exhibits enhanced proinflammatory potential (
19
–
21
), suggesting a role of mtDNA lesions beyond mutagenesis. The heightened vulnerability of mtDNA and its immunogenic potential have led to its proposed function as a genotoxic stress sensor (
22
,
23
).
In human mitochondria, base excision repair (BER) is the primary DNA repair pathway for removing nucleobase lesions and single-strand breaks (SSBs) (
24
,
25
). This process, analogous to nuclear BER, involves damaged base excision by DNA glycosylases, AP site cleavage by apurinic/apyrimidinic endonuclease 1 (APE1), 5’-deoxyribose phosphate (5’-dRp) removal, nucleotide insertion, and ligation. A critical BER intermediate is the abasic (AP) site, a highly reactive lesion that, if unrepaired, can lead to strand breaks and mutations (
26
,
27
). AP sites can undergo β-elimination, generating 3′-phospho-α,β-unsaturated aldehyde (3′-PUA) residues, which can react with cellular thiols, particularly glutathione (GSH), forming glutathionylated DNA (GSH-DNA) adducts (
28
–
30
). While GSH-DNA adducts have been detected in nDNA (
30
), their biological effects in the nucleus remain unknown. The formation of these relatively bulky adducts raises questions regarding their structural and biological impacts, especially considering the known protective roles of GSH in scavenging other toxic electrophiles, such as oxidative DNA damage products, base propenals (
31
), and lipid peroxidation byproducts (acrolein and 4-hydroxy-2-nonenal) (
32
).
Given the prevalence of mtDNA lesions and their potential signaling roles, quantifying GSH-DNA adducts in mtDNA and elucidating their biological implications are crucial. In this study, we clarified the level, regulation, and biological effects of GSH-DNA adducts in mtDNA. Using isotope dilution mass spectrometry, we quantified GSH-DNA adduct levels in both nDNA and mtDNA from cultured human cells under various genotoxic stress conditions. Our results demonstrate a preferential accumulation of GSH-DNA adducts in mtDNA compared to nDNA in two different cell lines under various conditions, with 13- to 300-fold higher levels in mtDNA than nDNA. We examined the roles of APE1 and tyrosyl-DNA phosphodiesterase 1 (TDP1), two key enzymes in 3’ DNA lesion repair, revealing their respective roles in preventing GSH-DNA adduct accumulation. In addition, the GSH-DNA adduct formation is catalyzed by TFAM and polyamines and regulated by local TFAM concentrations. The accumulation of GSH-DNA adducts is associated with downregulation of several ribosomal and complex I subunit proteins and upregulation of proteins related to redox balance and mitochondrial dynamics. Furthermore, GSH-DNA adducts enhance conformational flexibility of DNA and stabilize TFAM-DNA complexes, as revealed in molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. Together, our findings provide critical insights into the accumulation, regulation, and biological implications of GSH-DNA adducts in mtDNA. Considering that the abundance of GSH-DNA adducts in mtDNA is comparable to the commonly used oxidative DNA damage marker, 8-oxodG, and the emerging role of mitochondrial 8-oxodG in inflammatory response (
19
–
21
), our findings implicate a role of mitochondrial GSH-DNA adducts in innate immunity and inflammation.

TFAM and Polyamines Affect GSH-DNA Adduct Formation.
Within mitochondria, mtDNA is compacted into nucleoid structures by TFAM, potentially restricting GSH access and thus limiting GSH-DNA adduct formation. To further understand the effect of TFAM-DNA packaging on GSH-DNA adduct formation, we performed biochemical assays using AP-DNA substrates and varying TFAM concentrations. Given the millimolar concentrations of biological polyamines (e.g., spermine and spermidine) in the mitochondrial matrix (
45
), and their potential association with mtDNA [inferred from bacterial nucleoid studies (
46
)], we also included 1.2 mM spermine into these reactions. In addition, the ability of spermine to catalyze β-elimination at AP sites (
33
,
34
) motivated its inclusion. We designed two DNA substrates, AP
32
and AP
37

SI Appendix
, Table S1
), with AP lesions located within and outside the LSP, respectively. This design allowed us to explore the effect of TFAM-DNA interactions on GSH-DNA adduct formation, as TFAM preferentially binds to the LSP sequence (
47

Reactions were conducted with 1.2 mM spermine and 1.0 mM GSH under varying times and TFAM concentrations. In the absence of TFAM, AP-DNA was efficiently converted to GSH-DNA fragments, confirming spermine’s catalytic activity (“no TFAM” reactions in

and

Fig. 3

C
and
D

Effects of GSH-DNA Adducts On GSH Homeostasis and Protein Expression.
To understand the impact of GSH-DNA adduct formation on cellular GSH homeostasis, we measured GSH/GSSG ratios and total GSH levels in HEK293A cells. In WT HEK293A cells, 0.1 mM MNU treatment for 8 h did not significantly alter GSH/GSSG ratios, whereas 1 mM MNU induced a decrease (
Fig. 4
A
, black bars), which is likely due to direct reactions between MNU and GSH (
42

Fig. 4
A
, blue bars). Notably, APE1 KO cells exhibited a slight but significant increase in GSH/GSSG ratios relative to WT cells under VC treatment (
Fig. 4
A
, blue bar with #). TDP1 KO cells showed no significant changes in GSH/GSSG ratios upon MNU treatment (
Fig. 4
A
, red bars). We also found no significant difference in total glutathione levels (sum of GSH and GSSG) in any of the three cell lines following 1 mM MNU treatment. These results indicate that mitochondrial GSH-DNA adduct formation is not associated with a disruption of cellular GSH homeostasis under the current experimental conditions.

Given the observed effect of MNU on GSH levels, we next examined doxycycline-inducible Tet-On HeLa and HEK293 cells, with inducible expression of mitochondria-targeting UNG1-Y147A variant to form mitochondrial AP sites. Although a significant GSH-DNA adduct accumulation in mtDNA was observed under our treatment conditions (0.5 μg/mL doxycycline for 24 h) (
Fig. 1
C
), neither the GSH/GSSG ratio nor the total glutathione levels in HeLa cells were significantly altered (
Fig. 4
B
and
SI Appendix
, Fig. S5
A
). Because the mitochondrial GSH pool accounts for 10-15% of total cellular GSH (
51
), we attempted to measure the mitochondrial GSH/GSSG ratio directly. Despite multiple careful attempts at subcellular fractionation, the luminescence-based method is not sensitive enough to detect changes in GSH/GSSG ratios in the mitochondrial lysate. As an alternative approach, we measured the protein level of SLC25A39, a GSH transporter whose increased expression can indicate a local decrease in mitochondrial GSH (
52
). We used the buthionine sulfoximine (BSO)-treated HEK293A APE1 KO cells as a positive control, but there was no significant difference in SLC25A39 levels in either cell line after 72 h of doxycycline induction (
Fig. 4
C
and
SI Appendix
, Fig. S5
C
). These findings provide additional support that GSH homeostasis remains stable at both the cellular and mitochondrial levels, even when GSH-DNA adducts accumulate in the mitochondrial genome.
To further explore the mitochondrial protein level changes under GSH-DNA adduct accumulation, we performed stable isotope labeling by amino acids in cell culture (SILAC)-based quantitative proteomic analysis using Tet-On HeLa cells. Upon isolation of mitochondria from HeLa cells, mitochondrial proteins were isolated and digested, followed by fractionation on HPLC before proteomic analysis. Compared to the untreated control, doxycycline-induced mitochondrial AP sites resulted in significant changes in the expression level of several mitochondrial proteins (
Fig. 4
D
). A decrease in mitochondrial ribosomal proteins (MRPS35, MRPL15, MRPL37, MRPL13, and MRPL4) and complex I subunits (NDUFA12 and NDUFA13) was observed, suggesting a suppression of mitochondrial protein synthesis and energy production under these conditions. Since these proteins are encoded by nuclear genes, the decrease is unlikely due to the decline in mtDNA copy number (
18
).
In addition, the levels of ARF5 (ADP ribosylation factor 5), DNM1L (dynamin-1-like protein), and PRDX6 (peroxiredoxin 6) were upregulated. ARF5 is involved in protein trafficking, and DNM1L mediates mitochondrial and peroxisomal division. PRDX6 is a key player in cellular redox homeostasis, and its increase likely represents a cellular adaptation to GSH-DNA adduct formation. We confirmed the increase in PRDX6 levels in HeLa cells via western blotting (
Fig. 4
E
and
F
). A similar increase in PRDX6 levels was also observed in both WT and APE1 KO HEK293A cells under both VC and MNU-treated conditions. This is likely due to a compensatory upregulation in the absence of APE1, since PRDX6 has been shown to interact with APE1 through immunoprecipitation, particularly with a redox-deficient APE1 variant C65S (
53
). In sum, our results show that mitochondrial GSH-DNA adduct accumulation does not alter cellular or mitochondrial GSH homeostasis, but is associated with changes in mitochondrial protein expression. Specifically, we observed down-regulation of mitochondrial ribosomal proteins and complex I subunits and up-regulation of PRDX6, ARF5, and DNM1L.
Effects of GSH-DNA Adducts on TFAM-DNA Binding and Stability of TFAM-DNA Complexes.
To investigate the impact of GSH-DNA adducts on TFAM-DNA binding, we measured the equilibrium dissociation constant (
K
d
) of TFAM with AP
32
-GSH and canonical DNA substrates using fluorescence polarization assays. Although AP
32
-GSH resulted in a slightly lower
K

compared to the canonical substrate, the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.075). The Hill coefficient decreased significantly from 2.5 (canonical DNA) to 2.0 (AP

Discussion
Our study provides compelling evidence for the abundance and biological importance of GSH-DNA adducts in mtDNA. We have demonstrated that GSH-DNA adducts accumulate in mtDNA at significantly higher levels relative to nDNA in two cell lines under different DNA-damaging conditions, including induced mitochondrial AP sites and alkylation damage from MNU treatment. The accumulation is consistent with BER being the major DNA repair mechanism in mitochondria, with AP sites as a key intermediate. The formation of GSH-DNA adducts is regulated by TFAM-DNA packaging, polyamines, and repair proteins APE1 and TDP1. In addition, we found that the accumulation of these lesions is associated with the downregulation of mitochondrial ribosomal proteins and complex I subunits and the upregulation of a redox-related protein PRDX6, a vesicle trafficking related protein ARF5, and a mitochondrial fission-related protein DNM1L. Furthermore, MD simulations demonstrate that GSH-DNA adducts can also stabilize TFAM-DNA binding due to their conformational flexibility.
Our research highlights APE1 as a critical enzyme in regulating GSH-DNA adduct accumulation, particularly under genotoxic stress. The higher abundance of APE1 and its superior binding to DNA substrates, compared to TDP1, likely contribute to this role. The promiscuous exonuclease activity of APE1 may also contribute to the degradation of DNA fragments containing these adducts. On the other hand, TDP1 may be more adept at processing GSH-DNA lesions in DNA structures other than the tested substrate. For example, TDP1 can process 3′-phosphoglycolate lesions at double-strand breaks (
58
), whereas APE1 has little activity on the terminal modifications at double-strand breaks (
59
).
Our study also uncovers the intricate interplay between TFAM, polyamines, and GSH-DNA adduct formation. TFAM, a key mtDNA packaging and transcription factor, can inhibit GSH-DNA adduct formation by competing for AP sites. Conversely, polyamines, abundant in the mitochondrial matrix, promote GSH-DNA adduct formation, particularly in regions of low TFAM occupancy. We surmise that both TFAM-DPCs and GSH-DNA adducts may act as replication and transcription blocks, potentially marking damaged mtDNA for negative selection or further processing through repair or degradation. The increased stability of TFAM-DNA complexes in the presence of GSH-DNA adducts observed in our MD simulations supports this notion. This structural stability could shield damaged nucleoids from processes like transcription and replication.
The observed association between GSH-DNA adduct accumulation and upregulation of PRDX6 in HeLa and HEK293A APE1 KO cells suggests a protective role for PRDX6 against mtDNA damage. PRDX6, a member of the mammalian Prdx family, detoxifies H
2
O
2
and lipid hydroperoxides using GSH as a reductant. While PRDX6 is primarily localized in the cytoplasm, it can translocate to mitochondria under oxidative stress (
60
). While the upregulation of PRDX6 in HEK293A APE1 KO cells could be due to APE1 deficiency, its upregulation in HeLa cells, where APE1 and GSH homeostasis were not disrupted, argues for its link to GSH-DNA adduct formation. Clearly, the mechanistic linkage between GSH-adduct accumulation and upregulation of PRDX6 needs to be firmly established.
The formation of GSH-DNA adducts via 3′-PUA reactions with GSH shares similarities with GSH conjugation to other DNA oxidation products, such as base propenals (
31
), lipid peroxidation byproducts (acrolein and 4-hydroxy-2-nonenal) (
32
), and the proto-oncometabolite fumarate (
61
). These conjugates often play a role in the detoxification of these reactive electrophiles. The conjugates or their processed derivatives have been suggested as markers for oxidative stress or as a stimulant in ROS-mediated signaling. For example, the mercapturate derivatives of base propenal-GSH conjugates have been detected in rat urine under CCl
4
-induced oxidative stress or treatment with the DNA-cleaving antitumor agent, bleomycin (
31
). Similarly, the proto-oncometabolite fumarate can conjugate with GSH to amplify ROS-dependent signaling in cultured human cancer cells (
61
). Compared to these examples, our study is unique in demonstrating GSH conjugation with a repair intermediate on a macromolecule, mtDNA.
In conclusion, this study provides a comprehensive analysis of GSH-DNA adducts in mtDNA, clarifying their formation, regulation, and biological implications. The level of GSH-DNA adducts in mtDNA is comparable to that of 8-oxodG, a commonly used oxidative DNA damage marker. Given the emerging role of mtDNA and 8-oxodG in innate immunity and inflammation (
19
–
21
), further research into the potential immunomodulatory effects of GSH-DNA conjugates is warranted.
Materials and Methods
Cell Culture.
The doxycycline-inducible Tet-On HeLa (
18
) and Tet-On HEK293 cells (
62
) were prepared as described previously. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Gibco, 12100-046) supplemented with 10% Tet System Approved Fetal Bovine Serum (Takara Bio USA, 631106) under 5% CO
2
at 37 °C. Mitochondria-specific abasic (AP) sites were produced by overexpressing UNG1 Y147A variant under doxycycline induction. Cells were treated with 0.5 μg/mL doxycycline for 24 h when cells reached 50 to 70% confluence. HEK293A TDP1 knockout (TDP1 KO) and HEK293A APE1 knockout (APE1 KO) cells were prepared as previously described (
41
). Cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, 12100-046) supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (Fisherbrand, FB12999102) under 5% CO
2
at 37 °C. DNA alkylation damage was induced using 0.1 or 1 mM MNU (MedChemExpress, HY-34758) for 8 h after cells reached 80% confluence. The VC contained 0.1% DMSO in DMEM. For GS-DNA adduct quantification by nanoLC-MS/MS, cells from four to ten 150-mm dishes were pooled to generate a single data point, corresponding to approximately 35 to 100 million cells per measurement.
Isolation of Mitochondria From Cultured Cells.
Mitochondria were extracted from cells using homemade buffers and components from an mtDNA isolation kit (BioVision, K280). Briefly, 1 × 10
8
cells were resuspended in 10 mL of 1× cytosol extraction buffer, and the suspension was incubated on ice for 10 min. Cells were homogenized using an ice-cold Dounce tissue grinder with 50 passes. The homogenate was centrifuged at 1,200 g for 5 min at 4 °C, yielding a supernatant containing mitochondria, a pellet containing nDNA and residual mitochondria. The supernatant was transferred to a new centrifuge tube, followed by centrifugation at 1,200×
g
for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was then transferred into a new tube. The pellet was resuspended in another 5 mL of fresh 1× cytosol extraction buffer and homogenized again with 50 passes. The centrifugation, separation of the supernatant, and homogenization processes were repeated another two times. To pellet the mitochondria, the supernatant fractions from a total of three centrifugation steps were combined and spun at 16,000 g for another 30 min at 4 °C. To remove nDNA contaminants, the mitochondria pellet was treated with 200 U of turbo nuclease (Millipore Sigma, T4330) in 200 μL of reaction containing 5 mM HEPES buffer pH 7.4, 210 mM mannitol, 70 mM sucrose, 10 mM MgCl
2
, 5 mM CaCl
2
, 0.2% (w/v) BSA, 1× proteinase inhibitor cocktails (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A32955) and 20 mM ascorbate sodium for 30 min at 4 °C. The suspension was supplemented by 1 ml of buffered solution containing 5 mM HEPES (pH 7.4), 210 mM mannitol, 70 mM sucrose, 10 mM MgCl
2
, 5 mM CaCl
2
, 0.2% (w/v) BSA, and 1 M KCl followed by centrifugation at 16,000×
g
for 20 min at 4 °C. The pellet was washed with 1 ml of the aforementioned HEPES buffer twice before further processing.
Reactions of AP site–containing DNA with GSH, spermine, and TFAM.
The AP site–containing DNA oligomers were prepared using deoxyuridine-containing precursor oligomers with a 5′ fluorescein (FAM) label, as described previously (
17
). The resulting AP-containing oligomer was annealed with an unmodified complementary oligomer to form an AP-DNA substrate. Reactions with TFAM contained 0, 2, 4, or 8 µM TFAM, 1 µM of AP
32
(or AP
37
), 30 mM HEPES (pH = 7.4), 90 mM NaCl, and 10 mM EDTA. The reaction was quenched with 100 mM NaBH
4
, followed by incubation for 30 min. The sample was then mixed with 5 volumes of a stop solution containing 90% (v/v) formamide, 10% (v/v) water, 50 mM EDTA, and 0.005% (w/v) bromophenol blue, 0.005% (w/v) xylene cyanol. The reactions with GSH or spermine were performed under the same conditions with the addition of 1 mM GSH and 1.2 mM spermine. Reaction products were separated by the 8% SDS-PAGE stacking on 18% urea-TBE-PAGE containing 90 mM Tris, 90 mM boric acid, and 2 mM EDTA, running in 0.5X SDS-glycine (12.5 mM Tris, 96 mM glycine, 0.5% SDS) and 0.5X TBE (45 mM Tris, 45 mM boric acid, and 1 mM EDTA) mixed buffer at 150 V.
