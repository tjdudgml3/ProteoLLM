Introduction
Cellular senescence is one of the 12 hallmarks
of aging.
Senescent cells (SNCs) show
replicative arrest
with resistance to apoptosis and often increased metabolic activity.
Some also display senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP),
where the cells secrete high levels of inflammatory cytokines, immune
modulators, growth factors, and proteases.
Senescent cells accumulate in many tissues with aging and at sites
of pathology in multiple chronic diseases, including cancer, osteoarthritis
and degenerative disk disease, Alzheimer’s disease, and chronic
obstructive pulmonary disease.
,
Senescent cells also
cause signs of intrinsic chronological aging, such as frailty, osteopenia,
and skin changes. In healthy older adults, skin senescent cells increase
dramatically between ages 60–80.
−
Along with skin dryness
and wrinkling, the skin becomes more susceptible to cancer and shows
delayed healing.
,
It has been proposed that “therapies
that reduce the number of senescent cells or block SASP may be effective
treatments for aging-associated skin deterioration”.
Therapeutic strategies aimed at safely
interfering with the detrimental
effects of cellular senescence, such as the selective elimination
of SNCs or the disruption of the SNC secretome via senolytics, have
been garnering significant research attention.
,
Senolytics
are small active molecules that selectively induce apoptosis of senescent
cells, and are an ideal therapeutic agent in that they induce death
of aged cells in a normal, controlled manner, as would naturally occur
in an organism.
Senolytic agents have been identified by bioinformatics
approaches
as well as high-throughput drug library screens.
A large number of compounds, many from plants, have been
identified as having senolytic activity; many of these fit within
the general class of polyphenolic flavonoids. A subset of this class
are the flavonols, which include quercetin, fisetin, rutin, and others
(
Figure
A). These
compounds are highly redox active, accounting for their antioxidant
activity, and are also hydrophobic, making them insoluble in water.
Developing delivery mechanisms for stable, soluble formulations of
flavonoids is thus a significant challenge. The most common delivery
route is systemic and oral. There is an exploding market of ingestible
senolytic supplements which have not been evaluated by regulatory
agencies, as well as formulations in clinical trials.
,

Fisetin (
Figure
B), first extracted from venetian sumach (
Cotinus
coggygria
or smoke tree),
has
been shown to be
an effective
senolytic
agent in wild-type mice,
with effects of increased lifespan, reduced senescence markers in
tissues, and reduced age-related pathologies.
Fisetin induces apoptosis by inhibition of the antiapoptotic protein,
and inhibits the activity of several pro-inflammatory
cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor α, interleukin 6,
and nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB).
The anti-inflammatory action is due to deacylation of the pro-inflammatory
transcription factor NF-κB by sirtuin 1.
Fisetin has roughly twice the senolytic potency of quercetin.
The photophysical properties of fisetin have
been explored in several studies, demonstrating shifts in the excitation
and emission spectra between the neutral, photoautomer, and anionic
forms of the molecule
(
Figure
C).
One of the biggest
unsolved questions in the field of senolytic
research is that of deliverysystemic (usually oral), regional
(e.g., injection into a joint), or topical.
,
,
Oral and topical therapies may complement
each other, so the use of both may be recommended. A large number
of cosmetics and cosmeceuticals have appeared in recent years that
contain plant-based extracts containing polyphenols or purified polyphenols,
,
but these are mostly untested for activity or stability. Emulsions
are the most common delivery vehicle for topical application.
Stabilization of polyphenols for delivery may be performed using
micro-or nanoencapsulation.
,
Many methods for encapsulation
of hydrophobic molecules are available: lipid micelles or liposomes,
solid lipid nanoparticles, silica nanoparticles, nanogels, lecithin/chitosan
particles, and others. These encapsulation techniques are widely used
in medicine for delivery of hydrophobic anticancer drugs, and are
of emerging interest for local and systemic treatment of skin cancers
and other skin conditions.
,
Skin presents specific
barriers to delivery; any nano- or microparticle formulation for skin
must be able to penetrate the stratum corneum, placing specific demands
on particles not seen with injectable formulations.
Most reported
stable polyphenol preparations contain ethoxylated
surfactants such as polyethylene glycol (PEG) and its derivatives.
For example, the vitamin-E based D-α-tocopheryl polyethylene
glycol (TPGS) has been reported in fisetin encapsulation to enhance
anticancer properties of fisetin in breast cancer cell assays.
Quercetin has also been encapsulated in nanoparticles
containing TPGS.
,
A second-generation surfactant,
TPGS-750-M, is especially prevalent in medicinal chemistry reactions.
,
However, PEGs are not readily biodegradable and hence pose
environmental
concern, and may also cause side effects such as immunogenicity;
,
they also form peroxides upon prolonged exposure to air, which can
damage encapsulation structures such as liposomes. Because of these
concerns, there are emerging efforts to provide alternatives. A recent
report of a new PEG-free surfactant called Savie provides an alternative
to PEGs and in particular to TPGS.
Named
for “sarcosine + Vitamin E,” Savie has the same lipophilic
moiety as TPGS (tocopherol) but uses a polysarcosine polypeptide as
the alternative to PEG.
Savie has been reported in several subsequent
studies on micellar
catalysis,
,
but to our knowledge, this is
the first study using Savie-based nanoparticles in cell targeting
applications. This study reports synthesis and photophysical characterization
of lecithin-chitosan-Savie nanoparticles containing fisetin and delivery
of the encapsulated fisetin to cultured fibroblasts, with analysis
of the stability of the encapsulated fisetin over a period of ten
months.
Results and Discussion
Appearance, Size, Stability, and Encapsulation Efficiency
Fisetin encapsulated in lecithin-chitosan alone, without surfactant,
resulted in elongated, micron-sized particles that precipitated from
solution within several days (
Supporting Information Figure S1
). When Savie was added to the synthesis, the nanoparticle
emulsion was translucent in appearance and did not precipitate. No
discrete particles could be visualized under fluorescence microscopy.
Encapsulation efficiency is often performed using high-performance
liquid chromatography (HPLC).
However,
because of its bright fluorescence, the encapsulation of fisetin could
be estimated using a fluorescence-based assay. After separation of
nanoparticles from unencapsulated fisetin by centrifugation, the weight
of fisetin in the unencapsulated supernatant was estimated as WT­(SUP).
Encapsulation efficiency EE was then calculated as

Antioxidant Activity
DPPH is a stable free radical
with an absorbance peak at 517 nm, appearing purple. In presence of
antioxidants its odd electron becomes paired, resulting in loss of
this peak and a shift of its visible appearance from purple to yellow.
The antioxidant Trolox may be used as a standard to decolorize DPPH,
and the efficacy of any given antioxidant expressed as the Trolox
equivalent antioxidant capacity (TEAC) as given by
5

=
IC
50
_
Trolox
/
IC
50
_
fis
where the IC
50
of each compound
is the concentration required for half-maximal decolorization activity.
On 3 separate experiments run on 3 separate days, we found that
the 50% activity concentration of Trolox was 45.3 ± 0.6 μg/mL
(
Supporting Information Figure S3A
). For
pure fisetin, the results were more variable, with values of 17 ±
2 μg/mL found in 3 experiments (
Figure S3B
). The TEAC for pure fisetin was thus 2.7 ± 0.3, in excellent
agreement with literature results of 2.80 ± 0.06.
For the nanoparticles, 3 replicates were performed
on the same day. The fresh nanoparticles showed 50% activity at a
dilution factor of 0.091 ±. 004, or approximately an 11-fold
dilution, meaning that the undiluted particles had the antioxidant
capacity of (17 ± 2 μg/mL)/dilution factor, or 190 ±
20 μg/mL of pure fisetin, in very good agreement with the 250
μg/mL measured with absorbance (both peaks) and 190 μL/mL
measured using UPLC-MS. The 10-month aged particles had 50% activity
at a dilution factor of 0.48 ±. 07, or equivalent to 35 ±
4 μg/mL of fisetin, compared with the 15 μg/mL calculated
from absorbance at 320 nm and UPLC-MS (
Figure S3C
). DPPH was chosen for this assay because it is commonly
reported in the literature, allowing for a comparison with values
using free fisetin and other types of encapsulated fisetin.
,
−
However, DPPH is not the only antioxidant assay available
in vitro
,
and not a good measure
of what antioxidant activity might be expected
in vivo
; the DPPH assay also presents several kinetic and chemical pitfalls.
We thus used it only as a method of comparing
fisetin concentration with absorbance and UPLC-MS measurements. A
cell-based assay was used to evaluate effects in living cells (see
below).
Cell Uptake
When monitored using widefield fluorescence,
cultured fibroblasts could be seen to exhibit noticeable fisetin fluorescence
even over the background within 5–10 min of nanoparticle addition.
There was strong perinuclear labeling suggesting endosomal uptake
(
Figure
A,B). Co-labeling
the particles with rhodamine lipid showed that the majority of fisetin
was still associated with the particles after cell uptake, although
some free fisetin was seen, particularly near the cell membrane (
Figure
C). In fluorescence
lifetime images, fisetin around the exterior cell membrane was associated
with a very distinct short lifetime of <1 ns, while fisetin inside
the cells had a lifetime of ∼2 ns, varying slightly in different
cell compartments (
Figure
D,
Table
).
A phasor plot showed a clear separation of these two forms of fisetin
(
Figure
E). There
were no apparent qualitative differences between fresh and aged particles
in the cell experiments (not shown). Cytotoxicity was not observed
at these short incubation times, but a full investigation remains
to be performed on different cell lines, including cancer cells vs
healthy primary cells and cells driven to senescence by serial passaging,
radiation, or exposure to chemotherapeutic agents.

Conclusions
This study examined the use of a newly
reported surfactant, Savie,
for encapsulation of fisetin for delivery to cells. We found that
the capsules were remarkably stable, showing no significant change
in size after ten months of storage in water under ambient conditions
and retaining significant fluorescence and antioxidant activity.
All of the data are consistent with one fraction of the fisetin
being tightly complexed in the particles, and another fraction more
accessible to the solution (

).

activity. It does not show the absorbance peak at 370 nm but retains
a peak at 320 nm. The tightly complexed fraction likely corresponds
to an increased fluorescence lifetime of close to 6 ns, which represents
80% of the amplitude of the decay in the aged particles. The antioxidant
capacity as measured by DPPH is consistent with the concentration
estimated by A
320
and UPLC-MS. It must be noted that the
quantum yield calculation for the aged particles appeared artificially
high because there was no apparent absorbance peak at 374 nm.
The Savie particles are taken up rapidly by cultured fibroblasts.
The fisetin is released from the lipid as indicated by distinct partitioning
of fisetin fluorescence and labeled phosphatidylcholine. Fluorescence
lifetimes in the cell membrane were markedly shorter than inside the
cells. The nanoparticles show strong antioxidant activity in cells,
with empty nanoparticles showing an approximately half-maximal effect
that plateaus with concentration. The fisetin-containing nanoparticles
show additional concentration-dependent antioxidant activity with
an IC
50
lower than that seen of fisetin alone, indicating
high bioavailability, consistent with previous results using a variety
of other nanoencapsulation techniques.
Future work will involve quantifying senolytic activity of
the

in vitro
and
in vivo
using
different cell lines and animal models. Other hydrophobic molecules,
such as chemotherapeutic agents often encapsulated using TPGS,
,
may also be encapsulated using Savie and will be the subject of
future investigations.
Experimental Procedures
Synthesis and Purification
Savie was a gift of Bruce
Lipschutz, University of California, Santa Barbara. Unless specified
otherwise, all other chemicals were purchased from Millipore Sigma
and used as provided. Nanoparticles were synthesized according to
a procedure modified from the literature
,
by adding a mixture of fisetin (Phyproof Reference Substance, Catalog
#
PHL82542
) (0.04 wt %) and egg phosphatidylcholine (Catalog #P3556)
(0.2 wt %) in 95% ethanol dropwise to a 1% v/v acetic acid solution
(1 mL ethanol:11.5 mL acid) containing dissolved mushroom chitosan
(Sandream Specialties, Fairfield, NJ) (0.02 wt %) and Savie (1.5 wt
%), under rapid stirring to produce spheres of encapsulated fisetin
via ionic gelation. Particles were also produced without Savie for
comparison, with all other procedures being identical. To produce
red-fluorescent nanoparticles, 1% of rhodamine-labeled phosphoethanolamine
[1,2-dioleoyl-
sn
-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-(lissamine
rhodamine B sulfonyl)] (Avanti Polar Lipids catalog #810150) was added
with the phosphatidylcholine. The rate of dropwise addition was maintained
at 500 μL/min using a syringe pump (Harvard Apparatus PHD Ultra).
Particles were purified by dialysis by enclosing 5 mL of particle
solution in 3500 molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) dialysis tubing (SnakeSkin,
ThermoFisher Scientific) and dialyzing against 4 L of 0.9% saline
with 3 buffer changes over 24 h. The particles were then placed into
sterile 15 mL tubes and stored under ambient conditions.
Aging Studies
A sample of the particles was left in
a 15 mL Falcon tube at ambient conditions (fluorescent lighting, 20–23
°C) for 2–10 months and then subjected to the same analysis
as the fresh particles. Particles were also photoaged by placing a
sample in a UV-transparent tube lengthwise onto a UV transilluminator
(Fisher Scientific, Pittsburgh, PA) for a set period of time. The
transilluminator wavelength is 312 nm and power at the sample was
measured using a Coherent FieldMaster power meter with an IM2 UV sensor
to be ∼900 μW/cm
2
.
Encapsulation Efficiency
After synthesis, particles
were centrifuged in an Amicon Ultracel 10 kDa MWCO filter unit (MilliporeSigma,
Burlington, Massachusetts) and the flow-through was collected. Fluorescence
of the flow-through was compared with a standard curve in the same
solvent to determine the total weight of fisetin remaining in the
supernatant for use in
eq
.
Antioxidant Assay
Antioxidant activity of fisetin nanoparticles
was quantified using the DPPH antioxidant assay kit (Dojindo Molecular
Technologies), where antioxidant capacity is measured against a Trolox
standard and expressed as the Trolox equivalent antioxidant capacity
(TEAC).
Specifically, the absorbance
of a DPPH solution at 517 nm was measured vs concentration of Trolox
for 5 concentrations ranging from 0 to 80 μg/mL. A linear fit
to this curve gave the concentration of Trolox needed to reduce 50%
of DPPH (IC
50
_Trolox). A calibrated dilution series of
the fisetin nanoparticles was then used to get IC
50
_fis,
and TEAC was given by
eq
.
Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)
Glow discharged,
carbon coated Formvar copper grids, 400 mesh, were floated onto 5
μL of a 1:100 dilution of nanoparticles for 5 min. Excess solution
was wicked off with filter paper and stained with 1% aqueous uranyl
acetate for 30 s. Stain was removed with filter paper, then the sample
was air-dried and examined on a FEI Tecnai T12 TEM operated at 80
kV. Digital images were acquired with an AMT Nanosprint12 4k x 3k
camera.
Particle Sizing and ζ Potential
Nanoparticle
diameter and ζ potential were measured on a Malvern Zetasizer
ZS90.

Bulk time-resolved
measurements were performed with a Chronos frequency-domain fluorescence
lifetime spectrometer (ISS, Urbana, IL). An external function generator
modulated the polarized excitation source at high frequency, producing
a sinusoidal wave with a typical frequency range of 5–250 MHz.
A reference quantum counter and PMT were employed to adjust for any
variations in laser intensity and output. Excitation was at 374 nm
and 15 modulation frequencies were used, logarithmically spaced from
5 to 250 MHz. Temperature was varied and maintained using a water-circulating
thermostat. Samples were allowed to equilibrate for at least 10 min
after the water bath attained a new set point temperature before the
lifetime measurement. Emission was collected through a 513/50 bandpass
filter (Newport). All lifetime measurements were made with the emission
polarizer at the magic angle of 54.7° from the vertical polarized
excitation beam, and coumarin 153 (⟨τ⟩ = 4.3 ns)
in methanol as a lifetime reference.
Fits
were obtained using Vinci software (ISS).
Cells
The cell line WT SV40 MEF was obtained from American
Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA) and cultured in Iscove’s
Modified Dulbecco’s Medium (IMDM) (12440, Thermo Fisher Scientific,
Waltham, MA) with 10% fetal bovine serum (A5256701, Thermo Fisher
Scientific), 1% nonessential amino acids (11140, Thermo Fisher Scientific)
and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin (15140122, Thermo Fisher Scientific).
Cells were grown in a dry incubator at 37 °C with 5% atmospheric
CO
2
. For passage, cell detachment was induced with 0.25%
Trypsin-EDTA (25200056, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cells were then
transferred to 35 mm glass-bottom dishes and incubated overnight before
addition of nanoparticles. For imaging, 15 μL undiluted Savie-fisetin
nanoparticles were added and cells were monitored in real time using
widefield fluorescence imaging for uptake, which occurred within 10–20
min. When labeling was judged sufficient, cells were washed 2x in
phenol-red-free IMDM and fixed with 3% paraformaldehyde for imaging.
Cellular Antioxidant Assay
Cellular antioxidant assays
used the fluorescent reporter CellRox Orange (Life Technologies) according
to the manufacturer’s instructions. Subconfluent SV40 MEF cells
in a 75 mm
2
flask were harvested by trypsinization and
suspended in complete medium at a concentration of 10
5
–10
6
cells/mL. The cells were divided into 1 to 2 mL aliquots
according to the number of samples needed and incubated for 1h with
antioxidant (buffer only or empty nanoparticles as negative controls;
pure fisetin in DMSO; nanoparticles containing fisetin at concentrations
of 20–80 μM; or
N
-acetylcysteine as
a positive control at concentrations of 0.5–5 mM). The cells
were then subjected to oxidative stress by incubation with 400 μM
tert
-butyl hydroperoxide (TBHP) for 1 h, then stained with
1 μM CellRox Orange for 1 h. Cells were pelleted by centrifugation
(8 min at ∼1000
g
), resuspended in phosphate-buffered
saline to equal cell concentrations, and scanned immediately in a
plate reader in a 96-well black plate. After obtaining the scans,
SYTO60 staining and/or manual counting was used to confirm matching
cell concentrations. Three replicates were performed in each experiment,
and four independent experiments were performed using cells at different
passage numbers each 1 week apart. The normalized response was constructed
by using the negative control as the value of 1.0; this negative control
could represent no antioxidant or an equivalent volume of empty nanoparticles.
The value of 0 was determined by subtracting a curve representing
the maximal response at a given instrument gain, obtained using >5
mM NAC or >100 μL/mL nanoparticles. Plotting and curve fits
used Prism 9.0.
Fluorescence Lifetime Imaging Microscopy (FLIM) and Fluorescence
Microscopy
Lifetime images were obtained on a ZEISS LSM 880
confocal system with a Becker & Hickl SPC-150 FLIM module with
a BIG2 detector having an output channel of 500–550 nm. Excitation
was provided by a Chameleon Ultra Ti:sapphire laser (Coherent) operating
at a rep rate of 80 MHz. The two-photon wavelength used for excitation
was 750 nm. Data analysis was performed using SPCImage (Becker &
Hickl). Fluorescence images were taken on a Keyence BZ-X fluorescence
microscope with a 60x Plan Apochromat 60x oil (numerical aperture
1.40) and two filter cubes: DAPI excitation (360/40)/GFP emission
(525/50) for fisetin, and Texas Red (560/40 Excitation, 630/75 Emission)
for visualizing rhodamine lipids.
