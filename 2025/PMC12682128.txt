CCR4-NOT is a highly conserved complex that regulates various stages of gene expression in both the nucleus and the cytoplasm. Initially identified and characterized in yeast as regulators of the TATA-binding protein and transcription initiation, the subunits of CCR4-NOT have also been shown to play roles in transcription elongation and chromatin modifications by influencing the activity of histone-modifying complexes (
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
). However, comparatively less is known about its function in metazoans, particularly its nuclear functions. In the cytoplasm, CCR4-NOT functions as the major mRNA deadenylase, initiating mRNA decay by degrading the 3′ poly-A tail. It associates with translating ribosomes, where it monitors the translatability of mRNAs and directs the decay of untranslatable messages and those with many non-optimal codons (
1
,
2
,
3
,
6
,
7
). Multiple studies have demonstrated that the post-transcriptional functions of the yeast and human complexes are conserved (
6
).
The shortening of the poly-A tail is often the rate-limiting step in mRNA decay (
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
). In the classical model of mRNA degradation, deadenylation at the 3′ end of the message is initiated by the Pan2/Pan3 deadenylase, followed by the further shortening of poly-A tail by the CCR4-NOT complex, although other models have been proposed (
12
). The deadenylated RNA is then decapped by Dcp1/2, degraded in either 5′-> 3′ direction by Xrn1 or in the 3′ -> 5′ direction by the exosome. Human CCR4-NOT contains one of two CNOT7/CNOT8 DEDD type deadenylases (yCaf1) and one of two CNOT6/CNOT6L exonuclease-endonuclease-phosphatase [EEP] family of deadenylases (yCcr4) (
13
). The complex is recruited to target mRNAs
via
RNA-binding proteins (RBP) or the miRNA machinery to specific sequences located within the 3′UTRs. The large scaffold subunit CNOT1 is critical for bridging the RBP and the deadenylase subunits, targeting mRNAs for degradation. Thus, CNOT1 plays a critical role in the function and integrity of the complex (
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
).
Although this complex is well-studied in budding yeast, comparatively less is known about the functions of human CCR4-NOT. The yeast and mammalian Ccr4-Not complexes have similar overall composition and structures, especially the “core” subunits, but there are some key differences, including the presence of distinct subunits (
1
,
2
,
3
). Another function of the complex is protein ubiquitylation, which is carried out by yNot4/hCNOT4, a RING domain-containing E3 ligase. Yeast Not4p is an integral subunit of the complex and is tethered directly to Not1 through an alpha-helix in the C-terminus of Not4 (
19
,
20
,
21
). In contrast, CNOT4 in metazoans does not co-purify with the complex and lacks the alpha helix found in yeast Not4 that connects it to the core complex (
13
,
22
). Instead, CNOT4 binds to CNOT9, an integral subunit of the complex (
17
). The reason why the CNOT4-CCR4-NOT interaction evolved to be weaker and mediated through different subunits is not known. Not4 plays an essential role in co-translational quality control (
23
). Indeed, mutation of Not4 resulted in reduced polysomes, increased protein aggregation, and accumulated poly-ubiquitinated proteins (
24
). Furthermore, both yeast and mammalian CCR4-NOT ubiquitylate eS7, a 40S ribosomal subunit, that regulates the decay of mRNAs enriched in non-optimal codons (
25
,
26
).
Not1/CNOT1, the largest protein of the complex, is required for the assembly of CCR4-NOT. Knocking down CNOT1 in cells leads to decreased levels of many subunit proteins and disruption of the complex (
27
). It is, therefore, indispensable for the functions of CCR4-NOT. It forms a platform for many RNA-binding proteins and directs the recruitment of the CNOT7 and CNOT6(CCR4) deadenylases to target mRNAs (
28
,
29
,
30
,
31
). CNOT1 depletion leads to caspase-dependent apoptosis and cell death and is considered essential in most cell types (
27
). A recent study showed that siRNA depletion of CNOT1 resulted in increased global stability of mRNAs, preferentially those enriched for G/C-ending codons (
32
). Another study showed that siRNA knockdown of CNOT1 increased the level of the general transcription factor TATA-binding protein (TBP) leading to increased transcription, elevated R-loop formation, decreased genome stability, and cell death (
33
). Depletion of CNOT1 by siRNA suppressed cell growth in osteosarcoma cells by inhibiting Hedgehog signaling (
34
). Thus, CNOT1 is important for critical functions. A few studies have interrogated the function of human CCR4-NOT by targeting the catalytic deadenylases CNOT6/6L and CNOT7/8 (
35
,
36
,
37
). The aforementioned studies used days-long shRNA/RNAi treatments, knockout cell lines, and transgenic organisms. Since cells can adapt to chronic depletion of genes, it is important to analyze the immediate effects of rapidly depleting CCR4-NOT subunits. Furthermore, to our knowledge, a genome-wide analysis of the roles of CNOT4 in mRNA decay has not been conducted; however, a few studies have suggested that CNOT4 may function as a positive regulator of the deadenylase activity of the complex (
17
,
38
).
We established an auxin-inducible degron (AID) strategy to efficiently and rapidly deplete CNOT1 and CNOT4 to investigate the function of these critical subunits of the CCR4-NOT complex. We demonstrate that, consistent with other studies, depletion of CNOT1 results in the disassembly of the complex and global stabilization of mRNAs. Although CNOT4 did not co-purify with CCR4-NOT, proximity labeling (BioID) indicates it associates with the complex
in vivo
. Despite interacting with multiple CCR4-NOT subunits in cells, depleting CNOT4 did not affect the integrity of the complex and, remarkably, its depletion led to accelerated mRNA turnover. Furthermore, we demonstrated that the rapid depletion of CNOT1 and CNOT4 influenced mRNA turnover in a manner that correlated with codon optimality content. This finding supports the previously established relationship between codon optimality, mRNA half-lives, and the role of CCR4-NOT in co-translational decay.

Discussion
In this study, we utilized a rapid auxin-inducible degron (AID) strategy to investigate the functions of the hCCR4-NOT complex by specifically depleting its core subunit, CNOT1, and the E3 ligase subunit, CNOT4. Our findings confirm previous research on CNOT1 and expand our understanding of the role of CNOT4 in mRNA expression and degradation, as well as its functional relationship with the human CCR4-NOT complex. The results revealed both expected and unexpected outcomes, particularly highlighting that CNOT1 and CNOT4 exert opposite effects on mRNA regulation.
The rapid depletion strategy used here offers the advantage of minimizing secondary effects and allows us to observe the immediate effects of depleting CCR4-NOT on the transcriptome. In both cases, we achieved rapid (<60 min) and complete depletion of the proteins (<10%). Using this method, we detected changes in gene expression as early as 2 h after CNOT1 depletion. As anticipated, most transcripts increased in expression and were highly stabilized in CNOT1-depleted cells. An exception to this trend were the mRNAs belonging to the fourth quartile, which are the most stable; these mRNAs showed a decreased half-life. The reason for the increased decay of this group is unclear, but we speculate that, in the absence of CCR4-NOT, other deadenylases may be degrading these mRNAs. The overall stabilization of transcripts in CNOT1-depleted cells aligns with the role of CNOT1 in maintaining the integrity of the complex and stabilizing the deadenylase subunits (as shown in
Figs. 2
and
3
) and others demonstrating that depleting CNOT1 or the deadenylase subunits CNOT6/6L and CNOT7/8 extends mRNA half-life (
32
,
37
,
60
,
66
,
67
). Although the mode of depletion (acute
versus
chronic) differed between our study and that of Gillen
et al.
, the data on the effects on mRNA half-life between the two studies correlated quite well overall (R = 0.45,
p
< 4.8e-235). It was not surprising to find that their data (48 h depletion) correlated best with our longest depletion data set at 24 h. While the long-term depletion strategy for the essential CNOT1 protein has its limitations, both studies and approaches provide valuable insights into the function of CCR4-NOT. One difference between our study and that of Gillen
et al.
is that they reported mRNAs enriched for G/C-ending codons as being particularly sensitive to CNOT1 depletion, whereas we did not find that mRNAs with G/C terminal codons were more affected in CNOT1-depleted cells (not shown). It remains to be determined whether this discrepancy is due to differences in cell types or depletion methods. We did, however, find that mRNAs enriched in non-optimal codons were more strongly affected by CNOT1 depletion than those with more optimal codons (
Fig. 9
,
C
and
D
). This finding is consistent with a recent study showing that human CCR4-NOT binds to ribosomes and accelerates the degradation of mRNAs with non-optimal arginine codons (
21
,
25
). Further support for this function was provided by the finding that the cells depleted of CNOT3 showed increased expression and stability of reporter mRNAs featuring non-optimal codons (
21
). Ongoing studies aim to investigate how the depletion of CNOT4 impacts global translation. The pursuit of this is beyond the scope of this work.
Our proximity labeling experiments confirmed that although CNOT4 did not co-purify with the complex [
Fig. 3
and (
13
)], it does associate with the complex in cells (
Fig. 4
). A high-throughput analysis of proximity labeling of RNA-binding proteins using the original BirA∗ biotin ligase in HEK293 cells also found that CNOT4 was labeled by other CCR4-NOT subunits (
46
). Therefore, in at least two cell lines, CNOT4 is in close proximity to the “core” subunits of the CCR4-NOT complex. As suggested by other studies, the interaction is weak and unable to withstand biochemical isolation procedures (
1
,
5
). Furthermore, the collection of proteins interacting with CNOT4 and CNOT1 overlapped considerably, including proteins not found in the CCR4-NOT complex. Although CNOT4 associates with CCR4-NOT in cells, depleting CNOT1 and CNOT4 caused distinct phenotypes. Specifically, depleting CNOT1 destroyed the complex, led to G1/S arrest and ultimately cell death, while CNOT4-depleted cells displayed no such phenotypes. These differences can be explained by the effects of depleting either protein on the integrity of the CCR4-NOT complex. However, both
Cnot1
and
Cnot4
KO mice are embryonically lethal, indicating CNOT4 is essential for development (
52
,
68
).
The mRNA regulatory functions of the core subunits of the CCR4-NOT complex have been well studied. To our knowledge, this is the first report on the effects of CNOT4 depletion on mRNA decay rates. CNOT4 is dispensable for CCR4-NOT assembly (
Fig. 3
), and its role in mRNA regulation in metazoans was less clear. Surprisingly, CNOT4-depleted cells actually displayed accelerated RNA decay (
Fig. 7
C
). An analysis of adenylated
versus
non-adenylated histone mRNAs showed that this process is poly-A tail dependent (
Fig. S10
,
C
–
E
). The increased decay in CNOT4-depleted cells was unexpected, considering recent studies predicting it would promote mRNA decay. The first study demonstrated that tethering CNOT4 (and fly CNOT4) to a reporter mRNA accelerates mRNA turnover (
17
). However, the accelerated turnover of the reporter mRNA in this assay may have resulted from CNOT4's ability to recruit CCR4-NOT to the mRNA, rather than a specific function of CNOT4. Other evidence supporting Not4's positive role in mRNA decay comes from studies in yeast, where Not4-dependent ubiquitylation of the small ribosomal subunit eS7a enhances co-translational decay of mRNAs (
26
). Since CNOT4 ubiquitylates mammalian eS7a (
25
), we anticipated that CNOT4 would likewise promote degradation of mRNA.
The accelerated turnover in CNOT4-depleted cells is similar to what was observed in cells depleted of RNF219, a RING domain-containing E3 ligase that associates with CCR4-NOT. Depleting RNF219 accelerated mRNA decay (
48
). The authors of the prior study showed that RNF219 attenuates the deadenylase activity of CCR4-NOT. The authors proposed that RNF219 functions by either impeding the access of poly-A RNA to the catalytic module or by reducing the catalytic activity of deadenylases. CNOT4 could do the same. RNF219 binds more strongly to the CCR4-NOT complex than CNOT4, as it co-purifies with the complex, unlike CNOT4. Both CNOT4 and RNF219 bind to CNOT9 (
17
,
48
). CNOT4 and RNF219 may function redundantly in co-translational decay and in the absence of one, the other compensates.
We hypothesize that the accelerated mRNA turnover resulting from CNOT4 depletion is related to its function in inhibiting CCR4-NOT co-translational degradation of transcripts. We found that changes in mRNA half-life in CNOT4-depleted cells correlated with codon optimality, but in a manner that was contrary to our expectations and what was seen in CNOT1-depleted cells (
Fig. 9
,
E
and
F
). Interestingly, the mRNAs with the highest codon optimality—which are typically highly stable and less sensitive to CCR4-NOT-dependent decay—were the most destabilized in CNOT4-depleted cells. Since the core CCR4-NOT complex remained intact in CNOT4-depleted cells, it is capable of interacting with ribosomes and degrading messages. CCR4-NOT4-dependent degradation may be the “last resort” when translation cannot resume or is significantly delayed. CNOT4 could act as a checkpoint factor or an attenuator, allowing time to resolve translation arrest and rescue the message from degradation. In the absence of CNOT4, these messages are more rapidly degraded by CCR4-NOT. This suggests that CNOT4, like yNot4, is involved in the co-translational decay pathway, but its action on translation differs and may be related to the weaker mode of interaction with the CCR4-NOT core complex. CNOT4's binding to the CCR4-NOT complex may only occur on the ribosome.
Another non-mutually exclusive possibility is that CNOT4 may regulate the partitioning of mRNAs into distinct pools in the cell, each with distinct turnover dynamics. For example, the yeast Ccr4-Not complex regulates the solubility of mRNAs, which in turn impacts their degradation. The solubility of mRNAs correlates with the codon optimality, and Not1 and Not4 inversely regulate mRNA solubility (
69
). Although a similar mechanism has yet to be established in mammalian cells, it is plausible that the depletion of CNOT4 alters the solubility or partitioning of mRNAs into distinct pools, thereby affecting their decay rates.
Finally, another result was that depleting CNOT4 had minimal effects on steady-state mRNA levels, despite an increased turnover rate of these transcripts. This suggests that gene expression buffering (
70
,
71
,
72
,
73
) compensated for accelerated decay rates. Depleting CNOT4 resulted in changes in the expression of a few genes, and GO term analysis indicates that this collection of genes includes many transcription factors (
Fig. S8
A
and
Table S4
). Upon examining the transcription factors with altered expression, multiple activators and repressors of transcription were identified. This suggests that the depletion of CNOT4 triggers complex regulatory mechanisms to compensate for the increased mRNA turnover. This warrants future investigation into this phenomenon, which may reveal a mechanism of gene expression buffering.

The peptides were loaded on an Acclaim PepMap100 trapping column (75 mm × 2 cm, C18, 5 mm, 100 Å, Thermo), and separated on an Acclaim PepMap RSLC column (50 mm × 15 cm, C18, 2 mm, 100 Å, Thermo) at a flow rate of 300 nl/min in the following gradient of mobile phase B (80% ACN in 0.1% aqueous FA): 5% - 50% B in 210 min, 50% - 90% B in 30 min. A Thermo Orbitrap Eclipse mass spectrometer was operated in a data dependent mode using a method based on the TMT MS2 template. The resulting mass spectra were processed using Thermo Proteome Discoverer 3.1 software.

RNA extraction
Following the treatments, the media was removed and the cells were lysed directly in 1 ml Trizol. The cells were resuspended thoroughly by pipetting up and down several times and were incubated at room temperature for 5 min 0.2 ml of chloroform was added per 1 ml of Trizol. The tubes were capped and shaken vigorously by hand for 15 s, and then were incubated at room temperature for 2 to 3 min. The mixture was then centrifuged at > 12,000
g
for 15 min at 4 °C, the aqueous phase was collected in a separate tube, followed by addition of an equal volume of 100% ethanol. The RNA was cleaned up using Ambion RNA PureLink Kit according to manufacturer's instructions and eluted in 100 μl RNase-free water.
DNase treatment, tape station, cDNA synthesis, and RT-qPCR
10 μg of total RNA was treated with Turbo DNase (TURBO DNA-free Kit) for 30 min at 37 °C. 10 μl of DNase inactivation agent was added to the reaction, followed by incubation at room temperature for 5 min. The tubes were flicked 2 to 3 times during the incubation period to keep the DNase Inactivation Reagent suspended. The reaction was centrifuged at 10,000
g
for 90 s and supernatant was transferred in a new tube. The integrity of RNA was assessed by Agilent TapeStation-4150 at the Genomics Core Facility, Penn State University, USA. RNA samples with RIN score > 9.5 were used for further work. 1 μg of DNase-digested RNA was used to make cDNA using RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit according to manufacturer's instructions using Oligo (dT)18 primer and Random Hexamer primer (mixed 1:1). The cDNA product was directly used in PCR applications or stored at −20 °C until used. RT-qPCR was performed using PerfeCTa SYBR Green SuperMix (Quantabio). Two dilutions of cDNA were used in RT-qPCR to ensure the linear range of amplification. For each condition, at least 2 experimental replicates were used. The RT-qPCR was run on Agilent Real-time PCR machine (G8830A) and the data were analyzed using Agilent AriaMx and Microsoft excel.
RNA sequencing
Sequencing libraries for steady-state RNA-seq or triptolide RNA-seq to measure half-lives were generated using Illumina Stranded Total RNA Prep with Ribo-Zero Plus according to manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, 500 ng of Turbo DNase digested RNA was mixed with 2 μl of 1:200 diluted ERCC Spike-in mix (final volume 11 μl) which was subjected to the first step of library prep (
i.e.
probe hybridization). The sequencing libraries were analyzed on TapeStation-4150 at the Genomics Core Facility, Penn State University, USA and were quantified using NEBNext Library Quant Kit for Illumina. The libraries with multiple indices were pooled and treated with Illumina Free Adapter Blocking Reagent according to manufacturer's instructions. Sequencing was carried out at Genomics Research Incubator, Penn State University, USA using NextSeq1000/2000 P2 Reagents(100Cycles) on NextSeq 2000. FASTQ files were further processed as per the Bulk RNA-seq Data Standards and Processing Pipeline (
https://www.encodeproject.org/
), using STAR [ (
75
), version - STAR_2.5.1b_modified] and RSEM (RNA-Seq by Expectation-Maximization) tool. The reads were mapped to hg38 (GRCh38) using Gencode-v29 comprehensive gene set, predicted tRNA genes, and the ERCC spike-in controls (ThermoFisher Scientific). Read-counts for each gene generated by RSEM were used for differential expression analysis using DESeq2 (
76
). Genes with a sum of ≥ 8 across both replicates and conditions were used in DESeq2. The expression data were normalized to ERCC spike in controls using estimateSizeFactors function in DESeq2. Genes showing ≥ 1.5-foldchange at
p-
adj < 0.01 were denoted as differentially expressed.
