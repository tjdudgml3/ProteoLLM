Introduction
The free‐living nematode
Caenorhabditis elegans
has served as a foundational model organism in developmental biology and neurobiology since the 1960s.
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
Typically, a self‐fertilizing hermaphrodite,
C. elegans
has a transparent cuticle, a rapid life cycle, and a consistent somatic cell number, making it highly suitable for in vivo biological research. The adult hermaphrodite possesses 959 somatic cells, including 302 neurons.
[
4
,
5
]
Under unfavorable conditions at the end of the second larval stage (L2), the nematode can arrest development and enter the
dauer
stage. Upon return to favorable conditions, it transitions to the L4 stage within 10 hours.
[
6
]
Monitoring the full developmental cycle of individual worms remains challenging. Conventional immobilization techniques such as agar pads
[
7
]
can suppress movement but often compromise physiological processes. To address these limitations, microfluidic technologies have emerged as powerful tools for precise, minimally invasive manipulation of
C. elegans
and its environment.
[
8
,
9
]
Over the past decade, innovations in microfluidic platforms have enabled improved immobilization, automated sorting, and long‐term imaging with minimal disruption.
[
10
]
Advanced devices have demonstrated continuous imaging across multiple developmental stages and supported high‐resolution phenotypic analysis and aging studies.
[
11
]
In 2008, two seminal microfluidic systems were developed for nematode immobilization during live imaging,
[
12
,
13
]
including precise interventions such as single‐neuron ablation with femtosecond lasers.
[
13
]
However, these approaches imposed mechanical pressure and elevated temperatures, limiting their use for extended in vivo studies. Later designs introduced temporary or looser immobilization strategies,
[
10
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
]
using large chambers to support free movement and behavioral analysis
[
10
,
15
]
or high‐frame‐rate imaging to minimize motion artifacts.
[
16
]
Yet, such methods demand specialized imaging setups and intense fluorescent markers.
Hulme et al. introduced a microfluidic device using tapered channels and pressure‐driven flow for parallel immobilization,
[
18
]
with later adaptations incorporating serpentine geometries. Despite improved throughput, these platforms mostly addressed adult worms, not the full developmental span. Additional refinements have focused on channel geometry and orientation control,
[
11
,
19
,
20
,
21
]
such as Berger et al.’s vulva‐positioning system to facilitate egg laying during long‐term studies.
[
9
]
Alternative immobilization strategies have emerged using multilayer PDMS structures. These systems immobilize nematodes by applying pressure through a secondary control layer.
[
22
,
23
]
While effective across developmental stages,
[
17
,
24
]
their round chamber design often introduces imaging distortions requiring post‐processing. Other methods employ thermosensitive hydrogels like Pluronic F127 for reversible immobilization.
[
25
,
26
]
Though offering ease of release post‐imaging, these methods introduce variability in worm configuration, complicating consistent pathway tracking. Additional techniques include CO
2
exposure, dielectrophoresis, and temperature‐based immobilization.
[
12
,
27
,
28
,
29
,
30
,
31
]
However, many of these approaches still introduce stress to the nematodes or involve complex fabrication processes. None fully support continuous, longitudinal monitoring from larval to adult stages in a streamlined, non‐invasive system.
The need for robust microfluidic monitoring is expanding beyond
C. elegans
. “Fish‐on‐a‐chip” platforms have been applied to zebrafish embryo studies,
[
32
]
and similar systems have emerged for Drosophila and other small models,
[
33
]
underscoring the broader relevance of microfluidics in developmental biology. Nonetheless,
C. elegans
remains exceptionally suited for integrated microfluidic research due to its transparency and well‐characterized development.
[
34
]
To address these ongoing limitations, we developed a novel microfluidic device for uninterrupted, high‐resolution monitoring of individual
C. elegans
from L1 through adulthood. Our system integrates dynamic fluidic control for continuous nutrient perfusion, automated waste and progeny removal, and short‐term, non‐invasive immobilization. Capable of parallel analysis of up to 20 nematodes, it supports a wide range of physiological and functional assays including stress response imaging, pathogen interaction studies, and single‐nematode proteomics. This versatile and scalable platform bridges a critical gap in long‐term developmental monitoring, enabling high‐content experimentation with minimal physiological disruption.

Results
2.1
Device Design and Operation
The foundation of the system is based on mechanical immobilization through pneumatic pressure for the imaging sessions running through the entire nematode life cycle. The core structure of the device is centered around two primary layers: a control and a flow layer, both fabricated from polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS). We designed a system capable of single‐nematode monitoring from the L1 stage to adulthood, each within an isolated unit. The device contains 20 independent units, enabling simultaneous and continuous tracking of up to 20 nematodes, supporting high‐throughput
C. elegans
research.
System design –
As presented in
Figure
1
a,i,ii
, a manifold of inlets is integrated, serving as portals for introducing diverse substances into the system. Within each individual unit (Figure
1b,i,ii
), culture modules are incorporated, providing a segregated environment for the nematodes while accommodating separate monitoring. The system's feeding channels, characterized by microchannels that traverse each unit (Figure
1c,i
; Figure
S2a–c
, Supporting Information), enable the controlled circulation of nutrients to sustain the nematodes and facilitate waste removal, thus ensuring a dynamic and self‐sustaining ecosystem within the device. The control layer encompasses a series of valves, strategically positioned to exert control over fluid dynamics, nematode segregation and immobilization processes, including inlet valves to synchronized substance introduction, a common outlet valve for controlled substance output, a feeding valve to control nutrient flow, an immobilization valve to achieve nematode immobilization (Figure
1c,ii
), and a segmentation valve that divides each unit into discrete segments. The segmentation and the immobilization valves are designed as sieve valves that can be partially closed (Figure
S3b
, Supporting Information).
[
35
]
This unique design enables separation based on size allowing continuous organism washing, uninterrupted nutrient supply, efficient waste removal and selective offspring removal from the segmented units.

2.3
Stress Assay Using SOD‐3p::GFP as a Reporter Gene
Previous studies have claimed that prolonged mechanical immobilization can be stressful to the nematode, and based their findings on nematode survival and viability.
[
9
,
18
]
Since our model involves mechanical immobilization during imaging, we assessed its effect on nematode viability and vitality. To do so, we used a transcriptional reporter SOD‐3p::GFP strain
[
37
]
of nematodes that is commonly used to monitor animals stress and reflects activation of the stress‐related transcription factor DAF‐16/FOXO3. We applied two durations of mechanical immobilization episodes and imaged the transgenic nematode during and following these procedures. Half of the nematodes were immobilized only for the imaging period (2–5 minutes) – the control group, while the other half were immobilized for 24 hours. After 24 hours, the nematodes were imaged and their fluorescent signals recorded (
Figure
3
a,b
). As shown in Figure
3b,i
, fluorescent signal intensity was significantly increased 24 hours after the onset of immobilization. In contrast, the control group (Figure
3b,ii
) showed no significant difference between the initial image and the image taken 24 hours later. A statistically significant difference between the experimental and control groups (Figure
3b,iii
) indicates that only prolonged immobilization induces stress in the nematode, whereas short‐term immobilization does not. Therefore, the platform is suitable for long‐term experiments on individual worms, providing that prolong immobilization is avoided.

Real‐Time Protein Localization Assay
Protein localization assays are commonly used to study protein dynamics and interactions in vivo. Here, we conducted a localization assay for the pathogen
Pseudomonas aeruginosa
(Pa) and its toxin within the nematode.
P. aeruginosa
is an aerobic, Gram‐negative bacterium thriving in diverse environments, including nematodes, due to its versatile metabolic capabilities linked to its large genome.
[
39
,
40
]
It establishes acute infections using various virulence factors, notably the type III secretion system (T3SS),
[
41
]
a needle‐like structure injecting effector proteins directly into host cells.
[
42
,
43
]
Recently, bioinformatics and machine learning revealed additional T3SS effectors under study.
[
44

One such effector, PemB, exhibits toxicity towards
C. elegans
,
[
45
]
though its mechanism of action remains unclear.
We conducted a longitudinal localization assay in individual nematodes. The assay reveals PemB's distribution from ingestion through the intestinal passage to its final localization within the nematode. Two
P. aeruginosa

The negative control group were fed with bacteria PA14∆3tox with empty vector (control) and thus do not express PemB, whereas the experimental group nematodes were fed with PA14∆3tox/pemB that carries mutations in the genes exoU, exoY and exoT, and overexpresses PemB (Figure

2.6
Single Nematode Proteomics
Proteomics holds immense importance for the understanding of biological processes and mass spectrometry (MS) is the main tool for analyzing proteins. Individual cells contain minuscule amounts of protein, however, sample preparation steps, such as lysis, digestion, and purification, can introduce significant loss of proteins. Thus, a direct interface from individual cells to the MS is most desired. MS studies of
C. elegans
mostly occur in population level, using samples derived from ten thousand to millions of cells. The data reflect average protein levels within the population and may mask organism‐level variation in expression and function.
[
46
]
Here we propose using our microfluidic device for single‐nematode proteomics analysis. The platform enables recovery of a single nematode's proteome or transcriptome in a highly concentrated volume with minimal sample loss. To demonstrate the feasibility of extracting proteins from a single nematode, we lysed individual worms obtained from NGM plates, M9 medium, and our microfluidic device, and subjected the lysates to standard off‐chip MS analysis. As expected, a large number of
E. coli
proteins were detected, reflecting their presence in the nematode's nutritional environment. This high background interfered with accurate identification of nematode proteins. Therefore, only qualitative analysis was applied. MS analysis identified approximately 80
C. elegans
proteins despite the high
E. coli
background and the limited amount of nematode‐derived material extracted from a single worm (Figure
4c,i–iii
, Table
S1
, Supporting Information). The results indicate the ability of using MS for proteomic studies of a single nematode which can be applied following different models and time points.
3
Discussion
Our microfluidic platform offers several advantages over previously reported systems for long‐term
C. elegans
monitoring and functional assays. Unlike conventional methods that rely on continuous immobilization, anaesthetics, or complex imaging setups, our device enables repeated, high‐resolution imaging through transient, non‐invasive immobilization without compromising nematode viability. This design supports the parallel tracking of up to 20 individual worms throughout their entire development—from L1 to adulthood—while maintaining consistent orientation and enabling multifaceted physiological assays within a single device.
Several microfluidic platforms have been developed to support long‐term
C. elegans
culture, notably the device presented by Saberi‐Bosari et al.
[
11
]
Their system enables lifespan‐long culturing and imaging of individual nematodes in dedicated chambers, making it well‐suited for synchronized developmental studies and high‐throughput population assays. This design offers a robust solution for maintaining worm viability and represents significant progress in long‐term microfluidic systems. In contrast, our platform supports not only continuous culturing but also high‐resolution, longitudinal imaging of individual worms in a consistent orientation across all developmental stages. Importantly, it includes a fully automatable progeny removal mechanism, minimizing manual intervention, a feature absent from the Saberi‐Bosari system. Furthermore, our device enables downstream applications, such as single‐nematode retrieval for molecular analysis, broadening its utility beyond culture and imaging.
Compared to prior systems such as the “lifespan‐on‐a‐chip” design by Hulme et al.,
[
10
]
which utilized tapered channels and pressure‐driven flow to immobilize adult worms, our platform offers greater flexibility in developmental stage coverage and assay diversity. While devices employing high‐frame‐rate imaging,
[
16
]
pressurized PDMS layers,
[
22
,
23
]
or thermosensitive hydrogels
[
25
,
26
]
have enabled partial improvements in stress reduction and worm handling, they often lack the integration of multiple functionalities—such as automated feeding, waste removal, and downstream analysis—in a single system. Our platform addresses these limitations by combining modular automation with biological compatibility, facilitating a broader range of experimental workflows including stress reporter assays, pathogen localization studies, and single‐worm proteomics.
Beyond the device design and engineering aspects, our experimental results demonstrate broad functional capabilities including; normal motility, adequate nutrition, growth thorough the entire life cycle and normal fertility. These abilities support full life‐cycle progression from L1 to adulthood, along with observed egg‐laying suggests appropriate conditions for long‐term culture, reducing the need for overt stress response.
To assess the physiological impact of the transient immobilization mechanism, we employed SOD‐3p::GFP stress reporter strains. Our results showed no significant induction of stress signals under continuous growth in the microfluidic system or following repeated short‐term immobilization events, indicating that animals are well fed and in general homeostatic state and that the brief mechanical clamping required for high‐resolution imaging does not perturb worm homeostasis. In contrast, starvation stress assays within the same platform led to a marked increase in GFP expression, serving as a positive control and further confirming the system's sensitivity to biological stress conditions.
Furthermore, we demonstrated the platform's capability to support downstream molecular analyses by successfully performing single‐worm proteomics. Using an adapted lysis and processing workflow, we extracted and identified up to 80 proteins from individual nematodes using mass spectrometry. To our knowledge, this is among the first demonstrations of integrating continuous developmental monitoring and post‐imaging single‐worm proteomic analysis within the same microfluidic system. This capability enables coupling of live phenotypic imaging with molecular profiling at single‐organism resolution.
In a proof‐of‐concept application, we demonstrated the utility of the platform for pathogen interaction studies. By exposing worms to
Pseudomonas aeruginosa
strains engineered to express tagged PemB toxin, we visualized toxin localization within the nematode body over time. This experiment showcases the potential of the platform to model host‐pathogen interactions with spatiotemporal resolution in vivo, without the need for anaesthetic or chemical immobilization.
Nonetheless, we recognize that the complexity of our system may present barriers to widespread adoption. The platform relies on a multilayer pneumatic control system requiring precise pressure regulation and external instrumentation. This level of control enables advanced functionality, but it also increases the technical threshold for use compared to simpler, single‐line microfluidic devices that are more accessible to laboratories without specialized infrastructure. Minimalist systems offer ease of setup and operation but at the expense of dynamic experimental control. Future development efforts could aim to reduce system complexity by streamlining control interfaces or integrating plug‐and‐play modules that facilitate broader accessibility without compromising functionality.
Overall, our platform represents a step forward in integrating longitudinal imaging, automated sample handling, and post‐imaging analysis into a single device. By enabling sustained, high‐resolution monitoring with minimal stress and manual intervention, it advances the capabilities of microfluidic systems for
C. elegans
research and lays the foundation for more comprehensive, high‐content biological studies. Beyond its technical capabilities, this multifunctional platform supports real‐time functional assays and downstream proteomic analysis, offering a powerful tool for developmental biology, stress response studies, and translational applications in precision research. Its modular design and adaptability position it as a valuable addition to the expanding microfluidics toolkit for single‐organism analysis.

Experimental Section
The Microfluidic Device
Mold Fabrication
: The device was designed using AutoCAD 2022 (Autodesk, Inc.). Flow mold was fabricated on a 4″ silicon wafer (Silicon Quest International), with two layers of photoresists at different densities. For the first layer, the wafer was spin‐coated with SU8‐3005 (MicroChem), soft‐baked on a hot plate at 65 °C for 2 min then baked at 95 °C for 7mins. Then the mold was exposed in the MLA‐150 (Heidelberg Instruments) to UV light according to the manufacturer instructions. After exposure, the wafer was post‐exposure baked on the hot plate at 65 °C for 1 min then at 95 °C for 3mins and finally developed in SU8 developer (AZ EBR Solvent). After developing the first layer, the wafer was hard baked on a hot plate at 150 °C for 2 hours. The second layer was fabricated using the same protocol, substituting SU8‐3025 for the photoresist. For the Control mold, another 4″ silicon wafer was used and coated with one layer of SU8‐3025 along with the same protocol as the flow mold fabrication.
Chip Fabrication
PDMS devices were manufactured using standard methods in Gerber's lab. Briefly, the Flow and Control layers were prepared separately on silicone molds casting silicone elastomer Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS, SYLGARD 184, Dow Corning, USA). For the control layer, PDMS and curing agent at a 5:1 ratio, were mixed, followed by degassing, baking at 90 °C for 60mins, and access hole piercing steps. The flow layer was prepared by application of a 20:1 ratio of PDMS and curing agent, to create a thin layer the Flow mold has been coated with a spin‐coater (spinner model WS‐400‐6NPP‐Lite by Laurell Technologies Corporation) at 1800 rpm for 1 minute, baking in 80 °C for 30mins. The alignment of both layers was induced using a home‐built semi‐automatic alignment system. Holes were punched to allow the connection of tubes via pins, and the flowing of air or fluids within the chip during the experiment. Then the chip was placed in an oven for 24 hours at 80 °C for full curing. Once ready, the chip was bonded to a 2/3″ glass slide using plasma (HF Generator by Diener electronics). The Main device characteristics were as follows: Channel height – 75 µm, Channel width – 100 µm, Filter height – 5 µm, Filter width – 5 µm, and Filter Length – 380 µm.
Chip Operation
Medium flow, with or without nematodes, was controlled using a semi‐automated pneumatic system. Before the nematode was loaded the device was filled with M9 Buffer at 5 psi with closed outputs until the air inside the channels was purged out. Then OP50 in LB was loaded at 1 psi for 10 mins.
The working pressure for the nematode's insertion was 3 psi (flowrate of 5 µl min
−1
). The pressure applied to medium/food flowing was 1 psi. Control sieve‐valves were operated using 35 psi. The pressure applied to the immobilization valves was variable and depended on the nematode size and the immobilization approach.

The nematodes were lysed with a buffer solution of SDS 4%, 2‐mercaptoethanol 10%, TRIS HCl 0.125 M buffer, pH 6.8. 10 µL of the nematode lysate was inserted into 10 µL of the lysis buffer. The sample was heated at 100 °C for 5 mins and stored at −20 °C.

Mass Spectrometry
For MS analysis lysates (4% SDS, 50 mM Tris pH 7.4) were centrifuged at 14000 x g for 5 min. Samples were reduced in 6 mM dithiothreitol and alkylated with 12 mM iodoacetamide in the dark. Each sample was loaded onto S‐Trap micro‐columns (Protifi, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. In brief, after loading, samples were washed with a solution of 90% methanol and 10% 50 mM Triethylammonium bicarbonate. Samples were then digested with 100 ng trypsin for 90 min at 47 °C. The digested peptides were eluted using 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate; 100 ng trypsin was added to this fraction and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Two more elutions were made using 0.2% formic acid and 0.2% formic acid in 50% acetonitrile. The three elutions were pooled together and vacuum‐centrifuged to dry.
UPLC/MS grade solvents were used for all chromatographic steps. Each sample was loaded using splitless nanoUltra Performance Liquid Chromatography (10k psi nanoAcquity; Waters, Milford, MA, USA). The mobile phase was: A) H2O + 0.1% formic acid and B) acetonitrile + 0.1% formic acid. Samples were desalted using a reversed‐phase Symmetry C18 trapping column (180 µm ID, 20 mm length, 5 µm particles; Waters). The peptides were then separated using a T3 HSS nano‐column (75 µm internal diameter, 250 mm length, 1.8 µm particle size; Waters) at 0.35 µL mi
−1
n. Peptides were eluted from the column into the mass spectrometer using a 155 min, 4–28% B (99.9% Acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid) gradient. Samples were applied in a random order.
A nanoESI emitter (20 µm tip, Fossilion Tech, Madrid, Spain) was used on a FlexIon source (Thermo Fisher), mounted on a Tribrid orbitrap mass spectrometer (Fusion Lumos, Thermo Fisher). Data were acquired in data‐dependent acquisition (DDA) mode, using a Top Speed method for 3 sec. MS1 resolution was set to 120000@200 m/z, mass range of 375–1650 m/z, AGC at Standard and maximum injection time was set to Auto. Precursors selected for MS2 were limited to charge states of 2–8 with a minimum intensity of 5e4. MS2 fragmentation were acquired in the ion‐trap, set to Normal scan speed, quadrupole isolation set to 1 m/z, first mass at 130 m/z, AGC set at Standard. Dynamic exclusion of 30 sec with ±10 ppm window and maximum injection time set to 100 ms. Ions were fragmented using HCD at 30 NCE.
Data analysis was performed using MetaMorpheus (v0.0.311) search engine (PMID 29 578 715, 23 323 968, 29 083 185), searching against a combined database including the
E. coli
, K12 stain (SwissProt 2019),
C. elegans
(SwissProt 2019) and a common contaminant database.
Data were searched against specific C‐terminal cleavage at K and R, allowing one missed cleavage at a tolerance of 10 ppm in MS1 and 20 ppm in MS2, using HCD fragmentation. Modifications were searched using GPTMD module (PMID 23 323 968), with fixed Carbamidomethylation on C. Data was filtered for 1% FDR on the protein level. Quantification was performed by FlashLFQ (PMID 29 083 185) without “match between runs” or normalization since the samples were very different from one another.
