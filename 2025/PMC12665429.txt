Introduction
Human coronavirus OC43 (HCoV-OC43) was first identified as a betacoronavirus in 1967. It is an enveloped virus with a single-stranded positive-strand RNA genome (
Kesheh et al., 2022
). The most commonly reported symptoms in HCoV-OC43 infected individuals include sore throat, cough, and rhinorrhea. Additionally, the virus has been shown to possess neuroinvasive properties. As one of the seven coronaviruses capable of infecting humans, HCoV-OC43 is classified within the
Coronaviridae
family and belongs to the
Betacoronavirus
genus (
Vijgen et al., 2005
). HCoV-OC43 contains four structural proteins: the spike (S) protein, envelope E) protein, membrane (M) protein and nucleocapsid (N) protein (
Schmidt et al., 2017
). Among these, the S protein facilitates host attachment and mediates the fusion of the virus with the cell membrane during viral infection by binding to the receptor 9-O-acetylsalivaryl acid (9-O-Ac-Sia) (
Wang et al., 2022
), subsequently entering the cell
via
the endocytosis pathway. Viral genomic RNA (gRNAs) serve as templates for the translation of polyproteins (pp1a and pp1ab), which are subsequently cleaved to generate viral non-structural proteins (NSPs) (
Malik, 2020
;
Peng et al., 2020
). NSPs induce the rearrangement of the cell membrane, leading to the formation of double-membrane vesicles (DMVs) in which viral replication transcriptional complexes (RTCs) are anchored (
Chen et al., 2020
). Full-length gRNAs are replicated
via
negative-strand intermediates, and a nested set of gRNAs is synthesized through discontinuous transcription (
Malone et al., 2022
). These gRNAs encode viral structural components and accessory proteins (
Tan et al., 2006
). The resultant viral product is assembled within the endoplasmic reticulum-Golgi intermediate complex and subsequently buds onto the plasma membrane in the form of smooth-walled vesicles that are expelled from the cell
via
exocytosis (
Brian and Baric, 2005
;
Fehr and Perlman, 2015
).
Binding to the receptor initiates the process. The binding may induce direct fusion of the virus with the cell membrane; however, in most instances, the virus is internalized
via
the endocytosis pathway and subsequently encapsulated within smooth-walled vesicles (
Hussain et al., 2011
). One of the most common endocytosis pathways is clathrin protein-mediated endocytosis, and a variety of viruses are internalized
via
this endocytosis pathway. Upon recognition by the receptor, the viral particle docks into the clathrin-protein-mediated pit, recruits articulin protein 2 (AP2) to the nucleation site
via
Epsin1 (
Henne et al., 2010
) to form the vesicle's outgrowth structure. This structure is subsequently engaged to supply energy to kinesin, ultimately leading to the detachment of the vesicle from the cell surface and the formation of a cargo-containing vesicle (
Cocucci et al., 2014
).
SNX10 is a member of the sorting nexins (SNXs) family, which is involved in endosomal sorting processes. All members possess a phox (PX) domain (
Liao et al., 2024
), which serves as a phosphoinositide-binding module that facilitates the formation of endosomal transport carriers through either reverse-transcription-dependent or independent mechanisms (
Schmidt et al., 2017
;
Amatya et al., 2021
). The proteins in SNXs family play various roles in cargo sorting, retrograde protein transport, and lysosomal degradation (
Lu et al., 2022
). Compared with other SNXs family members, SNX10 has a relatively simple structure and does not contain any other known functional structural domains, except for the PX structural domain (
Van Weering et al., 2010
;
Norris et al., 2017
;
Tian et al., 2021
). In this study, we focused on the regulation of SNX10 during HCoV-OC43 infection.
Current research demonstrates that SNX10 plays a critical role in cilia formation. Knockdown of
SNX10
suppresses cilia formation by depending on V-ATPase activity, and the acidic pH environment of endosomes activates cathepsin L (
Tscherne et al., 2006
), triggering the fusion of the viral envelope with the membrane of the endosomes thereby releasing the viral genome. Knockdown of
SNX10
inhibits the activity of cathepsin A (
Wang et al., 2021
), enhances the stability of lysosome-associated membrane protein-2A (LAMP-2A), and activates chaperone-mediated autophagy (
You et al., 2018
). However, some viruses can facilitate their replication by actively modulating or channelizing autophagy. For instance, dengue virus utilizes autophagosomal membranes as scaffolds for its replication complexes (
Yousefi et al., 2022
). Upon viral infection, selective autophagy receptors typically recognize viral components, thereby initiating ‘xenophagy’ (
Kong et al., 2020
), enhancing autophagy levels, and inhibiting viral proliferation, which collectively safeguards cells from pathogen-induced damage (
Miller et al., 2020
).
In this study, we aimed to elucidate the role of SNX10 as a host factor that facilitates viral infection and tried to provide valuable insights for future responses to coronavirus outbreaks. Furthermore, we will investigate the potential of targeting SNX10 for the development of therapeutic agents.
Results
SNX10 deletion inhibits viral replication
in vivo
To investigate the role of SNX10 in viral pathogenesis, we generated conditional
Snx10
knockout (CKO) mice with lung epithelial cell-specific deletions as the experimental group and compared them with wild-type (WT) mice serving as the control group. Using HCoV-OC43 infection to establish a murine pneumonia model, we observed that while the virus did not cause severe pulmonary damage, WT mice exhibited more pronounced lung inflammation compared to CKO mice. Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated significantly elevated expression of the viral nucleocapsid (NP) protein in the lung tissue of the WT group (
Fig. 1
A, B). Histopathological examination revealed characteristic alveolar interstitial thickening, tissue edema, increased mucus secretion, and prominent inflammatory cell infiltration in the infected lungs. Notably, CKO mice demonstrated markedly reduced pulmonary inflammation and significantly lower pathological scores compared to WT mice (
Fig. 1
C, D). Although no significant intergroup differences were detected in lung indices or inflammatory cytokine levels (
Fig. 1
E–I), quantitative analysis revealed markedly decreased NP protein levels in CKO lung tissues (
Fig. 1
J and K). These findings suggest that
SNX10
deficiency compromises HCoV-OC43 viral replication
in vivo
.

Discussion
The emergence of three pathogenic coronaviruses (SARS-CoV, MERS-CoV, and SARS-CoV-2) in recent decades has resulted in severe human diseases, thereby highlighting the persistent threat posed by these novel coronaviruses and other emerging pathogens to global public health (
Gautam et al., 2020
;
Harrison et al., 2020
;
Shang et al., 2020
). Our investigation into the pathogenesis of the HCoV-OC43 virus elucidates the key mechanisms underlying coronavirus infection, thereby offering critical insights for the development of preparedness strategies against future coronavirus outbreaks.
Experimental evidence has shown that
SNX10
knockout suppresses viral replication both
in vivo
and
in vitro
. Additionally, viral infection stabilizes SNX10 protein levels, thereby underscoring its critical role in the pathogenesis of infection. We propose that HCoV-OC43 may enhance the stability of the SNX10 protein either by commandeering the E3 ubiquitin ligase (
Blanchet et al., 2012
) or through direct inhibition of proteasome activity (
Chavoshi et al., 2016
). As a host protein involved in cargo transport, SNX10 has been extensively studied for its role during the viral pre-infection phase (
Amatya et al., 2021
). As one of the simplest members of the SNXs family, SNX10 has been reported to play regulatory roles in the endosome pathway (
Lee et al., 2022
), encompassing phagocytosis and degradation of pathogens (
Wang et al., 2021
), antigen processing and presentation (
Wu et al., 2024
), inflammatory responses (
You et al., 2016
), and the maintenance of tissue homeostasis (
Lou et al., 2017
). These processes are essential for proper macrophage function. PX structural domains ensure the accurate transport of intracellular vesicles and their cargo proteins by facilitating the trafficking of specific cargo proteins from the endosome to the Golgi apparatus or the plasma membrane (
Lou et al., 2021
). By integrating the findings from RT-qPCR, viral envelope tracking, and immunofluorescence analysis, we propose that SNX10 facilitates viral cellular entry.
Given the critical role of SNX10 in vesicular trafficking, we first evaluated the impact of
SNX10
-knockout on viral entry. Pseudovirus assays revealed a significant inhibition of viral internalization upon
SNX10
deletion. During the early stages of infection, viral structural proteins generally interact with host membrane receptors or co-receptors to facilitate cellular entry (
Boulant et al., 2015
;
Koehler et al., 2020
). To elucidate the mechanistic involvement of SNX10, we established SNX10-overexpressing cell line and conducted immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry (IP-MS). AP2M1 was identified as a key interacting partner. AP2M1 plays a critical role in facilitating clathrin-mediated endocytosis (CME) by promoting vesicle formation and trafficking (
Ganapathy et al., 2022
). The internalization of cargo
via
CME necessitates the assembly of the AP2M1-clathrin complex, which is regulated in a phosphorylation-dependent manner. Intriguingly, SNX10 knockout significantly disrupts the AP2M1 phosphorylation cycles, indicating its crucial role in CME pathways (
Agajanian et al., 2019
;
Tongmuang et al., 2020
;
Wang, P.G. et al., 2020
). Notably, although AP2M1-viral YxxØ motif interactions are implicated in replication enhancement (
Yuan et al., 2020
), this mechanism remains to be fully elucidated in our experimental system.
Our findings revealed that SNX10 directly interacts with AP2M1, the μ-subunit of the AP-2 complex, thereby enhancing AP2M1-clathrin binding during viral entry. CME is a highly conserved cellular mechanism that facilitates the internalization of extracellular cargo
via
clathrin-coated vesicles (CCVs) (
Mettlen et al., 2018
). Viral exploitation of CME encompasses the following sequential stages: (1) virion attachment to membrane receptors, (2) initiation of signaling cascades, (3) localized clathrin polymerization, (4) membrane invagination, and (5) CCV scission (
Wang, G. et al., 2020
). During this process, the recruitment of clathrin to the plasma membrane is mediated by adaptor proteins such as EPS15, AP180, AP-2 complex, and Epsin1, rather than through direct interactions with cargo receptor (
Zhang et al., 2023
). SNX10 plays a critical role in regulating AP2M1 phosphorylation. The inhibition of AP2M1 phosphorylation disrupts the CME complex assembly, thereby suppressing viral entry. Following cellular internalization, the release of the viral genome necessitates acidification-dependent uncoating within endosomal compartments (
Fan et al., 2024
). This pH-sensitive process functions as a critical molecular checkpoint for a variety of viruses, including both enveloped viruses (such as influenza viruses and coronaviruses) and non-enveloped variants (such as rhinoviruses), to initiate infection process. Viral evolution has optimized pH-responsive structural components, such as influenza hemagglutinin and rhinovirus capsid proteins, to facilitate uncoating process (
Baggen et al., 2018
;
Yamauchi, 2020
;
Müller et al., 2021
). In contrast, host cells counteract viral infection by neutralizing endosomal pH or inhibiting proteases. Mechanistically, SNX10 contributes to viral genome liberation by destabilizing endosomal proton gradients
via
pH dysregulation.
It is widely recognized that cells, upon stimulation by antigens, typically activate the autophagy mechanism to eliminate these antigens and preserve the homeostasis of the intracellular environment. Likewise, when a cell is infected by a virus, it also triggers autophagy. Our studies demonstrate that SNX10-knockout during viral infection enhances early endosome biogenesis and promotes autolysosome formation
via
lysosomal conversion, indicating its crucial role in activating virus-specific autophagy pathways (
Chen et al., 2023
). The endosomal trafficking system consists of distinct compartments, including early endosomes, late endosomes, recycling endosomes, and lysosomes (
Weeratunga et al., 2020
). Notably, SNX10 promotes autophagy induced by viral infection by specifically regulating autophagosome-lysosome fusion, without influencing the maturation processes from early to late endosomes or from late endosomes to lysosomes.
SNX10
knockout induces sustained activation of autophagic flux, highlighting a complex host-pathogen interaction in which autophagy fulfills dual roles: serving as a cellular antiviral defense mechanism while also being subverted by viral strategies. While autophagy pathways eliminate invading pathogens through xenophagic degradation, evolved viral countermeasures suppress, evade, or co-opt these processes to gain a replicative advantage. Deciphering this dynamic equilibrium provides promising opportunities for the development of autophagy-modulating therapeutics.
Conclusions
In summary, this study demonstrates that SNX10 enhances coronavirus infection
via
two distinct mechanisms: (1) promoting clathrin-mediated viral entry by directly interacting with AP2M1 and (2) augmenting endosomal acidification to facilitate viral genome uncoating. Conversely, the deficiency of
SNX10
not only inhibits viral entry but also triggers autophagy in response to viral infection (
Fig. 7
). The dual roles of SNX10 in modulating both viral entry and autophagic processes deepen our understanding of SNX10-mediated host-virus interactions and underscore its potential as a multi-faceted target for the development of broad-spectrum antiviral therapeutics.
Materials and methods
Cell lines and viruses
Human return cystic cancer cells (HCT-8), human rhabdomyosarcoma cells (RD), and human embryonic kidney cells (293T) were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). 293T and RD cells were cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (PS; Thermo Fisher Scientific). HCT-8 ​cells were maintained in RPMI1640 medium containing 1% PS and 10% FBS. HCoV-OC43 was obtained by HCT-8 ​cells expansion. The plaque assay was used to quantify the viral titers.

Cell transfection
For the overexpression assay, the expression plasmid pCMV3-mSnx10-HA or negative control pCMV3-C-HA-NCV was purchased from Sino Biological (MG51516-CY, CV013). HCT-8 ​cells were transfected with the plasmid pCMV3-mSnx10-HA or the negative control pCMV3-C-HA-NCV using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, L3000001). To generate a stable cell line, after transduction for 24 ​h, the medium containing the lentivirus was replaced with RPMI1640 complete medium with 2 ​μg/mL puromycin. Using a single guide RNA (sgRNA), the CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing system recognizes and cleaves specific sequences, thereby knocking them out. Knockout cell line was verified using Western blotting.
Virus infection
Cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and incubated with HCoV-OC43 (MOI ​= ​1) (
López-Muñoz et al., 2023
) for 1 ​h. The inoculum was removed, and cells were cultured in fresh RPMI1640 blank medium, washed twice with PBS, and cultured in RPMI1640 medium supplemented with 0.1% FBS. The supernatant and protein were harvested at the indicated time points.
Plaque assay
The titer of the supernatant collected from influenza-infected cells was quantified by a plaque assay on RD cells. RD cells were washed twice and infected with serial dilutions of the samples at 37 ​°C for 1 ​h. The supernatant was discarded and replaced with 3 ​mL of replacement media (1.5 ​mL 2× ​DMEM and 1.5 ​mL Avicel supplemented with 1.5 ​μg/mL trypsin, 0.5% BSA, 20 ​mM HEPES, and 1% PS). After infection 72 ​h at 37 ​°C, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 10 ​min at room temperature and stained with 2% crystal violet staining solution for 30 ​min.
Western blotting analysis
Cell lysates were prepared using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein samples were separated on 10% SDS-polyacrylamide gels and subsequently transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk in Tris-buffered saline (TBS) containing 0.1% Tween 20 (TBS-T) at 20–25 ​°C for 1 ​h, the protein bands were incubated with primary antibodies diluted in 5% non-fat milk at 4 ​°C overnight. After incubation with secondary antibodies for 1 ​h at 20–25 ​°C, the protein bands were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescent (ECL) substrate (Thermo Scientific).
RNA extraction and real-time PCR
Total RNA was extracted from tissues or cultured cells using RNA extraction kits (Foregene, RE03113, and R220701), according to the manufacturer's instructions. Rervese transscript PCR was performed using a reverse transcription kit (TaKaRa) to synthesize the first strand of cDNA from 1 ​mg of total RNA, using random primers. Real-time PCR was performed on a LightCycler 480II (Roche) using SYBR Green (Bio-Rad). GAPDH was used as an internal control.
Immunoprecipitation (IP) and co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP)
For immunoprecipitation and co-immunoprecipitation, cells were lysed with lysis buffer (1 ​M Hepes-NaOH, pH 7.6; 0.5 ​M EDTA; 5 ​M NaCl; 2.5% NP-40; and protease inhibitors), supplemented with protease inhibitors, and incubated on ice for 30 ​min. The cell lysates were incubated with Protein G Magnetic Beads (MCE, New Jersey, USA) and antibodies overnight at 4 ​°C. The beads were washed three times with lysis buffer. The bound proteins were eluted with 1 ​× ​sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) and analyzed by Western blotting.
Immunofluorescence assay (IF)
Immunofluorescence assays were performed as described previously. After fixation with 4% (PFA) at 20–25 ​°C for 20 ​min, the cells were permeabilized with PBS containing 0.3% Triton X-100 for 15 ​min. After blocking with 3% BSA for 1 ​h, the cells were incubated overnight with primary antibodies at 4 ​°C, and then secondary antibodies were added and incubated for another 1 ​h at 20–25 ​°C. The nuclei were stained with DAPI for 20 ​min. The samples were imaged using a confocal laser scanning microscope (LSM 880, Carl Zeiss Jena, Germany). Image processing and quantification of fluorescence signals were analyzed using Zen software.
Virus binding assay and entry assay
HCT-8 ​cells were inoculated into confocal dishes. After transfecting the cells with siRNA or plasmid and continued to be cultured for 2 days, the cells were infected with HCoV-OC43 (MOI ​= ​10) (
López-Muñoz et al., 2023
) in serum-free RPMI1640 and left at 4 ​°C for 1 ​h. The cells were washed three times with cold PBS, followed by the extraction of viral RNA. The cells were then washed three times with cold PBS, followed by the addition of serum-free RPMI1640 medium and placed in an incubator at 37 ​°C. Following incubation, the cells were treated with trypsin to remove any remaining surface-adsorbed viruses, ensuring that only the internalized viruses were retained. Finally, the cells were collected to detect the internalized viral RNA.
Acid bypass assay
HCT-8 ​cells were incubated once with pre-cooled blank medium and for 10 ​min at 4 ​°C. Cells were then infected with pre-cooled HCoV-OC43 viral suspension (MOI ​= ​10) and incubated for 1 ​h at 4 ​°C to allow viral attachment to the cell surface (
Dong et al., 2020
). After discarding the viral inoculum, the cells were rinsed 3 times with ice-cold PBS to remove unbound viral particles. Cells were then incubated with cold PBS (pH 7.4 or pH 5.0) at 4 ​°C for 10 ​min. Afterwards, the complete medium was replaced and left at 37 ​°C for 2 ​h. Cellular RNA was collected for assay.
Membrane fluorescent staining probe DIO/DIL stain
First, the HCoV-OC43 viral suspension with DIO dye at 4 ​°C for 1 ​h, then discard the unbound dye through ultra-high speed centrifugation and re-dilution. Simultaneously, the cell membrane was stained with DIL dye. Infect the cells with the HCoV-OC43 virus bound by DIO dye at 4 ​°C for 1 ​h and washed three times with cold PBS for 3 times to wash off the unbound virus. After incubation at 37 ​°C incubator for 2 ​h, the cells were fixed with 4% PFA and observed under a microscope.
Lysosomal fluorescent probe assay
HCT-8 ​cells were grown to the appropriate abundance and infected with HCoV-OC43 virus (MOI ​= ​10) on ice for 1 ​h, followed by a change in serum-free medium to continue incubation. The medium was aspirated, and 150 ​μL of probe working solution (1 ​μM) was added. Place in incubator at 37 ​°C for 1 ​h. Discard the supernatant and wash with PBS for 3 times, add 150 ​μL of Hoechst33258 working solution for 30 ​min, and observed through the microscope.
Acidic endosomal assay
The cells were infected with the HCoV-OC43 virus (MOI ​= ​10) for 1 ​h. The cell culture medium was changed to serum-free medium after the infection and incubated in a 37 ​°C incubator for 1 ​h. The cells were washed 3 times with PBS and incubated with Protonex solution (1 ​μM) for 1 ​h at 37 ​°C. The supernatant was discarded, and the cells were washed with PBS for 3 times, and observed under a microscope.
Acid vacuole detection
The cells were infected with HCoV-OC43 virus (MOI ​= ​10) for 1 ​h, then replaced with serum-free medium, and continued to be cultured in the 37 ​°C incubator. After culturing for 1 ​h, the cells were rinsed with PBS for 3 times. The diluted neutral red reagent was added to the cell culture dishes and incubated for 1 ​h at 37 ​°C. The supernatant was discarded, and the cells were washed with PBS for 3 times, and observed under a microscope.
Lysosomal probe staining
Cells were infected with HCoV-OC43 virus (MOI ​= ​10) for 1 ​h, replaced with serum-free medium, and incubated for 2 ​h in an incubator. The cells were then rinsed with PBS for 3 times. The diluted LysoTracker green lysosomal probe (1 ​μM) was added to the dishes and incubated for 1 ​h. Then, the supernatant was discarded, and the cells were observed under a microscope.
Detection of cellular autophagy
Ad-mCherry-GFP-LC3B is a recombinant adenovirus that efficiently expresses the fusion proteins of the red fluorescent protein mCherry, green fluorescent protein (GFP), and LC3B in target cells after infection, with bright red and green fluorescence, which can be used for the detection of autophagy. Cells were pre-treated with Ad-mCherry-GFP-LC3B (MOI ​= ​10) for 24 ​h. HCoV-OC43 virus (MOI ​= ​10) was added to the dishes to infect the cells for 1 ​h, then replaced with serum-free medium and incubated for 2 ​h. Observations were performed under a microscope.
Pseudovirus (PsV) infection assay
Pseudoviruses were produced by the co-transfection of 293T cells with pNL4-3. Luc.R-E− and plasmids encoding either HCoV-OC43 or an empty vector using PolyJet (SignaGen). PsV supernatants were harvested at 48 ​h post-transfection, centrifuged at 2000×
g
for 10 ​min, and passed through a 0.45 ​μm filter to remove cell debris. For infection, HCT-8 ​cells were grown in 96-well plates until confluency reached 60%–80%. The respective mixtures were then added to the target cells (triplicate samples) (Romeo et al., 2020). The culture supernatants were discarded 72 ​h after transfection. The cells were lysed with 1 ​× ​cell lysis reagent (Promega) for 10–15 ​min at 20–25 ​°C, and the cell lysates were transferred to white opaque-walled 96-well plates. The luciferase activity was quantified using a luciferase assay kit (Promega).
