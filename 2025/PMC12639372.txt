Introduction
Pesticides and heavy metals are both ubiquitous in present-day environments, with co-exposures in occupational, agricultural, and residential settings
[10]
,
[1]
,
[2]
,
[37]
,
[54]
,
[55]
,
[61]
,
[68]
. According to the 2025 report released by the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations, total pesticide, including pyrethroid insecticides, has nearly doubled since 1990s
[19]
. It was estimated that the market for pyrethroids in 2024 was worth 3.9 billion USD and is expected to reach 5.7 billion USD by 2033
[44]
. As the use of more toxic organophosphate pesticides has declined, the usage of safer alternative pyrethroids has drastically increased in a variety of indoor and outdoor applications. The use of pyrethroids has led, however, to ecological contaminations in soil and in water and negative impacts on their resident biota along with increased potential for adverse effects on non-target organisms
[15]
,
[16]
,
[6]
,
[73]
,
[84]
.
Deltamethrin (DM), a synthetic pyrethroid insecticide targeting voltage-gated sodium channels, is extensively utilized for its effectiveness against a broad range of agricultural pests. While recognized for its relatively low toxicity in mammals, studies have indicated that DM can trigger pro-fibrotic processes
[32]
,
[3]
, suggesting a potential for greater toxicological risks when combined with other pervasive environmental contaminants such as cadmium (Cd). Investigations into the toxicity of pyrethroids have revealed disruption of mitochondrial function through the inhibition of respiratory complexes
[21]
,
[8]
, increased reactive oxygen species (ROS)
[43]
,
[47]
,
[70]
, altered mitochondrial membrane potential
[30]
, and a shift in cellular bioenergetics towards glycolysis
[62]
. These mitochondrial perturbations have been linked to impaired ATP synthesis, disruption in cellular energy balance, and oxidative stress.
A recent analysis of toxic metals in soils estimated that some 900 million to 1.4 billion people worldwide may be exposed to elevated levels of at least one toxic metal, with cadmium (Cd) having the highest global exceedance rate
[38]
. Cd exposure, in particular, remains a significant public health concern due to its widespread distribution in industrial settings, food, water, and air, along with its ability to accumulate in the body over time. Cd accumulation has been attributed to chronic health problems including kidney disease, bone disorders, respiratory and cardiovascular diseases, reproductive disorders, compromised immune system, neurological disorders, and an elevated risk of cancer
[64]
.
Cd at low environmental exposure levels causes mitochondrial dysfunction through the inhibition of antioxidant selenoproteins and associated oxidative stress
[39]
,
[63]
,
[72]
,
[87]
,
[88]
. Electron flow through the electron transport chain (ETC) is inhibited, particularly at complexes I and III, with the generation of reactive oxidants and compromised ATP production
[59]
. Decreased mitochondrial membrane potential further compromises bioenergetic output and can activate mitochondrial apoptotic pathways
[28]
,
[36]
. Cd inhibits fatty acid β-oxidation, increases concentrations of acyl-carnitine, disrupts normal function of the citric acid cycle, and impairs the pyrimidine metabolic pathway
[27]
,
[33]
,
[40]
. Cd may also bring about changes to cellular metabolism by inhibiting pyruvate dehydrogenase and blocking the entry of pyruvate into the mitochondria. Impaired mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation causes a metabolic shift towards glycolysis.
The pervasiveness of both DM and Cd in the environment increases the potential for interactions, specifically involving mitochondrial activities and metabolic pathways associated with mitochondria. We selected human lung fibroblasts (HLF) as a model relevant to fibrotic lung injury and useful for the study of Cd
[41]
,
[42]
,
[49]
,
[48]
. We used high-resolution metabolomics (HRM) and mitochondrial activity measures to elucidate cellular and mitochondria-associated metabolic processes affected by DM and Cd. Our findings show that the combination of this commonly used pesticide with low environmental Cd has the potential for pathological interactions.

Materials and methods
2.1
Cell culture
Human lung fibroblast cells (HLF) were obtained from the American Tissue Culture Collection [(CCL-153) ATCC, Rockville, MD]. Cells were cultured in F-12K growth media supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 100 U/ml penicillin/streptomycin (P/S). All treatments and vehicle controls were carried out in low serum media (0.5 % FBS, 100 U/ml P/S F-12K) to minimize the binding of low-dose Cd to serum albumin cysteine thiols. A standard dose of 1 μM Cd was based on dose used in previous research
[42]
,
[49]
,
[48]
showing that this exposure for 24 h was comparable to the Cd burden of human lung (0.54 ± 0.20 ng Cd/mg protein)
[11]
. A 5 μM dose for DM was selected to provide exposure approximately 2.5-fold the no observable adverse effect level (NOAEL) established by the US Environmental Protection Agency (US EPA) based on Wolansky and Crofton study of motor activity in rats after acute oral exposure
[83]
. Cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5 % CO
2
.
2.2
Analysis of mitochondria activities
HLF bioenergetics and mitochondrial function were determined using Seahorse extracellular flux analyzer (Seahorse Bioscience, MA), as previously described
[49]
. HLF were seeded onto XF96 microplates at approximately 40,000 cells per well in XF DMEM assay media (DMEM supplemented with 1 mM pyruvate, 10 mM glucose, 2 mM glutamine, and HEPES, pH 7.4). The Seahorse XF96 extracellular flux analyzer was used to measure oxygen consumption rate (OCR) at baseline, followed by three sequential injections of 25 μL of mitochondrial complex inhibitors, oligomycin (1 μM), carbonyl cyanide-4-trifluoromethoxyphenylhydrazone (FCCP, 1 μM), and antimycin A/rotenone (0.5 μM). Each treatment was carried out in 12 replicates, normalized to protein via the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay, and the results were expressed as pmol O
2
consumed/min/μg protein. Agilent Seahorse Wave Desktop software was used for processing and analysis of raw data.
2.3
High-resolution metabolomics
Treated HLF in 6-well plates were washed with 400 μL of PBS per well to remove residual media. A mixture containing acetonitrile and water, in 2:1 ratio, and an internal standard containing stable isotopic chemicals to cover a range of chemical properties was prepared
[26]
, and 300 μL of the solution was added to each well for 10-min incubation on ice. Cells were then scraped and pulse vortexed for 15 s and centrifuged for 10 min (16.1 g at 4 °C). Resulting supernatant was analyzed using a QExactive HF Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) within the mass-to-charge range of 85–1275
m/z
, ensuring high-resolution detection. To maintain analytical precision, each sample was run in triplicate. A quality control pooled reference sample (QStd3) was included at the beginning, middle, and end of each analytical batch and NIST SRM1950 was included at the beginning and end of the entire set of samples for quality control and quality assurance. Chromatographic separation was performed via two methods: HILIC using a Waters XBridge BEH Amide XP HILIC column (2.6 μm particle size, 50 × 2.1 mm) with positive electrospray ionization (ESI) and reverse phase C18 chromatography using a Higgins Targa C18 column (3 µm particle size, 50 × 2.1 mm) with negative ESI. These complementary separation techniques enabled efficient detection of a broad range of metabolite classes. The resulting mass spectral data were extracted and processed using apLCMS v6.6.8
[86]
and xMSanalyzer v2.0.6.1
[78]
, followed by batch correction with ComBat, extracting high-resolution mass-to-charge (
m/z
) features paired with their corresponding retention times (RT) and ion intensity for each analysis. To ensure the robustness of the data, features with nonzero values in at least 70 % of all samples and in more than 80 % of each group were retained for further analysis. Data from the triplicate analyses were median-summarized before proceeding to statistical and bioinformatic analyses, providing a solid foundation for downstream interpretation.
2.4
Metabolomics data analysis
A one-way ANOVA using
limma
was employed to identify features that differed among all groups, with an FDR threshold of 0.2 to decrease false discovery from multiple comparisons. For untargeted comparisons of signal intensities of annotated metabolic features, 2-way hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) was conducted, using all features selected at raw p < 0.05 (as determined by
limma
). Further analysis of these selected features for pathway associations was performed using
mummichog v2.6.1
[51]
, using permutation testing (n = 1000, p-value cut off = 0.05). Features were identified at Level 1 relative to standards, or annotated using xMSannotator
[79]
and the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB;
http://www.hmdb.ca/
), at Level 3 identification per the Metabolomics Standards Initiative
[82]
. This approach helps safeguard against Type 2 statistical error by including all features with p < 0.05, while protecting against Type 1 error through pathway enrichment analysis
[80]
. The resulting p-values for overlapping metabolic pathways from both ionization modes were combined using Fisher’s method. The values for pathway overlap and pathway sizes were combined using weighted averages with both modes having equal weight of 0.5.
2.5
Immunocytochemistry
HLF cells were plated on 12-well collagen-coated, glass-bottom plates for 24 h and then treated for 24 h. MitoSOX Red (Ex/Em, 396/ 610 nm), and Hoechst (Ex/Em, 340/450 nm) signal measurements were obtained following the manufacturer's protocol to assess the abundance of mitochondrial-associated oxidants and nuclei, respectively. MitoSOX Red and Hoechst dye were added to the media and incubated for 15 min at 37 °C. After incubation, cells were washed 3 times with PBS and fixed with formalin. Cytation-10 imager (BioTek, Winooski, USA) was used to assess fluorescence emissions of target fluorophores in each well. MitoSOX Red fluorescence emission intensities and number of Hoechst positive entities in nine (3 × 3), pre-determined regions in each well, equally distanced from each other, were quantified using the Gen5™ software (BioTek, Winooski, USA). Resulting total intensity of MitoSOX red fluorescence emission was then normalized by the number of nuclei identified. Three replicate wells were measured per treatment. To ensure consistency in image quality and fluorescence emission detection, consistent exposure time, camera gain levels, and contrast/brightness adjustments were uniformly implemented to all images. Representative images of each treatment group were obtained using Cytation-10 imager under consistent excitation and emission parameters.
2.6
Statistical analysis
All measures were analyzed using Prism v5 software (GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, California) and R v3.6. Results are expressed as mean ± standard error (SE). For comparisons across the six experimental groups, one-way ANOVA was employed, followed by post hoc analysis using Tukey’s honest significant difference (HSD) test for pairwise comparisons.
3
Results
3.1
Effect of Cd and DM on mitochondrial metabolism
HLF cells were exposed to Cd (1 µM) with or without DM (5 µM) for 24 h and analyzed by high-resolution mass spectrometry followed by ANOVA. Results for C18 negative (C18-) revealed 8125 differentially expressed metabolic features at p < 0.05, and further adjustment by applying FDR of 20 % resulted in 1259 features, while results for HILIC positive (HILIC+) revealed 13,293 differentially expressed metabolic features at p < 0.05 and further FDR adjustment of 20 % resulted in 3627 features. Two-way HCA of the 1259 metabolic features for C18- and 3627 features for HILIC+ displayed a clear separation of the Cd-only group, while those of the groups exposed to DM with or without Cd were more closely clustered separately (
Fig. 1
A).

Fig. 1
Combined pathway enrichment analysis based on output from both ionization modes C18- and HILIC+ showed an impact on multiple metabolic pathways, including those associated with redox homeostasis and oxidative stress (
Supplemental Table1
). These included methionine and cysteine metabolism, which support the synthesis of the essential thiol antioxidant, glutathione. Other pathways associated with oxidative stress include the antioxidant ascorbate (vitamin C) and the pentose phosphate pathway, which provides NADPH to support antioxidant systems to protect against oxidative stress
[46]
,
[58]
,
[73]
. Thus, the metabolic data provide strong support for the effects of Cd and DM on pathways protecting against oxidative stress. Metabolic pathways enriched with the highest number of metabolic features (p < 0.05) were related to mitochondrial function and bioenergetics (
Fig. 1
B). These included the citric acid cycle, glycolysis and gluconeogenesis, and pyruvate metabolism, each of which has important roles to support ATP production. Other important mitochondria-linked pathways, such as the urea cycle, arginine and proline metabolism, and fatty acid oxidation pathways, support nitrogen elimination and the integration of amino acid metabolism with bioenergetics. Among these, degradation of branched-chain amino acids occurs in the mitochondria, supporting energy production by oxidation of the fatty acid side chains. Additionally, the urea cycle is linked to glutamate and arginine metabolism, and arginine is a precursor for creatine, which is used for short-term energy homeostasis. Collectively, these results show that Cd and DM have widespread effects on mitochondrial metabolism and bioenergetics. Pathway enrichment analysis also showed that other amino acid pathways were widely impacted. These included 1-carbon metabolism (glycine, serine, alanine, and threonine), which has an important role in support of cell proliferation
[17]
. 1-Carbon metabolism is also essential, along with proline metabolism, to support collagen biosynthesis and turnover
[56]
. Because of the importance of collagen in lung fibrosis, these are addressed more specifically below.
3.2
Effect of Cd and DM on mitochondrial superoxide production
Measured effects of Cd and DM on oxidative stress pathways, along with prior evidence for effects on the mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC), prompted us to examine effects on mitochondrial superoxide anion radical production. In particular, superoxide generation by mitochondrial ETC complex I and III is known to contribute to mitochondrial dysfunction
[29]
,
[67]
,
[7]
. Increased superoxide production was assessed using mitochondrial superoxide indicator MitoSOX. Quantitative analysis of total fluorescence intensity of MitoSOX superoxide indicator showed HLF cells exposed to Cd or DM alone did not significantly increase superoxide, with estimated increases of around 7 % and 15 %, respectively (
Fig. 2
). However, the Cd + DM treatment group showed 60 % higher intensity than the control group (
Fig. 2
). These results provide important corroborating evidence for the above metabolomics evidence that the combined exposure to Cd and DM causes oxidative stress, specifically through enhanced mitochondrial superoxide production.

3.4
Alterations in citric acid cycle
Treatment with Cd and DM, individually and in combination, produced changes in key metabolites of the citric acid cycle and associated metabolic pathways, with DM having a more profound overall impact (
Fig. 4
). Regulation of the citric acid cycle occurs at several steps, i.e., pyruvate dehydrogenase, isocitrate dehydrogenase, α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase and malate dehydrogenase, with apparent regulation at each of these steps shown by relative abundances of substrates and products. Cd is known to activate poly-ADP-ribose polymerase, an enzyme that rapidly decreases cellular NAD
+
. Inhibition of citrate synthase by a relative excess of NADH could explain the increased abundance of acetyl-CoA in Cd+DM compared to DM alone. The inhibition of citrate synthase could also explain the decreased concentrations of citrate and cis-aconitate with Cd and DM. Increased α-ketoglutarate in the presence of DM and Cd+DM may reflect previously observed decreased activity of cytochrome c oxidase and expression of cytochrome c in rat brain following exposure to DM
[13]
. With complex III inhibition, downstream citric acid cycle changes in succinate, fumarate, and malate are consistent with a relative increase in NADH/NAD
+
ratio. Taking into consideration the increased superoxide generation (
Fig. 2
), impaired mitochondrial respiration (
Fig. 3
) and changes in abundance of intermediates of the citric acid cycle (
Fig. 4
), the result collectively show that Cd and DM independently and in combination cause oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction which could have adverse effects in lung disease etiology.

3.5
Quantitative effects of Cd and DM on proline and hydroxyproline
Proline, hydroxyproline, and glycine are highly abundant amino acids in collagen, a protein associated with fibrotic lung diseases. Pathway enrichment analysis showed changes in proline metabolism and 1-carbon metabolism, prompting us to examine the relative effects on quantities of these amino acids. Importantly, proline is incorporated into procollagen forms, and hydroxylation to hydroxyproline occurs during protein maturation. Thus, free hydroxyproline is largely a consequence of collagen degradation and turnover. Results showed that proline and hydroxyproline, both of which are integral building blocks of the alpha chains of collagen, were increased in DM-treated cells, and Cd had no independent effect (
Fig. 5
). However, glycine, another integral amino acid component of collagen alpha chains, displayed no observable changes upon exposure to either Cd or DM. These results show that DM stimulates collagen turnover and that Cd has no effect at the concentration studied.

4
Discussion
The current study shows that exposure of human lung fibroblasts to non-cytotoxic concentrations of Cd and DM, individually and in combination, caused metabolic alterations previously linked to lung disease etiology
[14]
,
[39]
,
[40]
,
[70]
,
[71]
. Results show changes in cellular metabolism linked to oxidative stress and mitochondrial activities. Furthermore, shifting of bioenergetics, characterized by decreased dependency on oxidative phosphorylation and increased dependency on alternative processes such as glycolysis and glutaminolysis-driven citric acid cycle, occurred in response to the respective treatments and was more prominent with DM. Altogether, changes in overall cellular metabolism and bioenergetics, coupled with increased mitochondrial ROS presence and an increase in levels of amino acids critical to collagen synthesis, indicate DM-induced fibrogenic cellular environment.
Exposure to environmental toxicants Cd and DM can induce metabolic reprogramming or dysregulation, which are involved in the pathogenesis of pulmonary fibrosis
[20]
,
[52]
,
[53]
,
[5]
,
[60]
,
[77]
. While such adaptive responses are tailored to the specific needs and capabilities of individual cell types, an overarching paradigm is that metabolic response, particularly in fibroblasts, is an integral step of myofibroblastic differentiation
[22]
,
[31]
,
[34]
,
[35]
. Our targeted analysis of the citric acid cycle intermediates showed varying levels of accumulation of citrate, α-ketoglutarate (α-KG), and oxaloacetate, indicating a potential for disrupted citric acid cycle flux at those steps. In particular, the accumulation of α-KG coincided with a similar accumulation in glutamate levels, which suggests the involvement of glutaminolysis, a process by which glutamine is converted into α-KG, feeding directly into the citric acid cycle to support energy production and biosynthetic processes
[81]
. Indeed, the citric acid cycle intermediates downstream of α-KG follow nearly identical patterns as those observed in both glutamate and α-KG. While an alternative explanation for the observed accumulation of α-KG could involve inhibition of α-KG dehydrogenase and subsequent impairment in the conversion of α-KG to succinate, the collective display of such similar outcomes in abundances of downstream intermediates may indicate increased dependency on glutaminolysis supplementation of citric acid cycle. This could help maintain citric acid cycle flux and production of NADH and FADH₂ for ATP generation
[45]
.
Cells rely on both oxidative phosphorylation and glycolysis for ATP production, with increased reliance upon glycolysis when mitochondrial function is compromised
[89]
. In the current study, we found that levels of fructose bisphosphate and lactic acid were increased in DM-treated groups, both alone and with Cd. While fructose bisphosphate is an important intermediate during the breakdown of glucose into pyruvate during glycolysis, the accumulation of lactic acid has been well-documented to be an indication of glycolysis
[69]
. Overall, taking into consideration the evidence of disrupted citric acid cycle, impaired oxidative phosphorylation, and accumulation of glycolysis-associated metabolites, we conclude that DM results in a shift in HLF bioenergetics independent of Cd effects.
The process of glutaminolysis, aside from its role as a supplemental source of components towards energy production, has been previously implicated in having a more direct role in the progression of fibrosis. A recent study involving fibrotic tissues of cardiac fibrosis patients showed that glutaminolysis is a critical mediator of activated myofibroblast persistence
[25]
. Another study showed that glutaminolysis increased both the production and stability of collagen, mediated by mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) activation and proline hydroxylation, respectively
[23]
. Glutaminolysis has been implicated in epigenetic modulation of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) fibroblasts
[85]
and also found to activate mTORC1 signaling, which is known as a central integrator of nutrient and growth factor sensing and cell growth, survival, and metabolism
[18]
.
Accumulation of α-KG was observed in human lung fibroblasts that were treated with TGF-β1, and α-KG was found to mediate glutaminolysis regulation of collagen translation by activating mTORC1
[24]
. Furthermore, administration of α-KG elevated serum levels of proline in both animal and human studies
[66]
,
[75]
. Proline, a key amino acid component necessary for synthesis of type I collagen, can further be hydroxylated in protocollagen to 4-hydroxyproline, which is necessary to form the triple helix of the collagen molecule. In the current study, we found that the abundance of both proline and 4-hydroxyproline was significantly increased by DM treatment, consistent with increased fibroblast synthesis and turnover of collagen.
Cd-induced ROS production within mitochondria is a key mechanism driving cytotoxicity and can contribute to fibrotic processes in chronic exposure
[9]
. Cd has been documented to target complexes I & III of the ETC, resulting in production of ROS
[59]
. DM also impacts mitochondrial function, leading to the production of ROS
[12]
. Such oxidative environment activates fibroblasts and can lead to excessive tissue fibrosis in chronic exposure involving multiple cellular response mechanisms. Excess ROS triggers activation of nucleotide-binding domain, leucine-rich repeat, and pyrin domain-containing protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome. NLRP3 activation contributes to fibrosis by chronically elevating IL-1β and IL-18, which drive fibroblasts toward a myofibroblast phenotype and, via autocrine loops, maintain an activated inflammasome
[4]
. Furthermore, activated myofibroblasts activate TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway, which results in expression of genes involved in extracellular matrix remodeling and in production of fibrillary collagens, α-smooth muscle actin, and fibronectin – critical processes involved in fibrosis
[50]
,
[57]
,
[74]
,
[76]
. Indeed, our findings show that co-exposure to low doses of Cd and DM resulted in a potentiated increase in mitochondrial superoxide generation, while the doses of Cd and DM used individually do not increase mitochondrial ROS. Mitochondrial superoxide is primarily produced by complexes I and III, and increased mitochondrial ROS is in line with redox imbalance being a key contributing factor to fibrosis. Redox imbalance is known to cause activation of fibroblasts as well as induction of pro-fibrotic signaling
[14]
,
[65]
. Future efforts incorporating morphological endpoints or ultrastructural imaging using transmission electron microscopy could be utilized to assess ROS-associated changes in cell membrane integrity and mitochondrial architecture, such as cristae remodeling/density, matrix swelling, and compromised outer-membrane. Such efforts can further elucidate the consequences of Cd/DM-induced oxidative stress and downstream pro-fibrotic processes.
5
Conclusion
This study shows that co-exposure of lung fibroblasts to low environmental levels of DM and Cd alters mitochondrial bioenergetics, and perturbs cellular metabolism, and induces oxidative stress, all of which may contribute to the activation of profibrotic signaling and increased lung pathology.
