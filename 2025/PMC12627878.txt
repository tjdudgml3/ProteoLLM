Introduction
Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) encompasses a highly heterogeneous group of neurodevelopmental disorders characterized by early-onset impairments in communication and social interaction, as well as restricted interest and repetitive behavior. A growing body of evidence owed this disruption toward the bidirectional gut microbiota-brain axis through influencing neuroendocrine, neuroimmune, metabolic, and autonomic nervous systems
[1]
. The involvement of gut microbiota (GM) in the pathophysiology of ASD across these systems has been studied independently. Earlier reports indicated that GM might affect the integrity of the intestinal barrier, a phenomenon known as “
leaky gut
”, or influence cytokine release by impacting vagal signaling across the blood–brain barrier (BBB)
[2]
. Other studies have demonstrated an association with mitochondrial dysfunction, short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), folate, cholesterol deficits, and various metabolic pathways
[3]
,
[4]
,
[5]
. The heritability of autism has been discussed for a long time, and some multi-genetic abnormalities have been linked to ASD
[6]
. However, genetic linkage analysis has been inconclusive
[7]
. Whether the causal factors are genetic, non-genetic such as environmental, or nutritional, the exact mechanism underlying the development of ASD remains unclear. Its pathogenesis is believed to be multifactorial, involving perturbation and dysbiosis of GM.
GM comprises trillions of symbiotic microorganisms, including bacteria, viruses, protozoa, fungi, and archaea, collectively known as the microbiome. In a healthy gut, 90 % of the bacterial population belongs to four major phyla: Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, Fusobacteria, and Verrucomicrobia. Despite variations in GM composition across different life stages, accumulating evidence supports the protective role of microbiome against pathogenic microorganisms through metabolite production, and by enhancing the maturation of the host's adaptive immune system
[8]
. Dysbiosis, or alteration in GM composition, has been extensively reported in ASD
[9]
,
[10]
,
[11]
, with profound shifts in taxa proportions observed in both animal models and autistic patients
[12]
,
[13]
. For instance, several studies have noted an overgrowth of
Clostridia
in regressive autistic patients' stool compared to healthy individuals
[3]
. This bacterium is known to produce macromolecules critical for brain functionality, such as dopamine, tryptophan, serotonin, SCFAs, and p-cresol, directly influencing the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis
[14]
. This dysbiotic event is likely a significant factor behind intestinal inflammation, leading to disruptions in gut epithelial permeability and translocation of molecules from the gastrointestinal tract to the bloodstream. A cascade that possibly may impact cognitive brain function
[15]
.
The advancement of cutting-edge 'multi-omics' interdisciplinary technologies in microbiome research has enabled a better understanding of the possible influence of GM on human health. In this comprehensive study, we utilized multi-omics approaches to explore the pathophysiological role of GM on severely autistic patients. The association between altered microbial community structure and biomass (via genomics) and dysregulated metaproteomics profiling, and metabolomics pinpointed essential perturbed pathways that might affect brain function and ultimately ASD behavior.
Materials and methods
Patient cohort and metadata
This study was conducted at the research department of Children's Cancer Hospital-57357, Egypt. Patients were recruited from the child and adolescent psychiatry outpatient clinic at the Suez Canal University Hospital, Ismailia, Egypt, between September 2019 and 2020. All participants provided consent through their guardians. The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Suez Canal University (APN 3839). The Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) group was clinically diagnosed according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th edition (DSM-5), and the International Statistics Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th Revision (ICD-10) (code F84.0), by a consultant
[16]
,
[17]
. Autism severity was assessed using the Childhood Autism Rating Scale (CARS), with all ASD children scoring within the “severely affected” range
[18]
. The control group included those who were symptomatically doing well mentally and physically. Typically developing children with no chronic or recent complaints of stomach/ gut problems such as chronic diarrhea, constipation, gas, heartburn, or bloating and no relevant disease history were recruited (Supplementary methods).
Stool sample preparation
Total fecal DNA was extracted following the International Human Microbiome Standards (IHMS) guidelines (SOP 03V1)
[19]
. Fecal samples were collected from participants on ice packs and immediately transferred to −20 °C for 24 hrs. To ensure consistency and accuracy in weight measurements, the samples were weighed while still frozen, aliquoted, and stored at −80 °C until further processing in a dedicated biosafety cabinet.
Metagenomics 16S rRNA PCR amplification and sequencing
Genomic DNA was extracted from 0.2 g fecal samples using the PureLink™ Microbiome DNA Purification Kit (Invitrogen™, USA), following the manufacturer's instructions. DNA samples were quantified after elution using DeNovix® dsDNA High Sensitivity Assay to verify the purity and integrity of DNA before NGS sequencing. The illumina MiSeqDx platform was used to isolate the V3 and V4 regions of bacterial 16S rRNA
[20]
(see Supplementary methods).
Metaproteomics shotgun analysis
Protein extraction and in-Gel digestion
A modified filtration-based protocol was used to purify proteins from fecal samples
[21]
. Briefly, 1 g fecal samples were rinsed with cold PBS, homogenized for 15 min, and centrifuged at 300 ×
g
at 4 °C for 5 min to remove food debris
[22]
. The supernatant was collected, and the pellet was re-suspended in 2 ml PBS and vortexed. This step was repeated, and the pooled supernatant was subjected to acetone precipitation overnight at −20 °C
[23]
. Proteins were recovered by centrifugation at 12,000 ×
g
at 4 °C for 30 min and dissolved in lysis buffer (4 % SDS) in 100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.5). Disulfide bonds were reduced by adding 2 µl of 1 M Tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP)
[24]
. To inhibit proteolysis, 100 µl of cOmplete, Mini, EDTA-free Protease Inhibitor Cocktail
TM
(complete ultra-tablets, mini, Roche, Mannheim) was added. After another acetone precipitation, the lysate was dissolved in 8 M Urea in 500 mM Tris pH 8.5 and assayed using bicinchoninic acid (BCA) method (Pierce, Rockford IL) at Å562 nm prior to digestion. Samples underwent SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) followed by
In-gel
tryptic digestion (additional information in Supplementary methods).
Metaproteomics profiling using IDA-based LC-MS/MS
Nano liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis was performed using TripleTOF 5600
+
(AB Sciex, Ontario, Canada) interfaced with an Ekspert nanoLC 425 pump and Ekspert NanoLC 400 autosampler (Supplementary methods). To ensure MS accuracy, pre-batch mass calibration was performed using a tuning solution (AB Sciex, Ontario, Canada). PepCalMix calibration solution (AB Sciex, Ontario, Canada) was used within the batch and scheduled every six hours to correct possible TOF deviation during runs.
Untargeted metabolomics analysis
Extraction of metabolites from fecal samples
For metabolite extraction, 100 mg fecal samples were used. The extraction was performed by adding 400 μl of pre-chilled extraction solvent (ACN: methanol (MeOH) [3:1]) to each sample. For validation and absolute quantification, amino acids standards (L-Threonine, L-Tryptophan, L-Tyrosine) were used (Sigma-Aldrich Co., MO, USA). A stock solution of amino acids standards (2 µg/mL) was prepared in a mixture of water: methanol: acetonitrile (H
2
O: MeOH: ACN, 2:1:1 V/V) (Supplementary methods).
Metabolome profiling using SWATH-based LC-MS/MS
Small molecule separation was carried out using AB Sciex ExionLC
TM
AC UHPLC with an Acuity XSelect HSS T3 analytical column (2.1 × 150 mm, 2.5 µm ID) (Waters Co, Milford, US) maintained at 40° C. Mass calibration was automatically performed every 2 h (every four runs) by the automated calibration delivery system using the APCI calibration solution (AB SCIEX, Ontario, Canada) (See Supplementary methods).
Data processing and database searching
16S rRNA sequence data pre-processing and quality control
Pooling and normalization of the library were performed according to illumina manufacturing instructions for the 60 samples. The quality of the paired-end reads (∼853,790 reads per sample) was assessed using FastQC (v0.11.8) and MultiQC (v1.8)
[25]
,
[26]
. According to the quality check, the read-pairs that have a low quality (mean per-base-sequence-quality <30) were filtered (one sample was excluded from the control group), and adapters were removed using Trimmomatic (v0.39)
[27]
. The filtered overlapping forward and reverse reads were merged using paired-end read merger (PEAR) (v0.9.6) to generate single-end reads for alignment
[28]
.
Alignment and taxonomy profiling of metagenomics data
After sequence pre-processing and quality control, alignment was performed using MALT aligner (v0.4.1) against the SILVA database (Release 132), with a minimum percentage identity cut-off of 99.5 % and minimum coverage cut-off of 100 %
[29]
,
[30]
. All alignment outputs were in Robust Multi-Array Average (RMA) format. Taxonomy profiling was performed in MEGAN 6 using the Lowest Common Ancestor (LCA) algorithm with MinScore = 50.0, MaxExpected = 1.0, MinPercentIdentity = 99.5, TopPercent = 10.0, MinSupportPercent = 0.001, MinSupport = 4, and LCA = naive for each sample
[31]
.
Metaproteomics and host proteomics data processing
Raw MS files were analyzed by Protein pilot
TM
Software (version 5.0.1). The search parameters were set as follows: cysteine reduction, Iodoacetamide alkylation, trypsin digestion, and urea denaturation with a 1 % false discovery rate (FDR) analysis. An in-house pseudo-metaproteome bacterial database was constructed based on the 16S rRNA metagenome analysis
[32]
. The database containing 21,949,895 metaproteins was used as a background for subsequent metaproteomic search. The Spectral count (SpC) was calculated for each metaprotein based on the identified peptides in the 660 fractions. This count was utilized to calculate the spectral abundance factor (SAF)
[33]
. A novel approach was used to simplify the redundancy of metaproteins without losing taxonomic origin. Gene symbols were retrieved for each metaprotein accession, and metaproteins with unknown genes were uniquely coded to replace the gene symbols. Duplicate entries were filtered by removing similar SAF and gene/code and concatenating accession information. Finally, metaproteome abundance was calculated by calculating the normalized spectral abundance factor (NSAF) (Supplementary Fig. 1)
[34]
. For host proteomics analysis, the same RAW files were searched against the
Homo sapiens
proteome database (UP00005640; 20,594 entries) with the same search parameters and processing.
Metabolomics data processing
Small molecules were identified using 'SASA tool'
[35]
. A High-resolution Human Metabolome database (feces metabolites database-version 4.0) was used as a search space (
https://hmdb.ca

[36]

Parent peaks were detected using PeakView 2.2 with MasterView 1.1 (AB SCIEX, Ontario, Canada), then aligned with their corresponding XICs of MS2 using MultiQuant 3.0.2 Software (AB SCIEX, Ontario, Canada). Parent and fragment peaks were filtered with an initial mass tolerance of 10 ppm and XIC signal-to-noise ratio (S/N) > 30, and sample: blank with (S/N) > 5. Fragments' relative intensities per component SPectraL hASH; database-independent identifier (SPLASH) were filtered and stratified depending on their relative intensity; fragments with > 75 % relative intensities were selected as the main daughter (base peak), while < 5 % were excluded. Statistical analyses and data visualization
All statistical analyses and graphical representations were conducted using R codes (see code availability)
[37]
. Alpha diversity was assessed using the Shannon-weaver index
[38]
. The 16S ribosomal RNA amplicon sequence variant (ASV) and NSAF abundances were used to build cladograms with a heatmap with LCA using GraPhlAn
[39]
. Netshift was used to identify critical microbial genera that serve as “drivers” in the most common sub-network between control and ASD based on pair-wise Spearman's rank correlation analysis between all genera
[40]
.
Metaproteomics, host proteomics, and metabolomics data were normalized using Probabilistic Quotient Normalization (PQN)
[41]
. The filtration process involved removing metaproteins and host proteins with missing values ≥50 % and metabolites with missing values ≥20 % in each group. Missing values were imputed by a randomized value of median ± 10 % around per group
[42]
,
[43]
. Before analysis, auto-scaling was applied. Each dataset was tested for normality using Shapiro–Wilk normality test with a
p
-value < 0.05. Appropriate statistical analysis (Student's
t
-test or Wilcoxon Mann-Whitney) was used to identify the significant differentially expressed host proteins, metaproteins, and metabolites between both experimental groups. Log
2
fold change (FC) ≥1 with
q
-value <0.05 (“BH”; Benjamini and Hochberg) was considered significant. Effect sizes were calculated for significant features, and 95 % confidence intervals (CIs) were provided to strengthen the interpretation of results. To address the potential impact of confounding elements, we utilized Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA) to exclude the impact of other possible confounders, including Gender, Socioeconomic Status (SES), Residence, Age, and Mode of Delivery. Subsequently, we applied 5 % FDR to recalibrate the
p
-values for each feature
[44]
.
Gene ontology, functional annotation, and enrichment analysis
Gene ontology (GO) was retrieved from the UniProt database using UniprotR
[45]
. A chord plot was generated to retrieve a functional annotation through Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG) and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG)
[46]
,
[47]
. For host proteomics data, gene ontology enrichment analysis was retrieved from KEGG and Reactome using g: profiler
[48]
. For metabolomics data, pathway enrichment analysis was drawn using IMPaLA to visualize the enriched pathways of the differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs) with a
q
-value < 0.05
[49]
. MetOrigin was used to discriminate the origin of microbial metabolites
[50]
. The tool utilizes statistical analysis (Spearman's correlation) and biological data (KEGG database) to identify all potential bacteria that might participate in a metabolic reaction. Metabolite-metabolite interaction was parsed using STITCH
[51]
.
Results
Cohort characteristics and clinical Assessments
The demographic and clinical characteristics of ASD and control groups are detailed in Supplementary Table 1. Both groups had a similar gender distribution, with 56.7 % male and 43.3 % female participants. The average age was comparable between both groups, with 7.2 ± 2.94 years for ASD group and 7.4 ± 2.92 years for the counterpart controls. Socioeconomic status (SES) was evenly distributed across both groups, with 56.7 % in the lower SES category and 43.3 % in the higher SES category. Autism severity, assessed by the Childhood Autism Rating Scale (CARS), showed significantly higher scores in the ASD group (44.77 ± 3.81) compared to the control group (19.73 ± 4.73). Similarly, the 6-GSI scores, an indicator for gastrointestinal symptoms, were elevated in the ASD group (6.5 ± 1.80) compared to healthy controls (1.33 ± 0.66).

Inter-microbial association and shuffling network depict altered microbiota crosstalks
We utilized the “NetShift” co-occurrence analysis to trace rewiring and community changes in microbial association networks between control and ASD groups. This analysis aimed to better understand gut inter-microbial association and key microbial groups that are likely act as ‘drivers’ in ASD children. These “drivers” are a set of gut microbiota that facilitate several changes and hence becomes an important factor for understanding the microbial basis of the disease.
The most common sub-network between the control and ASD groups is illustrated in

Pathway analysis and gene ontology of differentially expressed metaproteins
To understand the interaction between bacterial metaproteins and the host (ASD patients), we investigated their pathway involvement, focusing on shared pathways between bacteria and the host. The chord plot illustrates the pathway analysis of 112 metaproteins derived from 61 genes, linked to their bacterial origin (
Fig. 2

These metaproteins were implicated in a wide array of pathways, including carbohydrate transport and metabolism (CTM), pentose glucuronate interconversions (PGI), and glycolysis/ gluconeogenesis (GG). Additionally, other metaproteins were involved in genetic information processing pathways, such as ribosome (Rb) and translation (TR). Our study also identified metaproteins contributed to ATP-binding cassette transporters (ABC transporters; ABCT) and energy production and conversion (EPC), highlighting their potential functional importance in the interplay between gut microbiota and the host.
Gene ontology (GO) analysis further revealed that these metaproteins were associated with biological processes such as translation, gluconeogenesis, glucose, and D-xylose metabolic processes. The molecular functions of these metaproteins were involved in ribosomal structure, rRNA binding, and Atp binding as detailed in Supplementary Fig. 3f.
Proteome signature of host response
To elucidate the possible crosstalk between the host and associated gut microbiota, proteome profiles from control and ASD patients were searched against the human proteome database (20,594 entries). Bacterial sequence similarities were carefully evaluated to exclude bacterial source metaproteins (Supplementary Table 9). Partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) revealed modest yet significant differences in host fecal proteome profiles between the two cohorts, validated through diagnostic statistics (Supplementary Fig. 4a). Volcano plot and heatmap analysis further illustrated these differences (Supplementary Fig. 4b, c). Discussion
Reduced microbiome diversity has been associated with impaired intestinal resilience and increased susceptibility to environmental stressors
[53]
. In this study, ASD patients exhibited significantly lower microbial diversity (
Fig. 1
a), which may contribute to increased gut permeability (“leaky gut”), systemic inflammation, and immune dysregulation
[54]
. At the phylum level, Actinobacteria, Proteobacteria, and Verrucomicrobia were up-regulated in ASD compared to control. These phyla demonstrated higher bacterial biomass and metaprotein abundance (
Figs. 1
b and
2
c). Similar findings in previous studies have linked Proteobacteria and Verrucomicrobia to chronic low-grade inflammation in the gut
[55]
,
[56]
. The observed increase in Verrucomicrobia, particularly the genus
Akkermansia
, has been linked to immune regulation in ASD in earlier studies
[53]
,
[57]
. Although
Actinobacteria
exhibited higher biomass and metaprotein abundance in ASD, its diversity was notably lower compared to controls. This reduction in diversity is due to an increase in
Bifidobacteria
, which contributed to a lower diversity ratio of Actinobacteria to Firmicutes. This metric needs further validation and monitoring. Similar findings have been observed in the
Shank3
knockout mouse model (Autistic- like) and Chinese ASD children
[55]
,
[58]
, although a recent metaproteomic study presented contradictory results
[59]
. The Bacteroidetes/Firmicutes (B/F) ratio was reported in previous studies with opposing ratios in autism
[60]
,
[61]
,
[62]
. There was no significant difference in our study (Supplementary Fig. 2c). This heterogeneity might be explained by genetic, environmental, or even lifestyle habits.
Investigating the mechanisms underlying bacterial colonization in the gut is essential for understanding the gut-brain axis in ASD. The elevated
Bifidobacterium
at the genus level is thought to be incriminated in ASD neurophysiological deficits
[63]
. In this study, the integration of metaproteomics and host proteomics highlighted synergistic findings. Key
Bifidobacterium-
derived metproteins such as xylose isomerase, enolase, and NAD-dependent glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (NAD-GAPDH), were significantly up-regulated (
Fig. 2
b and 2e). These molecules have been reported to regulate the hyperactivity in the drosophila model
[64]
, suggesting their potential relevance to ASD-associated hyperactivity. Enolase and NAD-GAPDH, known as moonlighting proteins, are not only critical metabolic enzymes in glycolysis but also play an essential role in bacterial colonization of the host epithelium. Their involvement in maintaining gut barrier integrity and regulating immune signaling highlights their potential contribution to systemic inflammation and behavioral deficits observed in ASD
[65]
,
[66]
. The host proteome analysis further revealed up-regulation of kallikrein (KLK1), a plasminogen activator, which works in concert with bacterial metaproteins to convert plasminogen into the active protease plasmin
[67]
,
[68]
. This interaction underscores the potential cross-talk between microbial macromolecules and host proteins, linking gut dysbiosis to host responses. At the same time, we suggest that the recruitment of
Bifidobacterium
-derived metaproteins and their interaction with host proteome components may contribute to key pathological features of ASD, including hyperactivity, systemic inflammation, and neurodevelopmental deficits.
Peptostreptococcaceae and Lachnospiraceae families, affiliated with the order Clostridiale
,
have been previously associated with social behavior deficits in ASD patients
[69]
,
[70]
. Within genera of these 2 families,
Intestinibacter
and
Romboutsia
(Family Peptostreptococcaceae) are considered potentially harmful bacteria that implicate in neurodevelopmental disorders
[71]
. Meanwhile,
Dorea
and
Lachnoclostridium
(Family Lachnospiraceae) have been linked to liver metabolism and behavioral traits in ASD
[62]
.
Supporting above-mentioned observation, our study revealed correlations between unbalanced gut metabolites in ASD patients and specific genera within these families, including
Blautia
,
Romboutsia
, and
Anaerostipes
. These correlations further highlight the intricate interplay between gut microbial composition and metabolic disruptions in ASD.
We further explored how the microbiome coordinates in ASD. Microbiota relies on quorum sensing (QS) to communicate with each other in response to a diverse array of pathophysiological conditions
[72]
. A central feature of QS is the release of auto inducers, which facilitate the regulating microbiota social activities
[73]
. Interestingly,
Citrobacter
,
Klebsiella,
and
Enterobacter
had lower coreness values in control samples compared to their ASD counterparts, possibly due to reduced commensal effort in subduing pathogen colonization in ASD (Figures d-f). The increased abundance of these opportunistic pathogens in ASD children, alongside their high coreness values in microbial networks, indicates an altered gut community. Such disruptions may increase pro-inflammatory signaling, compromise gut barrier integrity, and impair ASD-related gastrointestinal and behavioral symptoms.
Klebsiella
and
Enterobacter
, in particular, are well-recognized opportunistic pathogens associated with GI symptoms and autism severity in previous studies
[74]
,
[75]
,
[76]
.
A reduction in SCFA-producing microbes, such as
Mitsuokella
,
Butyrivibrio
, and
Fusobacterium
, was observed in this study, potentially contributing to an imbalance in brain function and behavior
[77]
. A growing body of evidence supports the hypothesis that gut microbiota products, such as SCFAs, phenolic compounds, and free amino acids, play a vital role in the behavior of ASD patients
[78]
. SCFAs, particularly acetic acid (AA), propionic acid (PPA), and butyrate, are believed to affect ASD behavior via the gut-brain axis
[79]
,
[80]
. However, the underlying mechanisms are not yet fully elucidated.
In this study, SCFAs production was notably perturbed in ASD patients. Glycolysis and pentose-phosphate pathways showed significant down-regulation of enzymes such as phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PEPCK) and fumarate reductase, likely leading to reduced propionic acid (PPA) production. Conversely, enzymes involved in butyrate production via the reverse β-oxidation pathway, such as acetyl-CoA acetyltransferase and 3-hydroxy-butyryl-CoA dehydrogenase, were up-regulated, while butyryl-CoA: acetate CoA transferase was down-regulation. Notably, the up-regulation of acetyl-CoA acetyltransferase is linked to histone deacetylases (HDACs), key contributors to the pathophysiology of autism
[81]
. Interestingly, altered butyrate and PPA production, potent HDAC inhibitors, support the possible postulation of HDAC involvement in autism. Our metagenomics and metaproteomic findings reported Faecilbacterium as a potential 'driver' in ASD with significant down-regulation of butyryl-CoA:acetate CoA transferase. The latter is a coenzyme A transferase (CoAT) responsible for the final step of butyric acid synthesis
[82]
. This finding further supports the butyrate synthesis deficit in ASD patients.
The SCFA deficit was also reflected in disruptions of key signaling pathways, including signal transduction and activation of G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs). GPCRs are essential for gut homeostasis, linking the immune system to the microbiota and intestinal epithelial barrier
[83]
. Digging deep into the GPCRs pathway in ASD patients, we observed the down-regulation of certain polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFFs), such as alpha and gamma-linolenic acid, which are critical for the development of the central nervous system
[84]
. Interestingly, these metabolites showed a significant negative correlation with
Bifidobacterium
(
Fig. 4
c), an observation that warrants further investigation.
The role of microbiota in modulating reactive oxygen species (ROS) through mitochondrial pathways is well established
[85]
. Microbial-induced ROS generation in the intestinal lumen plays a critical role in maintaining redox balance through specific metaproteins and metabolites
[86]
. In ASD, we observed significant disturbance in redox homeostasis indicated by the altered expression of rubrerythrin and NADH peroxidase (
Fig. 2
b). These ROS scavenging metaproteins, primarily produced by
Blautia
and
Clostridium
suggest their potential role in mitigating oxidative stress. Additionally, metabolites of bacterial origin, including ursolic acid, indole-3-propionic acid (IPA), and hydroxycinnamic acid derivatives (
m-
coumaric acid, 4-hydroxycinnamic acid) were notably down-regulated in ASD compared to controls (
Fig. 3
c). These compounds are known for their protective effects against neurotoxic agents and intracellular ROS
[87]
,
[88]
,
[89]
.
The unique presence of
Tyzzerella
in the ASD group in our study was negatively correlated with hydroxycinnamic acid derivatives (
Fig. 4
c). Its abundance has been associated with autistic behavior in autistic-like rats
[90]
, as well as GI symptoms and repetitive behavior
[91]
. Taken together, it may incriminate its role in the disruption of oxidative stress.
We also observed an increased energy production, supported by higher ATP synthase subunit c in ASD patients. This proton channel is crucial for normal calcium homeostasis, redox signaling cascades, and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS)
[92]
,
[93]
. In turn, it influences the host mucosal immune system and subsequently impacts the microbial gut community by acting as an energy-exchange partner to facilitate the formation and survival of bacterial communities
[94]
.
Neurotransmitters such as glutamate and 3,4-Dihydroxyphenylacetate (DOPAC) were detected exclusively in the ASD group (
Fig. 3
d), highlighting their potential role in ASD pathophysiology. Both compounds are known neurotoxins capable of inducing neuronal death, particularly in neurodegenerative disorders
[95]
,
[96]
. Metaproteomics analysis revealed up-regulation of glutamate dehydrogenase, aligned with elevated glutamate levels in metabolomics profile. The increase in glutamate is thought to disrupt synaptic transmission and neuronal communication, potentially contributing to the neurological and behavioral features of ASD
[97]
. DOPAC, a key metabolite in the activation of the dopamine beta-hydroxylase deficiency pathway may further disrupt neurochemical balance by impairing dopamine regulation, which is crucial for mood and cognitive functions
[98]
.
Notably, 21 of the 25 differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs) identified in this study were shown to cross the blood–brain barrier (BBB), highlighting their possible direct impact on neurological manifestation in ASD patients (references in source data). Among them, tryptophan and tyrosine, precursors for serotonin and dopamine synthesis, influence mood regulation and repetitive behaviors
[99]
. Conversely, the down-regulation of neuroprotective metabolites such as indole-3-propionic acid could exacerbate oxidative stress and neuronal damage
[100]
. Collectively, these results illustrate how disruptions in metabolites that cross BBB, may drive neurological dysfunction and behavioral symptoms in ASD.
We further explored host responses to microbiota disruption, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 4. Differentially expressed host proteins between the experimental groups were mainly involved in nervous system development and immune response (Supplementary Table S10). The up-regulation of kallikrein 1 (klk1) and transthyretin (TTR) was reported in ASD patients. Both of which are implicated in neurodegenerative disorders but have not previously been associated with autism
[101]
,
[102]
. Altered TTR levels, often associated with neurodegenerative conditions, may impact cellular signaling pathways critical for neuronal health, highlighting their potential role in ASD pathology.
[103]
. This up-regulation suggests its potential involvement in neuro-inflammation and nervous system dysregulation, possibly influenced by gut microbiota composition. Additionally, the down-regulation of neprilysin, a key protective factor for neurons, supports the hypothesis of immune system dysregulation in ASD, specifically immunoglobulins (Supplementary Fig. 4f)
[104]
. These findings further confirm possible deficits in ASD patients’ immune systems. A finding was previously supported in an earlier study
[105]
. A schematic diagram illustrating the proposed host-microbiome interaction in ASD is shown in
Fig. 5
.

Conclusion and future perspective
The integration of multi-omics data in this study provides strong evidence that variations in gut microbiota, along with alterations in macromolecule production, significantly influence the manifestation of ASD-related symptoms and co-morbidities. By identifying specific microbial species and metabolites implicated in ASD, this research highlights their potential role in shaping the neurological and behavioral traits associated with the disorder. These findings contribute to a deeper understanding of the complex interactions between the gut microbiome and neurophysiological mechanisms in ASD.
While this study offers valuable insights, several limitations should be considered. The relatively small sample size (30 ASD and 30 control children) and cross-sectional design may limit the generalizability of these findings and make it difficult to establish causal relationships. Additionally, factors such as dietary habits and environmental exposures, which are known to influence microbiome composition and metabolic activity, were not specifically accounted for in this study and may warrant further consideration in future research. Despite these constraints, the results suggest promising findings for further exploration into how these microbial and metabolic molecules might be targeted to reduce ASD risk or serve as therapeutic interventions. Future studies with larger cohorts and longitudinal designs will be essential to validate these findings and discover their clinical potential.
