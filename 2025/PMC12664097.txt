1
Introduction
As living standards rise, there is an increasing demand for high-quality beef (
Li, Yan, & Zan, 2018
;
Smith, Gotoh, & Greenwood, 2018
). Beef quality is largely determined by the intramuscular fat (IMF) content, a trait influenced by multiple genes and quantitative trait loci (
Silva-Vignato et al., 2022
). IMF also impacts key meat attributes, including tenderness, shear strength, juiciness, and flavor (
Ueda, Hosoda, Yoshino, Yamanoue, & Shirai, 2021
). Simmental cattle, a globally recognized breed, are known for their fast growth and meat production. However, they tend to have low IMF deposition, which negatively affects the quality and flavor of their meat (
Wang et al., 2021
). In contrast, Wagyu cattle are distinguished by their high IMF content, which results in a rich flavor and characteristic marbling (
Gotoh, Nishimura, Kuchida, & Mannen, 2018
). One study found that Wagyu beef contains more IMF, less crude protein, and a more favorable fatty acid profile compared to Wagyu-Angus and Angus-Charolais-Limousine crosses (
Vázquez-Mosquera et al., 2023
). The Guyaun cattle, renowned for its high protein content, abundant glutamic acid, unique flavor, and tender, juicy meat, has garnered considerable attention in the Ningxia region and demonstrates significant potential for meat production. However, selective breeding and genetic improvement of this breed remain underdeveloped (
Liu et al., 2025
).
Previous research has primarily focused on genes related to IMF deposition at the transcriptional level. Ma et al. analyzed the genetic and flavor differences associated with varying IMF content in Xinjiang brown cattle and identified key genes related to flavor formation, including
AQP4
,
FZD2
,
FADS1
,
BPG1
,
CEBPD
, and
FABP4
. Their findings demonstrated that beef with higher IMF content possesses more pronounced flavor characteristics (
Ma et al., 2024
).Wang et al. utilized transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses of the
Longissimus dorsi
from Liangshan cattle and Simmental crossbred cattle, revealing that
FASN
promotes butanoyl-CoA biosynthesis, while
SLMAP
inhibits this pathway. Additionally, upregulation of
ALDOC
,
PFKL
,
PGAM1
, and
SDS
was observed, which may regulate citrulline flux. They established a potential regulatory model for the genetic-metabolic interactions influencing beef quality (
Wang et al., 2025
). For instance, transcriptome and metabolome analyses of the
Longissimus dorsi
muscle from Qinchuan cattle, Nanyang cattle, and Japanese Wagyu identified several key Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways influencing IMF content, including those involved in fatty acid metabolism, unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis, and fatty acid elongation. Additionally, the study highlighted ITGB1 as a critical gene associated with IMF content through weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) (
Yu et al., 2024
). This study assessed meat quality, transcriptome, and metabolomics to examine the characteristics of Pingliang Red Cattle, Wagyu cross F1 generation, and Simmental cattle. It identified key genes associated with fat deposition, including
FASN
,
ACACA
,
PLIN1
, and
FABP4
, as well as metabolites such as 3-iodo-L-tyrosine, arachidonic acid, and cis-aconitate (
Wang, An, et al., 2023
). The fatty acid composition of intramuscular fat (IMF) plays a significant role in health. Oleic acid (OA) and cis-9, trans-11 conjugated linoleic acid (c9t11-CLA) are two fatty acids known for their health benefits. To compare groups with high and low levels of OA and c9t11-CLA, both transcriptomic and proteomic analyses were conducted. The differentially expressed proteins were enriched in pathways related to fatty acid metabolism, proteolysis, thyroid hormone synthesis, cytoskeletal structure, and endoplasmic reticulum stress. Furthermore, TASP1, UBE4A, and HADHA were identified as potential biomarkers for these fatty acids (
Poleti et al., 2020
). These studies demonstrate significant differences in intramuscular fat deposition among different cattle breeds, and differential analysis between cattle breeds remains one of the primary research strategies.
To deepen the understanding of meat quality improvement, this study evaluated IMF content in different groups, collected tissue from the
Longissimus dorsi
, and performed differential protein and untargeted metabolomic analysis using DIA-PRM. Correlation analysis of differential proteins and metabolites was conducted to identify key proteins and metabolites associated with intramuscular fat deposition. This research established a protein and metabolite regulatory network for beef with varying IMF content, offering molecular markers for further enhancement of beef quality.
2
Materials and methods
2.1
Animals and sample collection
All cattle were raised at Guyuan Fumin Technology Development Co., Ltd. and slaughtered for food production purposes. Six cattle from each breed—18 Guyuan cattle, 21 Simmental cattle, and 19 Japanese Wagyu cattle—were selected for slaughter using random numbers generated by the RAND function in Microsoft Excel, matched with the animals' electronic ear tag identification numbers. All animals were healthy, provided with ad libitum access to feed and water, and reared under consistent nutritional and management conditions. The animals were reared until approximately 30 months of age before being transported to a commercial slaughterhouse. At the facility, the cattle were stunned with an electric shock for 5 s, followed by bleeding, skinning, evisceration, and splitting along the midline. The entire process of rearing, transportation, and slaughter complied with relevant animal welfare regulations and was supervised by Guyuan Fumin Technology Development Co., Ltd. Muscle samples were collected from the
Longissimus dorsi
muscle between the 12th and 13th ribs on the left side at 1 h post-slaughter. Samples were immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, transported to the laboratory, and stored at −80 °C until analysis. Proteomic analysis was conducted with three biological replicates, while metabolomic analysis utilized six biological replicates.
In addition, the intramuscular adipocytes were obtained from a healthy Guyuan calf, which was also slaughtered at a commercial slaughterhouse. The entire rearing, transportation, and slaughter process complied with relevant animal welfare regulations and was supervised by Guyuan Fumin Technology Development Co., Ltd. After the
Longissimus dorsi
muscle tissue was collected under sterile conditions, it was rinsed with 75 % alcohol, followed by two washes with phosphate buffer saline (PBS, HyClone, Logan, USA), and then placed in PBS containing 2 % penicillin-streptomycin (HyClone, Logan, USA) before being transported to the laboratory. Subsequently, intramuscular adipocytes were isolated using the collagenase digestion method (Solarbio, Beijing, China) described by
Ma et al. (2025)
and
Yang et al. (2024)
. This study and all experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of Ningxia University (approval no: NXU-2023-091), and permission was obtained from Fumin Agricultural Technology Development.
2.2
Meat quality traits
The IMF content was measured using the Soxhlet extraction method, following the Chinese GB/5009.6-2016 “National Standard for Food Safety Determination of Fat in Food.” To reduce lipid loss, samples were lyophilized using a freeze-drying machine instead of the traditional drying method. Prior to lyophilization, at least 4 g of each sample were weighed. Petroleum ether was used to extract the lipids. After extraction, the petroleum ether was evaporated, and the remaining samples were dried and accurately weighed to the nearest 0.001 g (each treatment had three parallel replicates).
The color of the meat samples was measured for lightness (L*), redness (a*), and yellowness (b*) using a CR-400 colorimeter (Konica Minolta, Inc., Japan). A 50 g meat sample (10 cm × 5 cm × 1 cm) was cut perpendicular to the muscle fibers, placed in a plastic bag, and stored at 4 °C. After 24 and 72 h of storage, the sample was removed from the container, gently blotted on filter paper, and reweighed. Drip loss (DL) was calculated as the percentage of the initial weight.
For the Warner-Bratzler Shear Force (WBSF) measurement, the meat sample was vacuum-sealed after weighing and then heated in a constant temperature water bath until the center temperature reached 72 °C. The sample was then cooled to room temperature, and a 1 cm meat column was sheared perpendicularly to the muscle fibers using a C-LM3 digital muscle tenderness meter (TENOVO, China), with the shear force (expressed in N) recorded. The average shear force value was taken as the WBSF. Additionally, cooking loss (CL) was calculated as the percentage difference between the initial weight and the weight of the sample before and after the Warner-Bratzler shear force measurement. Further phenotypic characterization of this study, including meat color (L, a, and b), DL, CL, and WBSF, is still under investigation. The results have not been published yet and are used solely for joint analysis.
2.3
LC-MS/MS analysis-DIA mode
The tissue samples for protein extraction were prepared and quantified by BioMarker Technologies, located in Beijing, China. The samples were first minced using liquid nitrogen and then lysed in a buffer containing 50 mM NH
4
HCO
3
(VN01H02, Sartorious, Göttingen, Germany), 10 mM MgCl
2
(A6141-500G, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA), 7 M urea (M123-1KG, Amresco, Pennsylvania, USA), and 2 M thiourea. Ultrasonication was performed on ice for 5 min, followed by centrifugation at 12,000×
g
for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was then collected for further analysis. Protein concentration was determined using the Bradford protein assay.
Next, 100 μg of protein from each sample was reduced with 10 mM DTT (M109-5G, Amresco, Pennsylvania, USA) at 56 °C for one hour and alkylated with iodoacetamide (M216-30G, Amresco, Pennsylvania, USA) in the dark at room temperature for one hour. Enzymatic digestion was performed using trypsin gold (V5280/100μg, Promega, Madison,USA) at a 1:50 enzyme-to-substrate ratio for 16 h at 37 °C. The resulting peptides were combined in equal amounts to create a mixture (mix-sample), while the remaining peptides were kept as single-sample. Both samples were desalted using a C18 cartridge to remove excess urea, then dried by vacuum centrifugation.
The single-sample was reconstituted in 0.1 % formic acid (
T79708
, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA), mixed with 0.2 μL of standard peptides from the iRT kit (Ki-3002-1, Biognosys Zurich, Switzerland), and analyzed using a U3000 UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, California, USA) coupled with an Orbitrap Fusion mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, California, USA). Data were acquired in data-independent acquisition (DIA) mode, with MS1 resolution set to 120,000 and MS2 resolution to 30,000. The
m
/
z
range covered 350–1350, with 60 variable cycles. Full scan AGC target was set to 4 × 10
6
with an injection time of 50 ms. DIA settings were NCE 35 %, target value 1 × 10
6
, and maximum injection time set to auto for parallel ion filling and detection.
Data analysis and visualization were performed using Proteome Discoverer 2.4 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, California, USA), Biognosys Spectronaut version 13, and the R statistical framework. Differentially expressed proteins were identified based on a corrected
P
-value < 0.05 and fold changes > 1.5.
2.4
LC-MS/MS metabolomic analysis
BioMarker Technologies, located in Beijing, China, provides untargeted metabolomics services. In the metabolite extraction process, 50 mg of
Longissimus dorsi
muscle tissue was mixed with 1 mL of an extract containing an internal standard. The extract was prepared by combining acetonitrile (75-05-8, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), methanol (67-56-1, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), and water in a 2:2:1 ratio, with 2-chloro-
L
-phenylalanine(103616-89-3, Aladdin, Shanghai, China) at a concentration of 20 mg/L. Steel beads were added to the mixture, and the sample was ground for 10 min at 45 Hz. Following this, sonication was applied for an additional 10 min. The extract was left at −20 °C for one hour, then centrifuged at 12,000×
g
and 4 °C for 15 min. The supernatant was collected and dried using a vacuum concentrator.
Resuspend the dried metabolites by adding 160 μL of extract solution with a 1:1 acetonitrile to water ratio. The sample was vortexed for 30 s, sonicated in an ice bath for 15 min, and then centrifuged at 12,000×
g
and 4 °C for 15 min. The resulting supernatant was used for subsequent analysis.
Metabolomics analysis was performed using a Waters Acquity I-Class PLUS ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography system coupled with a Waters Xevo G2-XS QTOF high-resolution mass spectrometer. Data acquired through MassLynx V4.2 was processed with Progenesis QI software, which included peak extraction, alignment, and other processing steps. This was done using the METLIN database and BioMarker's proprietary library for metabolite identification, with the theoretical fragment identification and mass deviation kept within 100 ppm.
Differential metabolites were identified using the following criteria: a fold change ≥ 2, a
P
-value < 0.05, and a VIP score > 1. The significance of the differential metabolites in KEGG pathway enrichment was assessed using a hypergeometric distribution test.
2.5
PRM
To assess the reliability of the differentially abundant proteins (DAPs) identified in DIA proteomics data, 17 significantly differentially expressed proteins were validated using the PRM method. Mobile phases A (100 % water with 0.1 % formic acid) and B (80 % acetonitrile with 0.1 % formic acid) were prepared in advance. The lyophilized samples were reconstituted in 10 μL of mobile phase A, centrifuged at 14,000×
g
for 20 min at 4 °C, and 1 μg of protein from the supernatant was injected for liquid chromatography analysis.
Mass spectrometry was performed using a Q Exactive HF-X with a Nanospray Flex™ (NSI) ion source. The ion spray voltage was set at 2.2 kV, the ion transfer tube temperature at 320 °C, and the data were acquired in data-dependent acquisition mode. The full scan range was 350–1500
m
/
z
, with a resolution of 120,000 at 200 m/z. The automatic gain control (AGC) was set to 3 × 10
6
, and the maximum injection time for the C-trap was 80 ms. Parent ions with the highest intensity in the full scan (Top 40) were selected for high-energy collision-induced dissociation (HCD) and further analyzed by secondary mass spectrometry. The resolution for secondary mass spectrometry was 15,000 at 200 m/z, with an AGC setting of 5 × 10
4
and a maximum injection time of 45 ms. A peptide fragmentation energy of 27 % was used to generate the raw mass spectra.
2.6
Bioinformatics analysis
The Protein-Protein Interaction (PPI) network was constructed using the STRING online platform (v12.0,
https://cn.string-db.org/
), and hub proteins were identified with the Cytoscape software (v3.9.1,
https://cytoscape.org/
) using the cytoHubba plugin. Trend analysis of the data was performed using the OmicShare tools, a free online data analysis platform (v1.3.13,
https://www.omicshare.com/tools
). After obtaining the files of differentially expressed proteins and metabolites, expression trends were analyzed with the Dynamic Trend Analysis tool. The analysis used ShortTime-series Expression Miner software, setting profiles ≤ 20 and FC ≥ 1.
To examine the IMF deposition of key proteins, the Weighted Gene Coexpression Network Analysis (WGCNA) package in R was employed for data analysis. Visualization of the results was conducted using R and Python. The initial dataset included 3107 proteins from 9 samples. Proteins with minimal expression fluctuation (standard deviation ≤ 0.5) were excluded, reducing the final dataset to 1155 proteins across 9 samples. To optimize the scale-free network distribution, a power value of 20 was selected, and the correlation coefficient and average connectivity of the network were calculated.
A weighted co-expression network model was built based on the chosen power value, dividing the 1155 proteins into 10 modules. A correlation coefficient threshold of 0.8 and a soft threshold of 20 were applied to fine-tune the topological model, yielding the best-fit values. The WGCNA v1.1.25 and KEGG v1.2.26 data were mapped using the OECloud tool (
https://cloud.oebiotech.com
, accessed on 31 July 2025), and the protein-protein interaction network was visualized using Cytoscape software. Use KOBAS 3.0 (v3.0,
bioinfo.org/kobas
, accessed on 22 June 2024) for functional annotation and enrichment analysis.
2.7
Isolation of intramuscular adipocytes and immunofluorescence staining of delta-like homologue 1 (DLK1)
Intramuscular adipocytes were identified using the marker gene
DLK1
(
Li et al., 2014
), Cells were seeded in 6-well plates (703,011, Nest, Wuxi, China) until reaching 70 % confluence. After removing the medium, cells were gently washed three times with pre-cooled PBS (pH 7.4, 5 min/wash), fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde (PFA, P0099, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) at room temperature (RT) for 30 min, and rinsed thoroughly with PBS. Permeabilization was performed using 0.5 % Triton X-100 (1 mL/well, RT, 60 min), followed by three PBS washes. Nonspecific binding was blocked with 5 % BSA (A8020, Solarbio, Shanghai, China). Cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with rabbit anti-DLK-1 primary antibody (abs125227, Absin, Shanghai, China), washed, then treated with FITC-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (P0183, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) (1500 dilution, 37 °C, 80 min, dark). After PBS washes, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (C1006, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Fluorescent signals were visualized under a microscope after final PBS washes, retaining PBS to prevent drying. When intramuscular adipocytes reached 100 % confluence, the differentiation induction medium was replaced with DMEM (Gibco, New York, USA) supplemented with 10 % FBS, 10 μg/mL insulin (I8040, Solarbio, Shanghai, China), 1 μM dexamethasone (ID0180, Solarbio, Shanghai, China), 0.5 mM IBMX (I8450, Solarbio, Shanghai, China), and 1 μM rosiglitazone (R8470, Solarbio, Shanghai, China). On day 2 post-induction, the medium was switched to differentiation maintenance medium, with medium changes every 48 h. Cell samples were collected at 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10 days post-induction.
2.8
siRNA synthesis, adenovirus packaging, and cell transfection
The CDS of bovine
HSD17B12
(Gene ID: 789567; RefSeq:
NM_001101307.1
) with a length of 939 bp was retrieved from the NCBI database. Three siRNA sequences targeting
HSD17B12
and one negative control siRNA (sequences listed in Table S1) were synthesized by Shanghai Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (China). Transfection experiments were performed using Lipofectamine™ 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, California, USA) following the manufacturer's protocol (each treatment had three parallel replicates). Recombinant adenoviral vectors expressing
HSD17B12
(HBAD-Adeasy-c-3xflag-EGFP) and the control vector (HBAD-EGFP) were constructed and packaged by Shanghai Hanbio Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (China). A preliminary gradient infection experiment was conducted at multiplicities of infection (MOIs) of 3, 10, 30, 100, 300, and 500. When cells reached 70–90 % confluence, a half-volume infection protocol was applied: 1 mL of fresh medium containing the optimal viral titer was added to each well, followed by supplementation with an additional 1 mL of medium after 4 h to achieve a final volume of 2 mL. The medium was replaced 10–16 h post-infection, and cells were maintained at 37 °C with 5 % CO₂. Viral infection efficiency and cell viability were monitored at 12-h intervals, with photomicrographs recorded for analysis.
2.9
EdU and CCK8 assay
CCK8 kits (Meulunbio, Shanghai, China) and EdU kits (Beeyotime, Shanghai, China) were used to detected to cell proliferation(
He et al., 2024
). In CCK8 assay, preadipocytes were placed to 96-well petri dishes. After 48 h of transfection, 10 μL CCK8 solution was added to each well, and the absorbance at 450 nm was measured with a microplate reader (SYNERGY|LX, BioRad, Hercules, CA, USA) after cells were incubated in the dark for 1 h (each treatment had six parallel replicates). For EdU staining, after transfection of plasmids and interfering fragments 48 h, cells were labeled with EdU solution and continued to culture for 6 h. Subsequently, cells were fixed with 4 % formaldehyde and infiltrated with 0.3 % Triton X-100 for 15 min. Then the click reaction mixture was added to each well and cells were incubated in the dark for 30 min. DAPI was added to dye the nucleus for 10 min, and finally cells were observed and imaged by a fluorescence microscope (each treatment had three parallel replicates).
2.10
Nile red and oil red O staining
Intramuscular adipocytes transfected/virus-infected for 48 h and induced for 10 days of differentiation (seeded in 6-well plates) were subjected to lipid staining. The medium was aspirated, and cells were washed three times with PBS. Cells were fixed with 2 mL of pre-cooled 4 % PFA per well for 20 min at room temperature (RT). After PBS rinsing, Nile Red (ajci15320, Amgicam, Wuhan, China) working solution (1:1000 dilution in PBS, 1 mL/well) was added and incubated in the dark for 10 min. Subsequently, 500 μL of DAPI solution (1:1000 dilution in PBS) was applied per well for 5 min in the dark. Following three final PBS washes, cells were preserved in PBS to prevent drying and imaged under a fluorescence microscope.
For Oil Red O staining (G1262, Solarbio, Shanghai, China), freshly prepared Oil Red O working solution (1 mL/well) was added to cover the cell monolayer and incubated at RT in the dark for 20 min. Excess dye was removed by three gentle PBS washes to minimize background staining. Cells were maintained in 300 μL PBS and immediately imaged using an inverted phase-contrast fluorescence microscope (each treatment had three parallel replicates). To quantify lipid content, 200 μL isopropanol was added per well, and plates were agitated at 800×
g
for 15 min to solubilize dye-bound lipids. The solution was transferred to 1.5 mL tubes, centrifuged at 12,000×
g
for 10 min at 4 °C, and 200 μL of supernatant was transferred to a 96-well plate. Absorbance was measured at 510 nm.

RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis and RT-qPCR
Total RNA was extracted from tissues and cells by TRIzol method. The operation was carried out according to the instructions of TRIzol kits (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). The concentration (ng/μL) and the value of OD
260/280
of RNA were detected by a multifunctional full-wavelength enzyme-labeled instrument (SYNERGY|LX, BioRad, Hercules, CA, USA), and the quality of RNA was detected by 1 % agarose gel electrophoresis. 100 ng RNA was reversely transcribed into cDNA according to the instructions of reverse transcription kits (TaKaRa, Kyoto, Japan). cDNA was used as a template (three parallel replicates were set for each biological sample),
GAPDH
as a standardized reference gene. RT-qPCR was performed according to the instructions of ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) (each treatment had three parallel replicates) (
He et al., 2024

2.12
RNA-sequencing
The RNA libraries were sequenced on the illumina Novaseq™ 6000 platform by LC Bio Technology CO.,Ltd. (Hangzhou, China). Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol® (15596018, Thermo Fisher Scientific, California, USA) with quality control performed via Qubit® 3.0 (
Q33216
, Thermo Fisher Scientific, California, USA) and Agilent 5300 Fragment Analyzer (Agilent, M5311AA), retaining only high-integrity samples (RIN > 7.0). Polyadenylated mRNA was enriched from 2 μg total RNA through two rounds of selection with mRNA Capture Beads 2.0 (Yeasen, 12629ES), followed by magnesium-based fragmentation at 94 °C (Yeasen, 12340ES97). Strand-specific cDNA libraries were constructed using: 1) First-strand synthesis with Reverse Transcriptase, 2) Second-strand synthesis incorporating dUTP via
E. coli
DNA Pol I/RNase H (Yeasen, 12340ES97), 3) End-adenylation and dual-index adapter ligation, and 4) 14-cycle PCR amplification (98 °C/10s → 60 °C/30s → 72 °C/30s) with high-fidelity polymerase to exclude dUTP-containing strands. Libraries were size-selected (400 ± 50 bp inserts; Hieff NGS Beads, Yeasen 12601ES75) and sequenced PE150 on Illumina NovaSeq™ X Plus (LC-Bio Tech). Raw reads were processed with Cutadapt v1.9 by removing adapters/poly-A|G sequences, reads containing >5 % N-bases, or >20 % Q ≤ 20 bases. Data quality was verified by FastQC v0.11.9. Filtered reads were mapped to the bovine reference genome (
Bos taurus
,
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/genome/GCF_002263795.3/
) using HISAT2. Gene function annotation was performed through integrated databases including Nr, Pfam, KOG, and Swiss-Prot. Gene expression abundance was quantified using FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads). Differential expression analysis was conducted with DESeq2 software. For the HSD17B12 overexpression comparison (OE_NC vs. OE_HSD17B12), DEGs required absolute fold change |FC| > 1 with FDR-adjusted
p
-value < 0.05. For the HSD17B12 knockdown comparison (SI_NC vs. SI_HSD17B12), a modified threshold of |FC| > 0.58 (equivalent to |log₂FC| > 0.53 or >1.5-fold change) with identical FDR < 0.05 was applied to capture biologically meaningful alterations in the partial-loss-of-function context.
2.13
Statistical analysis
The IMF content and PRM data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The
p
-values of differentially abundant proteins (DAPs), differentially abundant metabolites (DAMs), GO terms, and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method to control for false discovery rate (FDR). Statistical significance was assessed using an unpaired, two-tailed Student's
t
-test, and fold changes were calculated based on the mean values of each group.

Results
3.1
Identification and functional enrichment of differentially abundant proteins (DAPs)
The IMF content in the
Longissimus dorsi
muscle varies significantly among different cattle breeds. Specifically, Wagyu cattle (WA) exhibit a notably higher IMF content compared to both Guyuan cattle (GY) and Simmental cattle (SI), with the difference being highly significant (
P
< 0.01 for GY and
P
< 0.001 for SI). Additionally, the IMF content in SI is significantly lower than that in GY (
P
< 0.01) (Fig. S1). These results suggest that Wagyu cattle have the greatest capacity for fat deposition, followed by Guyuan cattle, while Simmental cattle have the lowest fat deposition ability.
Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was conducted to visually distinguish beef cattle based on different IMF deposition, with clear separation observed among the three groups (
Fig. 1
A). Further analysis through CorHeatmap and ExpHeatmap revealed distinct groupings, providing additional insight into the data (
Fig. 1
B & C). To assess differential abundance of proteins (DAPs), volcano plots were employed for statistical visualization and analysis (
Fig. 1
D, E, & F). In total, 3107 proteins were quantified, and 124, 550, and 298 DAPs were identified in the GY_vs_SI, GY_vs_WA, and SI_vs_WA groups, respectively. Additionally, 113 DAPs were common across all three groups. The number of upregulated and downregulated DAPs was summarized using a Venn diagram (
Fig. 1
G), with 33 shared DAPs identified across all three groups. A protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed for these 33 proteins, and the results revealed that ACTB, POSTN, and ANXA2 are core proteins (Fig. S2).

Discussion
Beef with a high IMF content enhances its texture and juiciness, ultimately improving consumer acceptance. Sensory evaluations have shown that increasing the crude fat content positively affects tenderness, juiciness, and overall fattiness (
Iida, Saitou, Kawamura, Yamaguchi, & Nishimura, 2015
). Meat with higher IMF content is typically more palatable, as the fat enhances its overall taste. Japanese Wagyu beef is renowned for its exceptional IMF levels, a characteristic resulting from selective breeding aimed at improving meat quality. In contrast, Chinese native breeds generally have lower IMF content. Comparative studies on IMF levels between Japanese Wagyu and Chinese native breeds are essential for understanding the genetic factors that influence meat quality. Recent advancements in genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics have provided valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms that govern IMF deposition. In recent years, the development of proteomics has enhanced the reliability of quantitative analysis. The comprehensive screening of proteins using DIA proteomics and targeted validation with Parallel reaction monitoring (PRM) provides valuable insights for studying intramuscular fat deposition and identifying potential intervention targets. To date, no relevant studies have been reported in the field of beef quality research. Our study utilized a DIA-based quantitative proteomic approach combined with untargeted metabolomics to explore these molecular pathways in greater depth. While much previous research has focused on transcriptional regulation, it is important to recognize that the translation of mRNA into protein is a complex biological process. This complexity can lead to significant differences between gene expression and protein levels, influenced by various mechanisms such as N6-methyladenosine methylation (
Dang et al., 2022
), alternative RNA splicing, phosphorylation (
Underwood et al., 2007
), acetylation, glycosylation (
Chang et al., 2022
), and ubiquitination.
In our research, we identified several proteins linked to IMF deposition and meat quality that have not been previously reported. These include POSTN, FMOD, VIM, PRKAG3, ANXA1, and SERPINH1, among others. Through PPI analysis of 33 commonly differentially expressed proteins and 371 proteins identified through joint screening, we found that ACTB and POSTN were core proteins involved in the process. Furthermore, the
ACTB
gene, widely used as an internal reference gene, was found to be stably expressed during the adipogenic differentiation of porcine intramuscular stromal-vascular cells. This stability was confirmed using the NormFinder and geNorm algorithms, reinforcing its suitability for gene expression studies in this context (
Li et al., 2016
). In vitro studies on bovine primary preadipocytes have shown that
ACTB
is not consistently stable during cell proliferation and differentiation. Instead,
GAPDH
,
RPS15A
,
RPLP0
, and
EIF3K
are more suitable as reference genes for these processes (
Wang et al., 2022
). Using a high-fat diet (HFD)-induced obese mouse model, we observed that
ACTB
expression fluctuates in adipose and liver tissues. Specifically, mRNA levels were elevated in the epididymal, perirenal, subcutaneous inguinal, and subscapular brown adipose tissues following HFD feeding (
Fan et al., 2020
). The
ACTB
gene has been shown to be unstable as a reference gene during adipogenic differentiation and thermogenesis in brown adipose tissue (
Lai et al., 2024
). RNA-seq analysis of
Longissimus dorsi
muscle in Guizhou Xiang and Large White pigs identified
ACTB
as a key gene associated with meat quality (
Wang et al., 2024
). It is plausible that the
ACTB
gene plays a critical role in IMF deposition; however, the expression patterns and molecular mechanisms involved in this process in bovine IMF are not yet fully understood.
POSTN, a matricellular protein that enhances extracellular matrix (ECM) stability, is crucial for cell adhesion and migration. It has been shown to upregulate the expression of α-SMA, collagen, and fibronectin through the PI3K/AKT, Wnt/β-catenin, and JNK signaling pathways (
Landry, Cohen, & Dixon, 2018
). A single-cell RNA sequencing study of bovine skeletal muscle identified distinct subpopulations of fibro/adipogenic progenitors. These included an endomysial adipogenic subset, enriched in
COL4A1
and
CFD
, and a perimysial fibrogenic subset, enriched in
COL1A1
and
POSTN
. Notably, differences in intramuscular fat (IMF) content were observed between the adipogenic and fibrogenic subpopulations in Brahman and Wagyu breeds (
Wang, Gao, et al., 2023
). In mice treated with lipopolysaccharide and HFD,
POSTN
expression in adipose tissue was significantly elevated. The absence of
POSTN
was found to reduce adipose tissue fibrosis and insulin resistance (
Yang et al., 2021
). Another study showed that silencing the
POSTN
gene enhanced adipocytogenesis and differentiation (
Qi et al., 2021
).
In our protein and metabolomic studies, we utilized various methodologies, including KEGG, WGCNA, and trend analysis. However, an integrated approach is needed to combine these methods. To gain a more comprehensive understanding of the regulatory mechanisms of IMF, we incorporated the analytical approaches used by the Tiannan Guo team into our research methodology (
Shen et al., 2020
). Eleven pathways related to fatty acid metabolism were identified as key factors in IMF deposition and meat quality. The regulation of unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis by ACAA1 and HSD17B12 influences the production of hexadecanoic acid, icosadienoic acid, (9Z)-octadecenoic acid, eicosadienoic acid, (13Z,16Z)-docosadienoic acid, and oleic acid. In chicken intramuscular preadipocytes,
miR-15a
has been shown to reduce fatty acid oxidation by targeting
ACAA1
, which indirectly promotes the differentiation of intramuscular adipocytes (
Li et al., 2019
). In sheep adipocytes,
HSD17B12
was identified as a target gene of
miR-136
. Suppression of
HSD17B12
was found to inhibit the proliferation of stromal vascular fractions (SVFs) and promote adipogenic differentiation (
Liu et al., 2023
). Unsaturated fatty acids (UFAs) play a key role in adipocyte formation, primarily by activating PPARγ. 5
Conclusions
In conclusion, this study offers a comprehensive analysis of the key factors influencing IMF deposition through DIA-PRM proteomics and metabolomics. It identifies critical proteins, such as ACAA1, ACACA, HSD17B12, ADIPOQ, ACAT1, FABP3, and FABP4, along with key metabolites including oleic acid, hexadecanoic acid, licosadienoic acid, and (9Z)-octadecenoic acid. These findings underscore their potential as key regulators of IMF deposition. This pioneering research serves as a valuable reference for improving beef quality and developing premium beef through targeted fat deposition strategies.
