Introduction
Foxtail millet (
Setaria italica
), one of the world’s oldest domesticated cereal crops, thrives across temperate, tropical, and arid regions. This resilient crop is valued for its high nutritional quality, offering a rich profile of essential nutrients [
1
,
2
]. Foxtail millet exhibits a short growth cycle, exceptional drought tolerance, and adaptability to nutrient-poor soils, making it a typical environmentally friendly crop [
3
,
4
]. The foliar structure of millet consists of three primary components: leaf sheath, blade, and ligule, with auricles being absent. The leaf sheath epidermis exhibits a keratinized outer cell layer, which serves critical physiological functions including mechanical protection of internal tissues, nutrient translocation, and prevention of water loss through transpiration.
The leaf sheaths of millet exhibit relatively rich coloration, and variations in this trait may influence the photosynthesis of millet. Currently, there is a dearth of research reports on the synthesis mechanism of pigments in millet leaf sheaths. It is hypothesized that the distinct colors observed in plant are likely attributed to differences in the accumulation of plant pigments. Plant coloration is principally determined by three key pigment classes: chlorophylls, anthocyanins, and carotenoids, each varying in type and concentration. Chlorophylls are the main components of normal green leaves. However, the loss of green color often results from disruptions in the chlorophyll biosynthesis pathway, which exposes other colorful pigments [
5
]. Anthocyanins constitute the predominant pigments responsible for plant coloration [
6
]. In reproductive structures such as flowers and fruits, anthocyanin-derived pigmentation functions as a visual attractant, facilitating ecological interactions with pollinators and seed dispersers through their diverse color vision systems [
7
]. Concurrently, carotenoids contribute to the chromatic spectrum, producing yellow, orange, or red hues whose specific manifestation depends on their spatial distribution and concentration within plant tissues [
8
].
At present, the biosynthetic pathway of anthocyanins had been well-established in plants such as potato leaves [
9
], sorghum petioles [
10
], and red-leaf poplar [
11
]. Furthermore, the metabolic processes governing chlorophyll interconversion, turnover, and degradation have been well confirmed [
12
]. The biosynthetic pathway of carotenoids has been comprehensively elucidated in microalgae [
13
] and marigold (
Tagetes erecta
L.) [
14
]. However, the key pigments regulating millet leaf sheath coloration and their underlying biosynthetic pathways remain largely uncharacterized.
In this study, local varieties of green and purple leaf sheaths were used as experimental materials. By measurement and analysis phenotypic and physiological indicators, the differences of the main substances (chlorophyll, anthocyanin, and carotenoids) in different colors leaf sheaths were clarified. Through the combined analysis of transcriptomics, metabolomics, and proteomics, the differentially expressed genes (DEGs), differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs), and differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) involved in the biosynthesis and degradation of chlorophyll, anthocyanins, and carotenoids in leaf sheaths of millet were explored. The pigment content of millet of different colors was evaluated. The results of this study provide new ideas for understanding the molecular mechanisms of different coloration of millet leaf sheaths and offer references for exploring the mechanisms of pigmentation in other plants.
Result
Analysis of phenotypic differences of leaf sheaths of different color
The phenotypic observation of leaf sheaths part of millet was conducted during the seven leaves and one heart period (Fig.
1
A). The color difference values of leaf sheaths of millet were measured by colorimeter, and a Lab 3D surface diagram was drawn to visually express the color of leaf sheaths (Fig.
1
B). According to CIELAB color space analysis (Supplementary Table 1), green leaf sheaths exhibited “L” value of 53.76 ± 0.50, “a” value of −6.99 ± 0.06, and “b” value of 34.18 ± 0.67. The “L” value of purple leaf sheaths was 35.09 ± 3.44, the “a” value was 4.45 ± 0.57, and the “b” value was 9.35 ± 3.15. The
ΔE
value between green and purple leaf sheaths was 33.17 ± 4.38. The above results indicate that a relatively obvious color phenotypic difference can be observed with the naked eye between green and purple leaf sheaths.
Difference content of anthocyanin, chlorophyll and carotenoids in leaf sheaths
The contents of total chlorophyll, total anthocyanin, and carotenoid in leaf sheaths of millet with different colors in this experiment were determined and compared (Fig.
1
C). The results show that the total anthocyanin content of green leaf sheaths was 0.19 mg·g⁻¹, and that of purple leaf sheaths was 0.52 mg·g⁻¹. The anthocyanin content of green leaf sheaths was significantly lower than purple leaf sheaths. The total chlorophyll content of green leaf sheaths was 0.36 mg·L⁻¹, and that of purple leaf sheaths was 0.20 mg·L⁻¹. The total chlorophyll content of green leaf sheaths was higher than that purple leaf sheaths. The content of carotenoids in green leaf sheaths was 0.097 mg·g⁻¹, and that in purple leaf sheaths was 0.08 mg·g⁻¹. There was no significant difference in the content of carotenoids between the two samples, and it was significantly lower than the contents of total anthocyanins and chlorophyll. It is speculated that the presentation of color in leaf sheaths of millet is closely related to the contents of anthocyanins and chlorophyll, but has no significant association with the change of carotenoid contents.

Primary transcriptomic, metabolomic, and proteomic data analyses
cDNA libraries of 6 samples were constructed in the transcriptome, and a total of 39.21 Gb of Clean Data was obtained (Supplementary Table 2). The results of Principal component analysis (PCA) showed that PC1 and PC2 accounted for 83.15% and 7.43% of the variations respectively, and the sample clustering was clear (Supplementary Fig. 1 A). The results of the sample correlation heat map analysis showed that the PCC was between 0.851 and 1 (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Through the correlation heat map analysis of metabolomic samples, it was found that the sample repeatability was in line with expectations and could meet the subsequent metabolome data analysis (Supplementary Fig. 2B). The PCA spectra showed that PC1 and PC2 explained 74.00% and 10.10% of the variations, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 2 A). It indicates that the metabolites in the samples have undergone significant changes under different treatment conditions. Proteomic analysis was conducted on leaf sheath samples, and 64,834 proteins and 72,565 polypeptides were identified. The molecular weight distribution of most proteins was between 21 and 41 kDa (Supplementary Fig. 3 A). The coverage of the complete protein sequence is shown in the following figure (Supplementary Fig. 3B). The correlation coefficients among each sample were calculated. It was found that the repeatability among the repeated samples of each variety was good, and the correlation coefficients were distributed between 0.993 and 1. The repeatability between the repeated samples of the two varieties was poor, and the correlation coefficient was distributed between 0.478 and 0.482 (Supplementary Fig. 3 C). The PCA results of protein expression levels showed that samples of the same variety were spatially clustered significantly (Supplementary Fig. 3D).
Primary identification of differentially expressed genes, metabolites, and proteins
A total of 16,368 and 17,028 genes were identified in green and purple leaf sheaths, respectively. The results of gene overlap analysis showed that there were a total of 15,707 genes in green and purple leaf sheaths, among which 661 and 1,321 genes were unique to green and purple leaf sheaths, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1 C). A total of 4,059 DEGs were identified, among which 1,818 and 2,241 were down-regulated and up-regulated DEGs, respectively. The number of up-regulated genes was 23.27% more than that of down-regulated genes (Supplementary Fig. 1D). Metabolite overlap analysis was conducted on the detected metabolites. A total of 1,401 metabolites were detected in both green and purple leaf sheaths, including 32 metabolites unique to green samples and 171 unique to purple samples (Supplementary Fig. 2 C). The detected metabolites were screened for DAMs, and a total of 481 DAMs were obtained, among which 244 and 237 were up-regulated and down-regulated DAMs, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 2D). These DAMs were enriched in 56 KEGG pathways. The method of comparative proteomics was used to research the changes of protein profiles in green and purple millet leaf sheaths during the seedling stage. A total of 990 DEPs were identified in the comparison group of green and purple leaf sheaths, among which 532 were up-regulated and 458 were down-regulated (Supplementary Fig. 3E). To verify the data reliability of RNA-Seq, 15 key DEGs in different metabolic pathways were selected for quantitative real–time PCR (qRT-PCR) verification (Supplementary Fig. 5). The results showed that the expression trend of the key DEGs was consistent with the transcriptome detection results, and the RNA-Seq data results were reliable.
Significant enrichment analysis of GO and KEGG in multi-omics
To further elucidate the functional roles of DEGs/DAMs, we conducted Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses. All DEGs/DAMs were classified into three main categories of GO classification, including biological process (BP), cell components (CC), and molecular functions (MF). GO functional enrichment analysis was performed on the top 20 GO terms with the highest enrichment in DEGs/DEPs in the transcriptome and proteome. In transcriptome, GO:0036094 has the largest number of DEGs enriched, reaching 734 (Supplementary Fig. 1E). In proteome, membrane (GO:0016020) enriched the largest number of DEPs, with a total of 343 DEPs enriched (Supplementary Fig. 3 F). The defense response (GO:0006952) was significantly enriched in both the transcriptome and the proteome.
In order to conduct an in-depth study of the metabolic pathways, the enriched DEGs, DAMs, and DEPs were compared with the KEGG database. The results of transcriptome KEGG metabolic pathway enrichment analysis showed that 127 pathways were enriched in the comparison groups of green-purple leaf sheaths. Analysis of the top 20 significantly enriched KEGG pathways revealed that Carbon fixation in photosynthetic organisms (ko00710) had the highest significant enrichment. Plant-pathogen interaction (ko04626) was enriched to 50 DEGs, with the largest number of enriched DEGs (Supplementary Fig. 1 F). KEGG enrichment analysis was performed on DAMs, and a total of 56 pathways were enriched. Statistical analysis was conducted on the top 20 pathways with the highest enrichment significance of DAMs. The results indicated that Glycerophospholipid metabolism (ko00564) had the highest enrichment significance. The most abundant DAMs were enriched by Flavone and flavonol biosynthesis (ko00944), reaching 11 DAMs (Supplementary Fig. 2E). KEGG enrichment analysis was performed on DEPs, and a total of 111 pathways were enriched. Statistical analysis was conducted on the top 20 pathways with the greatest significance in enriching DEPs. The results indicated that Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (ko00940) has the highest enrichment significance. The number of DEPs enriched by the Metabolic pathways (ko01100) was the largest, reaching 189 DEPs (Supplementary Fig. 3G). All three omics were significantly enriched in Flavonoid biosynthesis (ko00941) and Plant hormone signal transduction (ko04075).

DEGs/DAMs/DEPs related to carotenoid biosynthesis in millet leaf sheaths
Carotenoid biosynthesis begins with the “Terpenoid backbone biosynthesis, ko00900” pathway, and take the “Carotenoid biosynthesis, ko00906” pathway as the core. In the transcriptome, according to the KEGG pathway analysis, a total of 14 DEGs were identified as differentially expressed in the comparison group of green and purple leaf sheaths, among which 7 DEGs with both up-regulated and down-regulated expression. However, the proteome and metabolome did not detect the presence of DEPs and DAMs in the ko00900 and ko00906 pathways (Fig.
4
). The possible reasons for this result are as follows: (1) mRNA stability: Some mRNAs may be rapidly degraded, and the changes at the gene transcription level have not been fully transmitted to the protein level, resulting in the accumulation of transcripts but no corresponding increase in proteins. (2) Alternative splicing: The same gene generates multiple mRNA isomers through different splicing methods, and only some of these isomers may be translated into functional proteins. (3) Protein modification and degradation: Newly synthesized proteins may be rapidly phosphorylated, ubiquitinated or degraded by the proteasome, resulting in no significant change in protein abundance. (4) Protein localization and assembly: Proteins need to be correctly folded, localized or assembled with other subunits to function. An increase in transcriptional levels does not mean an increase in the amount of functional proteins. (5) Spatiotemporal regulation of metabolic pathways: The transcriptome may detect changes in gene expression for specific cell types, but the proteome and metabolome reflect the entire tissue sample, and the signals are diluted or masked. Transcriptional responses may be rapid and short-lived (such as in the early stage of stress), while changes in proteins and metabolites require a longer accumulation time or only occur at specific stages. Discussion
Millet is an ideal staple food crop widely planted in semi-arid areas or infertile land [
15
], and also an important crop for ensuring food and nutritional security [
16
,
17
]. The green and purple leaf sheath colors of millet are relatively common, but the specific synthetic mechanism is rarely reported. Previous studies have shown that the color of plants is mainly influenced by the concentrations of chlorophyll, carotenoids, and anthocyanins [
18
–
20
]. However, the factors regulating the synthesis and degradation of these pigments are complex. In this study, we determined the total anthocyanin content, total chlorophyll content and carotenoid content in leaf sheaths of millet. The results showed that the anthocyanin content in purple leaf sheaths was significantly higher than that in green leaf sheaths, the chlorophyll content was lower than that in green leaf sheaths, and the carotenoid content in leaf sheaths of millet was extremely low and showed no significant difference. It was speculated that the degradation of chlorophyll and the accumulation of anthocyanin content are the main reasons for the purple color of millet leaf sheaths, while the correlation between carotenoids and the color manifestation of millet seedling leaf sheaths is extremely low. Liu et al. [
21
] found that the anthocyanin content and phenolic compounds in red-fleshed kiwifruit (
Actinidia chinensis
Planch.) were higher than those in green-fleshed kiwifruit. Furthermore, the same result was found in the studies of green and purple
Perilla frutescens
L [
22
]. and
Chenopodium quinoa
Willd. Grains [
23
].
The metabolomic results indicated that the expression levels of nine metabolites in purple leaf sheaths were significantly higher than those in the green leaf sheaths, and their expression trends were consistent with the changing trends of the total anthocyanin content. It was speculated that the differential expression of these metabolites was closely related to the total anthocyanin content and jointly participated in regulating the differential expression of the color of the purple leaf sheaths of millet. Unlike others, the contents of Naringenin Chalcone, Naringenin, and Caffeoyl quinic acid in purple leaf sheaths were significantly lower than those in green leaf sheaths. Therefore, we infer that Naringenin Chalcone, Naringenin, and Caffeoyl quinic acid, as intermediate products of anthocyanin synthesis, have substrate preferences. Furthermore, Naringenin Chalcone, Naringenin, and Caffeoyl quinic acid as key hubs of flavonoids, whether the down-regulation of their content expression affects the antioxidant capacity, photosynthesis, and plant growth and development in plants. Its specific mechanism of action awaits in-depth exploration.
The synthesis of metabolites is influenced by structural genes and regulatory genes. In this study, we found that all three DEGs/DEPs (magnesium chelatase subunit H (chlH), magnesium chelatase subunit I (chlI), magnesium-protoporphyrin O-methyltransferase (chlM)) were down-regulated. These DEGs are involved in the synthesis and degradation of chlorophyll in the leaf sheaths of mille. It is speculated that the low expression of these DEGs/DEPs inhibited the biosynthesis of chlorophyll in purple leaf sheaths. Meanwhile, the increase in anthocyanin content made leaf sheaths exhibit the purple trait. In the anthocyanin biosynthesis pathway, 10 DEGs/DEPs (PAL, 4CL, CHS, CHI, F3H, CYP73A (trans-cinnamate 4-monooxygenase), CYP75B1 (flavonoid 3’-monooxygenase), DFR, ANS, UFGT) jointly participate in regulating the differential expression of color in leaf sheath of oryzae. Since anthocyanins are the main substances that determine the color of the leaf sheaths of millet, we conducted a more detailed screening of DEGs/DEPs in the anthocyanin biosynthesis pathway (log2(FC) ≥ 5), and identified
CHS
,
CYP75B1
,
DFR
,
F3H
,
ANS
,
ANR
, and
UFGT
in the transcriptome. In the proteome, we detected key DEPs of 4CL. Based on these findings, we hypothesize these DEGs/DEPs would facilitate pigment accumulation, ultimately contributing to the purple pigmentation observed in leaf sheaths.
The biosynthesis of anthocyanins and chlorophyll may not be independent processes. Some TFs may simultaneously bind to the promoters of anthocyanin synthesis genes and chlorophyll synthesis genes, achieving coordinated regulation by “activating the former and inhibiting the latter” [
24
]. In Arabidopsis,
PAP1
(
MYB75
) is a key activator for anthocyanin synthesis: when
PAP1
up-regulated expression, it forms an
MBW
complex with
bHLH
and
WD40
to activate anthocyanin structural genes such as
CHS
and
DFR
. In addition,
PAP1
can directly bind to the promoters of chlorophyll synthesis genes (such as
HEMA1
and
CHLH
), inhibiting their transcription [
25
,
26
]. In addition, there may be a competitive relationship between anthocyanins and chlorophyll precursors. ALA (δ-aminolevulinic acid) (the starting point of chlorophyll synthesis): ALA can flow either to chlorophyll (requiring magnesium ions) or to heme (requiring iron ions). When chlorophyll synthesis genes (
HEMA1
and
CHLH
) are down-regulated, the flow of ALA to chlorophyll is disrupted. The accumulated excess ALA may be shunted to the auxiliary pathways of anthocyanin synthesis. For instance, by facilitating the hydroxylation modification of flavonoids-thereby further promoting anthocyanin accumulation [
27
,
28
]. In this study, by predicting the target genes of the identified transcription factors, four (three WRKY families and one MYB family) DETFs related to chlorophyll synthesis and degradation, and six (five MYB families and one bHLH family) DETFs related to anthocyanin synthesis and degradation were finally screened out. Two DETFs (MYB family) related to the synthesis and degradation of carotenoids. These DETFs were all up-regulated in purple leaf sheaths. The MYB family of transcription factors are widely involved in the biosynthesis and degradation pathways of anthocyanins, chlorophyll and carotenoids. However, we are still exploring the mechanism by which it combines the promoter regions of regulatory genes to control the changes in the content of various substances.
In this study, the results indicated that Plant hormone signal transduction pathway (ko04075) was significantly enriched in all three omics except Flavonoid biosynthesis pathway. In metabolome, we identified the down-regulated differential expression of jasmonic acid. In the study of Zhao et al. [
29
]., jasmonic acid can regulate the mechanisms of anthocyanin pigmentation in fruit. Exogenous application of JA causes an increase in anthocyanin accumulation in a number of fruits, but the degree of increase varies with their concentration and stereoisomeric form [
30
]. The jasmonic acid signaling pathway promoted biosynthesis of secondary metabolites and regulates the anthocyanin synthesis mechanism in
Salvia miltiorrhiza
[
31
]. In addition, studies indicated that plant hormones affect pigmentation also include abscisic acid (ABA), brassinosteroid (BR), and Strigolactone (SL), etc [
29
]. In millet, the specific mechanism by which jasmonic acid regulates its color remains unclear. Moreover, apart from jasmonic acid, it is also not clear whether there are any other hormones jointly involved in regulating the deposition of pigments in leaf sheaths of millet. This will be the focus of our subsequent research.
Conclusion
The mechanism of color change of millet leaf sheaths was explored by combining the physiological indicators and multi-omics analysis. The main reasons for the color differences in leaf sheaths of millet are the accumulation differences of anthocyanins and the deficiency of chlorophyll. The analysis results of DEGs and DEPs with the common expression trend revealed that PAL, 4CL, CHS, CHI, F3H, CYP73A, CYP75B1, DFR, ANS, and UFGT were co-upregulated. These genes/enzymes may be involved in regulating the differential expression of anthocyanin in millet leaf sheaths. The low expression of chlH, chlI, and chlM reduced the content of chlorophyll in leaf sheaths of millet, resulting in chlorophyll deficiency and causing leaf sheaths to appear purple. In addition, the mRNA and protein expression levels detected in the transcriptome and proteome are not exactly the same. It is speculated that these proteins may have undergone post-transcriptional alterations and activations, releasing active proteins immediately without the need for transcription of related genes. The WRKY, bHLH, and MYB TF families are involved in regulating the synthesis and degradation of chlorophyll, anthocyanins and carotenoids in leaf sheaths of millet, and interact with the above-mentioned DEGs to jointly regulate the color phenotype of leaf sheaths. These findings provide a basis for the preliminary exploration of the main substances and molecular mechanism of the color of millet leaf sheaths. Materials and methods
Plant material, growth conditions and treatments
The experimental materials of this study were the local varieties of millet, flat-headed white (TDP007, green) and long-necked goose (TDP002, purple), with different leaf sheath colors. The experimental materials were planted on May 26, 2024, at the Experimental Station of the institute of Millet Crops, Hebei Academy of Agriculture and Forestry Sciences in Gaocheng District, Shijiazhuang City, Hebei Province (114°38’5’’E, 38°2’1’’N), with the row spacing was 40 cm and the plant spacing was 15 cm. When the millet is cultivated to the point of seven leaves and one heart, take leaf sheath samples, with 1–3 g of each sample. After rapid freezing in liquid nitrogen, store them in an ultra-low temperature refrigerator at −80℃ for omics sequencing as well as the determination of physiological indicators.
Determination of color difference values in leaf sheaths
The color difference values of green and purple leaf sheath samples were determined use colorimeter (CR-10plus, Japan). “L” represents black-white values, where “+” and “-” respectively represent whiter and darker values. “a” represents the red-green values, where “+” and “-” respectively represent red and green values. “b” represents the yellow-blue value, where “+” and “-” respectively represent light yellow and light blue.
ΔE
is calculated by use the measured values of L, a and b through the formula. The
ΔE
value is an indicator used to quantify color differences. When
ΔE
= 0, it indicates that the two colors are exactly the same with no difference. As the
ΔE
value increases, the differences between colors gradually become obvious, and the human eye can more easily detect the differences in colors.

Rio’s [
34
] TRIzol precipitation method was used to extract RNA from samples. The cDNA library was prepared and the library validation was completed. After library analysis, RNA-seq libraries were sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq (
http://www.illumina.com
) platform for three biological replicates. High-quality clean data can be obtained by removing reads with connectors, reads with N greater than 10%, reads with a quality value Q ≤ 10 and a base number greater than 50% of the entire read. The high-quality clean reads were compared with the millet reference genome (
https://phytozome.jgi.doe.gov/pz/portal.html#!info?alias=Org_Sitalica

Normalize the filtered data of all platforms and conduct further analysis using the resulting data matrix. Protein extraction and enzymatic hydrolysis
Transfer the sample to the shaking tube and add the protein lysis buffer (8 M urea + 1%SDS, containing protease inhibitor); Oscillate three times using a high-throughput tissue grinder, each time for 40 s; Pyrolysis on ice for 30 min, during which vortex mixing was carried out every 5 min for 5–10 s. Centrifuge at 12,000 g at 4℃ for 30 min and collect the supernatant. Subsequently, the protein content was determined by the BCA method, and the operation was carried out strictly in accordance with the BCA reagent instructions. Take 100 µg of the protein sample, supplement the lysis buffer, and add triethylammonium bicarbonate buffer (TEAB) with a final concentration of 100 mM. Add the final concentration of 10 mM TCEP and react at 37℃ for 60 min. Add the final concentration of 40 mM Iodoacetamide and react at room temperature in the dark for 40 min. Add pre-cooled acetone (V
acetone
: V
sample
= 6:1) to each tube and precipitate at −20℃ for 4 h. Centrifuge at 10,000 g for 20 min and take the precipitate. Dissolve the sample fully with 100 µL of 100 mM TEAB. Add Trypsin at a mass ratio of 1:50 (enzyme: protein) and enzymatically hydrolyze overnight at 37℃.
Peptide desalination and quantification
After digestion with trypsin, the peptide segments were drained dry with a vacuum pump. The peptide segments after enzymatic hydrolysis and extraction were redissolved with 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA). Peptide desalination was carried out using HLB and dried with a vacuum concentrator. Peptide quantification was performed using the Thermo Fisher Scientific Peptide Quantification Kit (Catalog No. 23275).
Database search and bioinformatics analysis
The original DIA data was imported into the Spectronaut™ software system for database search and analysis. Reliable proteins were screened with the criterion of > 2 peptide segments and FDR < 1%. On the basis of reliable proteins, DEPs were screened with the criteria of FC > 2 or < 0.5 and P-value ≤ 0.05. Subsequently, functional enrichment analyses of differential proteins were conducted, including GO and KEGG enrichment analysis.
