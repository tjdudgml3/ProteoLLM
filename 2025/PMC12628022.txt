1
Introduction
Selenium (Se) is an essential trace element that plays a crucial role in various biological processes [
1
,
2
]. The primary biologically active form of selenium in living organisms is selenoproteins, which contain selenocysteine (Sec, U), the 21st proteinogenic amino acid [
3
]. Notably, Sec is inserted at UGA codons that typically serve as stop signals. Selenoproteins perform a wide range of physiological functions, including the regulation of redox homeostasis [
4
], antioxidant defense [
5
,
6
], cell ferroptosis [
[7]
,
[8]
,
[9]
], thyroid hormone metabolism [
10
], and the maintenance of immune system function [
11
]. Selenium deficiency has been linked to various pathological conditions, including Keshan disease [
12
], cardiovascular disease [
13
], cancer [
[14]
,
[15]
,
[16]
], and neurodegenerative diseases [
17
].
It is becoming increasingly evident that the genome possesses vast coding potential beyond conventional annotations. The translation of numerous non-canonical open reading frames, noncoding RNAs, and stop codon readthrough events has given rise to a substantial and largely uncharacterized dark proteome [
[18]
,
[19]
,
[20]
,
[21]
,
[22]
,
[23]
,
[24]
]. As a representative event of stop codon readthrough, UGA-coded Sec insertion leads to the synthesis of extended polypeptides and functional selenoproteins. This process is governed by a distinct regulatory mechanism. Unlike canonical amino acids, Sec is not directly conjugated to its tRNA (tRNA
Sec
), but is synthesized through a multi-step biosynthetic pathway that begins with the formation of Ser-tRNA
Sec
[
25
]. The resulting Sec-tRNA
Sec
recognizes the UGA codon to facilitate Sec insertion during translation. The insertion of Sec in mammals requires the interaction of the Sec insertion sequence (SECIS) element in the 3′ untranslated region (3′ UTR) of mRNAs with SECIS-binding protein 2 (SECISBP2), and the recruitment of the Sec-specific elongation factor eEFSec, which ultimately facilitates the insertion of Sec into the polypeptide chain via Sec-tRNA
Sec
, guided by the UGA codon [
26
,
27
].
Despite the critical roles of selenoproteins, the established repertoire remains limited. To date, only 25 selenoproteins have been definitively identified in humans, and 24 in mice [
28
]. This characterization has primarily relied on stem-looped SECIS prediction, which has led to the creation of the selenoprotein database, selenoDB [
29
,
30
]. Notably, many Sec-containing sequences have Cys-containing homologs in related species, which facilitates their prediction. However, it is important to note that SECIS prediction may fail for RNAs that lack a typical SECIS structure, as most current approaches rely on cross-species genome-wide searches using canonical SECIS elements as references.
To overcome these limitations, a SECIS-independent selenoprotein (SIS) database was developed, in which UGA codons were
in silico
translated to Sec, regardless of whether a typical SECIS structure is present. Leveraging the unique properties of Sec, methods for the specific enrichment of Sec-containing peptides under low-pH conditions has been developed, facilitating the detection and analysis of selenoproteins [
27
,
[31]
,
[32]
,
[33]
]. Using SIS and Sec-specific mass spectrometry (SecMS) methods, several selenoprotein candidates were identified, decades after the first SECIS-based draft of the mamalian selenoproteome [
33
,
34
]. However, due to the large proportion of UGA stop codons in the genome, most of the Sec sites in the SIS database have little to no potential for Sec incorporation.
The SECIS- or UGA-based approaches have both advanced our understanding of this rare and mysterious protein family. In addition to canonical selenoproteins, many predicted or putative selenoprotein candidates, especially those in non-model organisms or newly annotated families, remain to be experimentally validated. In this study, we focus on SECISBP2, the core and direct RNA-binding protein in the selenoprotein biosynthesis machinery. By employing SECISBP2-bound RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing (SECISBP2-RIP-Seq), we profiled all RNAs that specifically bind to SECISBP2 and constructed 3S-DB (SECISBP2-bound RNA sequencing-supported selenoprotein database, SSS-DB or 3S-DB). The RNAs included in the 3S-DB have a potential to recruit the Sec insertion machinery, thereby facilitating the production of selenoproteins. Specifically, the 3S-DB comprises 1,333 RNAs, 18 of which correspond to known selenoproteins and were assessed with high confidence in our database. We further demonstrate that two candidate RNAs for ATP5MJ and PDF, and their segments exhibit SECIS activity and enhance Sec incorporation. Our study not only developed an experimental data-driven RNA repository for selenoproteins, but also identified novel SECIS elements, providing a critical resource for future exploration of SECIS and selenoproteins and refining our understanding of genetic code flexibility in redox biology and beyond. Additionally, this research strategy may be applied to and promote studies on other types of stop codon readthrough, opening new frontiers for decoding genetic dark matter underlying evolution and human pathologies.

Results
2.1
Establishing 3S-DB
During the translation of selenoproteins, SECISBP2 functions as the core RNA-binding protein, directly interacting with SECIS elements to recruit other necessary factors for Sec insertion into the protein sequence. To profile all SECISBP2-bound mRNAs, we developed an RNA immunoprecipitation-based strategy to construct the SECISBP2-bound RNA sequencing-supported selenoprotein database (3S-DB) (
Fig. 1
A). FLAG-tagged SECISBP2 was overexpressed in HEK 293T cells and used to pull down its binding RNAs for high-throughput sequencing. In theory, all mRNAs captured by the SECISBP2-RIP-Seq have the potential to recruit the Sec insertion machinery, thus incorporating Sec during protein biosynthesis, provided that other conditions are met.

2.2
Overview and validation of SECISBP2-RIP-Seq data

The SECISBP2 (Se) group was transfected with the FLAG-SECISBP2 vector and supplemented with 100 nM Na
2
SeO

Supplementary Fig. S1B

Supplementary Fig. S1C

Supplementary Fig. S1D

2.3
Construction of the 3S-DB
Among the hundreds of thousands of mRNAs binding to SECISBP2 in the RIP-Seq data, we identified 1,219 and 930 mRNAs that showed significant enrichment compared to both input and the Control samples. By overlapping these candidate mRNAs, we classified them into four categories (I, II, III, and IV), corresponding to three tiers of groups (G1-G3) with descending levels of enrichment confidence (
Fig. 3
A,
Supplementary Table 1
). The 269 RNAs in G1 exhibited an enrichment fold change ratio greater than 3 in both SECISBP2 and SECISBP2 (Se) experiments compared to the Control samples, representing the highest confidence. G2 RNAs showed an enrichment fold change of at least 1.5 in both comparisons, while G3 RNAs were enriched in only one comparison. Notably, 18 known selenoprotein RNAs were included in G1, further affirming that our strategy is capable of identifying real and novel SECIS elements, and potential novel selenoproteins. After examining the data in detail, we found that two RNAs, GPX2 and SELENOI, were captured by the SECISBP2-RIP-Seq but did not appear in the G1 category. This is primarily because their quantitative and statistical data did not meet our stringent criteria for inclusion in the highest-confidence group.

Therefore, the RNAs in the 3S-DB, especially those in G1, are highly likely to recruit the Sec insertion machinery during protein translation.
2.4
Characteristics of RNAs in the 3S-DB
To clarify the Known and other RNAs in G1, we separated the RNAs encoding known selenoproteins from G1 in the following analyses. The enrichment analysis of RNA in Known and G1-G3 groups revealed that in the Control group, the average enrichment levels of RNA among Known and G1-G3 groups were comparable. However, in the SECISBP2 and SECISBP2 (Se) groups, the average enrichment levels of RNA decreased from Known to G3, with Known exhibiting the highest average enrichment (
Fig. 3
B). This observation underscores the reliability of this strategy in characterizing RNAs for known selenoproteins.
The Sec in all known selenoproteins is encoded by the UGA codon, which functions as a stop codon during canonical translations. Therefore, UGA-containing RNAs in the 3S-DB have an elevated possibility to facilitate the insertion of Sec. By analyzing the types of stop codons, including UAA, UAG, and UGA, we found that the proportion of UGA codons in all three groups (G1-G3) was approximately 50 % (
Fig. 3
C), which is consistent with the natural ratio observed in the human coding genome [
36
]. Notably, the translation of known selenoproteins can read through the UGA codon to incorporate Sec, meaning their actual stop codon is often a non-UGA codon.
In our previous work, we established a SIS database, where UGA is used to code for Sec rather than signaling termination, with translation terminating only at a non-UGA stop codon. By matching 3S-DB to SIS-DB, we found that over half of these sequences contain at least one Sec residue in the SIS database (
Fig. 3
D). Specifically, 26.6 % of the sequences contain one Sec site, while 26.4 % have two or more Sec sites.
Since GC content is a critical factor influencing gene expression [
37
], we next examined the GC content across RNAs in the 3S-DB. Interestingly, the RNAs encoding known selenoproteins exhibit higher GC content compared to the other three groups in the 3S-DB, which show minimal differences (
Fig. 3
E). GC-rich regions are often located in promoter regions to enhance transcription efficiency. Therefore, our data provide another dimension of evidence for why known selenoproteins have a significantly higher success rate in Sec insertion compared to other RNAs in the 3S-DB.
Moreover, the 3S-DB genes are distributed across all chromosomes and did not exhibit significant chromosomal preference between groups (
Fig. 3
F). The majority of SECISBP2-bound RNAs are protein-coding RNAs (
Fig. 3
G), and the lengths of the 5′ UTR, CDS, and 3′ UTR showed minimal differences between groups in the 3S-DB (
Fig. 3
H). Strikingly, our findings reveal that SECISBP2-bound peaks were predominantly localized in the 3′ UTR (
Fig. 3
I and J), which aligns well with the known distribution of SECIS elements. Motif analysis of the 3S-DB identified two significantly enriched motifs (
Fig. 3
K). Biological process analysis highlighted several relevant pathways, particularly for G1, which shares oxidative detoxification functions with known selenoproteins (
Fig. 3
L), suggesting that G1 may contain novel selenoproteins yet to be identified.

2.6
RNA candidates exhibit SECIS activity in Sec-containing luciferase assay
To further investigate whether the RNA candidate fragments could function as SECIS elements, we employed a specialized luciferase reporter system. In this system, the Cys258 residue in luciferase was mutated to Sec by substituting TGC with a TGA codon [
38
]. Different RNA fragments were engineered to be fused to the 3’ UTR of luciferase gene. If the candidate 3′ UTRs possess SECIS activity, they would facilitate Sec incorporation during translation, leading to the expression of functional luciferase and resulting in fluorescence for quantitative measurement, otherwise they would produce inactive luciferase truncation.
The distance between acting SECIS and UGA greatly influences Sec insertion [
39
,
40
]. For instance, previous data demonstrated that efficient Sec insertion in GPX1 requires that the UGA codon be positioned more than 21 nt from the start codon and more than 204 nt from the SECIS element [
39
]. Thereafter, we aim to optimize the luciferase reporter system for appropriate UGA positioning. The engineered luciferase, luc2P, comprises the luciferase protein and a destabilizing sequence, hPEST. The luc2 contains four cysteine residues, while hPEST includes two (
Supplementary Fig. S2A
). Given that hPEST minimally impacts luciferase activity, we proceeded to mutate each of the four Cys residues (Cys81, Cys216, Cys258, and Cys391) in luc2 while fusing the 3′ UTRs of
GPX1
and
GPX4
, known to contain functional SECIS elements, to the coding region. Quantification of luciferase activity revealed that the luc2P C391U variant exhibited the highest activity (
Supplementary Fig. S2B
), indicating that Sec insertion at this position is more efficient. Consequently, we utilized the luc2P C391U construct to evaluate the SECIS functionality of
ATP5MJ-F4,5
and
PDF-F2

Various RNA sequences were appended to the luc2P C391U, including the full-length 3′ UTRs, the strong SECISBP2-binding fragments, and the full-length 3′ UTRs with the fragments deletion (

and
PDF-F2
exhibit a strong binding affinity for SECISBP2, possess SECIS activity, and can facilitate the expression of luciferase containing Sec. More importantly, this result also supports the possibility that Sec insertion may occur on a larger scale than previously recognized.

Validation of candidate proteins in 3S-DB as selenoproteins
Using
ATP5MPJ
and
PDF

To explore this potential, we analyzed the Sec incorporation capacity of the five selected genes, despite the fact that the insertion efficiency may be extremely low. Of note, due to its significant enrichment fold, the TAA-terminated
PDF

We overexpressed the FLAG-tagged candidate proteins in HEK 293T cells. Notably,

Discussion
Selenoproteins, found in all three domains of life, are rare but functionally important proteins. A longstanding goal in this field has been to identify new selenoproteins, though progress has been exceedingly slow since the first human selenoproteome map was drafted [
28
]. In this study, by focusing on SECISBP2, we developed 3S-DB, which profiles all RNAs that bind to SECISBP2. Using known selenoproteins as evidence, we demonstrate the validity and robustness of 3S-DB. More importantly, the validated novel SECIS-functional elements in ATP5MJ and PDF, underscore the utility of 3S-DB in advancing selenoprotein research. These discoveries not only expand the known repertoire of SECIS elements but also suggest the possibility that many other RNAs in the database may recruit the Sec insertion machinery to synthesize selenoproteins. SECISBP2 contains a Sec-incorporation domain (SID) and a C-terminal L7Ae-like RNA-binding domain (RBD) that act cooperatively in SECIS recognition and Sec insertion. The RBD specifically recognizes the kink-turn of SECIS elements and interacts with the ribosome, while the SID modulates this binding and facilitates recruitment of the Sec-specific elongation factor. Mutagenesis by Copeland and Driscoll identified key residues within these regions of rat SECISBP2 (e.g., 504–530 in SID, G669 in RBD) critical for high-affinity SECIS binding, ribosome association, and efficient Sec incorporation [
[41]
,
[42]
,
[43]
]. These insights provide an important structural framework for understanding SECISBP2-mediated selenoprotein synthesis.
Our initial expectation for this study was to validate Sec insertion in the selected protein candidates from G1. However, despite considerable effort, Sec insertion in these SECISBP2-binding RNA-encoded proteins was undetectable by either antibody analysis (mass shift on gel) or mass spectrometry (Sec-containing peptides). However, we should note that ATP5F1B, a close homolog (92 % sequence identity) of the murine selenoprotein candidate ATP5B [
33
], is classified into G3. Similarly, another novel human selenoprotein candidate, TSPAN14, is also classified into G3. This can be explained by the fact that UGA and SECISBP2 are the two major factors determining whether a Sec residue can be inserted during protein synthesis. However, other factors, such as the precise spatial relationship between the SECIS element and the UGA codon, also significantly influence whether Sec can be efficiently incorporated. Therefore, the proteins with weaker binding affinity to SECISBP2 may exhibit more pronounced selenoprotein products. In addition, several intrinsic factors also modulate the efficiency of Sec insertion. These include the nucleotide context surrounding the UGA codon, particularly the +1 position, which influences whether the codon is decoded as Sec or recognized as a termination signal [
44
]. Moreover, the functionality and stoichiometry of essential
trans
-acting factors—such as SECISBP2, eEFSec, and ribosomal protein L30—further fine-tune the assembly and stability of the Sec incorporation machinery [
44
]. Furthermore, the availability and isoform composition of Sec-tRNA
Sec
, which are dynamically regulated by cellular selenium levels, play a crucial role in establishing the hierarchy of selenoprotein synthesis under varying physiological conditions [
26
]. These findings provide robust evidence supporting the utility and reliability of 3S-DB as a powerful tool for the characterization and discovery of novel selenoproteins.
After these extensive efforts to identify new selenoproteins using SIS and SecMS, along with investigations into the RNAs in 3S-DB, we recognized that selenoprotein biosynthesis is not coincidental but a result of precise evolution and fine regulation. This makes the currently known selenoproteins the ultimate winners in achieving Sec insertion through such a specialized mechanism. In contrast, although the SECISBP2 binding affinities of the RNAs identified in this study are comparable to those of known SECIS elements, their activity—reflected by luciferase assays and their capacity to assist in expressing known selenoproteins—remains significantly lower than that of canonical SECIS elements.
Nonetheless, we cannot exclude the possibility that other selenoproteins may exist, particularly under specific conditions. Several lines of evidence support this hypothesis. First, many selenium-patterned peaks were observed in proteomic mass spectrometry data [
45
]. Our efforts with SIS and SecMS have revealed several Sec-containing peptides, whose validation is ongoing. Furthermore, 3S-DB suggests that over a thousand RNAs could potentially recruit the Sec insertion machinery, and these RNAs may incorporate Sec. As part of the “dark proteome”, these yet-to-be-defined selenoproteins may either be absent or present at extremely low levels under normal conditions. However, similar to non-coding RNAs with latent translational potential, Sec insertion might be induced under specific stress conditions to fulfill its functions [
46
]. A critical question that follows the identification of a new selenoprotein is understanding the purpose or function of Sec insertion. Obviously, Sec insertion leads to peptide chain elongation, which could result in changes to protein structure, alterations in protein-protein interactions, and regulation on enzymatic activity—if the selenoprotein candidate is an enzyme. These aspects would provide promising research directions for studying functions of Sec insertion.
Building on the foundation of our 3S-DB, further improvements can be made. First, the current 3S-DB was established using data from a normal HEK 293T cell line, which may overlook cell- and tissue-specific or context-dependent SECISBP2-binding events. Expanding SECISBP2-RIP-Seq to a broader range of species and cell lines, including under various stimuli, could significantly enhance this database. Although RIP-seq is effective in capturing RNA–protein interactions, its limited resolution makes it difficult to precisely map SECISBP2-binding sites or accurately identify functional SECIS elements. Future application of higher-resolution approaches, such as crosslinking immunoprecipitation (CLIP) [
47
], could help to refine and validate these findings. Additionally, although RNA structures are challenging to predict accurately, structural approaches could be employed to investigate the structural properties of newly identified SECISBP2-binding elements, uncovering their similarities and distinctions from canonical SECIS elements. These efforts could lead to the discovery of new SECIS structures, a deeper understanding of the regulatory mechanisms underlying Sec insertion, and insights into the functions of selenoproteins.
In summary, 3S-DB, constructed from SECISBP2-bound RIP-seq data, addresses a critical gap in selenoprotein research by providing experimental evidence at the RNA level. This database serves as a robust platform for identifying novel SECIS elements and potential selenoproteins. The discovery of two novel SECIS elements highlights the potential for further advancements in this field. Together with other resources developed for selenoprotein research, such as selenoDB, SIS-DB, and SecMS, their integrated ability to characterize candidate genes will be crucial for unraveling the complexities of the selenoproteome, selenoprotein synthesis, and their broader biological implications. The investigations exploring stop codon readthrough represent a fascinating deviation from the canonical genetic code, contributing to proteomic diversity and specialized protein functions, while shedding light on the complexity of translational regulation and the evolution of the genetic code.
4
Conclusions
In summary, our study established 3S-DB as a comprehensive resource for identifying RNAs that may be involved in UGA stop codon readthrough. These RNAs demonstrate significant potential to facilitate the recruitment of the Sec insertion machinery and support the biosynthesis of novel selenoproteins. The rich data and strategic methods provided in this study offer valuable insights for the exploration of other non-canonical translation termination events.

Materials and methods
5.1
Cell lines and cell culture
HEK 293T cells were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium (DMEM) containing 10 % FBS and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin (PS) at 37 °C under a 5 % CO
2
atmosphere.
5.2
Plasmid construction and transfection
Human SECISBP2, GPX1, and MSRB1 fused with N-terminal FLAG-tag were obtained by PCR amplification and subcloned into the lentiJ vector. These plasmids were transiently transfected in HEK 293T cells in accordance with the instructions provided by the PolyJet transfection reagent manufacturer (SignaGen, catalog: SL100688). Cell lysates were collected 48 h post transfection.
5.3
SECISBP2-RIP-Seq
HEK 293T cells transfected with FLAG-tagged SECISBP2 were harvested and washed three times with cold PBS. Cells were lysed in 0.5 mL of NP40 lysis buffer (50 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 150 mM KCl, 2 mM EDTA, 1 mM NaF, 0.5 % (v/v) NP40, 1 mM DTT, protease inhibitor cocktail, 100 U/ml RNaseOUT recombinant ribonuclease inhibitor) per 10 cm dish, and rotated for 30 min at 4 °C. After centrifuging at 20,000 g at 4 °C for 10 min, the supernatants were collected. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA method. 4 mg protein with 120 μl anti-FLAG magnetic beads (Biomake, catalog:
B26101
) were used for immunoprecipitation at 4 °C for 5h with rotation. Before starting the incubation, 1/5 of the sample was taken as Input. Beads were washed three times with IP wash buffer (50 mM HEPES-KOH, pH 7.5, 300 mM KCl, 0.05 % (v/v) NP40, 1 mM DTT, protease inhibitor cocktail), and further washed three times with high-salt wash buffer (50 mM HEPES-KOH, pH 7.5, 500 mM KCl, 0.05 % (v/v) NP40, 1 mM DTT, protease inhibitor cocktail).
RNAs from both Input and IP samples were extracted according to the instructions provided with the TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog: 15596026), followed by DNA removal with RNase-free DNase (Promega, catalog: M6101). RNA concentrations were measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Reverse transcription was performed using the PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Takara, catalog: RR037A) with 200 ng of RNAs per reaction. For quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR), SYBR Select Master Mix (Thermo, catalog: 4472908) was used on a QuantStudio 6 Flex Real-Time PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Gene enrichment was calculated using the 2
−ΔCt
method, where ΔCt represents the difference in cycle threshold (Ct) values between the IP samples and input samples.
RNA-seq libraries were prepared with the VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit for Illumina (Vazyme Biotech, catalog: NR604), following the manufacturer's instructions using 1 μg of RNAs. Library quality was assessed via a Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent), and concentrations were quantified by qRT-PCR against a standard library. Pooled libraries were denatured with 0.1 M NaOH (Sigma-Aldrich, catalog: 72068) to a final concentration of 2 nM, and paired-end sequencing (150 bp) was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform.
5.4
RIP-Seq data analysis
All sequencing reads were processed using Trim Galore (v0.6.6) with the parameters ‘‘–nextseq 30 –paired’’ to remove adapter sequences, retaining reads longer than 20 bp. Quality-filtered reads were then mapped to the human genome (GRCh38) using STAR (v2.7.6a) [
48
] with default settings. Uniquely mapped read pairs were counted against gene annotations from
Homo sapiens
(Ensembl: GRCh38.94) using featureCounts (v2.0.1) [
49
] with the parameters ‘‘-p -B –C’’ to generate gene-level counts. Read alignments were converted to bigwig files using bamCoverage [
50
] and visualized at selected genomic regions with IGV (v2.8.2) [
51
]. Gene-level count files were normalized using the DESeq2 (v4.0) [
52
] package in R, and differentially expressed genes were identified based on a
p
-value threshold of 0.05 and a fold-change cutoff of 2.
Human gene annotation information, such as stop codon, GC content, chromosome, gene-types and gene-lengths were retrieved from the Ensembl Genes 113 database using the BioMart tool. Peaks of SECISBP2-bound RNA were extracted using Piranha [
53
] software with a bin size set to 150 bp, focusing on the differences between IP and Input samples. The extracted peaks were then annotated and statistically analyzed for peak features using the ChIPseeker [
54
] package in R. Subsequently, motif analysis of the enriched SECISBP2-bound RNAs was conducted employing HOMER [
55
] software. Additionally, functional enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed genes was performed using the Metascape database to identify significant biological processes and pathways associated with these genes.
5.5
Expression and purification of SECISBP2 protein
The expression and purification of SECISBP2 protein were optimized based on previously reported methods [
56
]. Specifically, a pET28a-6 × His-Thrombin-TEV-SECISBP2 (409–854 aa) plasmid was constructed and transformed into Transetta (DE3) chemically competent cells (TransGen Biotech, catalog: CD801). After transferring to 1 L of LB medium containing kanamycin, cells were grown at 37 °C until OD
600
reached 0.8, followed by induction with 1 mM IPTG and incubated at 16 °C for 16–18 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4 °C, 4000 g for 30 min, and lysed in 20 mL of lysis buffer (20 mM Tris pH 8.0, 750 mM NaCl, 10 mM imidazole, 10 % glycerol, 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 5 mM MgCl
2
, protease inhibitor cocktail). The lysate was passed through a high-pressure homogenizer, and after centrifugation, the supernatant was loaded onto a Ni2+-charged column (HisTrap HP, GE Healthcare). Non-specifically bound proteins were removed with wash buffer (50 mM Tris pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl, 2 mM β-ME, 20 mM imidazole), and the recombinant protein was eluted with 500 mM imidazole. The eluate was further purified on a HiTrap Heparin HP column (GE Healthcare) to remove RNA contaminants, with final elution achieved using 1 M NaCl. The SECISBP2 protein was then subjected to size exclusion chromatography (Superdex 75, GE Healthcare) with a buffer of 20 mM Tris pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl, 2 mM DTT. The protein concentration was determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer, and the purified SECISBP2 was aliquoted and stored at −80 °C for future use.
5.6
In vitro
transcription
Gene sequences were synthesized (Azenta Life Science), and PCR amplification of DNA templates was performed using Phanta Max Super-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (Vazyme, catalog: P505).
In vitro
transcription was carried out using the RiboMAX Large Scale RNA Production System-T7 kit (Promega, catalog: P1300), following the manufacturer's instructions, to produce the target RNA fragments.
5.7
SECISBP2 pull down using biotinylated-RNA
The RNAs transcribed
in vitro
were labeled at the 3′ end using the Pierce RNA 3′ End Desthiobiotinylation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog: 20163) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The biotinylated RNAs were then enriched using streptavidin resins (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog: 20353). The enriched RNAs were subsequently incubated with SECISBP2 protein following the instructions provided in the Pierce Magnetic RNA-Protein Pull-Down Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog: 20164). The proteins obtained from the pull-down assay were analyzed by immunoblotting.
5.8
Luciferase reporter assay
The luciferase reporter assay was modified based on previously reported methods [
38
]. HEK 293T cells were cultured in 24-well plates and transiently transfected using the PolyJet reagent when the confluence reached 70–80 %. The transfection included plasmids encoding firefly luciferase with varying 3′ UTRs and a renilla luciferase plasmid as a control. After 24 h, cells were lysed, and luciferase activities were measured using the Dual Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (Vazyme, catalog: DL101). The lysate was mixed with firefly luciferase substrate to determine luminescence, followed by the addition of renilla luciferase substrate. The relative luciferase activity was calculated as the ratio of firefly to renilla luminescence for each sample.
5.9
Sample preparation for LC-MS analysis
HEK 293T cells were transfected with various plasmids encoding modified FLAG-tagged GPX1 and FLAG-tagged MSRB1. After 24 h, cells were harvested and washed twice with pre-chilled PBS, followed by lysis in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, catalog: P0013B) and sonication for 1 min to collect the cell lysate. The lysate was subjected to high-speed centrifugation at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. Protein concentration was quantified using the BCA assay. For immunoprecipitation, 2 mg of protein was incubated with FLAG magnetic beads overnight at 4 °C. The beads were washed three times with PBSN (1 % NP40) and three times with PBS. Subsequently, loading buffer was added to the beads to denature and elute the proteins, which were then separated by SDS-PAGE.
Following Coomassie blue staining, the target protein bands were excised, and in-gel digestion was performed according to the proteomics sample preparation protocol [
57
]. In brief, proteins were decolorized using a 100 mM NH
4
HCO
3
: ACN (1:1, v/v) solution, followed by reduction with 10 mM DTT at 56 °C for 30 min. Alkylation was carried out with 55 mM IAA at room temperature in the dark for 20 min, and digestion was performed with trypsin at a final concentration of 10 ng/μL overnight. The digested peptides were extracted using a mixture of 5 % FA: ACN (1:2, v/v) and concentrated by evaporation at 60 °C. The resulting peptide mixture was reconstituted in 0.1 % FA for LC-MS/MS analysis.
5.10
LC-MS/MS analysis
The peptide mixture was analyzed on an LC-MS/MS system consisting of an online EASY-nLC 1200 HPLC and Orbitrap Q-Exactive HF X mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). 15 cm capillary column (100 μm I.D.) with an ESI emitter tip was laser-pulled and packed in-house with C18 resin (C18-AQ, 1.9 μm) (Dr. Maisch, Germany). HPLC solvent A consists of 80 % H
2
O, 20 % ACN, and 0.1 % FA, and HPLC solvent B consists of 80 % ACN, 20 % H
2
O, and 0.1 % FA. The peptides were separated by a 120-min gradient (solvent B: 3 % at 0 min, 8 % at 4 min, 28 % at 90 min, 42 % at 106 min, 50 % at 114 min, 100 % at 117 min, 100 % at 120 min) at a flow rate of 300 nL/min. The MS data were acquired in positive mode using data-dependent acquisition (DDA) method (top-20) and parallel reaction monitoring (PRM) modes. In the PRM module, the target ions included two Sec-containing peptides from human GPX1 (
m/z
896.9572, charge +2;
m/z
598.3072, charge +3) and two Sec-containing peptides from human MSRB1 (
m/z
568.7481, charge +2;
m/z
379.5012, charge +3). For MS1, the scan range was set to 350–1500
m/z
with a resolution of 60,000, an AGC target of 3e6, and a maximum ion injection time of 20 ms. For MS2, fragmentation was performed on precursor with 2–5 charges at a resolution of 30,000, with an AGC target of 1e5 and a maximum injection time of 100 ms. HCD fragmentation was utilized with an energy level of 27, a separation window of 1.6
m/z
, and a dynamic exclusion time of 30 s.
5.11
MS data analysis
MS data were processed using Mascot (version 2.8.0) software [
58
]. Raw data were firstly converted to.mgf files using ProteoWizard msconvert application [
59
], and then submitted to the Mascot search engine. Database searches were conducted against the SwissProt human database (downloaded on 2022-06-29), with decoy database searches performed for quality control. Variable modifications included acetylation of protein N termini (+42.01 Da), oxidation of methionine (+15.9949 Da), carbamidomethyl of cysteine and selenocysteine (+57.021464 Da), and deselenation of selenocysteine (−81.932170 Da). The enzyme was set to trypsin, allowing for up to three missed cleavages. Peptide charge states were set to +2, +3, and +4, with precursor mass tolerance at ±10 ppm and fragment mass tolerance at ±0.02 Da.
13
C was set to 1 to avoid misassignment of monoisotopic peaks. The instrument type was specified as ESI-TRAP. Search results were filtered with a significance threshold of
p
< 0.05, and a Peptide-Spectrum Match (PSM) false discovery rate (FDR) of <0.05 was applied.

p
< 0.05 and was denoted as follows: ∗
p
< 0.05; ∗∗
p
< 0.01; ∗∗∗
p
