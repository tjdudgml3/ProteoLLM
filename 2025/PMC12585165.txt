Introduction
Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) disrupts systemic metabolic homeostasis, increasing cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk and cardiovascular mortality (
1
). The interplay between T2DM and CVD involves shared risk factors, including hypertension and coronary artery disease, alongside direct effects on cardiomyocyte (CM) structure and function, termed diabetic cardiomyopathy (DbCM) (
2
). DbCM is characterized by diastolic dysfunction and left ventricular hypertrophy, arising from structural pathologies including fibrosis and altered myofilament function (
3
). These changes are driven by profound alterations in CM metabolism, including insulin resistance, toxic lipid accumulation, mitochondrial dysfunction, elevated fatty acid (FA) metabolism, and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress (
4
).
Although DbCM is widespread (
5
), the development of therapies is limited by an unmet need for preclinical models that reflect human pathophysiology (
6
,
7
). Significant interspecies differences in cardiac metabolism, ion channel function, and inflammatory responses mean that findings from rodent models often fail to translate to patients, leading to costly and unsuccessful clinical trials (
8
,
9
). Complementary human-centric models, such as human induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC)–derived CMs, offer a viable alternative (
10
). hiPSC-CMs can be grown in two dimensions (2D) or three dimensions (3D) as organoids or engineered heart tissue (EHT) (
11
,
12
), with the latter 3D models showing greater morphological, metabolic, and sarcomeric maturation of CMs (
13
) traded off against greater time- and cost-intensive generation. Although hiPSC-CMs have been used extensively in modeling monogenic diseases (
14
), using them to study complex multifactorial, lifestyle-related diseases such as T2DM has been limited (
6
).
Initial attempts to model the diabetogenic environment in vitro have used short-term exposure to high glucose or FA (
15–19
). However, a robust model requires a multifactorial approach that captures the chronic, concurrent impact of hyperglycemia, hyperlipidemia, and hyperinsulinemia. Our approach first enhanced the maturation of hiPSC-CMs by culturing them with low FA concentrations, thereby providing a more physiologically relevant baseline for disease studies (
13
). We applied our multifactorial, longer-term insulin resistant (IR) protocol to these matured cells in both 2D cultures and advanced 3D EHTs to assess the ability to recapitulate the hallmarks of DbCM. This was assessed using multiomics analysis to gain a systems-level understanding of the pathways regulated in our hiPSC-CMs in response to insulin resistance. This broader perspective, which extends beyond single-pathway evaluations, provided a more comprehensive view of how different aspects of the diabetes milieu converge to affect cardiac function in DbCM.
Our findings showed that 2D and 3D IR models can replicate key hallmarks of DbCM. Additionally, in 3D EHT, physiological parameters, such as contractility, were also significantly impacted by insulin resistance. Importantly, we demonstrated that treatment with metformin reverses many of these metabolic and contractile abnormalities, highlighting the potential of IR EHTs as platforms for mechanistic studies and translationally relevant drug screening for DbCM.
Research Design and Methods
hiPSC-CM Differentiation
IMR90 (European Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures catalog no. 85020204 female Caucasian fetal lung origin) hiPSCs were differentiated into hiPSC-CMs over 15 days by modulating Wnt signaling (
20
), as previously described (
21
) (
Fig. 1
A
). Confluent hiPSCs cultured in TeSR-E8 media on growth factor reduced Matrigel–coated plates were moved to RB
−
media (RPMI with 1% B-27 minus insulin) containing CHIR99021 (6 µmol/L) for 2 days. The media were then replaced with RB
−
only for 1 day, followed by a 2-day treatment with RB
−
-containing Wnt-C59 (2.5 µmol/L). From day 5, cells were maintained in RB
−
media. For metabolic selection of CMs, glucose-free RB
−
media were used on days 11 and 13. Finally, on day 15, the cells were switched to RB
+
media (RPMI with 1% B-27 complete). This differentiation protocol resulted in >80% CMs (
13
). The cells were differentiated in six-well plates, and cells from different wells in the plate were used as technical replicates. For each experiment,
n
refers to separate differentiations as biological repeats.

Generation of 3D EHT
EHTs were generated using a previously described protocol (
22
) (
Fig. 5
A
). Following differentiation, hiPSC-CMs were dissociated into single cells. For a single EHT, 10
6
hiPSC-CMs were resuspended in 94.56 μL of hydration media (DMEM including 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 10% FBS, and 1% glutamine), 6.68 μL of 2× DMEM (20% 10× DMEM [1.3 g DMEM powder in 10 mL double-distilled H
2
O], 20% horse serum, 0.12 μL of Y-27632 rock inhibitor [9 mmol/L stock solution]), 12 µL of growth factor reduced Matrigel, 3.04 μL of a mixture of fibrinogen (200 mg/mL in 0.9% NaCl), and aprotinin (0.1 mg/mL). To this master mix, 3.6 μL of bovine thrombin (100 units/mL stock solution) was added immediately before pipetting the solution into a precast 2% agarose mold prepared using Teflon spacers, where it solidified around two silicone posts over 90 min. An additional 500 μL of hydration media was added for 30 min.
2D and 3D hiPSC-CM Maturation Protocol
Once beating, the 2D hiPSC-CMs and 3D EHTs were cultured in maturation media for 7 days, based on our previous publications (
13
,
21
). The maturation media comprised DMEM (1 g/L glucose) supplemented with 10% horse serum, 5 µg/mL vitamin B12, 0.5 mmol/L ascorbic acid, 0.84 μmol/L biotin, 1.7 µmol/L insulin, 80 µmol/L oleic acid:BSA (2:1), 1× minimum essential medium nonessential amino acids, and 0.1× penicillin/streptomycin.
Inducing Insulin Resistance in hiPSC-CMs and Hypoxic Exposure
Insulin resistance was induced over 6 days using a two-step media protocol (
21
). IR media comprised DMEM supplemented with 1× nonessential amino acids, 0.5 mmol/L ascorbic acid, 5 µg/mL vitamin B12, 0.84 µmol/L biotin, 0.1× penicillin/streptomycin, and 10% horse serum. Mature hiPSC-CMs or EHTs were cultured for 3 days in glucose-free IR media containing 0.3 mmol/L palmitic acid (bound to BSA) and 1.7 µmol/L insulin, and then switched to glucose-enriched IR media containing 12 mmol/L glucose, 0.3 mmol/L palmitic acid (bound to BSA), and 3.4 µmol/L insulin for a further 3 days. Control samples were maintained in maturation media for 6 days. A subset of control and IR EHTs was treated with 0.5 mmol/L metformin for the final 24 h.
In a separate experiment, mature hiPSC-CMs were cultured in DMEM (1 g/L glucose and the same vitamins as above) with or without palmitate (0.4 mmol/L bound to BSA) for 24 h. For hypoxia experiments, hiPSC-CMs were incubated for 16 h in 2% O
2
and 5% CO
2
at 37°C, whereas normoxic controls were in 21% O
2
and 5% CO
2
at 37°C. Hypoxic cells were rapidly lysed after removal from the incubator to minimize reoxygenation (
21
).
RNA Isolation and Quantitative PCR
RNA was extracted from cells using the QIAGEN RNeasy Mini Kit with QIAzol lysis agent. For quantitative PCR, cDNA was synthesized using a high-capacity RNA-to-cDNA kit before analysis using the StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR system with Power SYBR Green PCR Master Mix. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2
−ΔΔCT
method and normalized to the housekeeping gene ubiquitin C (
Supplementary Table 1
).
Transcriptomics Mapping, Quantification, and Differential Expression Analysis
mRNA from 2D hiPSC-CMs and EHTs was sequenced by Illumina. For hiPSC-CMs, Novogene performed stranded, paired-end (150 base pairs) poly-A enrichment sequencing. For EHTs, Illumina 3′ QuantSeq was conducted by Novo Nordisk Research Centre Oxford. Postsequencing, FastQC was used for quality control, and Cutadapt was used to remove adapters and low-quality reads (
23
). hiPSC-CM transcripts were quantified using Salmon (
24
) against a Gencode version 44 index, while EHT reads were mapped with STAR (
25
) using Ensembl version 109.
Differential expression analysis was performed with DESeq2, removing genes with low counts. Counts were normalized using variance stabilization transformation for exploratory analysis, including principal component analysis (PCA). Log fold changes were refined using apeglm Bayesian shrinkage (
26
). Statistical significance was defined as a Benjamini-Hochberg–adjusted
P
< 0.05. For the EHT data, surrogate variable analysis (
27
) identified a latent batch effect, which was incorporated into the DESeq2 design or corrected using the limma package (
28
). The
z
scores from normalized expression were used to visualize pathway expression in heat maps. Hierarchical clustering of both pathways (rows) and samples (columns) was performed using Euclidean distance with the complete linkage method. Differential transcript usage (DTU) was assessed using the DTUrtle R package (
29
) to identify proportional changes in gene transcript composition. DTUrtle implements the DRIMseq methodology, incorporating false discovery rate (FDR) correction via the stageR package and post hoc filtering to remove transcripts with low expression variance (
30
).
Proteomics Quantification and Differential Abundance Analysis
Proteomics analysis was conducted by Novo Nordisk Research Centre Oxford via liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry. Protein samples (∼100 µg) were prepared in 50 mmol/L ammonium bicarbonate with 10 pmol of horse myoglobin and 0.2% RapiGest, reduced with 10 mmol/L dithiothreitol, and alkylated with 15 mmol/L iodoacetamide. Samples were then digested overnight with trypsin (1:50 ratio), quenched with 0.5% trifluoroacetic acid for 45 min, and reconstituted in 0.1% formic acid before loading 500 ng for liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry analysis.
Peptides were identified using PEAKS DB (
31
), with protein abundance calculated from the summed area under the curve of extracted ion chromatograms. Subsequent analysis used the R packages DEP (
32
) and proDA (
33
). DEP was used for filtering, variance stabilizing transformation normalization, and imputation for PCA. Differential abundance analysis was performed using proDA.
Pathway-Based Analysis
Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using the fast GSEA (FGSEA) package (
34
), ranking genes by differential expression test statistics without filtering low-expression genes to preserve accurate null distributions. For proteomics data, UniProt identifiers were mapped to gene symbols. Pathways from WikiPathways (WikiPaths), the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and Hallmark (Molecular Signatures Database [MSigDB] [
35
]) were merged, and FGSEA’s collapsePathways function was used to reduce pathway redundancy.
P
values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg method, with an FDR <0.05 as the threshold of significance. Single-sample enrichment analysis via the gene set variation analysis (GSVA) (
36
) algorithm was used to visualize significant pathways. Overrepresentation and protein-protein interaction analyses were done using Metascape (
37
). Tissue-specific gene enrichment was analyzed with the TissueEnrich web portal (
38
) using the set of non–lowly expressed genes from 2D control and IR samples separately.
Measurement of Substrate Metabolism
Glycolytic rates were measured by culturing 2D cells or 3D EHTs in DMEM (5 mmol/L glucose) supplemented with
3
H-glucose (0.2 μCi/mL D-[5-
3
H(N)] glucose) with or without 50 nmol/L insulin for 6 h, with the
3
H-tracer released at the enolase reaction. In separate experiments, FA oxidation rates were measured by culturing cells or EHTs in DMEM (0.3 mmol/L oleate) supplemented with
3
H-oleate (0.2 μCi/mL [9,10-
3
H(N)]-oleic acid) for 8 h, with the tracer released at complex IV of the electron transport chain. Metabolic flux rates were measured by the rate of conversion of the
3
H-substrate into
3
H
2
O, as previously described (
13
). Oil red O staining was performed on EHTs using an oil red O staining kit after fixing in 4% paraformaldehyde (nuclei were stained with DAPI). Glucose and lactate concentrations in cell media were measured using an ABX Pentra 400 clinical chemistry analyzer before and after 24 h of culture.
Measurement of Contractile Function
Videos of contracting EHTs or 2D hiPSC-CM monolayers were recorded for 15–30 s at ×60 magnification using a Canon EOS-100D camera at room temperature. Recordings were converted to 50 frames/s TIFF stacks using ffmpeg and analyzed in MUSCLEMOTION (
39
) version 1.195-197 (Fiji/ImageJ plug-in) to calculate peak-to-peak time (cycle length), contraction duration, relaxation time, time to peak, and contraction amplitude (
Supplementary Fig. 5
). All preparations beat spontaneously, so no electrical pacing was applied.
Because each rate metric depends on cycle length, statistical tests were run on the natural log scale ln(metric), which was modeled as a linear function of ln(peak-to-peak). For single-factor comparisons, a log ANCOVA was fitted with group as the fixed factor and ln(cycle length) as the covariate; observations were back-transformed to a common reference cycle length (the geometric mean of the data set, typically 231–268 ms) for plotting. For two-factor comparisons, mixed-effects models were used as follows: ln(metric) ∼ tissue × sample + ln(peak-to-peak) + [1 + ln(peak-to-peak) | tissue:sample]. This allows each tissue-sample combination to have its own cycle length slope while still borrowing information across groups. Fixed-effect contrasts from these models provide the main culture effect (tissue), the main treatment effect (treatment), and their interaction, which gave the
P
values reported in the legends.
Western Blotting
Cells or EHT were washed with Dulbecco PBS and cultured for 30 min with or without 10 μg/mL insulin, followed by lysing and snap freezing in lysis buffer. Western blotting antibody details are provided in
Supplementary Table 2
.
Electron Microscopy
EHTs were removed from posts and immersion fixed in 2% paraformaldehyde/2.5% glutaraldehyde for 2 h, then stored in 10% fixative solution. Samples were washed in 0.1mol/L phosphate buffer, treated with 1% osmium tetroxide, and stained with 2% uranyl acetate. They were dehydrated in increasing concentrations of ethanol (70–100%) and acetone and embedded in Spurr resin. Sections of 50–80 nm in length were cut using a microtome and mounted on 200-mesh nickel grids. Samples were imaged using a JEM-1010 JEOL transmission electron microscope with a Gantan Orius camera. Images were analyzed using ImageJ by drawing a region around muscle fibers identified by filaments organized into sarcomeres.
Statistical Analysis and Plots
All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.1. The assumption of data normality was first evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For two-group comparisons, Student
t
tests were used for normally distributed data, while the nonparametric Wilcoxon rank sum test was used for data that were not normally distributed. Similarly, for factorial analyses, a two-way ANOVA with Tukey post hoc test was performed on normal data, whereas the aligned rank transform ANOVA was used for nonnormal data. Statistical comparisons for gene expression are based on DESeq2 results. Bar plots depict the mean and SD, while box plots show the median and interquartile range. Significance was set at
P
< 0.05. Illustrations were created using BioRender.com.

Results
Culturing 2D hiPSC-CMs in an IR Media Captures Many Characteristics of DbCM
During the progression of T2DM, the heart is exposed to hyperglycemia, hyperlipidemia, and hyperinsulinemia (
4
). Therefore, we used hiPSC-CMs exposed to an IR media (
Fig. 1
A
) to identify human-relevant mechanisms related to DbCM in vitro. All CMs were first matured with a low concentration of oleate, using a protocol developed in-house to activate key oxidative metabolic pathways (
13
) (without causing lipid overload [
Supplementary Fig. 1]
). The 2D hiPSC-CMs were cultured for 6 days in control or IR media before RNA sequencing to undertake a high-dimensional assessment of our model. Our hiPSC-CMs comprised >80% α-actinin–expressing cells (
13
), and expressed genes were strongly enriched for heart muscle signatures (
Supplementary Fig. 2
). PCA revealed a clear separation between groups, with principal component 1 (PC1) capturing 59% of the variance (
Fig. 1
B
). The IR treatment prompted a robust transcriptomic response, with 4,142 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) identified (
Fig. 1
C
), constituting ∼23% of all expressed genes (
n
= 18,854) and highlighting a substantial transcriptional reprogramming.
Pathway analysis indicated that DEGs were significantly enriched for CVD terms, including cardiomyopathies (
Fig. 1
D
), and known patient DbCM biomarkers robustly clustered the treatment groups (
40
) (
Fig. 1
E
). GSEA of the entire ranked gene list revealed 147 significantly altered pathways, which distinctly separated control and IR groups upon single-sample enrichment and hierarchical clustering (
Fig. 1
F
). Notably, the leading-edge genes driving the enrichment of core metabolic and signaling pathways, including glycolysis, oxidative phosphorylation, β-oxidation, ER stress, and phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)-Akt/insulin signaling, were themselves collectively overrepresented in the “Experimental Diabetes Mellitus” gene set (
Fig. 1
G
). Our IR protocol did not induce substantial cell death or apoptosis (
Supplementary Fig. 1
). Taken together, these data demonstrate that our in vitro model recapitulates several key transcriptional hallmarks of DbCM.
The 2D hiPSC-CM Model Captures the IR Phenotype With Decreased Glycolysis
Our transcriptomic data pointed to defective insulin signaling, a core feature of T2DM. Accordingly, the PI3K-Akt pathway was significantly downregulated in our IR model, with the expression of its leading-edge genes robustly separating the treatment groups (
Fig. 2
A
and
B
). To validate insulin resistance, we measured Akt phosphorylation (p-Akt) following insulin stimulation. While control cells showed a strong increase in the p-Akt/Akt ratio, this response was blunted in IR cells (
Fig. 2
C
). Downstream of p-Akt, insulin regulates myocardial glucose metabolism. The glycolysis pathway was negatively enriched in the IR model, along with significantly lower expression of multiple glycolytic enzymes (
Fig. 2
D
–
F
and
Supplementary Fig. 3
A
). We validated this with functional measurements of metabolic flux, finding that both insulin-stimulated glycolytic rates and total glucose metabolism were significantly lower in IR CMs (
Fig. 2
G
and
H
).

Culturing 2D hiPSC-CMs in IR Media Blunts the Response to Hypoxia
The adaptive hypoxic response is impaired in patients with T2DM following myocardial infarction (

While hypoxia induced a profound transcriptional shift in both control and IR CMs, PCA and hierarchical clustering analyses revealed a severely blunted response in the IR group. Strikingly, hypoxic IR cells were transcriptionally more similar to normoxic cells than to hypoxic controls (

Signatures of Cardiac Stress and Altered Splicing in 2D, yet Contractile Changes Were Confounded by Heterogeneity
Our IR model also exhibited hallmarks of cardiac stress, with significant enrichment of both transforming growth factor-β and ER stress pathways (
Supplementary Fig. 4
). Paradoxically, contractile gene sets were upregulated, prompting us to investigate posttranscriptional regulation (
Supplementary Fig. 5
A
). This revealed 314 significant DTU events concentrated in genes for myocardial structure and contraction, including the tropomyosin family (
Supplementary Fig. 5
B
–
D
). These splicing changes coincided with the upregulation of the master cardiac splicing regulators RBM20 and RBM24, indicating a mechanism for generating altered contractile protein isoforms (
Supplementary Fig. 5
E
). Despite this extensive molecular remodeling, direct contractility assays in our 2D model revealed no significant differences in contraction or relaxation dynamics, a result confounded by high experimental variability (
Supplementary Fig. 5
F
–
L
). This suggests that while our model recapitulates key transcriptional features of DbCM, our 2D culture system may lack the structural organization and mechanical load required to present the functional consequences of these molecular changes.
Integrated Transcriptomics and Proteomics Demonstrate Network Hubs in 3D EHT That Capture the Disease Phenotype
Culturing hiPSC-CMs as 3D EHTs more closely replicated the increased workload and shear stress of in situ CMs to better recapitulate the mechanical environment of the heart (
Fig. 5
A
). Following exposure of EHT to IR media, we performed parallel transcriptomic and proteomic analyses. PCA of both data sets revealed a clear separation between IR and control EHTs (
Fig. 5
B
and
C
). We identified 321 downregulated and 224 upregulated genes (FDR corrected), but only 11 proteins were significantly altered postcorrection, prompting us to include proteins with
P
< 0.05 (132 downregulated, 109 upregulated) for subsequent analysis. To overcome this limitation and reduce dimensionality, we integrated the transcriptomic data to prioritize biologically significant clusters via pathway and protein-protein interaction analyses. Pathway enrichment analysis of upregulated molecules revealed overrepresentation for FA metabolism and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling pathways (
Fig. 5
D
). Conversely, downregulated terms were related to receptor tyrosine kinase signaling (the family for the insulin receptor) and vesicle-mediated transport (involved in glucose uptake) (
Fig. 5
E
). A protein-protein interaction network integrating these differentially expressed molecules (
Fig. 5
F
and
G
) highlighted that the DbCM pathway and related metabolic pathways were significantly overrepresented (
Fig. 5
H
).

Discussion
Human-centric models of DbCM are crucial for understanding disease mechanisms and developing new therapies. We generated a DbCM model by culturing hiPSC-CMs in 2D or 3D in IR media, and applied a systems biology approach to assess affected pathways. Our model activated key pathological pathways, including a shift from glucose to FA metabolism, mitochondrial dysfunction, ECM remodeling, and ER stress. Crucially, the 3D EHT IR model further recapitulated contractile dysfunction, a clinical hallmark of the disease. Contractile and metabolic impairments were pharmacologically reversible with metformin, validating this hiPSC-CM platform as a potential tool for investigating DbCM pathophysiology and testing therapeutic interventions.
Culturing Cells in IR Media Induces a Transcriptional Profile Identified as Diabetes Mellitus
While hiPSC-CMs are well established for modeling monogenic disorders, their applications to complex, systemic diseases have been limited to single-substrate protocols, short exposure times, or immature hiPSC-CMs (
15–19
). To address this, we cultured hiPSC-CMs in a bespoke IR media that mirrors the chronic hyperglycemia, hyperlipidemia, and hyperinsulinemia of T2DM. Omic analysis demonstrated induction of a disease-relevant state enriched for “Diabetes Mellitus Experimental” and “Diabetic Cardiomyopathy” pathways, and known human DbCM biomarkers, such as natriuretic peptide A (electrolyte homeostasis), nebulin (cytoskeleton), and frizzled-related protein (Wnt signaling) (
40
), robustly clustered control and IR samples.
2D and 3D IR hiPSC-CMs Demonstrate the Metabolic Shift From Glucose to FA Use
A hallmark of the T2DM heart is the metabolic shift from glucose to FA use (
4
), and our IR models recapitulated this in both 2D and 3D formats. Increased FA metabolism was evident in transcriptomic and proteomic profiles, likely driven by PPAR
α
activation (
44
), and was functionally validated by radioisotope flux measurements showing increased FA oxidation and concomitantly decreased glucose metabolism. Additionally, upregulated lipid biosynthesis pathways led to enhanced triglyceride accumulation and activation of ceramide-related pathways linked to insulin resistance. Critically, a prior maturation step with low oleate concentrations established metabolic readiness in our cells (
13
). We propose that preconditioning was essential for mitigating apoptosis during the IR protocol by priming cells to effectively metabolize the high lipid load.
The IR Model Impacts Key Mediators Implicated in the Development of DbCM
DbCM is marked by contractile dysfunction and fibrosis driven by mediators including ER stress and ECM remodeling (
4
), and our omics profiling revealed upregulation of genes for these pathways. In 3D EHTs, proteins and genes linked to ECM remodeling and collagen biosynthesis formed a dense subnetwork, aligning with diabetes-associated fibroblast activation (
45
). The likely presence of hiPSC-fibroblasts in our cultures is therefore potentially advantageous for studying the fibrotic component of DbCM. Contractile dysfunction in DbCM stems from interconnected cellular abnormalities (
46
), and we identified several significant genes/pathways consistent with impaired actin-myosin cross-bridge cycling via myosin heavy chain alterations (
47
); energy deficits limiting ATP for actomyosin interaction and ion pumps (
48
); dysregulated ion channels disrupting calcium homeostasis and excitation-contraction coupling (
49
); and cytoskeletal remodeling via GTPases like RhoA, which, with excessive collagen deposition, increase myocardial stiffness (
50
,
51
).
Advantages and Disadvantages of Modeling Insulin Resistance in 2D Versus 3D
Our results show a key distinction between platforms. While both 2D and 3D IR models exhibited transcriptional and metabolic changes, only the 3D EHT model recapitulated contractile dysfunction, including longer contraction duration and peak amplitude, as well as increased relaxation time. The superior physiological fidelity of the 3D EHT platform is rooted in its promotion of a more mature CM phenotype via mechanical strain and complex cell-matrix interactions absent in monolayers (
22
). However, this does not discount 2D monolayers, which are well suited for high-throughput target identification for development of treatments for DbCM. For a detailed assessment of integrated pathophysiology, however, their shortcomings are clear. Future work that paces cells for more precise contraction analysis may further reduce the heterogeneity in these measurements.
Our objective was not to perfectly replicate a systemic pathology such as DbCM, but to engineer a fit-for-purpose hiPSC-CM system for probing the cell-intrinsic consequences of DbCM. We acknowledge that our protocol does not fully replicate the multifactorial nature of chronic T2DM; however, the modularity of this hiPSC-CM platform provides a powerful framework for dissecting pathogenic factors. The IR media could be further supplemented with proinflammatory cytokines to model inflammation-driven fibrosis, advanced glycation end products to recapitulate myocardial stiffening, or intermittent hypoxia to simulate microvascular insufficiency (
52
). Additionally, coculture with hiPSC-derived cardiac fibroblasts or immune cells would allow the model to evolve to include the multiple cell types implicated in DbCM (
53
). Systematically adding these layers of complexity will enhance the model’s predictive power for dissecting disease mechanisms and identifying novel therapeutic targets.
Given that up to 70% of patients with T2DM exhibit DbCM (
5
,
54
), amid limited treatment options (
6
,
7
), we evaluated our EHT model’s response to metformin therapy. We demonstrate that metformin restored glycolytic metabolism and concurrently alleviated contractile dysfunction in our IR EHTs, confirming the model’s suitability for assessing the direct effects of therapeutics on the CM.
Conclusion
Our multifactorial IR protocol, applied to metabolically mature hiPSC-CMs, induced key mechanisms of DbCM that were confirmed by a systems biology approach. The 3D EHTs further recapitulated a contractile dysfunction phenotype that was demonstrably reversed by metformin, underscoring the platform’s value for therapeutic screening.
