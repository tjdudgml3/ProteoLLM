1
INTRODUCTION
Collagen VI is a prominent component of the extracellular matrix (ECM), and plays a critical role in maintaining tissue structure, integrity, and function of the basement membrane in several tissues.
1
Collagen VI is composed of three genetically distinct α chains encoded by
COL6A1
,
COL6A2
, and
COL6A3
genes. Mutations in any of these three genes have been implicated in debilitating diseases characterized by various muscle/tendon disorders. Collectively known as collagen VI‐related myopathies, it encompasses a spectrum of disorders ranging from the relatively mild Bethlem myopathy to the more severe Ullrich congenital muscular dystrophy.
2
Bethlem myopathy is characterized by mild muscle weaknesses along with distal joint laxity and an overall slow progression of the disease to the point where most patients need assistance when walking by the age of 60.
3
An early onset of signs and symptoms such as hypotonia, extreme distal join laxity, and skeletal deformities characterizes Ullrich congenital muscular dystrophy. Learning to walk is delayed and sometimes only achieved with assistive devices.
3
Ullrich muscular dystrophy is more commonly caused by dominant‐negative mutations than by recessive mutations, and recessive mutations (that are not null, for example missense mutations) can cause a Bethlem myopathy phenotype. Overall, recessive null mutations are rare in the patient population, and they are more frequently found in the
COL6A2
gene than in
COL6A1
or
COL6A3
genes.
4
,
5
,
6
Biochemically, collagen VI is an heterotrimeric protein assembly involving a 1:1:1 stoichiometry of COL6A1, COL6A2, and COL6A3. This triple helical monomer subsequently forms dimers and then tetramers before its secretion into the extracellular space and assembly into higher‐order structures in the ECM called microfibrils. Dysregulation of these steps can lead to abnormalities in collagen VI structure and function, contributing to the pathogenesis of collagen VI‐related diseases. In fact, there seems to be a relationship between the degree to which a mutation affects collagen VI assembly and the severity of the phenotype observed in patients, regardless of the exact collagen VI gene mutated.
3
In certain contexts, COL6A3 can be substituted by three other chains encoded by
COL6A4
,
COL6A5
,
and COL6A6
genes.
7
,
8
Of note, in human cells, the
COL6A4
gene does not encode for a functional protein.
7
,
8
Mouse models interfering with collagen VI expression have been developed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms and pathogenic consequences of collagen VI mutations. One of the first collagen VI deficient mice models was a model with targeted inactivation of the
Col6a1
gene.
9
Despite the COL6A1 deficiency in muscle, mice developed a phenotype reminiscent of the milder Bethlem myopathy. This is also true when the
Col6a2
gene is inactivated.
10
One of the earliest
Col6a3
knockout mouse models was generated by Pan et al.,
11
utilizing homologous recombination to disrupt the
Col6a3
gene. However, inserting the neo cassette in exon 15 creates a variant transcript skipping exon 15 and exon 16. This model called Col6a3
hm
creates a hypomorph; the mouse expresses a very low amount of
Col6a3
transcript with an in‐frame deletion and the resulting COL6A3 protein with 49 amino acids less is still detectable although at a much lower level. Shortly afterwards, a new model was created by the same group by replacing the
Col6a3
exon 16 with a neo cassette resulting in an in‐frame deletion.
12
The resulting
Col6a3
d16/d16
mice models dominant disease. It retains the capacity to express a COL6A3 protein although lacking 18 amino acids. Of note, mutant COL6A3 can subsequently assemble into tetramer but not microfibrils.
12
As part of the international mouse phenotyping consortium (IMPC), the
Col6a3
em1(IMPC)Tcp
model was created by deleting 193 bp in exon 7; the model was further analyzed in a large phenotypic study.
13
Park and collaborators created the
Col6a3
em1(IMPC)/KMPC
targeting exon 12 that show complete knockout at the mRNA level. The knockout efficiency at the protein level was not confirmed using COL6A3‐specific antibodies although a pan collagen VI antibody showed that it abrogates collagen VI expression.
14
Recently, the
Col6a3
CTT/CTT
mouse model was engineered by inserting a premature stop codon in exon 36 in the C‐terminal domain, yielding a truncated COL6A3 protein with reduced expression compared to the wild type.
15
However, none of the above mouse models has been reported to have complete knockout of COL6A3 protein.
Recent advancements in genome editing technologies, particularly CRISPR‐Cas9, offer unprecedented opportunities to refine and enhance mouse models of COL6A3‐related diseases. Here we report the development, validation and preliminary phenotypic characterization of a CRISPR‐based
Col6a3
knockout mouse model completely abrogating the COL6A3 protein.

METHODS
2.1
Col6a3 d3 design and generation
Two single guide RNA (5′‐TGGCAATGCCTGAGAGGAGAAGG‐3′ and 5′‐GGGGGTGGTACAGTATAGCGAGG‐3′) were design based on the NCBI RefSeq
NM_001243008
sequence. The two single guide RNAs, along with Cas9, were co‐injected into fertilized eggs for knockout mouse production by Cyagen LLC.
2.2
Mouse husbandry
Col6a3
d3/+
mouse founders were purchased from Cyagen LLC. All mice were housed in the animal facility of the Institut de Recherche sur le Cancer de l'Université de Sherbrooke, Quebec, Canada. All procedures were approved by the Animal Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine and Health Sciences of the Université de Sherbrooke and strictly follow the Canadian Council on Animal Care (CCAC) requirements. All mice were housed in hermetically sealed cages and placed on a support equipped with a ventilation system. All mouse strains were maintained in a room with a 12/12 (day/night) light cycle at a temperature of 21–22℃.
2.2.1
Genotyping
PCR
At day 15, a piece of the ear is cut off and digested in 50 mmol/L NaOH (95℃, 1–2 h), neutralized with Tris buffer (pH = 7.5). The resulting DNA extract is subjected to PCR using the Apex Taq RED Master Mix 2X (Genesee Scientific, 42‐138B) and specific primers to detect
Col6a3
knockout (Col6a3_F1 5′‐ACTAATCACACACAAAGCCTGTTC‐3′ and Col6a3_R1 5′‐GTCATCGGTAGCTATGTGCTGAAG‐3′) and
Col6a3

PCR products were subsequently resolved on a 2% agarose gel containing Redsafe nucleic acid staining solution (Froggabio, 21141) in 1× TBE buffer.
2.3
Food intake measurement
Mice were separated by sex and genotype into different cages for up to 4 mice per cage. A controlled amount of food sufficient for a week was weighed and introduced into each cage. Food and mice were weighed daily for 5 days, repeated the following week for 5 days, for a total of 10 days. The food intake was recorded and normalized over the sum of all mouse weights for each cage per day.

2.6
Sanger sequencing
PCR reactions using DNA or cDNA as template were set up using FastPfu (CivicBiosciences, AP231) as per the manufacturer's recommendations in final volume of 50uL, using the same specific primers to detect Col6a3 knockout as mentioned in the genotyping PCR sub section (Col6a3_F1 and Col6a3_R1) under the following cycling conditions: 95℃, 30 s [(95℃, 30 s, 55℃, 30 s, 68℃, 30 s) × 35], 68℃, 5 min. Sample preparation. For the sample preparation, 20 mg of each skin samples from

The supernatant was aliquoted into low protein binding tubes and the concentration of the protein content within each sample was quantified using the BCA method as per the manufacturer's instructions. Subsequently, 50 μg of the protein was aliquoted into new low binding tubes and the total volume per sample was adjusted to 50 μL using the same lysis buffer. DTT (5 mmol/L final concentration) was added, and the samples were incubated (30 min; r.t.), then (2 min; 95℃). HCO
3

The proteolytic activity of the enzyme was stopped by using trifluoroacetic acid (0.2% final concentration) and the samples were desalted using 100 μL pipet tips containing a C18 resin (Pierce). Solvents were removed and the concentrated peptides were resuspended in formic acid (FA) (1%) and quantified by NanoDrop at 205 nm.
Liquid chromatography–Tandem Mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS). Cleaned peptides (500 ng) were injected into an HPLC system (nanoElute, Bruker Daltonics) equipped with a trap column (Acclaim PepMap100 C18, 0.3 mm id × 5 mm, Dionex Corporation) and an analytical C18 column (1.9 μm beads, 75 μm x 25 cm, PepSep). Peptides were separated over 2 h using a linear gradient of 5%–37% acetonitrile in 0.1% FA at a flow rate of 400 nL/min and injected into a TimsTOF Pro ion mobility mass spectrometer equipped with a Captive Spray nano electrospray source (Bruker Daltonics). The target intensity was set to 20 000, with an intensity threshold of 2500. Sample processing was acquired using diaPASEF mode. Briefly, for each single TIMS (100 ms) in diaPASEF mode, we used 1 mobility window consisting of 27 mass steps (m/z between 114 and 1414 with a mass width of 50 Da) per cycle (1.27 seconds duty cycle) and collision energy of 42.0 eV. These steps cover the diagonal scan line for +2 and + 3 charged peptides in the m/z‐ion mobility plane.

DISCUSSION
Our study used a CRISPR‐based knockout approach to abolish
Col6a3
expression in mice by successfully targeting
Col6a3
exon 3. The resulting
Col6a3
d3/d3
mice do not produce COL6A3 protein and display a gross small size phenotype, and a muscle phenotype at the histological level consistent with collagen VI‐related myopathies. However, it is unclear why
Col6a3
d3/d3
mice still express Col6a3 mRNA transcript. Although not reaching statistical significance, we observed a trend toward lower
Col6a3
mRNA level on average in mutant mice, which may reflect some degree of nonsense‐mediated decay. We cannot rule out that the
Col6a3
d3/d3
mice still retain some protein expression capacity at the
Col6a3
gene locus in a tissue‐specific and/or context‐dependent manner.
Several mouse models inactivating the
Col6a1
,
Col6a2
or
Col6a3
genes were developed to mimic the cardinal features of collagen VI‐related myopathies, such as muscle weaknesses (see Table
2
). Muscle weakness can be evaluated by behavioral tests (e.g. grip strength, inverted screen). Myopathies can be determined by estimating the muscle mass using imaging, and by histological evaluation (internally nucleated muscle cells, fiber diameter range).
24
So far, the resulting muscle phenotype of collagen VI mouse models is much milder than the associated human diseases.
2
Inactivating the
Col6a1
gene in mice led to an absence of collagen type VI in tissues and showed muscle fiber necrosis, muscle fiber diameter variation, and a loss of contractile strength.
9
,
25
The
Col6a3
hm/hm
mice exhibit a decreased organ mass compared to wild‐type (WT) mice and mild myopathic features. Their muscle fiber sizes varied greatly; they also showed internalized nucleation, fibrosis, and a decrease in contractile force.
11
The
Col6a3
d16/d16
model shows a variation in muscle fiber diameter, internalized nucleation, and decreased muscular contractile strength. Here, the muscles of
Col6a3
d3/d3
mice have an internalized nucleation, but the muscle fiber diameter has not significantly changed. We also confirmed the presence of muscle fibrosis in the
Col6a3
d3/d3
model, as observed in
Col6a1
25
and
Col6a2
26
mice models. Among the publications that report histological analysis of muscle, the most robust feature consistent with a myopathy phenotype, regardless of the collagen VI mouse model tested, seems to be internally nucleated muscle cells in skeletal muscle.

CONCLUSION
We present the development, validation and preliminary phenotypic characterization of a novel CRISPR‐based knockout mouse model targeting
Col6a3
exon 3 (
Col6a3
d3/d3
). In this mouse model,
Col6a3
mRNA is still expressed at a similar level to wild‐type littermates, although the expected protein is undetectable. Histological analysis of
Col6a3
d3/d3
quadriceps revealed an abnormally high frequency of muscle cells with internally nucleated muscle cells, consistent with a myopathy phenotype. Interestingly,
Col6a3
d3/d3
mice are smaller in size with their fat, muscle, and bone to body ratios remaining proportional compared to wild‐type littermates.
