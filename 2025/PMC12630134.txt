Introduction
Nucleic acid-binding proteins (NABPs), including DNA-binding proteins (DBPs) and RNA-binding proteins (RBPs), govern cellular identity by regulating gene expression at the levels of replication, transcription, and translation [
1
–
3
]. Since they play a pivotal role in cellular regulation, alterations in NABP composition and expression have been implicated in a variety of human diseases [
4
]. Inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD), such as ulcerative colitis, are characterized by chronic inflammation, immune dysregulation in the gut, and impaired epithelial barrier integrity [
5
]. Influenced by environmental factors and genetic susceptibility, aberrant interactions between the microbiome and the host immune system contribute to IBD development. Recent studies indicate that shared mechanisms of dysregulated inflammation share specific alterations in NABPs and similar epigenetic modifications [
6
–
8
]. For example, transcription factors (TFs) are closely associated with the pathogenesis of ulcerative colitis and have been investigated as targeted therapies [
9
,
10
]. Benoit
et al.
demonstrated that polycomb repressive complexes maintain transcriptional repression in intestinal epithelial stem cells by facilitating histone modifications such as H3K27 trimethylation via PRC2, leading to a more compact chromatin state [
11
]. Liu
et al.
reported that the enhancer of zeste homolog 2 (EZH2) integrates the multifaceted effects of TNF-α signaling to promote inflammation and apoptosis in colitis [
12
]. However, the comprehensive role of the NABPome in colitis remains largely unexplored. A detailed understanding of the NABPome is essential for elucidating IBD mechanisms and discovering new drug targets.
To date, several methods have been developed to enrich NABPs. These include orthogonal organic phase separation (OOPS) [
13
,
14
], phenol-toluol extraction (PTex) [
15
], and protein-crosslinked RNA purification (XRNAX) [
16
], which uses guanidinium thiocyanate-phenol-chloroform phase separation to profile RBPs. In addition, the total RNA-associated protein purification method [
17
] was developed to isolate RNA-associated proteins via exploiting the strong affinity between RNA and silica matrices. For example, Ding
et al.
designed transcription factor response elements as affinity reagents to capture TFs from cells and mouse tissue lysates [
18
]. More recently, we developed a selective and powerful tool, the titanium ion immobilized metal affinity chromatography (Ti
4+
-IMAC) capture method, enabling comprehensive and dynamic characterization of the NABPome at cellular and tissue levels [
19
,
20
]. Using formaldehyde crosslinking and organic solvent purification, the global NABPome was identified with high-resolution mass spectrometry (MS). Although these approaches have advanced NABP research, they remain limited in detecting dynamic and low-abundance NABP, such as TFs, which play key regulatory roles despite their low abundance.
To address these limitations, more advanced approaches are required. In high-throughput proteomics, two major mass spectrometry-based acquisition strategies are widely used: data-dependent acquisition (DDA) and data-independent acquisition (DIA) [
21
]. DDA selects the top N precursor ions for MS/MS analysis, which favors high-abundance proteins and is less effective with complex or low-input samples [
22
]. In contrast, DIA offers a more comprehensive and unbiased solution by dividing the full m/z range into predefined precursor isolation windows, systematically fragmenting and analyzing all precursor ions in a cyclic manner [
23
]. This approach yields reproducible and accurate quantification and enables robust identification of low-abundance proteins. Traditionally, DIA workflows depend on spectral libraries generated from DDA data, which often require off-column sample fractionation to achieve deep proteome coverage [
24
,
25
]. In this classical workflow, DIA analysis matches acquired MS/MS spectra against a peptide spectral library for identification and quantification [
26
]. However, extensive pre-fractionation is time-consuming and resource-intensive. To streamline this process,
in-silico
spectral prediction has recently emerged as a powerful alternative, enabling computationally generated spectral libraries without experimental DDA data [
27
–
30
]. A variety of advanced software solutions are now available, including OpenSWATH, Skyline, MaxQuant, Spectronaut, and DIA-NN [
25
,
30
,
31
]. Since the choice of DIA analysis software strongly influences NABP identification and quantification, benchmarking is essential to determine the most suitable pipeline for NABPome analysis.
In this study, we developed a two-dimensional NABP-DIA method that combines NABP enrichment with DIA-based proteomics to enable rapid, accurate, and in-depth analysis of the NABPome in colitis. First, we applied the affinity chromatography-based Ti
4+
-IMAC approach to selectively enrich NABPs from mouse spleen samples for strategy development. Second, we evaluated the performance of typical DIA analysis software (DIA-NN, SWATH™, and Skyline), and the comparative analysis showed that DIA-NN software, when combined with an experimental spectral library, identified significantly more NABPs with greater quantification accuracy and specificity. Third, using the optimized method, we generated comprehensive NABPome maps of the immune system using a mouse model of colitis. Our results revealed that nucleic acid-protein interactions were significantly altered in the spleen but not in the thymus. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) further demonstrated that epigenetic regulation of gene expression and histone modification were markedly dysregulated in the spleen of colitis mice. In addition, we profiled dynamic post-translational modifications (PTMs) of histones, identifying distinct patterns of mono-, di-, and trimethylation on histones H2B, H3, and H4 in colitis mice. Further validation indicated that epigenetic histone modulation contributes to the amelioration of IBD through inhibition of histone methylation.
Materials and methods
Reagents
IMAC materials were obtained from J&K Scientific Ltd. (Beijing, China). Ti
4+
-IMAC beads were prepared as previously described. Dithiothreitol (DTT), iodoacetamide (IAA), lipopolysaccharides (LPS), and other chemical reagents were purchased from Sigma (St. Louis, MO, USA). Protease inhibitor cocktails (Roche, 04 693 132 001) were purchased from Roche (Mannheim, Germany). Trypsin was obtained from Promega (Madison, WI, USA). DNase I, RNase A, and cell culture media (DMEM and RPMI 1640) were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Rockford, IL, USA).
Biological resources
Male C57BL/6 mice (8–12 weeks old) were purchased from the Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Company (China). Mice were then housed under a 12 h light/dark cycle with food and water provided ad libitum. Animals were randomly assigned to a control group (
n
= 7) or a DSS group (
n
= 7). Mice in the DSS group received dextran sulfate sodium (DSS) by drinking a 5% DSS solution freely for 7 days. All mice were sacrificed by cervical dislocation, and the thymus, spleen, and colon were collected. All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, and all experimental procedures reported here were approved by the Biological Research Ethics Committee of Shanghai Jiao Tong University (Approval No. A2024167-002).
NABP isolation and digestion
We first isolated NABP using a protocol previously reported by our lab that involves Ti
4+
-IMAC capture [
19
]. Briefly, the spleen and thymus were aseptically transferred into sterile culture dishes containing 5 mL of complete DMEM medium. Tissue was gently dissociated using a sterile syringe plunger by repetitive circular grinding. The resulting cell suspension was passed through a 70 μm strainer and centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min at 4°C. Red blood cells were depleted with lysis reagent (Thermo Fisher). Single-cell suspensions (5 × 10
6
) were crosslinked with formaldehyde (1% v/v, 10 mL) for 10 min at 37°C. Cells were lysed with TRIzol (1 mL, 5 min), followed by phase separation initiated by chloroform (200 μL) with vigorous vortexing (15 s) and isopropanol precipitation (10 min). Complexes were resuspended in 0.1% SDS (100 μL) with protease inhibitor and RNase inhibitor, incubated at 55°C for 20 min, then finally sonicated for 2 min (250 W, 5 s ON/10 s OFF cycles). Subsequently, crude nucleic acid-protein complexes were enriched by incubation with Ti
4+
-IMAC beads for 1 h. To remove nonspecific binding proteins, pellets were washed twice with 500 μL of high-salt detergent buffer (200 mM NaCl, 0.1% TFA, 0.1% NP-40, 10 mM EDTA) for 20 min. Enriched nucleic acid-protein complexes were eluted from Ti
4+
-IMAC using 10% NH
3
·H
2
O with vortexing and ultrasonication, and crosslinks were reversed by incubation at 65°C for 6 h.
The enriched NABPs were digested into peptides using the filter-aided sample preparation protocol [
32
], which is advantageous for efficient detergent removal prior to MS. Briefly, NABPs were reduced with DTT at 60°C for 40 min and alkylated with IAA in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. Protein solutions were transferred to ultrafiltration tubes (10 kDa cutoff) and centrifuged at 12 000 g at 4°C for 20 min. Samples were washed twice with 6 M urea and 50 mM NH
4
HCO
3
, then digested with trypsin for 16 h at 37°C. Peptides were dried and stored at −80°C for subsequent experiments.
High-pH peptide fractionation
For peptide fractionation, 200 μg of peptides from each sample were pooled. Ammonium formate (final concentration 20 mM) was added before fractionation by high-pH reverse-phase chromatography. Peptides were fractionated on an Agilent 1290 Infinity HPLC system (Agilent) using a Gemini C18 column (100 × 1.0 mm, 3 μm, 110 Å, Phenomenex, USA) with a 60 min linear gradient from 0% to 35% (v/v) acetonitrile in 20 mM ammonium formate (pH = 10) at a flow rate of 0.1 mL/min. Elution was monitored by UV detection at 254 nm. A total of 48 fractions were collected and pooled into 12 final fractions per sample, as described by Batth
et al.
[
33
].
DDA and DIA data acquisition
NABP peptides were analyzed on a TripleTOF 5600 mass spectrometer (AB Sciex, USA) interfaced with an Eksigent MicroLC 400 system. Peptides were first loaded onto a trap column (10 × 0.3 mm, 5 μm, ChromXP C18CL, 120 Å) and then separated on an analytical column (150 × 0.3 mm, 3 μm, ChromXP C18CL, 120 Å). The mobile phase contained solvent A (0.1% formic acid in water) and solvent B (0.1% formic acid in 95% acetonitrile). A flow rate of 5 μL/min was used with a 120 min gradient: 5% B (0–5 min), 5%–22% B (6–95 min), 22%–32% B (96–110 min), 32%–90% B (111–113 min), and 90% B (114–120 min). The MS parameters for DIA mode were: ion source spray voltage, 2.5 kV; nitrogen pressure, 30 psi; spray pressure, 15 psi; and spray interface temperature, 150°C. MS scans were acquired at 350–1250 Da with an accumulation time of 0.25 s. MS/MS scans were performed at 100–1500 Da with an accumulation time of 0.05 s in high-sensitivity mode. For DIA mode, a 0.05 s MS scan (m/z 350–1250) was followed by 80 variable Q1 windows (
Supplementary Fig. S1
), each with a 3 ms accumulation time and collision energy of 5 eV. Precursor isolation windows were defined using the SWATH Variable Window Calculator v1.1 (AB Sciex) based on precursor m/z density from DDA spectra.
Library generation for DIA proteomics benchmark analysis
Spectral libraries were generated using four computational platforms: FragPipe [
34
], MaxQuant, Skyline, and ProteinPilot. FragPipe used EasyPQP for library generation with retention time calibration based on iRTs. MaxQuant generated its library from DDA search results using
msms.txt, evidence.txt
, and
peptides.txt
files, with stringent parameters including a 20 ppm peptide mass tolerance and a 0.01 false discovery rate (FDR) for PSMs and proteins. Skyline built its Blib library using
interact.pep.xml
files from FragPipe, with precursor charges set to 2–6 and a 10 ppm mass accuracy, and employed the optimized
protein.fas
file from Philosopher. ProteinPilot conducted DDA searches with the Paragon algorithm against the mouse UniProtKB database, specifying trypsin digestion, iodoacetamide alkylation, and a detected protein threshold [Conf] > 10%. Detailed protocols and software settings are provided in the Supporting Information.
DIA data processing
DIA-NN (v23.1), SWATH™ (v4.5), and Skyline (v23.1) software were used to analyze the DIA data. DIA-NN, developed by Demichev
et al.
[
30
] was applied for both library-free and library-based SWATH data processing. Skyline analyses [
35
] followed the tutorials “Analysis of DIA/SWATH data” and “Advanced Peak Picking Models.” SWATH™ analyses were performed using PeakView software (v2.0, AB Sciex). Detailed protocols and software settings are provided in the Supporting Information.
Histone extraction and PTM analysis
Single cells were harvested from mouse spleens and resuspended in 1 mL nuclei isolation buffer (20 mM HEPES, 10 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl₂, 0.1% NP-40, and freshly added 1 × protease inhibitor cocktail), then rotated for 10 min at 4°C to solubilize the plasma membrane. Nuclei were collected by centrifugation at 1500 g for 10 min at 4°C, washed once with 0.5 mL nuclei isolation buffer and once with 0.5 mL PBS, each followed by centrifugation at 1500 g for 10 min at 4°C. The final pellet was resuspended in 0.1 mL 0.2 M HCl and incubated for 30 min at 4°C to extract histones. After centrifugation at 16 000 g for 10 min at 4°C, the histone-containing supernatant was collected and neutralized with 20 μL 1 M Tris-HCl. Protein concentration was determined by a BCA assay. Around 25 μg of the histone preparation was separated on 4%–20% Tris-glycine SDS-PAGE gels and visualized using a colloidal blue staining kit. Bands corresponding to histones were excised and subjected to in-gel digestion as previously described [
36
]. Briefly, gel bands were cut into small pieces and destained through sequential dehydration in 50% acetonitrile and 100% H
2
O. Gel pieces were chemically reduced with DTT for 1 h at 37°C and alkylated with IAA in the dark for 40 min. To obtain ArgC-like digestion, the gels were derivatized with propionic anhydride and digested with trypsin (100 ng/μL in 50 mM NH
4
HCO
3
) overnight at 37°C. The resulting peptides were desalted and concentrated using Ziptip C18 columns. Four biological replicates (
n
= 4) were performed for histone PTM analysis.
Raw MS data were analyzed using MaxQuant software (v2.4.10) [
37
] against the mouse protein database (release 2021_12_26, 27 414 entries). The peptide search focused on lysine methylation and acetylation, with variable modifications of lysine mono-methylation (+14.016 Da), lysine di-methylation (+28.031 Da), lysine tri-methylation (+42.046 Da), and lysine acetylation (+42.010 Da). The FDR cutoff, using a target-decoy strategy, was set at 1% for both proteins and peptides.
Cell culture and inflammatory cell model construction
We first cultured the RAW 264.7 mouse mononuclear macrophage cell line in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. Cells were incubated at 37°C in 5% CO
2
and passaged at 80% confluence. RAW 264.7 cells were seeded in six-well plates and allowed to adhere for 12 h. For the inflammatory model group, cells were treated with LPS at a final concentration of 1 μg/mL for 24 h. For the experimental group, the EHMT2 inhibitor (EHMT2-IN-2, MedChemExpress, #HY-111904) was added 0.5 h before LPS treatment at a final concentration of 50 nM.
Induction of intestinal inflammation and treatments
DSS colitis was induced by adding 3% DSS to drinking water for 7 days, followed by normal drinking water for the remaining days. For the therapeutic experiment, mice were i.v. injected with EHMT2 inhibitor every 3 days, starting from day 0 (DSS induction) until they were euthanized. When we monitored the survival rate, mice received 2.5% DSS to induce intestinal inflammation, and the natural death time of mice in the indicated groups within 15 days was recorded.
Quantitative PCR
Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed in accordance with the MIQE guidelines [
38
]. Total RNA was extracted from mouse colon tissue or RAW 264.7 cells using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration and purity (A260/A280 and A260/A230 ratios) were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using HiScript II Q RT SuperMix (Vazyme, R233-01). Primers were designed with the PrimerBank database (
https://pga.mgh.harvard.edu/primerbank/
), and detailed sequences are listed in
Supplementary Table S1
. Amplicon sizes ranged from 90 to 150 bp. qPCR reactions were performed in 20 μL volumes containing 10 μL SYBR Green Master Mix (YEASEN, #11 185), 0.5 μL each of forward and reverse primers (10 μM), 2 μL cDNA template, and 7 μL nuclease-free water. Reactions were run in six technical replicates for each sample on a qTOWER3 touch real-time PCR thermal cycler (Analytik Jena AG, Germany).
GAPDH
served as the internal control, and relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2(−ΔΔCt) method. No-template controls confirmed the absence of contamination and genomic DNA.
Western blot analysis
Enriched histones from tissue and cell samples were quantified using the BCA method. For SDS-PAGE, 10 μg of protein was loaded per lane and transferred to PVDF (polyvinylidene fluoride) membranes (Pall Life Sciences, India). PVDF membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies: anti-H3 (1:1000; ab1791, Abcam), Anti-EHMT1 (1:1000; 118 542, Absin), Anti-EHMT2 (1:1000; 3306T, CST), Anti- H3K9me3 (1:1000; 13969T, CST), anti-H3K27me3 (1:1000; 9733S, CST), and anti-actin (1:1000; ab8226, Abcam). After washing with 0.1% Tween in PBS, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were detected using ECL™ Western Blotting Detection Reagents (GE Healthcare) and visualized on a Chemidoc imaging system (Bio-Rad). Band intensity was quantified using NIH ImageJ software (v1.49) [
39
].
Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
Proteins in cell culture supernatants were quantified using human IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α ELISA kits (eBioscience, Thermo Scientific), following the manufacturer’s instructions (
https://www.thermofisher.cn/cn/zh/elisa/product
).
Statistical analysis
As reported above, statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 8.0. Unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests were applied for pairwise comparisons, and one-way ANOVA was used for multi-group analyses. Data are presented as mean ± SEM unless otherwise indicated. Cut-offs for proteomics were set at
P
< 0.05 and |log₂ fold change| > 1 for significance in differential expression. Replicates are described in the relevant subsections (biological replicates
n
= 4 for histone PTM profiling;
n
= 6–8 per group for
in vivo
DSS experiments).
Results
NABP identification using different DIA software packages
The Ti
4+
-IMAC-based NABP capture strategy involves single-cell preparation and formaldehyde crosslinking, followed by TRIzol lysis and Ti
4+
-IMAC capture, which relies on the specific affinity between Ti
4+
and phosphate groups on nucleic acids [
19
]. We hypothesized that accurate and comprehensive NABPome profiling could be achieved by combining the Ti
4+
-IMAC capture strategy with DIA. The overall sample preparation and analysis workflow is shown in Fig.
1
. Briefly, total protein, including enriched NABPs from thymus and spleen, was then digested with trypsin. A portion of the peptides was fractionated using high-pH separation and analyzed by DDA to construct a comprehensive DDA library for both total protein and enriched NABP samples. The remaining peptides were subjected to DIA acquisition.
Figure 1.
Workflow of the NABP-DIA benchmarking experiment. Single cells were isolated from spleen and thymus, and NABPs were then captured using Ti

-IMAC enrichment. Total protein and enriched NABPs from spleen and thymus were digested with trypsin; peptides were pooled, fractionated, and acquired by DDA to construct total protein and NABP enrichment DDA libraries. Digested peptides were also acquired in DIA mode. Four software-specific DDA-dependent libraries were generated using FragPipe, Skyline, ProteinPilot, and MaxDIA. DIA data were analyzed with three software platforms: DIA-NN, Skyline, and SWATH™.
Since the size, quality, and composition of a spectral library strongly affect DIA analysis, we generated four software-specific DDA libraries to ensure compatibility and performance across platforms. The FragPipe experimental DDA library comprised 72 370 peptide precursors mapped to 6323 proteins, exceeding the coverage of the other three libraries (Fig.
2A
and
B
). In addition, DIA-NN can generate an
in-silico
library in library-free mode, eliminating the need for an experimental DDA library. The
in-silico
library built from the mouse protein sequence database contained 3 716 437 peptide entries mapped to 17 042 proteins (Fig.
2C
).

Next, DIA data were analyzed using DIA-NN, Skyline, and SWATH™ software with a DDA library generated by FragPipe. In addition, DIA-NN was also applied in library-free mode using an in-silico library. These results showed that DIA-NN gave the highest number of proteins (2,984) with the experimental DDA library generated by FragPipe (Fig.
2D
and

2F
and

2I
). Collectively, these results demonstrate that DIA-NN combined with the experimental DDA library (NABP-DIA) achieved the best performance in both identification depth and quantitative specificity.
NABP-DIA enables in-depth identification of NABPs
Using both DIA (DIA-NN with the DDA library generated by FragPipe) and DDA methods, we analyzed the total proteome and NABPome of mouse thymus and spleen. Different categories of the NABPome, including RBP, DBP, and TF, defined by GO annotations [
40
], were assessed to evaluate the efficacy of this method. First, we noted that the number of reviewed RBPs, DBPs, and TFs present in the mouse protein database were 1047, 1982, and 490, respectively. Using total proteins from thymus and spleen for DDA library construction, we identified 783 RBPs, 631 DBPs, and 232 TFs (Fig.
3A
,
B
). When enriched NABPs were used for DDA library construction, the number of identifications increased to 890 RBPs, 1023 DBPs, and 292 TFs (Fig.
3A
and
B
). Using the NABP enrichment DDA library, single-shot DIA identified 634 RBPs, 468 DBPs, and 165 TFs in the thymus, and 581 RBPs, 408 DBPs, and 145 TFs in the spleen. These numbers were nearly double those detected by DDA (Fig.
3E
). Protein quantification with the NABP-DIA method was also more reproducible, with median CVs of 3.7% compared to 16.8% for DDA (
Supplementary Fig. S2A
). Differences were even more pronounced at the precursor level (
Supplementary Fig. S2B
). In addition, annotated NABP showed markedly fewer missing values across replicates, improving both quantification precision and data completeness (
Supplementary Fig. S2C
). Using the total protein DDA library, single-shot DIA identified only 383 RBPs, 342 DBPs, and 102 TFs in the thymus, and 316 RBPs, 261 DBPs, and 95 TFs in the spleen, far fewer than identified by NABP-DIA (Fig.
3A
and
B
). Dynamic range plots displayed GO-annotated NABP expression across total protein and NABP-enriched datasets (
Supplementary Fig. S2D
), highlighting specific enrichment of NABPs relative to whole-proteome analysis.

To further evaluate the NABP-DIA method, we analyzed the abundance of TF families annotated in the DNA-binding domain database. Components of TF families such as zinc finger Cys2His2 (ZF-C2H2), high mobility group (HMG), v-myb avian myeloblastosis viral oncogene homolog (

42

Immunological activation of the spleen in colitic mice
Recent studies have shown that inflammation can alter chromatin organization and induce epigenetic modifications, thereby modifying nucleic acid-protein interactions [
6
]. We applied NABP-DIA to study dynamic changes in NABP during IBD. DSS-induced colitis is a widely used mouse model that recapitulates the clinical pathology of IBD [
44
]. Accordingly, a well-established DSS-induced model that exhibited significant body weight loss compared with controls was used in this study (Fig.
4A
). Consistent with previous reports [
12
], mice in the DSS group displayed shorter colons and heavier spleens than controls (Fig.
4B
–
D
). Histopathologic examination further revealed widespread damage in the distal-to-middle colon, with extensive epithelial denudation and more ulcerations relative to control mice (Fig.
4E
). The mRNA expression levels of proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines, including CXCL2, IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, were significantly elevated in the intestine of the DSS group (Fig.
4F
).

Unique inflammatory mechanisms of colitis in the mouse spleen
To test our hypothesis, we performed total proteome and NABPome analyses of the thymus and spleen from control and DSS groups using the NABP-DIA method (
Supplementary Table S1
). A PCA showed clear differences between the NABPome and the total proteome (Fig.

and

). In the spleen, control and DSS groups were well separated (Fig.

), whereas in the thymus, only minor differentiation was observed (Fig.
5B
). In particular, compared with the control group, 118 NABPs were differentially expressed in the spleen of the DSS group (
P
< 0.05, |log2(fold change)| > 1), including 43 significantly upregulated and 75 significantly downregulated NABPs (Fig.

). Notably, proteins involved in DNA synthesis and repair, including ribonucleotide reductase subunit M1 (Rrm1), polymerase δ-interacting protein 2 (Poldip2), and WRN helicase-interacting protein 1 (Wrnip1), were significantly altered. In contrast, in the thymus, only 25 NABPs were differentially expressed, of which 10 were upregulated and 15 downregulated (Fig.

). We also observed stronger correlations between control and DSS groups in the thymus than in the spleen (Pearson

= 0.56 for spleen,

and

). Similar differences were found at the relative chromatin-binding and total proteome levels (

and B).

Dynamics of histone PTMs in mouse spleen during colitis
The overrepresentation of epigenetic regulation, particularly histone methylation, in the NABP-DIA data prompted us to investigate histone PTMs that influence DNA-templated processes at the molecular level. We first analyzed potential enzymes regulating histone methylation in the NABP-DIA dataset and found that expression levels of several enzymes were significantly altered (Fig. 6A;
Supplementary Table S2
). Enzymes involved in H3K9/H3K27 methylation, including EHMT1 (fold change = 14.82), EHMT2 (fold change = 2.20), EZH2 (fold change = 2.48), and SUZ12 (fold change = 2.96), were markedly upregulated.
To validate these observations at the protein modification level, we examined histone methylation dynamics in the DSS colitis model. Single cells from the control and DSS groups were prepared, and histones were isolated by acidic extraction and separated via SDS-PAGE. Histone PTMs were then profiled through ArgC-like in-gel digestion prior to MS analysis (Fig.
6B
). We identified 25 differential histone modifications on 15 peptides derived from histones H2A, H2B, H3, and H4, and quantified their abundances in control and DSS groups (Fig.
6C
;
Supplementary Table S3
). Mice in the DSS group exhibited distinct histone PTM patterns, including dysregulation of mono-, di-, and trimethylation on H2B, H3, and H4. Histone methylation is a dynamic process in which methyl groups are added or removed by specific enzymes [
48
]. Notably, trimethylation of lysine 27 on histone H3 (H3K27me3), a marker of poised or silenced promoters that strongly influences tumor progression and malignancy [
49
,
50
], was significantly enriched in the DSS group. We also observed acetylation modifications of histones. For example, hyperacetylation of the C-terminal tail of histone H2B (H2BK120Ac), a PTM associated with transcriptional activation [
51
] was enriched in DSS-induced mice. Together, these histone PTM profiles revealed abnormal levels of histone methylation in the spleen of colitis mice, consistent with our NABP-DIA proteome data.

Epigenetic modulation inhibiting EHMT delays the onset of colitis
Previous studies have identified EHMT2 as a potential therapeutic target in colorectal cancer, where it alters cytokine expression to reprogram the immunosuppressive microenvironment of microsatellite-stable tumors into an immunomodulatory one, thereby activating T cell-mediated cytotoxicity and enhancing responsiveness to anti-PD-1 therapy [
52
]. Based on these findings, we hypothesized that EHMT inhibition could reduce inflammatory cytokine expression and thereby relieve inflammation by decreasing histone methylation. To test this, we established an LPS-induced RAW 264.7 cell model. LPS stimulation significantly increased H3K27me3 levels and elevated inflammatory mediators, including TNF-α, IL-1β, CXCL2, and IL-6. Treatment with an EHMT inhibitor markedly suppressed both H3K27me3 expression and inflammatory mediator levels at the protein and mRNA levels in the LPS-induced RAW 264.7 model (Fig.
7A
and
B
;
Supplementary Fig. S6A
and B ).

Next, we examined the effect of targeting EHMT methyltransferase activity on colitis development
in vivo
. To do so, C57BL/6 mice were treated with DSS and administered an EHMT inhibitor (EHMTi) intravenously at different time points during disease progression (Fig.
7C
). Overall, we found that preventive EHMTi treatment reduced DSS-induced weight loss (Fig.
7D
). The disease activity index (DAI), a composite measure of colitis severity, was also decreased with EHMTi treatment (Fig.
7E
). Furthermore, EHMTi-treated mice displayed significantly longer colons than DSS-only controls (Fig.
7F
). Histological analysis of DSS-treated colitic mice revealed severe pathology, including disrupted crypt architecture and extensive inflammatory cell infiltration. In contrast, EHMTi treatment preserved mucosal architecture, reduced inflammation, and limited crypt loss (Fig.
7G
). At a DSS concentration of 2.5%, ∼75% of vehicle-treated mice died from acute colitis, whereas ∼50% of EHMTi-treated mice survived (Fig.
7H
).
Although the improvement in body weight and DAI index after EHMT inhibitor treatment was modest, we observed a significant reduction of inflammatory cytokine expression in both intestine and spleen tissues, indicating pharmacodynamic activity at the molecular level (Fig.
7I
). Notably, our data revealed a tissue-specific response of histone methylation: in the spleen, DSS-induced colitis strongly upregulated EHMT1/2 expression along with their associated histone marks (H3K27me3 and H3K9me3), both of which were significantly reduced upon EHMT inhibition. In contrast, in the colon, baseline EHMT1/2 expression was very low and H3K27me3 levels were not substantially altered by treatment (Fig.
7J
and
K
;
Supplementary Fig. S6C
–F). Overall, these findings suggest that EHMTi treatment mitigates the onset and clinical severity of DSS-induced colitis.
Discussion
Previous studies have established methods to capture, identify, and quantify NABP to investigate their functions in biological processes at both cellular and tissue levels [
19
,
20
,
53
,
54
]. However, when combined with DDA-based proteomics, these approaches show limited accuracy and depth, often failing to detect low-abundance proteins such as TFs (Fig.
3C
and
D
). Affinity-based capture technology has gained prominence for its ability to selectively isolate and identify target components in complex matrices [
55
–
57
]. In this study, we developed a two-dimensional NABP-DIA method that integrates affinity chromatography-based NABP enrichment with an optimized DIA strategy to achieve in-depth identification and high-precision quantification of NABP.
We believe that our approach has several distinct advantages relative to other recent NABP enrichment methodologies. First, given the wide range of software tools available for DIA analysis [
58
], we benchmarked different methods to determine the most effective software-library combination for NABP profiling. Our results showed that DIA-NN achieved more protein identifications and higher quantitative accuracy when used with DDA-dependent libraries generated by the Ti
4+
-IMAC capture method. Notably, relative to the large mouse database from Guo
et al.
, our NABP-enriched DDA library provided deeper coverage of RBPs, DBPs, and TFs. Using DIA-NN, the number of NABP identified was 2.1-fold higher than with the traditional method (from 597 to 1267 in the thymus and from 545 to 1134 in the spleen), and quantitative reproducibility improved markedly (median CV decreased from 16.84% to 6.01%). Consistent with our findings, Shui
et al.
[
58
] benchmarked four widely used software packages (DIA-NN, Spectronaut, MaxDIA, and Skyline) combined with different spectral libraries for global proteome analysis, and reported that DIA-NN achieved the most reproducible total protein quantification measurements (median CVs of 4.9%–11.8%). The superior performance of DIA-NN in in-depth NABPome profiling is likely attributable to its advanced peak selection algorithm and scoring model based on a fully connected neural network [
30
]. Taken together, we recommend DIA-NN with an experimental DDA library as the optimal NABP-DIA pipeline.
Second, we assessed the performance and robustness of NABP-DIA in analyzing the NABPome of immune organs in colitis mice. Moreover, by comparing NABPome and proteome data from spleen and thymus in control and disease groups, we found that colitis-induced immune dysregulation significantly altered nucleic acid-protein interactions in the spleen but not in the thymus. Ulcerative colitis is one of the most common chronic inflammatory diseases and is associated with an elevated risk of colorectal cancer [
59
,
60
]. Although intestinal dysbiosis, epithelial barrier dysfunction, and immune imbalance are known contributors to ulcerative colitis, its pathogenic mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Here, we demonstrate that colitis affects nucleic acid-protein interactions in the spleen, consistent with previous reports showing that patients with ulcerative colitis often exhibit splenomegaly [
61
].
Third, our results indicate that epigenetic histone modulators, especially histone methyltransferases, may represent potential therapeutic targets for inflammation. By integrating NABP data with histone post-translational modification (PTM) analysis, we observed that both histone methyltransferases (including EHMT1, EHMT2, EZH2, and SUZ12) and histone methylation marks such as H3K9me1, H3K27me3, and H3K36me1 were significantly elevated in colitic mice. DSS-induced upregulation of EHMT2 was accompanied by changes in H3K27me3 and H3K9me3, consistent with its role in chromatin remodeling and transcriptional regulation of inflammatory genes under immune-inflammatory conditions. In vitro, an LPS-induced RAW 264.7 inflammation model demonstrated that inhibiting EHMT activity reduced H3K27me3 levels and suppressed expression of key proinflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and CXCL2, at both mRNA and protein levels. In vivo, EHMT inhibition partially alleviated DSS-induced colitis, yielding modest improvements in body weight and disease activity index, along with a clear reduction of inflammatory cytokines in the spleen and intestinal tissues.
Recent studies have reported that dysregulated histone modifications can be closely linked to gastrointestinal disorders [
62
,
63
]. Likewise, aberrant histone modifications have been identified in colonic mucosa from IBD patients and are thought to contribute to persistent intestinal inflammation [
64
], further supporting our observations. It should be noted, however, that DSS-induced colitis primarily activates inflammatory responses through epithelial barrier disruption, innate immune signaling pathways (e.g. TLR4/NF-κB, NLRP3, MAPK), and imbalance of the Th17/Treg axis [
65
,
66
]. Histone methyltransferases such as EHMT2 may therefore act as epigenetic regulators within the immune system in this model. Together, our findings highlight that histone-modifying enzymes contribute to DSS-driven inflammation, and additional functional studies are warranted to confirm their therapeutic potential.
Taken together, our results suggest that targeting histone-modifying enzymes and their associated chromatin marks could represent a promising therapeutic strategy for gastrointestinal disorders. However, further experimental validation remains necessary to further test whether this hypothesis is correct.
Conclusion
In this study, we established a robust NABP-DIA method that enables comprehensive identification and precise quantification of NABPs, including low-abundance TFs. We generated a detailed resource of proteomes and NABPome in the immune organs of colitic mice, providing a foundation for investigating novel regulatory mechanisms in colitis. Further analysis of histone modification-specific NABP revealed a critical role for epigenetic regulation in IBD. The integrated profiling of histone PTMs in colitis mice, supported by
in vitro
validation, demonstrated that histone methyltransferase inhibitors may serve as potential therapeutic strategies for IBD intervention.
