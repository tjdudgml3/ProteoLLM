Introduction
The core cellular metabolic pathways undergo reprogramming to satisfy the demands of rapid proliferation and metastasis during human carcinogenesis. Altered metabolism has consequently emerged as a hallmark of cancer, with ongoing efforts to identify and target vulnerabilities within these pathways (
1
–
4
). The tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle serves as a central hub for carbohydrate metabolism in the mitochondria. The TCA cycle is intricately intertwined with the synthesis of oncometabolites and transcriptional remodeling, collectively facilitating tumorigenesis (
5
–
13
). The essential and active role of the TCA cycle in colorectal carcinogenesis has been supported by a growing body of evidence, in which an increased turnover rate of the TCA cycle has been observed in the comparison of tumor tissues with normal tissues (
14
–
18
). Yet in-depth investigations into differentially expressed genes or neighboring pathways in the TCA cycle have failed to establish a consensus on this dysregulation. The mechanism underlying the reprogramming of this pathway during cancer progression remains poorly understood.
Colorectal cancer is the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide (
19
). Reprogrammed metabolism is a hallmark of colorectal cancer (
20
), which supports tumorigenesis through metabolic adaptations. Citrate synthase (CS) is the first rate-limiting enzyme that catalyzes the primary irreversible reaction in the TCA cycle. In this study, we discovered a previously unannotated alternatively spliced CS transcript that harbors exon 4 skipping (hereinafter referred to as the
CS-ΔEx4
variant). Focusing on the metabolic activity underscored by
CS-ΔEx4
, we found new evidence to support augmented TCA flux through metabolic reprogramming by CS-ΔEx4 in interaction with the canonical CS full-length (CS-FL) isoform. Our study provides new insights into the metabolic mechanism of colorectal cancer and a basis for therapies targeting CS-ΔEx4.
Materials and Methods
Clinical samples
A total of 132 patients with colorectal cancer who underwent colorectal surgery at the Prince of Wales Hospital (PWH) from 2001 to 2020 were enrolled in this study. Colorectal cancer tumor and adjacent normal tissue samples from patients were resected at the PWH. All studies involving human samples were conducted with written informed consent obtained from participants. The study was approved by the Joint Chinese University of Hong Kong New Territories East Cluster Clinical Research Ethics Committee (reference no. 2023.428) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.
Cell culture
All cell lines used in this study were obtained from the ATCC and incubated at 37°C with 5% CO
2
. HT-29, SW1116, SW480, HCT116, Caco-2, DLD-1, Colo205, LoVo, and SW620 are human colorectal carcinoma cell lines that are stored in liquid nitrogen in the laboratories of the Department of Surgery, Chinese University of Hong Kong. All cell lines were cultured in DMEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. #11995065), supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. #A5256701), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. #15140122). The cells were trypsinized and passaged using TrypLE Express (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. #12604021) after reaching 80% to 90% confluency.
SW620 (RRID: CVCL_0547), SW1116 (RRID: CVCL_0544), HCT116 (RRID: CVCL_0291), SW480 (RRID: CVCL_0546), and 293FT (RRID: CVCL_0045) cells were tested with short tandem repeat analysis to confirm identities via CLASTR 1.4.4 and tested negative for mycoplasma contamination using the Myco-Lumi Luminescent Mycoplasma Detection Kit for High Sensitivity Instrument (Beyotime, cat. #C0298M). The cells were trypsinized and passaged using TrypLE Express after reaching 80% to 90% confluency. Experiments utilized cell lines after passage >2 and <30.
The oligonucleotides used for short hairpin RNA or siRNA knockdown are listed in Supplementary Table S1.
Tissue RNA extraction
Frozen tissues weighing approximately 10 to 30 mg (wet weight) were placed in a 1.5 mL tube, and 1 mL of TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, cat. #12183555) was added to the tube. Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol Plus RNA Purification Kit (Invitrogen, cat. #12183555). Briefly, the homogenate was incubated at room temperature for 5 minutes to ensure the complete dissociation of the nucleoprotein complexes. Then, 200 μL of chloroform was added to the homogenate, and vigorous vortexing was performed for 15 seconds. The samples were centrifuged at 12,000 ×
g
for 15 minutes at 4°C. After centrifugation, approximately 600 μL of the aqueous phase containing RNA was carefully transferred to a new 1.5 mL tube. Next, a 0.7-fold volume of 100% ethanol (Merck, cat. #100983) was added and mixed thoroughly by vortexing. The mixtures were transferred to spin columns and centrifuged for 30 seconds at 12,000 ×
g
and 25°C. The columns were then washed 3 times using the following procedures, with each wash followed by a 12,000
g
centrifugation for 30 seconds: (i) 700 μL of Wash Buffer I, (ii) 500 μL of Wash Buffer II twice, and (iii) drying the membrane with no wash buffer for 1 minute. Finally, 35 μL of RNase-free H
2
O was added to elute the final RNA. RNA concentration was determined using a NanoDrop One/One
C
Microvolume UV-Vis Spectrophotometer by measuring the absorbance at 260/280 nm. RNA samples were stored in a −80°C freezer.
Reverse transcription and qRT-PCR
Total RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Perfect Real Time; TaKaRa, cat. #RR036A), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The cDNA was diluted with nuclease-free H
2
O and stored at −80°C or used directly. qRT-PCR was performed using the QuantStudio 7 Flex Real-Time PCR system to determine the expression level of the gene or isoform of interest. Power SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, cat. #4368702) was used for gene evaluation according to the manufacturer’s protocol.
ACTB
was used as an endogenous control. For the accurate measurement of
CS-ΔEx4
RNA expression levels, a TaqMan PCR assay (Applied Biosystems, cat. #4304437) with a junction-specific TaqMan probe was performed. Please refer to Supplementary Table S2 for the list of qRT-PCR primers used.
Bioinformatic pipelines for hybrid sequencing
Long-read sequencing data were processed following the IsoSeq pipeline (
https://github.com/PacificBiosciences/IsoSeq
). High-quality reads were aligned against the hg38 genome with GMAP. Then, the transcripts were collapsed by cDNA_Cupcake (
https://github.com/Magdoll/cDNA_Cupcake
). Cuffcompare was used to identify novel and canonical isoforms.
PepQuery validation
The CS-ΔEx4 isoform was validated using PepQuery2 (
21
) by searching through all available Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) proteome data via the web server (
http://pepquery.org/
). The input peptide sequence used for this analysis was TVVGQITVDMGIR, representing the shortest tryptic peptide that spans the novel splice junction of CS-ΔEx4. Peptide spectrum matches (PSM) that passed competitive filtering (
P
value < 0.01) against UniProt Swiss-Prot references were accepted as true identifications.
CPTAC data reanalysis
The CPTAC Prospective Colon dataset from the Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PDC000116; ref.
22
) was reanalyzed using the TMT10 workflow integrated within FragPipe (
https://github.com/Nesvilab/FragPipe
, RRID: SCR_022864). All processed mass spectrometry files (mzML) were analyzed against the UniProt reference sequence, along with the complete CS-ΔEx4 protein isoform sequence. Specifically, tandem mass spectrometry spectra were searched using MSFragger (
23

CS-ΔEx4
isoform. The results from the MSFragger search were subsequently processed using the Philosopher pipeline for statistical filtering and protein group assembly (
24

https://github.com/Nesvilab/TMT-Integrator
) to normalize protein abundance across TMT batches. Finally, the normalized protein abundances were subjected to differential expression analysis using Limma through FragPipe-Analyst.
Cell viability
Cell viability was assessed using the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay, which measures mitochondrial oxidation of MTT to formazan. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 2,000 cells/well in 200 μL of medium and incubated for 4 hours (day 0, if necessary), 24 hours (day 1), 48 hours (day 2), 72 hours (day 3), and 96 hours (day 4). After the desired time point, 20 μL of MTT (Invitrogen, cat. #M6494) was added to each well, and the plates were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO
2
. After 2 to 4 hours, the medium containing MTT was removed, and 200 μL of DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. #D2650) was added to each well. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a spectrophotometer. The experiment was independently repeated in triplicate.
Cell viability was also assessed using a clonogenic assay in monolayers. After Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. #11668027) transfection, the cells were seeded at a density of 1,000 to 2,000 cells per well in triplicate in a six-well plate for 10 days. Colonies were stained with a crystal violet solution for 1 hour and counted using a digital scanner. The experiment was performed independently in triplicate.
Migration
In vitro
metastatic potential was assessed using a Transwell assay. Colorectal cancer cell lines were seeded in the upper chamber of a Transwell (Corning, cat. #3422) with serum-free DMEM. The lower chamber of a 24-well plate was filled with complete DMEM. After 24 to 48 hours of incubation, the migrated cells were fixed and stained with crystal violet solution for 1 hour at room temperature. The images were captured using a microscope.
In vivo
metastatic potential was assessed using a tail vein model. SW1116 cells stably expressing CS-FL, CS-ΔEx4, or lentiviral vector (pLV) control were intravenously injected (1 × 10
6
in 200 μL) into 6-week-old female NOD/SCID gamma mice (
n
= 6 for pLV control and CS-FL groups,
n
= 7 for CS-ΔEx4 group) via the tail vein with randomized mouse selection. Mice were euthanized using pentobarbitone 6 weeks after the injection or when the humane endpoint was reached. To minimize mouse suffering, euthanasia was performed at an early stage when respiratory symptoms were observed. The lungs were resected and paraffin-embedded for further analyses. Tumor nodes and areas were manually counted under a microscope after hematoxylin and eosin staining of slides. Histopathologic evaluation was performed by blinded pathologists using hematoxylin and eosin staining.

Apoptosis
Apoptosis was assessed by FACS. After selection, 3 × 10
5
cells were plated in a six-well plate and incubated for 24 to 48 hours. Subsequently, the cells were trypsinized and harvested by centrifugation at 1,600 rpm for 5 minutes. The resulting pellets were washed twice with PBS and resuspended in 100 μL binding buffer [0.01 mol/L HEPES (pH 7.4), 0.14 mol/L NaCl, 2.5 mmol/L CaCl
2
]. Each group of cells was stained with 5 μL APC-conjugated Annexin V (BD Biosciences, cat. #550475) and 5 μL 7-aminoactinomycin D (7-AAD; BD Biosciences, cat. #559925). Following a 15-minute incubation in the dark at room temperature with shaking, the cells were diluted with 200 μL of binding buffer and analyzed by FACS within 1 hour. Unstained cells, cells stained with APC-conjugated Annexin V alone, and cells stained with 7-AAD alone served as positive controls for compensation and quadrant setup. In total, 20,000 events were recorded for each sample. Apoptosis profiles were analyzed using FlowJo software (RRID: SCR_008520). The experiments were performed in triplicate.
DNA fragmentation was detected using the terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase–mediated dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) assay with the
In Situ
Cell Death Detection Kit, Fluorescein (Roche, cat. #11684795910). Briefly, 3 × 10
5
cells per group were plated in a six-well plate following the selection process. The TUNEL assay was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The cells were counterstained with DAPI to label the nuclei. TUNEL fluorescein staining and DAPI staining (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. #DUO82040) were excited using filters with wavelengths of 470 to 495 nm and 360 to 370 nm, respectively, and emissions were collected using filters with wavelengths of 510 to 550 nm and 420 to 460 nm, respectively. Images were captured using a Nikon microscope. Fluorescence and signals were quantified using ImageJ (RRID: SCR_003070) software.
Protein extraction and protein quantification
The cells were centrifuged, and the resulting pellets were collected. Cell pellets were lysed in lysis buffer (150 mmol/L sodium chloride, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, cat. #SC-203274A; 1.0% NP-40, Sigma-Aldrich, cat. #I8896; 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, Sigma-Aldrich, cat. #D6750; 0.1% SDS, Sigma-Aldrich, cat. #L4390; 50 mmol/L Tris, pH 8.0) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche Diagnostics, cat. #11836170001). Lysates were kept on ice for 30 minutes with occasional vortexing. After 30 minutes, the lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 ×
g
for 10 minutes at 4°C, and the supernatant was transferred to new tubes. Protein concentrations were determined using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. #23225) with a BSA standard curve ranging from 0.25 to 2 mg/mL. Absorbance at 560 nm was measured in a 96-well plate for the BCA assay. The protein concentrations were normalized in each experiment when quantitative comparisons were performed.
Immunoprecipitation
For immunoprecipitation (IP) experiments, cells were lysed using Pierce IP lysis buffer [25 mmol/L Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mmol/L NaCl, 1 mmol/L EDTA, 1% NP-40, and 5% glycerol]. The lysates were extracted and quantified following the same protocol as described in the “Protein extraction and protein quantification” section. The lysates were then incubated with a primary antibody against the target protein at 4°C for 2 hours, followed by overnight incubation with Dynabeads Protein G (Invitrogen, cat. #10004D) at 4°C. The next day, the lysates were washed four times with IP lysis buffer and denatured by heating at 95°C with 2× loading buffer (0.1 mol/L Tris-HCl, 0.2 mol/L DTT, 138.5 mmol/L SDS, 3 mmol/L bromophenol blue, 2.15 mol/L glycerol). The proteins were stored at −20°C or immediately subjected to Western blot analysis.
Western blots
Ten to thirty micrograms of proteins with loading dye were loaded per lane and separated by 10% to 14% SDS-PAGE using the Mini-PROTEAN II apparatus (Bio-Rad). The proteins were then electroblotted onto equilibrated pure PVDF transfer membranes, 0.45 μm (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. #88518). Membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. After rinsing with TBS with Tween 20 buffer (20 mmol/L Tris-HCl, 137 mmol/L NaCl, 0.1% Tween 20), the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 hour at room temperature. Antibody–antigen interactions were detected using the ChemiDoc Imaging System (Bio-Rad) or by developing an X-ray film using enhanced chemiluminescence.
Antibodies used included anti-actin (Invitrogen, cat. #MA5-32530, RRID: AB_2809807), anti–α-tubulin (Invitrogen, cat. #MA1-80017, RRID: AB_2210201), anti-H3K4me3 (Abcam, cat. #ab8580, RRID: AB_306649), mouse anti-CS (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, cat. #sc-390693, RRID: AB_2813783), rabbit anti-CS (Invitrogen, cat. #PA5-22126, RRID: AB_11154268), rabbit anti-Flag (Cell Signaling Technology, cat. #14793, RRID: AB_2572291), IgG control (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, cat. #sc-2025, RRID: AB_737182), mouse anti-Flag (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. #F3165, RRID: AB_259529), rabbit anti-Myc (Cell Signaling Technology, cat. #2278, RRID: AB_2396971), and anti-VDAC (Invitrogen, cat. #PA1954, RRID: AB_2304154).
Immunofluorescence staining
Cells were grown on coverslips, washed twice with PBS, and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Alfa Aesar, cat. #A11313) on ice for 5 minutes, followed by an additional 25 minutes at room temperature. Fixation was stopped by removing the 4% paraformaldehyde and adding ammonium chloride for a 10-minute incubation. The cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 3 minutes, followed by three rinses with PBS. To block nonspecific binding, cells were incubated with 1% BSA for 1 hour. After blocking, the cells were incubated overnight at 4°C with the primary antibody, followed by incubation with the secondary antibody for 1 hour. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. The images were captured using a microscope (Nikon).
Antibodies used included mouse Alexa Fluor 488 (Invitrogen, cat. #A11008, RRID: AB_143165), rabbit Alexa Fluor 594 (Invitrogen, cat. #A11005, RRID: AB_2534073), mouse anti-Flag (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. #F3165, RRID: AB_259529), and anti-TOM20 (Cell Signaling Technology, cat. #42406, RRID: AB_10517894).
CS enzymatic activity assessment
A colorimetric activity assay was performed to assess enzymatic activity. Assay reagents were obtained individually. In brief, a master solution was prepared that contained all substrates and enzymes, except OAA. The recipe included 200 mmol/L Tris-HCl (pH 8.0) with 0.2% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. #T8787), 5,5-dithio-bis-2-nitrobenzoic acid (Ellman’s reagent; Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. #22582), 10 mmol/L acetyl-CoA (MedChemExpress, cat. #HY-113596), protein samples, and water in a 96-well plate. After measuring the baseline absorbance at 405 nm, 10 mmol/L of OAA (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. #O4126) was added to initiate the reaction. The absorbance at 405 nm was measured immediately after the addition of OAA, and repeated measurements were taken to construct the absorbance–time curve.

Mitochondrial mass, membrane potential, and mitochondrial respiration
Cells were seeded for 24 hours and then trypsinized to start the experiment. After being washed with PBS twice, cells were stained in tubes with 200 nmol/L MitoTracker Green (for assessing mitochondrial mass; Invitrogen, cat. #M7514) or 50 μmol/L rhodamine-123 (for assessing mitochondrial membrane potential; MedChemExpress, cat. #HY-D0816) for 30 minutes. After staining, cells were washed twice with PBS again, and the mitochondrial parameters were analyzed using flow cytometry.
Cells were seeded in XFe96 cell culture plates and incubated for 24 hours. Following this incubation period, the growth medium was replaced with Seahorse XF DMEM assay medium. Mitochondrial respiration, quantified as the oxygen consumption rate, was measured using the Mito Stress test (Agilent Technologies, cat. #103015-100) with the Seahorse XF96e flux analyzer (Agilent Technologies), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The analyzer provided measurements for relative nonmitochondrial respiration, as well as basal, maximal, and ATP production–related respiration rates.
Metabolomic profiling
Gas chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (GC-MS/MS) runs were acquired using mass spectrometers maintained by the Centre for PanorOmic Sciences–Proteomics and Metabolomics Core, Li Ka Shing Faculty of Medicine of The University of Hong Kong (HKUMed), University of Hong Kong. Gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC-MS) methodologies were provided by the HKUMed Centre for PanorOmic Sciences–Proteomics and Metabolomics Core. The cell pellet was washed with ice-cold saline at least 3 to 4 times, after which, it was extracted in 2 mL of 0.02 mg/L internal standard in 80% ice-cold methanol on dry ice. The lysate was centrifuged at 14,000 ×
g
for 20 minutes at 4°C for protein precipitation. One milliliter of the supernatant was dried under a gentle stream of nitrogen at room temperature. The dried aliquot was resuspended in 500 μL of 50% acetonitrile. The sample was vortexed for 10 seconds and centrifuged for 2 minutes at 14,000
g
. The supernatant was dried under a gentle stream of nitrogen at room temperature and subjected to derivatization. The dried residue was redissolved and derivatized for 2 hours at 37°C in 40 μL of methoxylamine hydrochloride (30 mg/mL in pyridine), followed by trimethylsilylation for 1 hour at 37°C in 70 μL of N-methyl-N-(trimethylsilyl)trifluoroacetamide (MSTFA) with 1% trimethylchlorosilane (TMCS). Up to 1 μL of the sample was injected for the GC-MS/MS analysis. The GC-MS/MS chromatogram was acquired in full-scan aquisition (SCAN) and multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) modes using an Agilent 7890B GC–Agilent 7010 Triple Quadrupole Mass Spectrometer system. The sample was separated using an Agilent DB-5ms capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm ID, 0.25 μm film thickness) under a constant flow of helium at 1 mL/minute. The GC oven program started at 60°C (hold time, 1 minute) and was increased at a rate of 10°C/minute to 120°C, then at 3°C/minute to 150°C, at 10°C/minute to 200°C, and finally at 30°C/minute to 280°C (hold for 5 minutes). The inlet and transfer line temperatures were 250°C and 280°C, respectively. The characteristic quantifier and qualifier transitions were monitored in MRM mode during the run. Mass spectra from m/z 50-500 were acquired in SCAN mode. Data analysis was performed using Agilent MassHunter Workstation Quantitative Analysis Software. Linear calibration curves were generated for each analyte by plotting the peak area ratio of the external/internal standard against the standard concentration at different concentration levels. The analytes were confirmed by comparing the retention time and ratio of the characteristic transitions between the sample and the standard.

Chromatin IP followed by sequencing and chromatin IP–qPCR

Online colorectal cancer transcriptome reanalysis and gene set enrichment analysis
The RNA-seq dataset PRJNA387172 was downloaded from the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database. SRA format files were converted to paired fastq files by SRA Toolkit (
https://github.com/ncbi/sra-tools

https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp

The alignment and quantification of canonical and novel isoforms were performed by STAR (RRID: SCR_004463) and RSEM (RRID: SCR_000262) with default parameters. Samples were divided into two groups according to the median value of isoform expression levels. Differential expression analysis was performed by LIMMA (RRID: SCR_010943). Genes were ranked by log

https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb
, whereas gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using clusterProfiler (RRID: SCR_016884).
Colorectal cancer single-cell RNA-seq data analysis and single-sample GSEA
Single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) data were processed using the Cell Ranger pipeline, with a custom reference constructed by the
CS-ΔEx4
gene from the NCBI reference sequences. The FASTQ files were then converted into count matrices. After obtaining the raw counts, we performed quality control by filtering out cells with fewer than 500 detected genes, fewer than 200 counts, or more than 20% mitochondrial gene expression. Cells expressing significant levels of hemoglobin were also excluded to remove red blood cell contamination. The filtered data were normalized, log-transformed, and subjected to principal component analysis for dimensionality reduction. Batch effects were corrected using the Harmony algorithm to adjust for technical variations across different experimental conditions. Dimensionality reduction was further explored using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP), followed by Leiden clustering to identify distinct cellular subpopulations. Common marker genes were visualized through scatter plots to validate cell type identification. Tumor cells were isolated and subjected to similar dimensionality reduction and clustering steps. The expression of the
CS-ΔEx4
gene was specifically visualized in tumor cells. To assess functional gene set enrichment in tumor cells, AUCell was used to perform GSEA, and the results were visualized on the UMAP plot to observe enriched pathways across different tumor cell clusters.
For single-sample GSEA (ssGSEA), scRNA-seq data were first averaged at the sample level, generating a representative gene expression profile for each sample. Subsequently, ssGSEA was performed using a gene set database associated with regulatory T cell (Treg) activation to calculate enrichment scores for each sample. Finally, linear regression analysis was conducted to investigate the relationship between the ssGSEA scores of Treg activation and the expression of the
CS-ΔEx4
gene.
In silico
inhibitor initial model generation
AlphaFold 2.2.3 (AF2 model; RRID: SCR_025454; ref.
25
) was used to generate five initial models of CS-ΔEx4 by adapting the pTM monomer model. The top-scoring model generated by AF2 model was selected for subsequent refinement. The chosen model had an average pLDDT score (also known as “AlphaFold Confidence”) of 91.09, which is higher than the acceptable threshold of 80. The loop in the AF2 model that represents the connected sequence upon deletion of exon 4 has a low confidence score, and upon close inspection of the structure, we observed less ideal packing of the region. Hence, molecular dynamics (MD) was used to refine the AF2 model in explicit solvent and to better sample the conformation of CS-ΔEx4. For the CS-FL reference simulation to investigate monomer active site conformations, entry 5UZQ was downloaded from the Protein Data Bank (
https://rcsb.org
). The modified residues were restored to the standard amino acids. The crystal water was kept, and other nonstandard residues were removed before proceeding.
MD simulation
MD preprocessing, simulation, and analysis were performed using the Amber 21 suite of software (
26
). The AF2 model was protonated by the PDB2PQR server under physiologic pH conditions (
27
). The protonated model was then processed in the leap module of Amber, with the Amber ff19SB parameters assigned to the protein. The protein was solvated in a 10 Å octahedral box of optimal point charge (OPC) water with chloride counter ions to neutralize the system charges. The MD simulation was performed using the CUDA-accelerated PMEMD module of Amber. SHAKE was used to constrain all bonds involving hydrogen atoms (
28
), and a time step of 2 fs was used for all dynamic stages. The solvated system was minimized with 2,500 steps of conjugate gradient and 2,500 steps of steepest descent and then heated to 300 K with a Langevin thermostat. Pressure was then introduced into the system with a position restraint on the protein heavy atoms to relax the solvent density at 1 bar. Additional NPT equilibration was performed to relax the system for 5 ns without any restraints. A production MD of 100 ns was done using the NPT ensemble for data collection. The simulation trajectory was processed and analyzed using CPPTRAJ (
29
). A clustering analysis of the MD trajectory was performed to select representative snapshots from the most populated cluster (MD model). Part of the analysis and visualization was performed using UCSF Chimera (
30
) and PyMOL (Supplementary Fig. S16; ref.
31
).
Structure-based virtual screening
The MD model extracted from the simulation trajectory was used to prepare the receptor for virtual screening. Water molecules and counter ions were stripped from the system, and the protein was converted into PDBQT format by AutoDockTools. The grid box for docking was prepared in UCSF Chimera to include the aligned substrate and part of the binding interface in the box (
32
). To prepare a small-molecule library for virtual screening, a subset of in-stock lead-like compounds was downloaded from the ZINC database (
33
). This small-molecule dataset was filtered according to properties including reactivity at reference and mid pH and clustered by structural fingerprinting using the Taylor–Butina method (
34
,
35
) through RDKit (
36
). The cluster centers were extracted from the dataset, protonated, and converted into PDBQT format using AutoDockTools and Open Babel (
37
). Virtual screening was performed using AutoDock Vina 1.1.2 as the docking engine. Top-scoring compounds were selected for further analysis, including inspection of docking poses in PyMOL/USCF Chimera and physiochemical property estimation. A two-dimensional interaction diagram was generated using LigPlot+ (
38
).

Cellular thermal shift assay
293FT cells overexpressing CS isoforms were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS until they reached 90% confluency in a 10 cm dish. Prior to collection, cells were treated with either DMSO or ZINC110492 (synthesized by MedChemExpress) in serum-free DMEM for 1 hour. The cells were then harvested using scrapers in IP lysis buffer. The lysates were divided equally into eight groups (with a 2°C difference between groups) in 0.2 mL PCR tubes. PCR tube strips were heated using a thermocycler with gradient temperature control, resulting in specific temperatures for each tube. Immediately after heating, the tubes were incubated at room temperature for 5 minutes. After three freeze-thaw cycles, the lysates were centrifuged, and the supernatants containing the proteins of interest were transferred to 1.5 mL tubes. The loading dye was added to each sample, and Western blotting was performed to visualize the target interactions.

Cox proportional hazard regression models were formulated using SPSS, incorporating data and annotations of in-house patients with colorectal cancer.

A novel exon 4 skipping CS variant predicts inferior survival of patients with colorectal cancer
A previous metabolic profiling study using capillary electrophoresis-mass spectrometry indicated that several TCA cycle metabolites, including citrate, isocitrate, α-ketoglutarate, succinate, and malate, were significantly elevated in the urinary samples of patients with colorectal cancer compared with healthy controls (
43
). Further pathway analysis confirmed the upregulation of the TCA cycle in patients with colorectal cancer compared with that in healthy controls in that study. Although increasing evidence showed an increased flux in the TCA cycle among patients with colorectal cancer, a majority of TCA cycle–related enzymes, including the first and rate-limiting regulatory enzyme, CS, showed either no or reduced expression at the gene level in The Cancer Genome Atlas Program [colon adenocarcinoma (COAD) and rectal adenocarcinoma (READ) datasets; Supplementary Fig. S1; ref.
44
]. This discrepancy between increased TCA metabolites and unchanged levels of the rate-limiting CS enzyme prompted us to assess the possible presence of functional alternatively spliced isoforms.
Our group has recently endeavored to profile alternative splicing events using fidelity-assured hybrid sequencing of nontumoral and colorectal cancer organoids derived from patients with colorectal cancer. We identified a total of 56 transcripts generated from 31 TCA cycle–related genes (KEGG has00020), including 9 previously unannotated isoforms (Supplementary Table S4). Among these TCA cycle–related transcripts, we identified a novel CS isoform with cassette exon 4 skipping (
CS-ΔEx4
) that ranks at the top (
Fig. 1A
and
B
).
CS-ΔEx4
mRNA levels were significantly increased in colorectal cancer organoids compared with nontumoral organoids (
Fig. 1C
). The mRNA ratio of
CS-FL
versus
CS-ΔEx4
ranged from 33.6 to 86.2 in normal organoids, whereas it decreased to approximately 20 in tumor organoids (
P
< 0.05,
Fig. 1D
). The presence of
CS-ΔEx4
was further confirmed in human colorectal cancer tissues and colorectal cancer cell lines using Sanger sequencing of the junction site between exons 3 to 5 (
Fig. 1E
; Supplementary Fig. S2A). In comparison with the canonical CS-FL, protein structure prediction using AlphaFold revealed the loss of the β-sheet structure formed by exon 4 (residues 68–89) upon the splicing event (
Fig. 1F
). We confirmed the existence of the endogenous CS-ΔEx4 using IP followed by Western blot analysis in the colorectal cancer cell lines HCT116 and SW1116 (
Fig. 1G
; Supplementary Fig. S2B). Based on the band intensity quantifications of the IP result, we estimated that the protein abundance ratio of endogenous CS-FL versus CS-ΔEx4 in colorectal cancer is approximately 6.7:1. Moreover, we investigated the peptides from CS-ΔEx4 using PepQuery2 by searching CPTAC colorectal cancer proteome data to provide further evidence of its existence. PSMs that met the competitive filtering criteria (
P
< 0.01) were accepted as valid identifications. Using this criterion, we identified the peptide “TVVGQITVDMGIR” in the CPTAC colorectal cancer dataset, which uniquely corresponds to the shortest tryptic peptide that spans the novel splice junction of CS-ΔEx4, confirming that the CS-ΔEx4 protein is indeed expressed in colorectal cancer tissue (
Fig. 1H
). In addition, we reanalyzed the CPTAC prospective colon dataset (PDC000116). After normalization, we observed that the CS-ΔEx4 protein showed significantly higher abundance in tumors compared with nontumoral tissues. In contrast, no significant difference was noted in the comparison of CS-FL protein (
Fig. 1I
).

CS-ΔEx4 elevates central metabolic activity through forming a heterocomplex with CS-FL
The localization of the CS-ΔEx4 protein was characterized by isolating the mitochondrial and cytosolic fractions from SW1116 and SW480 cells that express CS isoforms. Western blot results indicated that CS-ΔEx4 was localized in the mitochondrial fractions of both cell lines (
Fig. 4A
). Immunofluorescence staining was conducted using TOM20 as a marker for mitochondria. We observed that CS-ΔEx4 exhibited colocalization with TOM20 within the mitochondria, similar to the pattern observed for CS-FL, indicating a potential role of CS-ΔEx4 in mitochondria (
Fig. 4B
). To elucidate the mechanism underlying the oncogenic phenomena associated with CS-ΔEx4, we examined the protein structure and key domains of CS. Skipping exon 4 in CS led to a deletion of 22 amino acids (residues 68–89), resulting in a partial loss of the binding interface domain (
Fig. 1F
). Coimmunoprecipitation experiments were performed using 293FT cells expressing the two
CS
isoforms at a ratio similar to the endogenous ratio (
Fig. 4C
). We found that CS-ΔEx4 was capable of forming both a heterocomplex with CS-FL and a homocomplex with CS-ΔEx4 (
Fig. 4C
). A sequential IP was conducted to elucidate the stoichiometry of the CS complexes. CS isoforms fused with different tags (Flag or Myc) were coexpressed in colorectal cancer cells following the outlined schematics (Supplementary Fig. S6A). We performed the first IP using anti-Flag beads to enrich all protein complexes with CS-FL–Flag. After extensive wash steps and elution with Flag peptides, the second IP was conducted on this fraction using anti-Myc beads to further isolate the protein complexes expressing both Flag and Myc tags. The ratio of CS isoforms in the complexes was then determined using Western blot with an anti-CS antibody. The results indicated that the CS-FL–Flag (∼55 kDa) and CS-ΔEx4–Myc (∼52 kDa) exhibited a band intensity ratio of approximately 3:1 (Supplementary Fig. S6B).

Canonical CS catalyzes the irreversible Claisen condensation between acetyl-CoA and OAA to form citryl-CoA. Given that the catalytically essential residues involved in coordinating OAA and acetyl-CoA are well preserved in CS-ΔEx4, we evaluated the enzymatic activity of CS homo- and heterocomplexes using purified recombinant CS-FL-Flag and CS-ΔEx4-Flag proteins. In the CS activity assay supplied with excess substrates, the CS-ΔEx4 homocomplex maintained a baseline reading similar to the blank control group throughout the 25-minute incubation, suggesting that the CS-ΔEx4 homocomplex was unable to facilitate the Claisen condensation reaction. The enzymatic reaction of the CS-FL homocomplex reached a plateau after approximately 20 minutes of incubation, indicating that the CS-FL homocomplex gradually lost its enzymatic activity. Remarkably, the CS-ΔEx4 heterocomplex group (ratio 1:3) generated a rapid and significant elevation in CS activity (
Fig. 4D
; Supplementary Fig. S7A and S7B). Increasing the CS-ΔEx4 to a ratio of 1:1 further boosted the enzymatic activity (
Fig. 4D
). A previous study suggested that residues H274, H320, and D375 in CS-FL are responsible for accelerating the enzyme-catalyzed reaction. We therefore performed site-directed mutagenesis to create a mutated version of CS-ΔEx4 on these residues and evaluated the activity of the heterocomplex with mutated CS-ΔEx4 using a CS activity assay (Supplementary Fig. S8A). Additional mutations in CS-ΔEx4 did not alter the heterocomplex activity significantly (Supplementary Fig. S8B).
To investigate the function of CS-ΔEx4 in the mitochondria, we reanalyzed a scRNA-seq dataset of colorectal cancer tumor samples from NCBI (data accession code:

and

To eliminate potential mitochondrial toxic effects of CS-ΔEx4, we evaluated changes in mitochondrial mass and membrane potential using FACS, employing the appropriate indicators: MitoTracker Green for measuring mitochondrial mass and rhodamine-123 for assessing membrane potential. Our findings indicated that overexpressing
CS-ΔEx4
in the SW1116 and SW480 cell lines had a limited effect on mitochondrial health or function (Supplementary Fig. S10), indicating that the observed changes in mitochondrial respiration are derived from a healthy mitochondrial background.

The metabolic pathways for 2HG generation are still under investigation; nonetheless, there are two acknowledged metabolic sources for 2HG: (i) neomorphic enzymatic activity of the mutated isocitrate dehydrogenase 1/2 that converts 2HG from α-ketoglutarate (αKG) and (ii) metabolic enzyme promiscuity that generates pathogenic concentrations of 2HG under an elevated NADH/NAD
+
ratio background (
46

Considering the accelerated TCA cycle and elevated respiratory capacity by CS-ΔEx4, as shown in the isotope tracing experiment and the Seahorse Mito Stress test, we hypothesize that the increased NADH generated from the TCA cycle could favor the promiscuous enzyme functions. We quantified the NADH/NAD
+
ratio in SW1116 and SW480 cells overexpressing CS isoforms. Overexpression of
CS-ΔEx4
led to a significant increase in the NADH/NAD
+
ratio in both cell lines (
Fig. 5F
), suggesting a possibility of elevated production of NADH by CS-ΔEx4 favoring 2HG accumulation.
Among the enzymes producing 2HG, phosphoglycerate dehydrogenase (PHGDH) catalyzes the NADH-dependent reduction of αKG to D-2HG (
47
), and a fraction of PHGDH localizes to mitochondria (
48

CS-ΔEx4
–overexpression background. The successful siRNA knockdown of
PHGDH
in SW480 cells was confirmed using qRT-PCR (Supplementary Fig. S13A). Silencing
PHGDH
significantly reduced D-2HG levels in SW480 cells with
CS-ΔEx4
overexpression, indicating that PHGDH is a crucial player downstream of CS-ΔEx4 in the production of D-2HG (Supplementary Fig. S13B).
Overexpression of CS-ΔEx4 instigates histone remodeling in colorectal cancer
Previous studies have shown that elevated citrate and 2HG contribute to histone modification (

–overexpressing cells compared with the control and

As a proof of concept to support the role of CS-ΔEx4 in transcriptional regulation, we studied the cytokine/chemokine-related

GDF-15

50
–
56

GDF-15
in metabolic adaptation has made it a promising target for alleviating cancer cachexia, motivating our selection of it as a proof-of-concept target in this study. In the proteome profiling cytokine assay, we observed the upregulation of
GDF-15
in the
CS-ΔEx4
overexpression group (
Fig. 6F
; Supplementary Fig. S16). Moreover, specific knockdown of
CS-ΔEx4
in HCT116 and SW620 cells significantly suppressed the expression of
GDF-15
(
Fig. 6G
), whereas overexpression of
CS-ΔEx4
in SW1116 and SW480 cells promoted
GDF-15
transcription (
Fig. 6H
). These results about
GDF-15
were consistent with the documented upregulation observed in tumor tissues compared with normal tissues, which was also validated in our in-house patient cohort (
Fig. 6I
). Furthermore, qRT-PCR analysis of the colorectal cancer cohort provided additional support for a positive correlation between
GDF-15
expression and
CS-ΔEx4
mRNA levels (
Fig. 6J
). Aligned with these phenotypes, 2HG significantly restored the mRNA expression of
GDF-15
and
MMP19
in HCT116 cells upon
CS-ΔEx4
knockdown (Supplementary Fig. S12B). As overexpression of
CS-ΔEx4
upregulates cytokine- and chemokine-related genes, we further performed bioinformatic analysis on the colorectal cancer transcriptome data from the NCBI SRA database (data accession code: PRJNA387172). We stratified samples into high and low expression groups based on
CS-ΔEx4
median expression values and focused on immuno-related signature pathway analysis. Observations include increased Treg activation and decreased immune responses, such as T-cell activation, proliferation, migration, and toxicity (
Fig. 6K
). This result was also consistent with the ssGSEA from scRNA-seq reanalysis, showing upregulated Treg activation pathways (Supplementary Fig. S9C). We calculated the level of immune cell infiltration in colorectal cancer samples using the xCell package by TPM classification using median cutoff as well (
Fig. 6L
). Colorectal cancer samples with higher
CS-ΔEx4
expression exhibit lower microenvironment scores and immune scores, denoting lower immune cell infiltration. These findings collectively suggest that metabolic rewiring of the TCA cycle induced by CS-ΔEx4 leads to epigenetic modifications, resulting in increased expression of oncogenic cytokines and cancer progression with an immunosuppressive background.
Synthetic small molecule antagonizes CS-FL-ΔEx4 heterocomplex to elicit antitumor activity
The specific and significant increase in CS-ΔEx4 in colorectal cancer tissue offers a therapeutic avenue for targeting the dysregulated TCA cycle in tumors while minimizing damage to normal tissues. In our systematic effort to identify novel antagonists of CS-ΔEx4, we generated a model of CS-ΔEx4 using AF2 model and refined it through explicit solvent MD simulation to serve as the receptor for docking-based virtual screening. We predicted their binding by docking lead-like molecules from the purchasable ZINC dataset into the receptor pocket. Based on the docking results, we identified a top-scoring ligand, ZINC110492, which bound within the cleft pocket of CS-ΔEx4 while sparing CS-FL (
Fig. 7A–D
; Supplementary Fig. S17). The docking pose of ZINC110492 in the MD model exhibited specific interactions with the rearranged residues T64, V65, and V327 of CS-ΔEx4 (
Fig. 7C
and
D
). The rearranged residues were further analyzed through energy decomposition to explore their energy profile upon mutation to alanine. The energy decomposition revealed that the three residues (T64, V65, and V327) exhibit energy-stabilizing contributions greater than 0.2 kcal/mol. Mutating these three residues to alanine resulted in a higher Gibbs free energy (
ΔG
), indicating less favorable ligand binding. Other residues demonstrating more favorable interactions than these three amino acids are primarily catalytic residues that form salt bridges or hydrogen bonds with the drug (Supplementary Fig. S18A and S18B).

To further investigate the direct interaction of ZINC110492 with CS-ΔEx4, we performed a cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA) in cells expressing CS isoforms. Based on the thermal stability of ligand binding, CETSA assesses the extent of target engagement of a particular agent on a molecule of interest (
57
). Compared with DMSO treatment, the addition of 20 μmol/L ZINC110492 significantly stabilized CS-ΔEx4 at elevated temperatures, whereas no such effect was observed for CS-FL (
Fig. 7E
; Supplementary Fig. S19A and S19B). Moreover, we conducted a CETSA to compare the protein stability between CS-ΔEx4 and CS-ΔEx4
mut
(T64A, V65A, and V327A). In the presence of 20 μmol/L ZINC110492, CS-ΔEx4 exhibited greater stability compared with CS-ΔEx4
mut
as the temperature increased (
Fig. 7E
; Supplementary Fig. S18C).
In vitro
testing of ZINC110492 demonstrated potent inhibition of colorectal cancer cell growth in cells overexpressing
CS-ΔEx4
, with an IC
50
of 7.840 μmol/L, whereas the IC
50
for the control and
CS-FL
groups was found to be 12.71 and 13.05 μmol/L, respectively, indicating a selective inhibitory effect of ZINC110492 on the CS-ΔEx4 molecule (
Fig. 7F
). To highlight the clinical potential of targeting oncogenic CS-ΔEx4 with ZINC110492, we further assessed its effects on a series of patient-derived human normal colonic organoids, as well as colorectal cancer organoids. ZINC110492 demonstrated robust inhibition of cell viability in patient-derived colorectal cancer organoids while exerting minimal effects on normal colon organoids, which naturally exhibit low levels of
CS-ΔEx4
(
Fig. 7G
). With respect to enzymatic function, ZINC110492 profoundly suppressed the activity of the CS-ΔEx4 heterocomplex to an equivalent level to that of the CS-FL homocomplex while showing limited influence on the activity of the CS-FL compared with DMSO (
Fig. 7H
). In addition, we measured citrate and 2HG levels after incubating SW1116 cells with 20 μmol/L ZINC110492. Our results indicated that treatment with ZINC110492 significantly decreased both citrate and 2HG levels in the
CS-ΔEx4
–overexpressing SW1116 cells compared with the control groups (
Fig. 7I
and
J
). In contrast, the metabolites in the
CS-FL

Fig. 7I
and
J
). Additionally, treatment with 20 μmol/L ZINC110492 significantly suppressed
GDF-15
and
MMP19
expression in the
CS-ΔEx4
–overexpressing groups (
Fig. 7K
). Collectively, these findings indicated that CS-ΔEx4 is a promising therapeutic target for ZINC110492.
Discussion
Extensive studies have provided strong evidence supporting the importance of an altered TCA cycle in malignant transformation (
15
,
58
–
61
) although the underlying cause remains largely unclear. In the TCA cycle, CS is the earliest rate-limiting enzyme involved in citrate synthesis. Although TCA flux plays a critical role in the development of colorectal cancer (
14
), neither
CS
shows significantly altered expression nor gain-of-function mutations in colorectal cancer tissues. In this study, the novel isoform
CS-ΔEx4
exhibited specific upregulation in colorectal cancer, but not in hepatocellular carcinoma, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, gastric carcinoma, and lung adenocarcinoma, suggesting a relatively unique role for
CS-ΔEx4
in colorectal cancer development. High
CS-ΔEx4
expression in colorectal cancer cells was associated with a more aggressive phenotype in
in vitro
cultures, murine models, and patients. Conversely, the depletion of CS-ΔEx4 resulted in significant apoptosis. CS-ΔEx4 interacting with canonical CS-FL can lead to enhanced enzymatic activity, amino acid synthesis, and 2HG production. The oncometabolite 2HG has been shown to contribute significantly to tumorigenesis by inhibiting histone/DNA demethylation and promoting amino acid synthesis in cancer cells (
46
,
62
–
64
). Importantly, we established a novel connection between increased CS-ΔEx4 activity and elevated 2HG levels in colorectal cancer cells, which resulted in a significant change in H3K4me3 histone modification. Thus, we propose that colorectal cancer cells adopt an “efficient” cellular metabolic reprogramming via CS-ΔEx4, which controls the first rate-limiting step of the TCA cycle. Nonetheless, it is still unknown why a heterocomplex is catalytically more active than a homodimer. Future structural characterization using X-ray crystallography would provide more insights into the architecture of the CS-ΔEx4 complex in distinct substrate-bound states and the molecular basis of the increased catalytic efficiency.
Earlier studies showed that the L-2HG enantiomeric form also accumulates under acidic conditions due to the enhanced promiscuous activity of malate dehydrogenase 1 and 2 (MDH1 and MDH2) and lactate dehydrogenase A (
65
–
67
), in addition to PHGDH. In our study, mass spectrometry was unable to differentiate between enantiomeric species. Consequently, we are currently not capable of conclusively deciphering the relationship between lactate dehydrogenase A/MDH-mediated L-2HG production and the CS-ΔEx4 oncogenic program. Future studies are needed to elucidate a clearer role of CS-ΔEx4 in the production of specific 2HG enantiomers.
In our study, we employed
in silico
docking along with a series of
in vitro
biological and activity assays to identify a specific inhibitor that could potentially interfere with the CS-FL-ΔEx4 heterocomplex. We discovered that ZINC110492 [International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry name: 6-(3-nitrophenyl)-5H-dibenz[c,e]azepine-5,7(6H)-dione; CAS: 100264-64-0] was initially reported in 1961 during a search for antimalarial drugs (
68
). Subsequent investigations of derivatives of dibenz[c,e]azepines-5,7-dione suggested potent antihyperlipidemic, anti-inflammatory, and local analgesic activities in rodents at μmol/L concentrations (
69
–
71
). Although no biological activity of ZINC110492 has been reported to date, our study represents the first demonstration of its anticancer effect through specific inhibition of the catalytic domain of the CS-FL-ΔEx4 heterocomplex while sparing the CS-FL homodimer. This finding suggests that ZINC110492 could provide a safer option for fine-tuning the TCA metabolic pathway in tumors while minimizing collateral damage to normal tissues. Given the association between high
CS-ΔEx4
expression and poor survival in patients with colorectal cancer, the utilization of ZINC110492 holds promise as a treatment option for patients with advanced and inoperable colorectal cancer.
In conclusion, we report a novel
CS-ΔEx4
variant that is differentially upregulated in colorectal cancer and is associated with inferior clinical outcomes, along with a mechanistic analysis of CS-ΔEx4 demonstrating aggressive oncogenic phenotypes through heterocomplex formation and driving TCA flux elevation. The screening and discovery of an efficacious small-molecule compound with preliminary antagonistic effects on inhibiting the enzymatic function of the colorectal cancer–specific CS-FL-ΔEx4 heterocomplex build the foundation for future antitumor treatment targeting molecules in central metabolic pathways.
