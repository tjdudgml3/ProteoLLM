Background
Premature ovarian insufficiency (POI) is the decline in ovarian function in women before the age of 40 years, characterised by abnormal menstruation, decreased fertility, and oestrogen deficiency and accompanied by primary or secondary amenorrhoea [
1
]. Owing to the complexity of POI, several treatment methods have been proposed. However, none have proven to be reliable alternatives. Long-term hormone replacement therapy (HRT), mainly comprising oestrogen and progesterone, is the most common treatment for POI in women. The therapeutic and protective effects of certain hormones, including anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH), melatonin, and growth hormone (GH), have been confirmed [
2
]. However, long-term HRT may increase cardiovascular disease risk and cancer [
3
]. To date, POI has remained irreversible in clinical practice. Therefore, advanced treatment strategies are urgently needed.
Platelet-rich plasma (PRP), a regenerative medicine technique [
4
], is a concentrated autologous plasma containing platelets. Platelets are anuclear cell fragments formed after the differentiation and maturation of megakaryocytes belonging to the myeloid cell lineage [
5
]. They contain complete growth factors such as vascular endothelial growth factor, as well as coagulation and differentiation factors, which are conducive to angiogenesis, immunosuppression, and the regeneration process [
6
]. PRP promotes angiogenesis within the ovaries by releasing factors such as vascular endothelial growth factor and increasing blood supply to follicles, thereby improving their nutritional support and oxygen supply. This enhances the responsiveness of follicles to gonadotropins and the potential to develop oocytes [
7
]. Growth factors in PRP may stimulate ovarian stromal cells and promote their proliferation and differentiation, thereby increasing the number of primordial follicles in the ovary. In addition, PRP may affect the development and maturation of oocytes by regulating the expression of genes related to cell cycle regulation, DNA repair and energy metabolism in oocytes [
8
]. Recently, PRP therapy has been gradually applied to the treatment of POI. The high number of growth factors involved in this therapy promote tissue repair and regeneration and provide a new research direction for improving ovarian function [
9
,
10
].
PRP was initially used for ovarian rejuvenation by Pantos et al. in 2016 [
11
]; the authors reported that autologous ovarian PRP treatment successfully restored temporary ovarian activity in premenopausal women. In several observational studies, ovarian PRP treatment improved follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and AMH levels, making spontaneous pregnancy possible and leading to better in-vitro fertilisation outcomes; this was confirmed in a clinical trial involving a group of patients with an extremely poor prognosis [
12
]. Despite these positive results, ovarian PRP treatment is still not widely acknowledged or applied in clinical practice due to the current small sample size of PRP, lack of randomised trials, and short follow-up period. The lack of reliable clinical evidence also contributes to its ineffectiveness.
Ideal animal models and evaluation indicators are crucial for understanding drug mechanisms and development. Models induced by chemotherapeutic drugs are classic, simple, and commonly used in POI research [
13
]. Among various chemotherapeutic drugs, cyclophosphamide has been the most widely used for inducing POI. POI. Recent studies have shown that PRP has great application potential in rat follicular culture and may promote follicular growth and oocyte maturation by providing abundant growth factors and improving the microenvironment. Especially in the rat model of ovarian injury induced by cyclophosphamide, PRP demonstrated a protective effect on in vitro fertilization [
14
,
15
]. Therefore, in this study, we aimed to establish a rat model of POI induced by cyclophosphamide and systematically analysed the mechanism and effectiveness of PRP on POI in these rats. The results may contribute to the establishment of ovarian PRP treatment in future clinical reproductive practices.
Methods
Preparation of PRP
We purchased a total of 22 female SD rats (2 months old, weighing approximately 220 g) from Beijing Sibefu Experimental Animal Co., Ltd. [Production License No. SCXK (Beijing) 2019-0010; License number for use: SYXK (Yunnan) K2022-0007]. PRP was prepared from four female Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats. Blood samples were collected under anaesthesia using a right ventricular cardiac puncture and deposited in a test tube containing 3.2% sodium citrate. Platelet enrichment was performed using the two-stage centrifugation method to prepare PRP: The first centrifugation: At 20℃, centrifuge at a speed of 200 g/min for 15 min and then draw the upper- and middle-layer liquids into a brand-new centrifuge tube to prepare for the second centrifugation. Second centrifugation: Centrifuge at a speed of 300 g/min for 10 min at 20℃. The blood sample after the second centrifugation was divided into two layers. The upper layer was discarded, while the PRP layer was retained for future use. A small amount of PRP was taken to test the platelet concentration. Before enrichment, the platelet concentration of the blood sample was 610 × 10
9
/L, and after enrichment, it increased to 2337 × 10
9
/L, approximately 3.8 times the platelet concentration before enrichment, indicating the successful preparation of PRP. Before injection, PRP needs to be activated: Mix bovine thrombin with 10% CaCh buffer in a ratio of 1:9 evenly, then add 0.15 mL of this mixture to each millilitre of PRP, stir thoroughly, and incubate at 37℃ for 1 h to complete the activation.
POI modelling and intervention
The remaining 18 female rats were selected for POI animal modelling. All animals were fasted for 6–8 h before the experiment. Rats were intraperitoneally injected once with 60 mg/kg cyclophosphamide solution (Shengdi Medicine, China). Approximately 24 h later, they were intraperitoneally injected with 10 mg/kg cyclophosphamide solution once daily for 14 consecutive days [
16
]. The control group was injected with the same dose of normal salin1e. After all injections were administered, daily vaginal secretions were collected to prepare smears for 5 consecutive days in order to observe the oestrous cycle of the rats. If the cycle was prolonged or disordered, replication of the POI model was considered successful. After successful modelling, the intervention was administered. In group A, the control group, 200 µL of normal saline was intraperitoneally injected for 14 days. In group B, the model group, 200 µL of cyclophosphamide was intraperitoneally injected for 14 days. In the PRP intervention group, after successfully modelling POI, rats were intraperitoneally injected with 200 µL of PRP for 14 days. Samples from all groups were taken for 21 consecutive days (si × SD rats each).
Detection of oestrous cycle and ovarian indices in POI rats
Every morning at 9:00, the rats should be captured, 200 µL of normal saline should be drawn, and the solution should be injected into the female rats’ vagina. Rinse it three to five times to make it turbid, then transfer it onto a slide. Draw 10 µL of the prepared turbid normal saline liquid and use another pipette to draw the crystal violet staining solution for staining. Fix the staining solution with a cover slip and absorb the surplus liquid with absorbent paper after thorough mixing. Then, it can be placed under an optical microscope for observation. Vaginal smears are used to monitor the estrous cycle, which is divided into four stages: preestrus (P), estrus (E), next estrus (M), and interestrus (D). Estrous cycle disorder is defined as a significantly prolonged cycle (≥ 6 days) or no change at a certain stage for ≥ 3 days.
After the rats were anaesthetised, the bilateral ovaries were removed and placed in normal saline to wash away the surface bleeding of the ovaries. The fat and other tissues attached to the ovaries were removed. After immersing the filter paper in dry water, the wet weight of the bilateral ovaries was measured using a precision electronic scale. Photographs were taken and recorded. Calculate the ovarian index: In this study, the Ovarian index/(mg/g) = Ovarian moisture mass /mg ÷ body weight /g.
Haematoxylin and eosin staining
Rats were euthanized by carbon dioxide inhalation and sacrificed without the use of anesthesia. Animals were divided into groups and transferred to a clean euthanasia chamber. Carbon dioxide gas with a purity of 99.5% was infused into the chamber at a filling rate of 30% to induce death.Wax-block preparation was performed by wrapping fresh ovarian tissue in gauze and placing it in a dehydration frame. The cells were fixed in Bouin’s fixative for 12 h. The dehydration frame was removed, and the following operations were performed in sequence: gradient ethanol was dehydrated; xylene was made transparent, and wax immersion was performed. Subsequently, the ovarian tissue in the dehydration frame was transferred to the embedding frame, paraffin liquid was injected, and the tissue was left at room temperature until the paraffin solidified for subsequent use. Next, tissue sections were obtained by fixing the paraffin blocks of ovarian tissue on a micrometre sample tray and slicing them at a thickness of 5 μm each. Every two tissue sections were placed in a 40 °C water bath to spread the slices. When the paraffin became nearly transparent, and no cut marks were observed, the tissue sections were placed on slides treated with polylysine and baked in a 50 °C oven for 2–3 h. After cooling at 25 °C, appropriate sections were selected under a microscope, packed in boxes, and stored in a refrigerator at 4 ℃ for future use. Haematoxylin and eosin staining was performed for the film by successively subjecting it to dewaxing, haematoxylin staining, bluing, eosin staining, dehydration, and transparency, and subsequently sealing it for full-film scanning.
Western blot
Proteins in the ovarian tissues were separated using 4–20% gradient sodium dodecyl sulphate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). The polyvinylidene fluoride membrane was sealed at room temperature in a solution containing Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST; 10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, and 0.05% Tween 20) and 5% skimmed milk powder for 1 h. It was then incubated overnight with the corresponding primary antibody at 4 °C. The antibodies used were as follows: anti-DNA-PK (1:2000, AF5340, Affinity), anti-ATR (1:2000, 15091-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-XRCC1 (1:3000, AF0296, Affinity), anti53BP1 (1:2000, DF7472, Affinity), anti-hnRNPs (1:2000, AF5260, Affinity), anti-β-actin (1:2500, 66009-1-Ig, Proteintech). After washing with TBST, the membrane was conjugated with an HRP-HRP anti-rabbit antibody (7074, diluted 1:3000) and incubated at room temperature for 1 h (Cell Signalling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA). After washing with TBST buffer, the signal was detected using chemiluminescent reagents (Millipore), imaged using a gel imaging system (Tanon-UK Limited, London, UK), and quantified using Tanon images.
Detection of serum hormone levels
The rats were divided into the model group, the modeling group, and the PRP intervention group (intervention for 7 days and 14 days) (
n
= 6). Serum was collected from rats in each group at 10 a.m. Using the rat specific enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (China Beteng Biotechnology Co., LTD.), in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions, the serum FSH (sensitivity: 0.5U/L- 16u /L) and luteinizing hormone (LH) (sensitivity: Levels of 1.5 ng/L-40 ng/L, estradiol (E2) (sensitivity: 1pmol/L − 48pmol /L), T (sensitivity: 0.4nmol/L-12nmol/L), and AMH (sensitivity: 0.8ng/ml − 24ng/ml). Measure the absorbance at 450 nm.
Measurement of mitochondrial membrane potential using flow cytometry analysis with JC-1 staining
The tissue cells were collected and resuspended in a cell culture medium. JC-1 staining working solution (0.5 mL) was then added, and the mixture was reversed several times. The cells were incubated in a cell incubator for 20 min at 37 °C. At the end of the incubation period, the cells were centrifuged, the supernatant was discarded, washed with JC-1 staining buffer (1X), and precipitated. After resuspension with 500 µL of JC-1 (Beyotime Biological Technology Research Institute, Shanghai, China) staining buffer (1X), the samples were observed using a fluorescence or laser confocal microscope, and analysed with a fluorescence spectrophotometer or via flow cytometry.
Metabolite identification using High-Performance liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry analysis
Each sample (100 µl) was transferred to a 2-ml Eppendorf tube and resuspended in pre-cooled methanol at -20 °C through an eddy current for 60 s. The blood sample was then incubated on ice for 15 min, centrifuged at 4 °C for 20 min at 12,000 rpm, and the supernatant was transferred to a new centrifuge tube. Liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS)-grade water was used to dilute part of the supernatant to the final concentration of 53% methanol. The samples were then transferred to a fresh Eppendorf tube and centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 20 min. The initial samples for LC–MS analysis were obtained using 0.22-µm membrane filtration and were injected into the LC–MS/MS system for analysis.Each sample (100 µL) was transferred to a 2-mL Eppendorf tube and resuspended in pre-cooled methanol at -20 °C through an eddy current for 60 s. The blood sample was then incubated on ice for 15 min and centrifuged at 4 °C for 20 min at 12,000 rpm; then, the supernatant was transferred to a new centrifuge tube. Liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS)-grade water was used to dilute part of the supernatant to the final concentration of 53% methanol. The samples were then transferred to a fresh Eppendorf tube and centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 20 min. The initial samples for LC–MS analysis were obtained using 0.22-µm membrane filtration and were injected into the LC–MS/MS system for analysis. High-performance liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC–MS/MS) analyses were performed using an ExionLC™ AD system (SCIEX, Framingham, MA, USA) coupled with a QTRAP
®
6500 + mass spectrometer (SCIEX) from Novogene Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Samples were injected onto an Xselect HSS T3 column (2.1 × 150 mm, 2.5 μm) using a 20-min linear gradient at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min in the positive/negative polarity mode. Eluents A (0.1% formic acid–water) and B (0.1% formic acid–acetonitrile) were used. The solvent gradient was set as follows: 2% B, 2 min; 2–100% B, 15.0 min; 100% B, 17.0 min; 100–2% B, 17.1 min; and 2% B, 20 min. The QTRAP
®
6500 + mass spectrometry settings in positive polarity mode were curtain gas, 35 psi; collision gas, medium; IonSpray voltage, 5500 V; temperature, 550 °C; ion source gas 1, 60 psi; and ion source gas 2, 60 psi. For negative ionisation mode, the settings were the same, except that the IonSpray voltage was ₋4500 V.
Metabolite identification and quantification
Analysis of experimental samples using multiple reaction monitoring was based on the Novogene in-house database. Q3 was used for metabolite quantification. Q1 and Q3 retention time, declustering potential, and collision energy were used for metabolite identification. The data files generated using HPLC–MS/MS were processed using SCIEX OS version 1.4 to integrate and correct the peaks. The main parameters were set as follows: minimum peak height, 500; signal/noise ratio, 5; Gaussian smooth width, 6; the area of each peak represents the relative content of the corresponding substance.
Proteomics
Serum samples were thawed, washed, and homogenised with a lysis solution to extract proteins. The reduced protein was incubated with 100 µg of protein solution and 10 mM dithiothreitol. Next, 25 mM iodoacetamide was added, and the mixture was incubated in the dark for alkylation. The protein solution was digested overnight with trypsin at a protein: trypsin ratio of 50:1. The protein was precipitated in each reaction by adding 10% trifluoroacetic acid. Desalination was performed using an MCX C18 column, and the peptides were dried in a centrifugal vacuum evaporator following the manufacturer’s instructions.
HPLC‒MS/MS analysis and quantification
The dried peptide samples were reconstituted with 0.1% FA and centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 10 min; then, the supernatant was injected. Separation is conducted through Thermo Scientific’s EASY-nLC™ 1200 system. The sample enters the self-packed C18 column (with an inner diameter of 100 μm, a column material particle size of 1.8 μm, and a column length of approximately 35 cm) and is separated at a flow rate of 300 nL/min through the following effective gradient: From 0 to 103 min, the 4% mobile phase B (98% ACN, 0.1% FA) linearly increased to 27%. From 103 to 111 min, the mobile phase B increased from 27% to 40%. At 111 to 113 min, the mobile phase B rose from 40% to 90%. 113 to 120 min, 90% mobile phase B. The peptides separated via liquid phase were ionised by nanoESI source and then entered the mass spectrometer Orbitrap Exploris™ 480 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose). CA conducts DIA (Data Independent Acquisition) pattern detection. Main parameter settings: Scanning range of first-stage mass spectrometry 350–1,500 m/z; Set the resolution to 120,000 and the Normalised AGC Target to 300%; In DIA mode, 400–1,200 m/z is evenly divided into 53 Windows for continuous window fragmentation and signal acquisition. The ion fragmentation mode is HCD, and the fragmentation energy is set at 32%. Fragment ions were detected in Orbitrap with a resolution set at 30,000. Set the Normalised AGC Target to 200%.
Use the DIA-NN software (
https://www.nature.com/articles/s41592-019-0638-x
) (version 1.8) for the DIA to protein mass spectrometry data search identification and quantitative analysis. The main parameters of the DIA-NN are as follows: the protein identification mode is DirectDIA (without the need for actual DDA data to build a spectral library, directly using information analysis methods by the corresponding species). The theoretical virtual spectral library of the Protein sequence library was constructed. The enzyme digestion parameter Trypsin/P was set, with the maximum missed cutting number being 2. The fixed modification was Carbamidomethyl (C), and the variable modifications were Oxidation (M) and Acetyl (protein term). The protein identification credibility strategy is Target-decoy, and the protein identification threshold is a false positive spectrum (PSM FDR) of < 0.01 and a protein FDR of < 0.01.
Statistical analysis
In this study, statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 26.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism 23.0 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA). Measurement data were expressed as mean ± standard error (mean ± SEM). was Independent-samples t-test used for comparison between groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for comparison among multiple groups, and Tukey’s HSD method was used for post hoc tests (for pairwise comparisons among all groups). A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, while p values < 0.01 and
p
< 0.001 indicated highly significant and extremely significant statistical differences, respectively.

Discussion
In this study, we explored the effects of PRP on hormones involved in POI, recovery of ovarian function, oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses in POI rats. The results showed that hormone levels in POI rats improved after PRP intervention (
p
< 0.05). Moreover, PRP significantly increased the expression of the germ cell markers Ddx4, PCNA, and BMP4 and the ovarian tissue generation and granulosa cell markers CD34, Bcl2, Caspase3, AMH, and FSHR in the ovarian tissues of POI rats (
p
< 0.05). PRP improved oxidative stress, mitochondrial damage, and DNA damage in granulosa cells of POI rats. Proteomic and metabolomic analyses revealed a dysfunction in energy metabolism in rats with POI, and that inflammation may damage the ovarian tissue. Following PRP intervention, the inflammatory response in the ovarian tissue was significantly relieved.
Ovarian follicles are completely exhausted in the histology of early ovarian failure. The basic processes of POI are follicular depletion and follicular dysfunction [18.19]. In the early follicular stage, FSH, E2, and inhibin B FSH/LH levels are used to diagnose POI. Elevated FSH levels and decreased AMH levels clearly indicate ovarian failure [
10
,
19
]. In this study, cyclophosphamide-treated SD rats showed obvious symptoms of POI. After treatment with PRP, hormone levels, including FSH, AMH, and LH, significantly improved [
20
]. Notably, the body weight of the rats in the treatment group was higher than that in the model group, indicating that the drug intervention helped improve the physiological condition of the model rats and enhanced their quality of life.
PRP concentrates can stimulate the superphysiological release of growth factors to start the healing process of chronic injuries and accelerate the repair of acute injuries [
5
]. At all stages of the tissue repair process, various growth factors, cytokines, and local regulators are important in the most basic cellular functions through endocrine, paracrine, and autocrine mechanisms. In our study, we found that PRP significantly improved the expression levels of the germ cell markers Ddx4, PCNA, and BMP4 and the ovarian tissue formation and granulosa cell markers CD34, Bcl2, Caspase3, AMH, and FSHR in the ovarian tissues of rats with POI.
POI is closely associated with oxidative stress and inflammation in a complex way. Recently, this association has attracted considerable attention in medical research. Increasing evidence indicates that chronic low-grade inflammation is a potential consequence of POI and may be an important inducing and promoting factor [
21
]. Cyclophosphamide reduces the production of antioxidant enzymes, promotes oxidative stress and inflammation, and disrupts the ovarian microenvironment, leading to an imbalance between pro- and anti-apoptotic genes. This destruction eventually causes apoptosis of germ cells and follicular atresia [
22
–
24
]. Several studies have shown that PRP can produce therapeutic effects in orthopaedics and improve damaged, inflammatory joints owing to the effects of growth factors [
25
]. PRP is widely used to treat various diseases, including arthritis and rheumatoid arthritis; however, PRP use in the treatment of POI has not been reported [
26
]. In this study, we found that PRP administered in rats with cyclophosphamide-induced POI significantly improved the expression levels of oxidative stress-related proteins, mitochondrial membrane potential, and the expression of DNA damage repair-related proteins in ovarian tissue.
Recently, advances in technologies, including proteomics and metabolomics, have provided new insights into disease aetiology and diagnosis. Proteomic analysis involves identifying all protein components in biological samples. This method helps identify and quantify changes in protein expression levels, post-translational modifications, and protein interactions [
27
]. Metabolome analysis characterises the metabolome profiles of biological samples and can be used to identify changes in the metabolic pathways associated with specific diseases or conditions [
28
]. These methods are also expected to help understand the potential mechanisms of POI and identify novel biomarkers for its diagnosis and treatment [
29
,
30
]. Principal component analysis of proteomic characteristics was used to compare cyclophosphamide-induced POI rats with controls. DEPs were mainly enriched in innate immune responses, cholesterol metabolism, responses to oxidative stress, and cholesterol transport. Metabolomic analysis revealed that the main differential metabolites were concentrated in efferocytosis, unsaturated fatty acids, and fatty acid biosynthesis. Efferocytosis is initiated by inflammatory mediators released by apoptotic cells and essential in addressing inflammation [
31
]. These results indicate that inflammation may damage ovarian tissue. Following PRP intervention, the inflammatory response in ovarian tissue was significantly relieved.
Pathway analysis revealed changes in protein and metabolic pathways in POI rats after PRP intervention. The metabolites LysoPC/LPC, FFAs, and PUFAs were significantly increased following PRP intervention. LysoPC lipid levels are abnormal in chronic inflammation, polycystic ovary syndrome, POI, diabetes, and cardiovascular diseases. These lipids play roles in antioxidation, the defence against oxidative stress, regulation of immune cells, and insulin sensitivity [
32
,
33
]. FFAs have the potential to metabolise and regulate fatty acids, as well as anti-inflammatory and insulin-sensitive functions [
34
]; The metabolism of PUFA is influenced by multiple enzymes, including cyclooxygenase, lipoxygenase, and cytochrome P450. These enzymes mediate the conversion of PUFA into various bioactive lipid mediators, which play a role in inflammation and cellular signalling [
35
–
37
]. Node network clustering indicated key proteins and metabolites that contribute to changes in signalling pathways. Scin, Myth14, Enpep, Gatm, Lman1, and Sqor were the most important nodes in both groups. These proteins are associated with the secretory function and hormone regulation of follicular granulosa cells, ovarian energy balance, mitochondrial dysfunction, antioxidant stress, and maintenance of the functional stability of ovarian cells [
38
,
39
]. Lipid metabolism disorders play an important role in POI, and Scin and Myth14 may indirectly affect lipid metabolism by influencing cytoskeletal regulation, signal transduction, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and other pathways, thereby affecting the occurrence and development of POI. Future research can focus on exploring the specific roles of Scin and Myth14 in lipid metabolism of ovarian cells and how they affect the pathophysiological processes of POI. This evidence aligns with previous results that energy metabolism dysfunction in POI rats, including inflammation and hormonal imbalance, promotes the onset of the disease and that PRP intervention can significantly alleviate this disorder.
Although PRP therapy has shown great clinical application potential in the treatment of POI, research in this field still has obvious limitations. This study utilised a rodent model for a preliminary mechanism exploration. However, the rat POI model cannot fully replicate all the features of human POI, although it provides a controllable and operable system for studying specific pathological mechanisms and testing potential treatment methods. Notably, by integrating multi-omics analysis, not only was the gene expression change profile after PRP intervention described, but also a batch of differentially expressed genes that might drive the recovery of ovarian function, such as Scin and Myth14, were screened out through bioinformatics analysis. Future research will clarify the specific functions of these genes in disease mechanisms in cell models and animal models through technical means such as gene editing and RNA interference, thereby ultimately elucidating the molecular pharmacology of PRP in treating POI and laying a solid theoretical foundation for the development of more precise and efficient targeted drugs.
