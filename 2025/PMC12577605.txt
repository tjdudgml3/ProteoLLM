Introduction
Metabolic reprogramming, known as the Warburg effect, is a major hallmark of cancer progression (
1
). Tumor cells generate lactate through glycolysis, supporting rapid production of energy to promote cell proliferation. In addition to endogenous lactate production
via
the Warburg effect, recent studies its supply from neighboring normal cells
via
the lactate shuttle has recently received attention (
2
). High concentrations of lactate in cancer are well established, as they facilitate immunosuppression and promote the invasion and metastasis of tumor cells (
3
,
4
). Similar to other intracellular metabolites that form acyl-CoA derivatives and modify proteins through acylation, the elevated lactate levels observed in tumor cells are converted to lactyl-CoA, which leads to the lactylation of the lysine residues of proteins (
5
).
Since the discovery of protein lysine lactylation (Kla) on histones in 2019, numerous Kla-modified proteins have been identified in both histones and non-histone contexts (
Figure 1
A). The role of Kla in disease has been increasing reported. In particular, global lactylome profiling has been used in various tumors, including hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), gastric cancer, and oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC), has been employed to identify lactylated sites in histones and non-histones, thereby elucidating the role of Kla (
6
-
8
). In HCC, 9,275 Kla sites have been identified, of which K28la inhibits the function of adenylate kinase 2, which promotes the proliferation and metastasis of HCC cells (
6
). In another study, 1,011 Kla sites in 532 proteins under normoxic conditions and 1,197 Kla sites in 608 proteins under hypoxic environments were identified, in OSCC. Lactylation was validated in the spliceosomal proteins such as hnRNPA1, SF3A1, hnRNPU and SLU7 (
7
). The order was 2,375 Kla sites found in 1,014 proteins in gastric cancer AGS cells, and these lactylated proteins were significantly enriched in the spliceosome (
8
).

To determine the function of a newly identified protein, a post-translational modification (PTM), in a specific cancer, it is necessary to identify the modified sites and substrate proteins. To our knowledge, systematic lactylome studies on the substrates of protein lactylation in prostate cancer (PC) are limited compared with other cancers (
9
). PC is a major cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide. It is the fourth most commonly diagnosed cancer globally, and the second most prevalent among men (
10
). Although new therapies for PC, including various androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) regimens, continue to be developed, transformation to CRPC and metastasis of primary PC remain bottlenecks in the management of PC.
Lactylomic profiling of Kla substrates involved in PC progression is therefore critical for identifying therapeutic targets. Given the limited research on the role of Kla in PC, we conducted a global lactylome analysis using affinity enrichment of lactylated peptides with pan-anti-Kla antibodies in a PC cell line. This study presents the first proteomic survey of Kla in PC, providing a valuable foundation for further investigations into the molecular mechanisms of PC progression.
Materials and Methods
PC-3M culture and sample preparation.
The human prostate cancer cell line (PC-3M) was obtained from the Korean Cell Line Bank (Seoul, Republic of Korea). Cell lines were cultured under standard conditions according to the manufacturer. Briefly, PCa cell lines were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Pierce HyClone, Fremont, CA, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (GE Healthcare HyClone, Logan, UT, USA) and 1% penicillin and streptomycin (Gibco, Rockville, MD, USA). The cells were incubated at 37˚C with 5% CO
2
in a humidified incubator.
Protein extraction.
The cell pellets were suspended in RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with a 1× protease inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1× phosphatase inhibitor (Thermo Fisher Scientific), nicotinamide (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), and sodium butyrate (Sigma Aldrich). The suspension was disrupted by sonication and subsequently centrifuged at 16,000 ×
g
for 10 min at 4˚C to eliminate cellular debris. The supernatant was collected into a fresh tube, and protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific).
Immunoblotting.
Denatured protein samples (20 µg) were separated on 10% SDS-PAGE gels by electrophoresis at 80 mA for 90 min and subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany) at 90 V for 2 h. To prevent nonspecific antibody interactions, the membranes were blocked for 2 h at room temperature using 5% (v/v) bovine serum albumin (GenDEPOT, Barker, TX, USA) dissolved in TBST buffer (137 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris, 0.1% Tween-20). Primary antibody specific for anti-lactyllysine (PTM Biolab, Hangzhou, Zhejiang, PR China) was applied, and membrane was incubated overnight at 4˚C. Following incubation, the membrane was washed with TBST and treated with secondary antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. Membrane was then exposed to ECL detection reagent (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA), and protein bands were visualized using an iBright imaging system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). In parallel, SDS-PAGE gel was stained with Coomassie brilliant blue (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) to assess total protein loading.
In-solution digestion and enrichment of lactyl-peptides.
Protein samples isolated from PC-3Ms were reduced with 5 mM dithiothreitol at 55˚C for 30 min, followed by alkylation using 15 mM iodoacetamide at room temperature for 30 min in the dark. Proteins were precipitated with 10% trichloroacetic acid at 4˚C for 1 h, then washed three times with ice-cold acetone. After drying, the protein pellets were resuspended in 25 mM ammonium bicarbonate buffer. For enzymatic digestion, trypsin (20 µg; Promega Corporation, Madison, WI, USA) was added at a 1:50 (w/w) enzyme-to-substrate ratio and incubated overnight at 37˚C. Digestion was quenched with 10% (v/v) trifluoroacetic acid, and samples were dried using a speed vacuum concentrator. Peptides were resuspended in 0.1% TFA and desalted with C18 Sep-Pak cartridges (Waters Associates, Milford, MA, USA). The desalted peptides were then dried and stored at -80˚C until further use.
Peptide concentration was quantified using a colorimetric peptide assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Peptides were resuspended in ETN buffer (1 mM EDTA, 50 mM Tris-HCl, 20 mM NaCl; pH 8.0) supplemented with 1% NP-40. A total of 2 mg of peptide was used for Kla enrichment. Antibody-conjugated beads specific for Kla (PTM Biolab) were washed prior to use and incubated with the peptide solution overnight at 4˚C under gentle agitation. After incubation, the mixtures were centrifuged at 1,000 ×
g
for 1 min at 4˚C, and the supernatant was carefully removed to collect the beads. Beads were washed sequentially with cold NETN buffer, ETN buffer, and distilled water. To elute the bound peptides, 100 µl of 0.15% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) was added and allowed to stand at room temperature for 10 min. Following elution, samples were centrifuged at 1,000 ×
g
for 1 min, and the resulting supernatants were collected. To ensure complete bead removal, a second centrifugation was performed at 16,000 ×
g
for 5 min, and the final supernatants were transferred to fresh tubes and vacuum dried. Enriched Kla peptides were desalted twice using 5 µg C18 ZipTips (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol prior to mass spectrometry analysis.
Nano-LC-MS/MS analysis.
Enriched peptides were dissolved in 0.1% formic acid in water and subjected to separation using a nano-liquid chromatography system (Eksigent, Dublin, CA, USA). The mobile phases were composed of solvent A (0.1% formic acid in water) and solvent B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile). Liquid chromatography was performed with a 70-min linear gradient, starting with 3% acetonitrile (in 0.1% formic acid) for 2 min, increasing to 23% over 52 min, reaching 90% at 60 min, and returning to 100% aqueous phase (0.1% formic acid in water) at 70 min. The flow rate was set to 300 nl/min. Peptide separation was performed using a custom-packed C12 column (10 cm length, 75 μm outer diameter, 150 μm inner diameter; Polymicro Technologies, Molex, Lisle, IL, USA) containing C12 resin (Proteo C12, 4 μm particle size, 90 Å pore size; Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, USA). The separated peptides were analyzed using an LTQ-Orbitrap Velos mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The electrospray ionization source was operated at a voltage of 1.8 kV. Data were acquired in data-dependent acquisition mode, selecting the top 10 most intense precursor ions from each full scan. Full MS scans were acquired over a mass-to-charge (m/z) range of 300-1,800 with a resolution of 60,000. The automatic gain control (AGC) target value for MS scans was set to 1.0×10
6
. Fragmentation was carried out using collision-induced dissociation (CID) with a normalized collision energy of 35%.
Database search using MaxQuant.
The MaxQuant (version 2.1.2.0) search engine was used to analyze the mass data. Variable modifications included acetylation (Protein N-term), oxidation (M) and Kla for protein and peptide identification. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine residues was designated as a fixed modification, with a maximum of five modifications allowed per peptide. Trypsin/P was selected as the digestion enzyme, permitting up to two missed cleavage sites. MS/MS spectra were searched against the Uniprot Mus musculus reference proteome database. For data filtering, the false discovery rate (FDR) threshold was set at 1%, and site localization probability was required to exceed 0.75 to confidently identify Kla sites. Peptides labeled as “contamination” or “reverse” were excluded from further analysis. Mass proteomic data were deposited in the ProteomeXchange Consortium
via
the PRIDE (
11
) partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD063266.
Bioinformatics analyses.
The UniProt Subcellular Location database was utilized to predict the subcellular localization of proteins modified by Kla. Functional enrichment analyses, including Gene Ontology (GO) terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways, were conducted using DAVID Bioinformatics Resources (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/). Among the enriched pathways, the five most statistically significant ones were selected based on
p
-values. To explore sequence patterns of Kla in PC-3M cells, motif analysis was performed using the pLogo tool (https://plogo.uconn.edu/). Peptide sequences consisting of 13 amino acids centered around the identified Kla sites were used as input. Kla motif was analyzed based on the default background sequence in pLogo.
Results
Enrichment of lactylation in prostate cancer cell-line.
We ran SDS-PAGE and western blot to measure Kla protein expression was checked in immortalized prostrate and prostate cancer cell lines (Figure 1B). Among the five cell lines, examined, PC-3M cells, a highly metastatic subline of PC-3 cells derived from a PC-3-induced tumor (
12
). The presence of multiple protein patterns against Kla indicated non-histone proteins. To perform global proteomic profiling of lactylated peptides in PC-3M cells, we employed immunoprecipitation (IP) using pan-anti-lysine lactylation antibodies and an LTQ-Orbitrap Velos mass spectrometer as shown in Figure 1C. The resulting tryptic peptides were analyzed using HPLC-MS/MS, followed by protein sequence alignment to identify peptides with a mass shift of +72.021 Da at the Lys residues. Three replicate sets of the biological samples were prepared. Our data revealed 681 Kla sites across 379 lactylated proteins (localization probability >0.75 and FDR <1%) in PC-3M cells.
Of the proteins with detected Kla, 254 had one Kla site, 77 had two Kla sites, 32 had three Kla sites, and four had nine or more Kla sites (Figure 1D). Among the detected Kla proteins, several were found to have multiple sites (Figure 1E). The protein with the highest Kla was the neuroblast differentiation-associated protein AHNAK (AHNAK, UniProt code:
Q09666
), which has 79 Kla sites and is a large structural scaffold protein consist of 5,890 amino acids. High expression of AHNAK is associated with poor outcomes in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma and laryngeal carcinoma and promotes the metastasis of lung cancer cells (
13
-
15
). The treacle protein (TCOF1) and nucleolin (NCL) had 16 and 15 Kla sites, respectively. We investigated the motif characteristics of the Kla sites using pLogo software to identify possible specific sequence patterns surrounding lactylated lysine residues (13 amino acids upstream and downstream of the lactylation sites) (Figure 1F). In Kla, proline (P) frequently appears to be enriched at positions -3, -2, -1, +2, and +4, while glycine (G) appears to be enriched at positions −4, +1, and +2, which is consistent with our motif analysis of Kla in immortalized Kupffer cells (
8
,
16
). The 681 Kla sites identified in prostate cancer (PC) were compared with global lactylome datasets from other cancer types to assess the uniqueness of Kla site distribution across proteins. Compared to oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) and gastric cancer (GC), 283 Kla sites were detected only in PC. Similarly, 143 Kla sites were observed exclusively in PC, when compared to gastrointestinal cancers (GIC) and colorectal cancer (CRC) (
17
,
18
).
Functional categories of protein lactylation in PC-3M cells.
The functions of proteins are closely linked to their subcellular localization. To characterize Kla protein in PC-3M cells, we performed a subcellular distribution analysis (
Figure 2
A). The results showed that Kla proteins in PC-3M cells were primarily found in the nucleus (54.1%), cytoplasm (28.0%), membrane (4.2%), secreted (1.3%), and mitochondria (1.1%). We conducted GO enrichment analysis using the DAVID online tool for proteins corresponding to Kla to better understand the biological functions in PC-3M cells (Figure 2B). DAVID is a bioinformatics tool used for the functional annotation clustering, functional classification, and enrichment analysis of genes (
19
,
20
). In GO biological process (GOBP) analysis, proteins involved in chromatin organization, mRNA splicing and mRNA processing were more likely to be lactylated. In terms of GO molecular function (GOMF), RNA binding, cadherin binding, protein binding, structural constituent of chromatin and mRNA binding were significantly enriched for lactylation. KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that Kla proteins in PC-3M cells were enriched in the spliceosome, neutrophil extracellular trap formation, and ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling.

Based on the results of GOCC analysis, the proteins were reclassified into nucleus-related and cytosol-related categories to explore the detailed roles of Kla proteins and analyzed the KEGG by group. The KEGG results for Kla proteins belonging to the nucleus are presented in Figure 2C. Kla proteins were enriched in the spliceosome, neutrophil extracellular trap formation, transcriptional misregulation in cancer, and ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling, consistent with previous studies on Kla substrates in ImKCs and human lungs (

Kla proteins in PC-3M cells are enriched in ribosomes, the cytoskeleton of muscle cells, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, the HIF-1 signaling pathway, amino acid biosynthesis, and carbon metabolism.

To identify the regulators of increased Kla levels in PC-3M cells, the expression of sirtuins (SIRT 1-7), also known as deacetylases, was assessed by immunoblotting (
Figure 3

A recent study reported that SIRT3 acts as a delactylase that removes lactyl moieties from protein lysine residues in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) (
28

Discussion
This study presents the first global profile of Kla in PC and provides valuable insights into its potential role in cancer progression. First, the correlation between Kla and PC progression can be explained by the fact that Kla expression was highest in PC-3M when comparing Kla expression in five PC cell lines. The cell lines used were, RWPE-1 (immortalized normal human prostate cells) (
29
), LNCaP and LNCap-LN3 (androgen sensitive PCa cells), and C4-2 cells (androgen-insensitive, as LNCaP’s metastatic
in vivo
-derived variant) (
30
) and PC-3 and PC-3M cells (bone metastatic PCa cells). This indicates that the cellular protein Kla levels increases as prostate cancer progresses.
We identified 681 Kla sites across 379 proteins in PC-3M cells, a highly metastatic prostate cancer cell line, highlighting the prevalence and diversity of Kla beyond histones. The lactylated proteins were enriched in key biological processes such as chromatin organization, mRNA splicing, and glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, with predominant localization in the nucleus and cytoplasm. Notably, pathways such as the spliceosome and glycolytic metabolism appear to be hotspots for Kla, suggesting a possible regulatory role for Kla in both gene expression and energy metabolism. A characteristic of the distribution of lactylated proteins detected in PC-3M cells was the KEGG analysis of the spliceosome in the nucleus and glycolysis/gluconeogenesis in the cytoplasm. In the nuclear category of our global profiling results, Kla of proteins involved in the spliceosome was consistent with the results of previous studies (
7
,
8
). Similarities have been observed between the distribution of Kla proteins on spliceosome components in gastric cancer cell lines and proteins detected as Kla substrates in prostate cancer cells (
8
). Furthermore, there is a significant overlap with Kla substrates in the spliceosome components detected in oral squamous cell carcinoma (
7
). Therefore, it is preferable to enrich Kla in the spliceosomes.
Moreover, the downregulation of SIRT3, SIRT5, and SIRT6 in PC-3M cells was correlated with increased Kla levels, indicating a potential mechanism of lactylation regulation
via
sirtuins. These findings provide a foundational understanding of Kla in PC and underscore the significance of lactylation in tumor progression. Although this study provides a comprehensive profile of Kla in prostate cancer cells, it does not assess the functional consequences of specific Kla modifications, which warrants further investigation.
