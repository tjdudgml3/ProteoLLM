Introduction
Synaptotagmins (SYTs) in animals constitute the protein family that participates in membrane trafficking as calcium sensors, mainly in the nervous system [
1
]. They contain a transmembrane domain at the N-terminus connected through the linker with two calcium-binding C2 domains at the C-terminus [
2
]. Recently, extended synaptotagmins (E-SYTs) and yeast tricalbins (TCBs), having similar structures, were defined [
3
,
4
]. However, they possess more than two C2 domains and an additional region called synaptotagmin-like mitochondrial lipid-binding protein (SMP) domain, a lipid transfer module present in proteins that regulate lipid homeostasis at membrane contact sites [
5
].
Arabidopsis, a model plant, contains proteins that share structural similarities with animal SYTs. Five of them, designated SYT1 to SYT5, possess an N-terminal transmembrane domain and two C2 domains on the opposite side [
6
]. Interestingly, they also comprise the SMP domain, which is a characteristic feature of E-SYTs. A closely related homologue, SYT6, shows even greater similarity to E-SYTs because it contains three C2 domains. SYT1 is by far the most studied member of the family in Arabidopsis. Pioneering studies using T-DNA insertion mutant [
7
] and knockdown [
8
] lines proposed the role of SYT1 in plasma membrane resealing during exposure to stresses. Consequently, the T-DNA insertion allele
syt1-2
has been used in several studies demonstrating the role of SYT1 in tolerance responses to various environmental pressures, such as mechanical [
9
] and ionic [
10
] stresses or low [
11
] and high temperature [
12
]. Intensive research using this allele, also called
syta-1
, confirmed the participation of SYT1 in cell-to-cell trafficking of the virus through plasmodesmata [
13
–
17
]. Finally, SYT1 negatively controls the two distinct immune secretory pathways to powdery mildew fungi [
18
].
As plant SYTs possess the SMP domain, recent studies have focused on the role of SYT1 and family members to confirm their presence and function at connections between the plasma membrane and the endoplasmic reticulum [
19
]. In fact, in cotyledon epidermal cells of very young seedlings growing in vitro, the accumulation of SYT1 at these contact sites was demonstrated by several studies [
9
–
11
,
20
,
21
]. There SYT1 but also SYT3, and SYT5 should help in the transportation of diacylglycerol from the plasma membrane to the endoplasmic reticulum which helps prevent plasma membrane damage and maintain its integrity [
10
,
22
].
Other members of the SYT family in Arabidopsis and other species were studied sporadically. For example, SYT2 was demonstrated to be expressed in gametophytes and essential for pollen germination and pollen tube growth [
23
]. We recently characterized SYT4 as expressed mainly in the phloem and shoot and root stem cell niches. The T-DNA insertion mutant with the C2B domain interrupted by an insertion exhibited increased sensitivity to different stresses in the root growth test [
24
].
Surprisingly, very little information is available on the consequences of the lack of
SYT1
function at the adult plant level. We have shown that the suppression of the SYT1 function in the
syt1-2
elevates the adverse effect of sodium chloride on photosynthesis [
25
]. However, we did not observe differences when plants were grown without saline irrigation. Interestingly, also, under in vitro conditions, the root growth was restricted only when seedlings were grown under salinity stress [
7
].
This study examines how sodium chloride treatment affects the proteomic profile in wild-type and
syt1-2
roots. We found many proteins whose abundance was affected by salt stress in both genotypes. However, the mutation in SYT1 affected the abundance of only a few proteins when the seedlings were grown under standard conditions, and their number did not increase much under the influence of sodium chloride. Together, this motivated us to perform a more detailed molecular biology analysis of the
syt1-2
allele. We showed that a shorter sequence was still transcribed; however, the abundance of the altered
SYT1
transcript in the mutant allele was significantly lower than the quantity of
SYT1
transcript in wild-type plants.
Materials and methods
Plant material, growth conditions, and salt treatment
Arabidopsis thaliana
, ecotype Col-0 (wild-type, WT), was used in this study as experimental material. The mutant
syt1-2
line (SAIL_775_A08 [
7
]), from the Syngenta population [
26
] and the AtSYT1-Dendra2 transgenic line [
27
,
28
] were characterized previously. Further, for actin visualization, we used the Lifeact strategy [
29
], with the codon usage for the Lifeact peptide optimized for Arabidopsis.
pAMPAT-Lifeact
plasmid construction was described previously [
30
], but the original 35 S promoter was kept in the binary plasmid
pAMPAT-MSC
(GenBank:
AY436765.1
) vector. A Lifeact-EGFP transgenic line was created by transforming the plants with Agrobacterium by using the floral-dip method.
Plants for seed production were cultivated in pots containing soil substrate (50% peat moss, 30% perlite, and 20% sand) under a temperature of 22 °C, humidity of 40‒60%, and 14 h light of 150 µmol m
− 2
s
− 1
photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD) provided by white-colored LED panels alternated with 10 h dark photoperiod. For in vitro experiments, the seeds were surface sterilized with 70% ethanol for 1 min and then with 1% sodium hypochlorite for 20 min. After washing 3 times with sterile water, seeds were sown in Petri dishes on standard cultivation medium (SCM, half Murashige and Skoog basal salts, 0.4 mg L
− 1
thiamine HCl, 100 mg L
− 1
myo-inositol, 10 g L
− 1
sucrose, 7 g L
− 1
agar). Plates were kept at 4 °C for 3 days to stratify seeds and then moved to the cultivation chamber and kept in a vertical position under permanent light of 150 µmol m
− 2
s
− 1
PPFD until moving seedlings for treatment. For proteomic and gene expression analyses, 10-day-old WT and
syt1-2
seedlings were moved onto fresh SCM or SCM with 150 mM NaCl for another 2 days. For microscopy examinations, Lifeact-eGFP and AtSYT1-Dendra2 seedlings were moved onto fresh SCM or SCM with 150 mM NaCl after 5 days of germination. Reagents and solvents of the highest analytical grade were acquired from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) if not specified otherwise.
Genotyping of the
syt1-2
allele
Genomic DNA was isolated from rosette leaves of pot-grown WT and
syt1-2
plants using the innuPREP plant DNA kit (Analytik Jena, Jena, Germany). PCR was performed with gene-specific primers located upstream (GF primer) and downstream (GR primer) of the expected insertion site, and their combinations with LB2 and RB2 T-DNA insert-specific primers. PCR amplicons were sequenced with the LB3 primer. Primer sequences are listed in Table S1.
RNA isolation, cDNA synthesis, RT-PCR, and qPCR
We used inflorescences to analyze
SYT1
transcripts in
syt1-2
and WT plants. For the analysis of the effects of salt treatment on the abundance of transcripts of different selected genes, we used the roots of seedlings. Total RNA was isolated using the RNeasy plant mini kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). After removing genomic DNA with a TURBO DNA-free kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), the first-strand cDNA was synthesized from 500 ng of total RNA using a FIREScript RT cDNA synthesis KIT (Solis BioDyne, Tartu, Estonia) and oligo-dT primer or random hexamers. Transcript abundance assessment was performed on the CFX Connect real-time PCR detection system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) with SsoAdvanced universal SYBR green supermix.

(

MONENSIN SENSITIVITY1
(
MON1

AT2G2839

Label-free quantification of proteins by mass spectrometry
Total protein extracts were digested according to the filter-aided sample preparation (FASP) protocol described earlier [
31
]. Protein aliquots of 50 µg were adjusted to 200 µL with urea buffer (8 M urea in 100 mM Tris, pH 8.5) and loaded onto Microcon Ultracel YM-10 centrifugal filters (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). Extracts were reduced with 10 mM dithiothreitol and alkylated with 50 mM iodoacetamide. After quenching the excess iodoacetamide with 10 mM dithiothreitol, the proteins were digested overnight at 37 °C using sequencing-grade trypsin (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) in an enzyme-to-protein ratio of 1:60, buffered with 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate. Next, peptide mixtures were acidified by 0.5% trifluoroacetic acid and purified on custom-made microtips filled with LiChroprep RP-18 25–40 μm particles (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). The concentration of peptides was measured by DS-11+ (DeNovix, Wilmington, DE, USA).
Peptide mixtures (500 ng) were profiled using nano liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) according to the published workflow [
32
]. Samples were loaded onto a trap column (PepMap100 C18, 300 μm × 5 mm, 5-µm particle size) (Thermo Scientific, Vilnius, Lithuania) and separated with an EASY-Spray C18 analytical column having an integrated nanospray emitter (75 μm × 500 mm, 2-µm particle size) (Thermo Scientific, Vilnius, Lithuania) on Ultimate 3000 RSLCnano system (Dionex Softron for Thermo Scientific, Germering, Germany) in a 120-min gradient (3–43% acetonitrile), and flow rate 250 nL min
− 1
. Eluted peptides were sprayed into an Orbitrap Elite mass spectrometer (ThermoFisher Scientific, Bremen, Germany), which was equipped with an EASY-Spray ion source. Spectral datasets were collected in the Top15 data-dependent mode to select precursor ions. Precursors were measured in the mass range 300‒1,700 m z
− 1
with a resolution of 120,000, and fragments were obtained by the HCD mechanism with normalized collision energy 25 and a resolution of 15,000.
The obtained datasets were processed by MaxQuant v1.6.17.0 with a built-in Andromeda search engine. Permanent modification carbamidomethylated cysteine and variable modification oxidized methionine were selected. Peptide tolerance in the first search was 20 ppm, and in the main search, after recalibration, it was 4.5 ppm. The fragment tolerance was 20 ppm. Based on the reverse decoy database search, the false discovery rate for peptides and proteins was 1%. The search was performed against Arabidopsis sequences downloaded from UniProt (
https://www.uniprot.org/
) in June 2021 (39,342 sequences) with the activated match between runs. The label-free quantification (LFQ intensities) relied on sums of precursor ion intensities of unique proteotypic peptides upon normalization by the median distribution of all ions.
Western blotting
Total proteins for detecting SYT1, tubulin α, and actin were extracted from roots of seedlings exposed to 150 mM NaCl for 48 h as described above using the Pierce Plant Total Protein Extraction Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA). Isolation buffer was supplemented with Aldrich Protease Inhibitor Cocktail for plant cell and tissue extracts (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). The concentration of proteins in extracts was determined by Pierce Rapid Gold BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA). Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to an Immun-Blot PVDF Membrane (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA), and 20 µg of total protein was loaded per line. The equal loading was verified by staining the parallel gel with Coomassie blue.
Subcellular fractions of proteins were isolated from the same material mentioned above using a Minute Plasma Membrane Protein Isolation and Cell Fractionation Kit (Invent Biotechnologies, Plymouth, MN, USA) to study SYT1 distribution. Cytosolic fraction was diluted in buffer A available in the kit, while fractions from the plasma membrane, membrane organelles, and nuclei were dissolved in the RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, 0.1% SDS, 1 mM EDTA, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Triton X-100, pH 7.4). Equal volumes of samples were loaded per line in SDS-PAGE and transferred to the Immun-Blot PVDF Membrane.
After transfer all membranes were blocked for 1 h in the solution of 5% skim milk in TBST buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 180 mM NaCl, and 0.1% Tween 20, pH 7.4), membranes were probed with antibodies recognizing SYT A (PHY0821S - PhytoAB, San Jose, CA, USA, dilution 1:3,000), plant actin (A0480, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany, dilution 1:10,000) or α-tubulin (N356, Amersham Pharmacia Biotech, Piscataway, NJ, USA, dilution 1:5,000). After washing with TBST, membranes were probed with goat anti-rabbit IgG: HRP (#403005, Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA, dilution 1:3,000) or goat anti-mouse IgG: HRP (#103005, Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA, dilution 1:3,000) secondary antibodies. After washing with TBST and TBS, the Clarity Western ECL Substrate (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) was applied onto a membrane, and the signal was captured on photographic paper, which was processed in the standard way. Original blots are presented as Supplementary Information file 1.
Confocal microscopy
Five-day-old seedlings of the AtSYT1-Dendra2 line were treated with Sirofluor (Biosupplies Australia Pty Ltd, Parkville, Victoria, Australia) to label callose in plasmodesmata or with basic fuchsine to visualize the cell wall. Both compounds were dissolved in liquid SCM at concentrations of 0.003 and 0.1%, respectively. The seedlings were treated for 10 min and then washed 3 times with SCM. Treated or untreated seedlings were mounted on microscopic slides with a solid SCM and then covered with cover glass. Imaging was performed using an Olympus FV1000 confocal laser scanning microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) with UPlanFL 20x/0.50, UPLanSApo 40x/0.90, or UPlanSApo 60x/1.35 Oil objectives. The green signal emitted by Dendra2 or GFP was excited with a 488 nm laser and captured with a 505–525 nm barrier filter. The red form of Dendra2 and basic fuchsine were excited with a 543 nm laser, and signals were collected with a 560–620 nm barrier filter. The blue signal from Sirofluor was excited with a 405 nm laser and captured using a 480–495 nm barrier filter. The photoconversion experiment was performed as described previously [
33
,
34
].
Primary root growth assay
Seeds of
syt1-2
and wild type were sown in Petri dishes on SCM and cold stratified at 4 °C for 3 days. Petri dishes were then moved to permanent light with parameters as specified above and germinated vertically for another 4 days. Then, seedlings of the same size with roots approximately 8 mm long were transferred onto the media with different concentrations of sodium chloride (0, 50, 75, 100, and 150 mM) and kept in a vertical position under permanent light. The root length increments were analyzed after two days. 10–15 seedlings of
syt1-2
were grown with corresponding wild-type seedlings on the same Petri dish. The experiments were repeated 6 times.
Statistical analysis and visualization of data
The statistical analysis of proteomic data was performed using Perseus v1.6.15.0. The output protein Group table from MaxQuant was filtered for the reverse proteins, the contaminants, and the low confidence proteins identified only by the site. After log2 transformation of the LFQ intensities, only proteins with 4 or more valid values out of 5 biological replicates in at least 1 experimental group were retained, and missing values were imputed from a lower range of the normal distribution curve. Principal component analysis was used to evaluate sources of variability among samples and replicates. Next, ANOVA was performed with permutation correction for multiple testing at
Q
≤ 0.05. Post hoc Tukey’s test was used for pairwise comparisons with the same statistical threshold. Hierarchical clustering was performed on Z-score-normalized averages of LFQ intensities to reveal protein abundance trends across experimental conditions.
To uncover cellular processes and metabolic pathways affected by sodium chloride and the mutation in the
SYT1
and to classify functional categories of proteins, the identified proteins were compared with the background libraries of gene ontology (GO) annotations from Ensembl Plants database [
35
] and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) [
36
] using the ShinyGO tool, version 0.81. STRING database was used to generate diagrams representing protein-protein interaction networks derived from proteins identified as significantly down- or up-regulated in the presence of NaCl in the medium or due to the
SYT1
mutation. Proteomic data were summarized and visualized using the SRplot tool.
Results in charts are expressed as means ± SEM. Two-way ANOVA and Student t-test were used for statistical data analysis in the remaining experiments. Graphs were prepared using Excel (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA); figures were processed with the Publisher (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA) and Photoshop (Adobe, San Jose, CA, USA). The fluorescence signal intensities and the intensities of bands in the Western blot assay were evaluated by the ImageJ package (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). In charts, means with different letters above the bars differ significantly
(P
< 0.05).
Results
Salt stress caused a decrease in SYT1 abundance in the plasma membrane fraction and its lower turnover at the cell periphery.
Under standard conditions, a strong signal was detected at the cell periphery of meristematic root cells; however, the signal was also present inside the cells, forming a net and a circle around the nucleus (Fig.
1
A). At the cortical plane, the fusion protein exhibited a reticulated beads-and-string pattern, similar to that observed in cotyledon pavement cells (Fig.
1
B). When we studied SYT1 in the elongated cells treated with basic fuchsine, which labels the cell wall, the green signal only partially overlapped with the red signal emitted by this dye (Fig.
1
C). The green signal was on the sides of the red cell wall, but in many places, the cell wall was traversed by the green signal (arrows in Fig.
1
C). Finally, we studied the co-localization of SYT1 signal with the signal emitted by sirofluor, which marks callose in plasmodesmata. The blue signal emitted by sirofluor was in the form of separate patches, and the green signal often co-localized with these blue patches (arrows in Fig.
1
D).
Then, we wanted to find out whether sodium chloride affects the amount and patterning of SYT1. At first, we performed a quantitative laser microscopic investigation of SYT1 distribution. We found that the relative abundance of the SYT1-Dendra2 fusion protein at the cell periphery decreased after 24 h of NaCl treatment (Fig.
1
E), and an even more pronounced decrease was observed after 48 h (Fig.
1
F).
Next, we examined SYT1 abundance using Western blot analysis and a commercially available antibody raised against a peptide from the C-terminus of SYT1. We found that SYT1 abundance in the protein extract from roots was not visibly affected by 48 h of NaCl treatment (Fig.
1
G). A band of 65 kDa, which corresponds to SYT1, was not observed in syt1-2 seedlings. We then analyzed whether sodium salt causes the redistribution of SYT1 in cells and investigated the abundance of SYT1 in specific cellular fractions. In the roots of seedlings growing on a standard medium, we detected SYT1 in the nuclear fraction, the fraction of inner membranes, and the plasma membrane fraction. However, only a feeble signal was present in the cytosol (Fig.
1
H). In seedlings growing under salinity, we recorded a significant decrease in SYT1 abundance in the plasma membrane fraction. We next examined the effect of NaCl on the turnover of SYT1 at the cell periphery and in the cytoplasm. We employed the photoconvertible technology using the Dendra2 tag [
33
,
34
,
37
,
38
]. The time-lapse experiment showed that the sodium chloride caused a slowdown in the recovery of green signal intensity, emitted by the newly synthesized fusion protein, in the cytoplasm and at the periphery of the root meristematic cells. In contrast, the decrease in the intensity of the red signal, emitted by the old fusion SYT1-Dendra2 population existing already before conversion, was slowed at the periphery of the cells, while no effect was observed for the cytoplasm (Fig.
1
I, J). Decelerated protein turnover was even more pronounced in the cells of the elongation and differentiation root zones (Fig.
1
I).

Essential metabolic pathways and biological functions were affected by salt stress
We selected the following most affected pathways based on the GO enrichment results: glucosinolate biosynthesis and metabolism, antioxidant enzymes and proteins related to oxidative stress, ascorbate-glutathione cycle, proteins related to water stress, protein synthesis and folding, components and regulatory elements of the proteasome, and lipid transfer proteins.
Proteins involved in glucosinolate biosynthesis and metabolism
After salt treatment, significant changes in the abundance were detected in 18 of 37 identified proteins of the glucosinolate biosynthetic and metabolism pathway. Most proteins (14) were less abundant, and 4 were more abundant under salt treatment. Notably, there were more altered proteins in the wild-type than in
syt1-2

Sodium chloride-sensitive proteins contribute to different steps of biosynthesis, 6 proteins are involved in the side-chain elongation of amino acids, 1 participating in the formation of the glucosinolates core, and 2 proteins modifying the natural side-chain of glucosinolates (Fig. S1A). All but one of the significantly altered biosynthetic proteins were depleted. Among the 21 identified proteins in the glucosinolate metabolic (degradation) process (Fig. S1B-D), 10 were affected significantly in response to NaCl.
Antioxidant enzymes and oxidative stress-related proteins
Our proteomic analysis, summarised in Fig. S2, reveals 40 proteins from 89 successfully quantified ones that participate in scavenging and detoxifying generated reactive oxygen species. Of the peroxidases, the amounts in 12 cases were significantly affected by salt stress, and a similar trend was observed in both the wild type and the mutant (Fig. S2A). The vast majority of peroxidases were depleted (9), with only 3 being more abundant in
syt1-2
and 1 of these in the wild type. Variability in response to salt stress was observed for the rest of the proteins. For 2 crucial enzymes, the accumulation of CATALASE 3 (Fig. S2B) and the depletion of Phenylalanine ammonia-lyase 1 (Fig. S2C) were observed for both genotypes. Finally, a significant decrease in the abundance of cytosolic L-ASCORBATE PEROXIDASE 1, only in the

Ascorbate-glutathione cycle proteins
We identified 60 proteins participating in the processes of this cycle, and 41 selected proteins are shown in Fig. S3. Most belonged to glutathione S-transferases tau (Fig. S3A), phi, theta, zeta, and lambda classes and DHAR (Fig. S3B). The significance of differences in abundance was confirmed in 14 of them. Water stress-related proteins

Discussion
SYT1 has a lower turnover and partially relocates to the inner membrane system upon salt stress
Salt stress is a major problem globally, causing significant losses in world crop production [
41
]. Many genes have been identified that affect responses to this abiotic stressor, including plant synaptotagmins.
SYT1
, the dominantly expressed gene of the family, is particularly involved in responses to abiotic and biotic stresses in the model organism Arabidopsis. Originally, plant SYTs were believed to help repair the damaged plasma membrane [
7
,
8
] through vesicles, but recent studies suggested that plant SYTs may act as animal E-SYTs [
19
]. In epidermal cotyledon cells, SYT1, SYT3 and SYT5 are enriched at the endoplasmic reticulum and plasma membrane contact sites [
11
,
20
], where they should help in transporting diacylglycerol from PM to ER to prevent PM damage [
10
,
21
]. We confirmed the accumulation of SYT1 at the cell periphery in root cells in this study, although the protein is also present in intracellular membranes, as it is documented by microscopic (Fig.
1
A, B, E, F) and Western blot analysis (Fig.
1
H). In our study, SYT1 at the periphery partially co-localizes with a marker for callose in plasmodesmata (Fig.
1
D). This is not surprising since SYT1 is known to function in the cell-to-cell movement of viruses via plasmodesmata [
13
,
14
].
Furthermore, we observed a change in the localization patterning of SYT1 upon application of salt stress when the ratio of SYT1 at the periphery to the amount of protein in the inner membrane system was reduced (Fig.
1
E,H). Interestingly, SYT1 turnover was also slowed under salt stress, as we showed using the photoconvertible probe Dendra2 (Fig.
1
I and J). Whether this is a functional adaptation of SYT1 to salt stress or simply a consequence of a general slowing of intracellular processes due to adverse conditions during salt stress requires further analysis.
Accumulated proteins: Salt stress-associated, water deficit-related, and without direct function
In this study, we sought to provide new insight into the function of the
SYT1
by proteomic analysis of its mutant
syt1-2
line under salt stress conditions. This T-DNA insertion allele has been used frequently in previous studies [
7
,
10
,
13
–
17
,
21
]. The vast majority of proteins that we revealed as affected by salt stress in WT and
syt1-2
were not yet known to be involved in salt stress responses (Data S1 and S3). In fact, we identified only 18 proteins in our dataset that increased in amount and that could be found in the gene ontology response to salt stress or related GOs. Only 6 depleted proteins in this study were listed in those categories.
Among the accumulated proteins, we mention, for example, the LOW-TEMPERATURE-INDUCED 78 KDA PROTEIN (Fig. S4D, Fig.
3
F), also known as DESICCATION-RESPONSIVE PROTEIN 29 A, whose amount increased more than 20-fold in WT and nearly 16-fold in
syt1-2
. ALCOHOL DEHYDROGENASE CLASS-P (ADH1), with a more than 16-fold increased abundance in both genotypes (Fig. S3C, Fig.
3
A), the unknown protein (
Q9FE54
, AT1G16850) with the transmembrane domain, with 4 times higher abundance (Fig.
3
O), and LIPID TRANSFER PROTEIN EARLI 1, increased 2.5- and 3.5-fold (Fig. S8).
In addition to the toxic effects of Na and Cl accumulated in plants, the deleterious effects of salt also result from reduced water availability. In this way, salt stress leads to water deficit or osmotic stress [
41
]. In fact, when we analyzed accumulated proteins in our study, 22 of them were already known to be affected by the response to water deprivation and related GO categories. The most accumulated protein ever in our study (more than 120-fold in WT and 40-fold in
syt1-2
) was LEA family member ABA-RESPONSE PROTEIN (
Q9M892
, Fig. S4A, Fig.
3
G). Our analysis revealed a remarkable increase in the amount of the STRESS-INDUCED PROTEIN KIN1 (more than 30-fold in both genotypes) and, to a lesser extent, for the close homolog KIN2, also known as COLD-RESPONSIVE 6.6 (Fig. S4 A, Fig.
3
I). Finally, NON-SPECIFIC LIPID-TRANSFER PROTEIN 2 increased in amount 4- to 6-fold in salt-treated roots (Fig. S8).
Importantly, we found the accumulation of several proteins under salt conditions without prior direct association with NaCl toxicity or water deficit. Among those listed in Data S1 and S3, we mention VEGETATIVE STORAGE PROTEIN 2 (increased in amount 8.6-fold in WT and 4-fold in
syt1-2
(see also Fig.
3
K), PROBABLE FRUCTOSE-BISPHOSPHATE ALDOLASE 2 (around 4-fold), UDP-GLYCOSYLTRANSFERASE 89B1 involved in flavone synthesis (7- and 5-fold), FORMATE DEHYDROGENASE, MITOCHONDRIAL (around 5-fold), PROBABLE S-ADENOSYLMETHIONINE-DEPENDENT METHYLTRANSFERASE (6- and 4-fold) and VACUOLAR-SORTING RECEPTOR 1 accumulated around 3-fold. Further, we have found that RIBULOSE BISPHOSPHATE CARBOXYLASE SMALL CHAIN 1 A and RIBULOSE BISPHOSPHATE CARBOXYLASE LARGE CHAIN increased in amounts 7- to 15-fold in both genotypes under salt stress. The presence of these photosynthetic proteins in roots and the remarkable increase in amount under stress is puzzling.
Depleted proteins showed a lower magnitude and effect size
When we analyzed the proteins with reduced abundance after salt application, we found in our list (Data S1 and S3) only a few proteins with prior salt stress association, including MITOGEN-ACTIVATED PROTEIN KINASE 6, HEAT SHOCK PROTEIN 90 − 2, PLASMA MEMBRANE-ASSOCIATED CATION-BINDING PROTEIN 2, or BETA-GLUCOSIDASE 21 and 22. However, even these were depleted moderately. The others were not associated with salt stress. Overall, the decrease in abundance of the most altered proteins was not as drastic as the increase in abundance. For example, Cytochrome P450 71A1, showing the largest effect size, depleted 16-fold in WT and 6-fold in
syt1-2
under salt treatment, and another member of the same family, CYTOCHROME P450 705A5, around 4-fold (Fig.
3
E). PEROXIDASES 21 and 50 depleted 6- to 8-fold (Fig. S2A and Fig.
3
J), and INDOLE GLUCOSINOLATE O-METHYLTRANSFERASE 1 and 3 depleted 4- to 5-fold in both genotypes (Fig. S1B). Interestingly, there was a 7.5-fold decrease in KUNITZ TRYPSIN INHIBITOR 5 in WT and 5.6-fold in
syt1-2
(Fig.
3
L); however, its homolog KUNITZ TRYPSIN INHIBITOR 4 was significantly more abundant under salt in both genotypes (Data S3). METHYLTHIOALKYLMALATE SYNTHASE 3, and a member of the Major latex protein family (
Q93WB5
) was depleted roughly 4-fold (Fig.
3
C).
The cytoskeleton is impaired by salt stress
Based on GO enrichment analysis (Data S2), we found the cytoskeleton and associated proteins to be greatly affected by salt stress. It is well known that actin filaments and microtubules are very dynamically rearranging in response to endogenous or exogenous cues, and such reorganizations are necessary for the plant to adapt to environmental challenges [
42
]. Several studies have also indicated a close relationship between the plant cytoskeleton and plant salt stress tolerance [
43
]. Many cytoskeleton-associated proteins are well known to mediate cytoskeleton reorganization, and therefore, the rate of rearrangement of microtubules and microfilaments also depends on their availability. We found several actin-associated proteins, such as ADF3, VILLIN 2 and 3 or PROFFILIN 1 (Fig.
5
D) and several microtubule-associated proteins, among them TUBULIN-FOLDING COFACTOR A, or several dynamins (Fig.
5
B), whose abundance was altered by salt stress. Surprisingly, we have found that 9 out of 10 quantified tubulin subunits were statistically significantly affected by salt stress; specifically, they were depleted (Fig.
5
A). Finally, the abundance of two vegetative actins, ACTIN2/8 and ACTIN7, was also significantly reduced under salt stress (Fig.
5
C). We were able to confirm the depletion of actin and α-tubulin by Western blot assay, as shown in Fig.
5
E. Interestingly, Chun et al. [
44
] have found TUB3, TUB4, TUB7, TUB9, and ACT7 accumulated in the cell culture established from Arabidopsis roots and adapted to salt stress. Further, we demonstrated that salt causes the breakdown of the actin network in the swelling cells of the root transition zone after several hours under sodium chloride stress, and the loss of polymeric actin in cells after salt application (Fig.
5
G). The image also shows the reduction in size of the apical meristem. Since root growth is dependent on cell division and elongation, and both vital attributes are dependent on the cytoskeleton [
45
–
47
], we can conclude that an insufficient amount of cytoskeleton-building units, as well as modifying proteins caused by salt stress, contribute to plant stunting under salt stress conditions.
Depletion of glucosynolate biosynthesis and rebalancing of antioxidant enzymes and water deficit-related proteins
Glucosinolates are secondary metabolites commonly accumulated by members of
Brassicaceae
. Multiple studies confirm their importance in plant response to biotic and abiotic stress [
48
]. Short-chain aliphatic glucosinolates have been proposed to participate in water balance maintenance under salt stress, but the exact mechanism and the relationship between glucosinolates and aquaporins are still not fully explained [
49
,
50
]. However, surprisingly, we observed an effect opposite to that in previous studies, and NaCl caused the depletion of multiple enzymes involved in the glucosinolates biosynthetic pathway, as seen in Fig. S1. Further, exposure to various environmental stresses, including salinity, triggers the accumulation of reactive oxygen species [
51
,
52
], causing oxidative damage to cell structures. As expected, we recorded changes in an abundance of dozens of enzymes participating in ROS and H
2
O
2
regulation. Two classical peroxidases from class III, which were among the most depleted proteins, were already mentioned above. However, other members of the family, such as PEROXIDASE 16, 27, 45, 56, 57, 69, and 71 were also significantly depleted under stress, but three family members, PEROXIDASE 54, 15, and 22, were more abundant, and the increase was more pronounced in
syt1-2
. Interestingly, the most abundant members, such as PEROXIDASE 23, 32, 34, and 37, were not affected by salt (Fig. S2 A). In Arabidopsis, peroxidases from class III form multiple families of 73 members [
53
]. The exact function of the individual peroxidases is unknown, and their activity does not always perfectly correspond to quantity. Glutathione peroxidases use glutathione as an electron donor when converting toxic hydrogen peroxide to water. Reduced glutathione is subsequently regenerated in the glutathione-ascorbate cycle [
54
]. Enhanced tolerance of transgenic plants overexpressing GST genes to sodium chloride stress is well documented [
55
,
56
]. Our results are inconclusive because we observed both an increase and a decrease in the amount of proteins in this pathway.
NaCl in higher doses in the cultivation medium creates hyperosmotic conditions and limits water uptake into the plants. Apart from osmotic stress-related proteins mentioned above, we detected LEA46, LEA14, LEA30, an unknown protein
Q8S8R1
from LEA group 6, and related dehydrins XERO 2, ERD10, and COR47, which accumulated considerably under salt stress (Fig. S4 A). Further, inhibition of aquaporin activities and water transport [
57
] and a decrease in the abundance of their transcripts [
58
] were described as a direct consequence of water deficiency. We observed a significant depletion in three aquaporins (Fig. S4 C), as well as in other members under stress. Finally, we have noticed a significantly increased abundance in SUCROSE SYNTHASE 1 and 3 (Fig. S4D), which are involved in the synthesis of sucrose. Although differentially expressed in various organs, both genes were induced at the transcriptional level by stresses such as low temperature, water deprivation or osmotic stress [
59
,
60
]. Increased protein abundance is not surprising since accumulating soluble sugars is an effective strategy to avoid desiccation.
Impaired protein synthesis and folding are accompanied by depleted components of the regulatory proteasome subunit
Effective adaptation to stress conditions is closely associated with the synthesis and degradation of proteins. In general, protein synthesis is reduced under adverse conditions, and it is not surprising that almost all components of protein biosynthesis, which are significantly altered, are depleted under the influence of salt, as seen in the case of ribosomal proteins. Interestingly, more ribosomal proteins were depleted in WT than in
syt1-2
. Nevertheless, this happened because, in the mutant, the levels of these proteins were reduced slightly even under standard conditions (Fig. S5). When we analyzed proteins involved in protein folding, almost all significantly affected proteins were depleted (Fig. S6), and notably more in
syt1-2
(20) than in WT (10). It is well known that salt affects protein folding thermodynamics [
61
]. Damaged proteins by oxidation or misfolded proteins have to be effectively eliminated. Proteolysis mostly occurs in 26 S proteasomes via an ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis pathway [
62
]. Although microarray data showed changes in the expression of components of the 20 S and 19 S proteasome subunits [
63
], our proteomic study revealed alterations only in proteins of 19 S regulatory particles (Fig. S7 A, B). In contrast, proteins of 20 S core particles were not statistically significantly affected by NaCl (Fig. S7 C, D). Interestingly, according to Kurepa et al. [
64
], the protein degradation equilibrium under stress is shifted in favor of Ub-independent proteolysis mediated by 20 S core protease at the expense of Ub-dependent degradation mediated by the complete 26 S proteasome. The reduction in regulatory particle proteins can be explained by the fact that they are unnecessary, as the 20 S protease is sufficient to degrade oxidized proteins produced massively upon salt stress.
Why does the
syt1-2
allele manifest a weak phenotype?
Given the strong and ubiquitous expression of the
SYT1
[
7
], it is surprising that the
syt1-2
allele has no apparent phenotype when seedlings grow in vitro under standard conditions or plants in pots, but inhibition of root growth or decreasing photosynthetic efficiency appears under sodium chloride stress as demonstrated previously [
7
,
25
] and also in this study (Fig.
6
A and B). Our proteomic analysis also revealed a relatively small number of proteins with altered abundance in
syt1-2
compared to the wild type. A common explanation for the lack of phenotype in mutants is the possible redundancy of homologs, as has been suggested for SYT5 and SYT1 or SYT3 and SYT1 in studies addressing the role of these proteins at the plasma membrane-endoplasmic reticulum contact sites [
10
,
21
,
65
]. However, it cannot be ruled out that the
syt1-2
allele is a partial loss-of-function mutant. It should be mentioned that according to the TAIR database, the T-DNA insertion in the
syt1-2

When we analyzed the sequence of the possible
SYT1
transcript in the mutant, we found it could encode a protein that has the last 55 amino acids of the original SYT1 replaced by a 30 amino acid chain encoded by the DNA filler and the LB2 border sequence upstream of the TGA stop codon present in this sequence (Table S2). Indeed, we confirmed the presence of an altered
SYT1
transcript in
syt1-2
whose 3’ end is encoded by filler and T-DNA sequences, even using cDNA synthesized with oligo-dT primers (Fig.
6
K). However, one may argue that the C terminus of the C2B domain was also partially disrupted, and the latter might not be functional. In fact, this domain in the mutant protein lost 34–37 amino acids (depending on the program used to detect the protein domains) compared to the wild-type protein. However, when we did a detailed analysis of the domain in the mutant protein, all prediction programs showed the presence of the C2B domain in the mutant protein with a score almost identical to the prediction for the wild-type protein (Table S2). This suggests that the new amino acid stretch in the mutant protein could functionally replace the one in the original protein. However, it is also a fact that we were unable to detect SYT1 in
syt1-2
by Western blot analysis (Fig.
1
G). However, a commercial antibody used in this study grew against a peptide at the C terminus of the protein, not present in the mutant protein. Finally, the proteomic analysis also failed to confirm SYT1 in the mutant, and SYT1 peptides were present only in the wild-type samples, yet not far from the quantification limit. This was because sample preparation was not ideal for membrane proteins. As seen in Fig.
1
and H, SYT1 is present primarily in plasma membrane and inner membrane fractions. Thus, the presence of an altered SYT1 in the
syt1-2
cannot be ruled out. It is exciting that SYT4, which we have recently characterized [
24
], had an insertion at a similar location in the gene and showed growth inhibition only under stress. We concluded that
syt1-2
is a weak allele, and a true knockout allele of this vital gene should be created using new approaches such as CRISPR/Cas9.
