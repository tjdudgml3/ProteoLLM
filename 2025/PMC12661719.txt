Introduction
Ovarian aging is considered the “pacemaker” of systemic female aging, with its progression typically preceding that of other tissues and organs. The ovary is a vital reproductive and endocrine organ in women, responsible for producing oocytes and secreting steroid hormones, thus playing a central role in female fertility, endocrine stability, and overall health [
1
]. Ovarian aging can be categorized into physiological and pathological types. Physiological aging occurs naturally with age and is characterized by gradual follicular depletion and functional decline, whereas pathological aging manifests prematurely due to internal or external insults such as chemotherapy, genetic mutations, oxidative stress, superovulation, or environmental toxins [
2
–
4
], leading to early follicular apoptosis and diminished ovarian reserve. Despite similarities in clinical manifestations, the two types differ markedly in their underlying pathophysiological mechanisms and molecular regulatory pathways. Accumulating evidence indicates that metabolic dysregulation, oxidative stress, immune imbalance, and chronic inflammation are critical contributors to pathological ovarian aging [
2
,
5
–
7
]. However, the precise molecular interactions among these factors and their roles in driving ovarian dysfunction remain incompletely understood. Notably, systematic investigations into the distinct regulatory pathways and therapeutic targets of different ovarian aging types are still lacking.
Cyclophosphamide (CTX) and busulfan (BUS) are classic chemotherapeutic agents commonly used in clinical cancer treatment. They exhibit severe reproductive toxicity and have been used to induce ovarian aging in animal models [
8
,
9
]. Nonetheless, whether these drug-induced models can accurately recapitulate the features of natural ovarian senescence—and how their molecular mechanisms compare—remains unclear. We hypothesize that, beyond direct cytotoxic effects on growing follicles, CTX–BUS may also trigger persistent oxidative stress and activate xenobiotic metabolism pathways(such as cytochrome P450–mediated detoxification), thereby promoting gradual ovarian dysfunction resembling natural aging. In clinical practice, the early signs of ovarian senescence are often insidious. Although anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) is commonly used to assess ovarian reserve, its levels are susceptible to a range of influencing factors [
10
–
14
], and reliance on a single biomarker limits diagnostic accuracy [
15
,
16
]. Therefore, there is a pressing need to identify more sensitive and specific early predictive indicators to facilitate timely intervention.
Proteomics, a high-throughput mass spectrometry-based technology, enables systematic analysis of protein expression patterns, functional pathways, and post-translational modifications across various physiological and pathological states [
17
,
18
]. It offers unique advantages in elucidating molecular mechanisms underlying ovarian aging.
In this study, using quantitative proteomics, we comprehensively analyzed ovarian tissues from normal control mice (CT group), chemotherapy-induced ovarian aging mice (CH group), and naturally aged mice (OLD group). Through differential protein screening, GO, KEGG, and WikiPathways enrichment analysis, clustering, and qRT-PCR validation, our aims were: (1) to evaluate the CTX-BUS-induced ovarian aging model in terms of histomorphology, hormone levels, and proteomic profiles, and to assess whether it can effectively mimic physiological ovarian aging; (2) to compare key enrichment pathways and differential proteins between pathological and physiological ovarian aging to delineate their molecular differences; and (3) to identify proteins and pathways with significant alterations for early prediction and potential therapeutic targeting of ovarian aging, thus providing both theoretical insight and experimental evidence for intervention strategies. Materials and methods
Reagents and instruments
The detailed list of main reagents and experimental instruments used in this study is provided in Appendix Table S1 and Tables S2.
Animals
Female specific-pathogen-free (SPF) grade C57BL/6 mice aged 8 weeks and 18 months were obtained from Zhuhai Bestest Biotech Co., Ltd. (License No. SCXK (Yue) 2020-0051). All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with ethical guidelines and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Approval No. 20241011001). Mice were provided with ad libitum access to food and water and maintained under standardized environmental conditions (temperature: 20 ± 2 °C, humidity: 45–55%, 12-hour light/dark cycle). Animals were randomly housed in groups of 5–6 per cage. Any mice that died prematurely were excluded from the analysis.
Animal model establishment
Chemotherapy-induced ovarian aging (CH group, 8 weeks old) was modeled by intraperitoneal injection of 30 mg/kg Busulfan (B2635-10G, Sigma-Aldrich) and 120 mg/kg Cyclophosphamide (C0768-1, Sigma-Aldrich), both dissolved in DMSO (DH105-2, Sigma-Aldrich), to induce pathological ovarian aging. The physiological ovarian aging group (OLD group) consisted of 18-month-old mice. Control group mice received an injection of DMSO alone.
General observation and estrous cycle monitoring
Throughout the modeling period, mice were observed daily for changes in coat condition, water and food intake, activity, and behavior. Vaginal epithelial cells were collected at the same time each day and stained with Gram’s method. Estrous cycles were evaluated microscopically. A regular estrous cycle was defined as lasting 4–5 days; cycles longer than 5 days or with disordered staging were considered abnormal.
Ovarian morphological assessment and follicle counting
At 21 days after modeling, mice were sacrificed and ovaries were collected. The tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (LGSJ01-333-1, Biosharp), dehydrated, paraffin-embedded, sectioned at a thickness of 5 μm, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). The largest ovarian section and its five consecutive adjacent sections were selected for analysis. Ovarian morphology was examined under a light microscope, and the numbers of primordial, primary, secondary, and atretic follicles were counted. When the follicle type was difficult to determine, consecutive sections were used to assist in classification.
Serum hormone detection via ELISA
Serum samples were thawed and assayed for follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), estradiol (E2), and AMH levels using ELISA kits (Elabscience, China), following manufacturer instructions. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm. Modeling success in the CH group was confirmed by evaluating vaginal cytology, ovarian histology, and follicle counts, and preliminarily compared with the OLD group.
Protein extraction and trypsin digestion
Ovarian tissues stored at − 80 °C were ground under liquid nitrogen and lysed in four volumes of lysis buffer (8 M urea, protease inhibitors, etc.). After ultrasonic disruption, samples were centrifuged at 12,000 ×g at 4 °C for 10 min, and supernatants collected for protein quantification. Equal amounts of protein were precipitated with 20% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) at 4 °C for 2 h, centrifuged at 4500 ×g, and washed 2–3 times with cold acetone. Pellets were air-dried and dissolved in 200 mM TEAB buffer. Proteins were digested overnight with trypsin at a 1:50 ratio, followed by reduction and alkylation with DTT and IAM, respectively.
LC-MS/MS analysis
Peptides were separated using a NanoElute ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography system with mobile phases A (0.1% formic acid + 2% acetonitrile) and B (0.1% formic acid + acetonitrile). The gradient elution was as follows: 6–24% B (0–70 min), 24–35% B (70–84 min), 35–80% B (84–87 min), 80% B (87–90 min), at a flow rate of 450 nL/min. Separated peptides were analyzed using a timsTOF Pro mass spectrometer (2.0 kV voltage, 100–1700 m/z scan range), operating in PASEF mode with 10 PASEF scans per full MS scan and a dynamic exclusion time of 30 s.

Protein annotation, classification, enrichment, and clustering
Differentially expressed proteins were identified based on fold change and Student’s t-test (
P
< 0.05), referencing mean and coefficient of variation (CV < 0.1) for replicates or relative quantitation for non-replicates. KEGG classification was used to generate heatmaps, focusing on significantly enriched pathways (
P
< 0.05). GO annotation was performed using eggNOG-mapper and covered the categories of cellular components (CC), molecular functions (MF), and biological processes (BP). Enrichment was further supported by KEGG and WikiPathways databases. Hierarchical clustering was applied to explore pathway-function correlations. One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD test was used to identify significantly different proteins (
P
< 0.05), which were then subjected to k-means clustering and heatmap visualization.
Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis
Proteins with |log2FC| >0.80 and detected in both experimental groups were selected for further analysis. To identify potential interactions and functional relationships, these proteins were combined with known clinical predictors of ovarian aging, such as FSH. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks were constructed using the STRING database (
https://string-db.org/
, v11.5) with a confidence score threshold > 0.4, followed by network visualization and analysis.
Shared differential protein screening
Differential proteins present in both experimental groups were analyzed using Metascape (
https://metascape.org/gp
) for enrichment analysis, selecting the GO, KEGG, Reactome, CORUM, and WikiPathways databases. Enriched terms were selected with
P
< 0.01, enrichment factor > 1.5, and containing ≥ 3 genes. Functionally similar pathways were clustered using Kappa statistics, and representative terms were extracted. Key pathways and target proteins were identified and validated via qPCR.
Statistical analysis
Data were analyzed using SPSS 20.0 and visualized with GraphPad Prism 9.5.0. For comparisons among three groups, one-way ANOVA was used. Depending on the homogeneity of variance, either LSD or Dunnett’s T3 test was applied for post-hoc pairwise comparisons.
Results

Mice in the CH and OLD groups exhibited typical aging phenotypes, including reduced locomotor activity, sparse fur, poor mental status, and decreased appetite. Estrous cycle monitoring showed that mice in the Control group exhibited a regular cycle averaging 4–5 days, whereas those in the CH and OLD groups showed disrupted cycles, characterized by prolonged phases, stagnation, or irregular transitions (Fig.

A). Body weight, ovarian weight, and ovarian index were measured (Table
1
), and both CH and OLD groups showed significant decreases in ovarian weight and ovarian index compared to the Control group (
P
< 0.05). H&E staining revealed intact ovarian structure and normal follicular distribution in the Control group, while the CH and OLD groups showed a reduction in mature follicles and corpora lutea, along with an increase in atretic follicles (Fig.
2
B). Serum hormone analysis further supported ovarian dysfunction (Fig.
2
C), showing significantly reduced AMH and E2 levels, and elevated FSH levels in both the CH and OLD groups (
P
< 0.05). Collectively, these findings confirm significant ovarian dysfunction in both CH and OLD groups, with a high degree of consistency, suggesting that the chemically induced ovarian aging model adequately recapitulates physiological ovarian aging.

Quality assessment of proteomic data
To evaluate the overall trends in protein expression profiles and biological reproducibility, we performed principal component analysis (PCA), Pearson correlation analysis, and relative standard deviation (RSD) plotting on the quantitative proteomic data from the CT, CH, and OLD groups. The PCA results (Fig.

Collectively, these results indicate that the proteomic data are of high quality, with reliable reproducibility and distinct protein expression patterns among the groups, providing a solid foundation for subsequent differential protein screening and functional enrichment analyses.

Protein difference analysis and ANOVA-Based clustering of differential proteins
Pairwise comparisons of differentially expressed proteins among the three groups identified 298 proteins (142 upregulated, 156 downregulated) in the CH vs. CT comparison, 299 proteins in the OLD vs. CH comparison, and 355 proteins in the OLD vs. CT comparison (Fig.
4
A). Detailed information for all differential proteins, including fold changes, p-values, and regulation type (UP/DOWN relative to the corresponding control group for each comparison), is provided in Supplementary Table S3. Hierarchical clustering and heatmap analysis revealed six distinct clusters of differential protein expression (Fig.
4
B, D and E). Cluster 1 was primarily associated with DNA replication and cell cycle regulation, whereas Cluster 4 was enriched in lipid metabolism and energy production pathways. Other clusters were enriched in mitochondrial function, immune response, and neurodegenerative pathways. Volcano plot analyses (Fig.
4
C) revealed significant changes in protein expression across comparisons. In the CH vs. CT comparison, Gpnmb, H1-4, Atp5if1, Atp6v0d2, and Cyp7a1 were significantly upregulated—proteins implicated in cellular metabolism, energy homeostasis, and inflammatory regulation—whereas Nlrp5, Ooep, Nlrp4f, Uhrf1, and Mtmr14 were downregulated, reflecting roles in oocyte development and cell proliferation. In the OLD vs. CT comparison, Gpnmb and Atp6v0d2 remained upregulated, accompanied by increased Tpsb2, A1bg, and Plvap, suggesting enhanced immunomodulatory and inflammatory processes during aging. Downregulated proteins included Hmgb3, Tsen15, Ybx2, Rpl38, and Calb1, which are involved in nucleic acid metabolism, protein translation, cell differentiation, and calcium signaling. We identified ten overlapping significantly altered proteins (five upregulated, five downregulated) in both the CH/CT and OLD/CT comparisons as potential biomarkers for ovarian aging. Comparative analysis between CH and OLD groups revealed upregulation of Ighm, A1bg, Tpsb2, Cma1, and Ca1, and downregulation of Hmgb3, Rpl38, Rpl31, Abhd2, and Tsem15. These findings indicate that both chemotherapy-induced and physiological ovarian aging share enrichment in DNA replication, cell cycle regulation, energy metabolism, inflammation, and immune response pathways, yet exhibit distinct molecular characteristics.

Incorporating FSH into the PPI network revealed interactions among CYP17A1, LHCGR, AMH, and FSHB, suggesting CYP17A1 and LHCGR as potential novel predictors of ovarian aging. Further enrichment analysis of shared differentially expressed proteins highlighted significant involvement in inflammatory and oxidative stress pathways, such as detoxification (GO:0098754), regulation of inflammatory response (GO:0050727), and regulation of small GTPase-mediated signal transduction (GO:0051056). Discussion
In this study, we applied quantitative proteomics to analyze 6,968 proteins from ovarian tissues of healthy controls, chemotherapy-induced senescent mice, and naturally aged mice, thereby elucidating distinct molecular mechanisms underlying physiological and pathological ovarian aging and identifying several candidate key targets.
Model evaluation of CTX-BUS-induced ovarian senescence
Cyclophosphamide (CTX) is a DNA-alkylating chemotherapeutic agent whose metabolites cross-link guanine residues, thereby impeding DNA replication and transcription. Busulfan (BUS) is another alkylating drug extensively employed in oncology and for bone marrow ablation before transplantation [
19
]. Alkylating agents have been associated with ovarian insufficiency in up to 42% of treated women [
20
], and the CTX-BUS regimen is a standard method for establishing murine models of pathological ovarian aging [
8
,
9
,
21
]. Nevertheless, critical questions remain regarding the extent to which this model recapitulates natural ovarian aging in mice, and how its tissue-, cellular-, and molecular-level alterations compare.
Cellular senescence is characterized by a stress-induced, stable cell cycle arrest [
22
,
23
]. In response to stimuli such as DNA damage, telomere attrition, or metabolic perturbations, cells enter an irreversible growth arrest and acquire a senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), secreting chemokines, pro-inflammatory cytokines, reactive oxygen species (ROS), pro-angiogenic factors, and proteases [
24
]. Through SASP, senescent cells engage the immune system—including T cells, macrophages, and natural killer cells—to contribute to tissue homeostasis [
24
]. Aging is accompanied by immunosenescence, characterized by diminished clearance of senescent cells and their subsequent accumulation, thereby exacerbating chronic inflammation—or “inflammaging” [
25
,
26
].
We compared chemotherapy-induced and natural ovarian aging models at multiple levels, observing concordant changes in body weight, ovarian index, follicle counts, and serum AMH, E2, and FSH levels in both models. Proteomic profiling further identified enrichment of DNA replication (e.g., DNA-dependent replication, DNA conformation change), cell cycle regulation (e.g., G1 to S transition), and immune-related pathways (e.g., phagocytosis, engulfment, B cell receptor signaling) in both models. These shared pathways align with canonical hallmarks of cellular senescence and its mechanistic underpinnings. Furthermore, WikiPathways clustering showed significant co-enrichment in ovarian dysfunction and infertility pathways (e.g., ovarian infertility), underscoring the deleterious impact of CTX-BUS on ovarian tissue, consistent with clinical reports of severe ovarian damage following alkylating-agent therapy [
27
].
In the KEGG enrichment analysis, we found that the Drug metabolism – cytochrome P450 and Vitamin B6 metabolism pathways were significantly enriched in both types of aging ovaries. The cytochrome P450 enzyme system is one of the major oxidative metabolic systems in the body, participating in the metabolism of drugs and various endogenous compounds, during which ROS are often generated. Excessive ROS are considered key drivers of ovarian aging, leading to DNA damage, mitochondrial dysfunction, and cell apoptosis. Currently, studies on the relationship between vitamin D and ovarian aging are relatively extensive, and evidence has demonstrated that vitamin D plays an important role in maintaining redox homeostasis and regulating granulosa cell function [
14
,
28
]. However, research on vitamin B6 and ovarian aging remains limited. An animal experiment found that vitamin B6 deficiency significantly reduces ovarian responsiveness to FSH and interstitial cell-stimulating hormone (ICSH), requiring higher doses to promote follicular development and estrogen synthesis [
29
]. These findings suggest that vitamin B6 plays an important role in regulating ovarian hormone sensitivity and steroidogenesis. Future studies should further elucidate the molecular mechanisms of vitamin B6 in ovarian aging and explore its potential interactions with other vitamin-related signaling pathways, which may provide new insights and therapeutic targets for delaying ovarian aging and improving female reproductive health.
We also identified the SCMC as a shared critical module. The SCMC—comprising highly conserved components NLRP5 (MATER), OOEP, TLE6, and KHDC3L localized beneath the oocyte cortex—is essential for oocyte maturation, fertilization, and early embryonic development. Mutations in SCMC-related genes have been implicated in human reproductive disorders [
30
,
31
]. For example, pathogenic variants in PADI6, NLRP5, KHDC3L, and NLRP2 have been detected in patients with recurrent assisted-reproduction failure, resulting in oocyte structural defects and early embryonic arrest [
32
–
34
]. Our findings substantiate the association between SCMC dysfunction and ovarian aging, thereby laying groundwork for potential genetic diagnostics in female infertility.
Based on this, we believe that CTX-BUS-induced pathological ovarian senescence can serve as an effective model of ovarian aging that can mimic the process of ovarian decline at multiple levels, and that despite certain biological differences, this model still provides strong evidence for studying the mechanisms of ovarian aging, exploring early markers, and exploring therapeutic treatments.
Differences in molecular mechanisms between pathologic and physiologic ovarian aging
As mentioned above, we used a chemotherapeutic agent-induced ovarian senescence model to mimic pathological ovarian aging, and histological results suggested that the two aging models showed a consistent trend in the expression of a variety of key proteins and signaling pathways, proving that chemical aging can effectively mimic natural ovarian aging. However, further exploration of the heterogeneity between these two aging models is crucial for a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms involved in different types of ovarian aging.
We combined pathway enrichment and clustering analysis and found that differential proteins in the CHE group were mainly enriched in DNA replication, cell cycle progression, and ribosome production, suggesting that chemotherapeutic drug-induced pathological ovarian senescence is primarily characterized by DNA damage and disruptions in cell proliferation. In contrast, differential proteins in the OLD group were widely enriched in lipid metabolism disorders, immune activation, and chronic inflammation-related pathways, such as lipid metabolism, B cell receptor signaling pathway, and microglial pathogen phagocytosis pathway, reflecting immune system dysfunction and the development of phagocytosis during the physiological aging process. These findings highlight the dysfunction of the immune system and the gradual increase of chronic low-grade inflammation associated with physiological aging.
Pathological ovarian aging is typically associated with genetic factors [
35
], autoimmune diseases [
36
,
37
], infections, environmental pollutants (e.g., heavy metals, pesticides, tobacco smoke), radiotherapy [
38
], metabolic diseases [
39
], and conditions such as anxiety and depression [
40
]. Genetic factors play a significant role in ovarian aging, including mutations in genes such as BRCA1/2 [
41
], the SCMC complex, MCM family, FMR1 [
42
], and FIGLA, which impact processes such as DNA replication and ribosome production. A study on long-term exposure to the environmental pollutant pyrethroid-cypermethrin (CP) showed significant upregulation of the pro-apoptotic protein BMF and the cell cycle inhibitor p27 in the ovaries of mice chronically exposed to CP, accompanied by ovarian reserve depletion and reduced fertility [
43
]. We suggest that pathological ovarian senescence accelerates follicular depletion and functional decline primarily through direct DNA damage in ovarian cells, disruption of the cell cycle, and inhibition of cell proliferation [
38
]. This damage is typically rapid and irreversible, leading to ovarian dysfunction that occurs earlier than the typical age of menopause. In contrast, physiological ovarian aging is a chronic, long-term cumulative process that occurs alongside the overall aging of the organism. Many studies have closely linked ovarian aging to chronic low-grade inflammation, ROS accumulation, decreased immune function, and lipid metabolism imbalances [
44
–
51
], all of which are supported by our findings.
Screening of predictors and key targets of ovarian aging
Ovarian aging is insidious, and current early diagnostic criteria lack specificity. FSH and AMH are the most commonly used clinical markers of ovarian reserve [
52
,
53
]. AMH, in particular, is widely employed to evaluate ovarian reserve and declines with increasing age in reproductive-aged women. However, AMH levels are modulated by numerous factors, such as menstrual-cycle fluctuations [
10
], racial/ethnic variation [
11
], body mass index, hormonal contraceptive use [
12
], and vitamin D status [
13
,
54
]. Although AMH can predict age at menopause at the population level, its individual-level predictive accuracy is limited. Follow-up studies demonstrate that AMH’s prognostic value diminishes with advancing age [
15
] and exhibits substantial inter-individual variability in decline rate [
16
]. Upstream regulators of AMH and FSH synthesis during ovarian aging remain poorly defined.
Proteomics enables global profiling of ovarian aging, facilitating the discovery of early, sensitive biomarkers and the construction of a comprehensive predictive marker panel for both physiological and pathological ovarian aging. Shared molecular features between physiological and pathological ovarian aging may reveal core aspects of the aging process. PPI network analysis of co-varying proteins identified interactions among Cyp17a1, Lhcgr, AMH, and FSHB, with Cyp17a1 and Lhcgr emerging as potential novel predictors of ovarian aging.
Cyp17a1 is a key steroidogenic enzyme [
55
] with both 17α-hydroxylase and 17,20-lyase activities, mainly expressed in theca cells. It catalyzes the conversion of pregnenolone and progesterone into androgen precursors such as DHEA, providing substrates for estrogen biosynthesis. Polymorphisms in Cyp17a1 influence hormone synthesis levels [
56
], and its dysregulation has been associated with PCOS, ovarian insufficiency, and infertility [
55
,
57
,
58
]. In this study, Cyp17a1 was significantly upregulated in the ovarian aging model, indicating its key role in steroidogenesis and ovarian functional decline. Lhcgr, expressed in theca and granulosa cells, mediates LH signaling to activate the cAMP–PKA pathway, enhancing CYP17A1 expression and steroidogenic activity [
59
]. FSH, through FSHR, induces aromatase (CYP19A1) expression to convert DHEA into estradiol (E2), promoting follicular maturation [
60
,
61
]. FSH also upregulates AMH, which negatively regulates follicular recruitment and aromatase activity, forming a fine feedback loop. During ovarian aging, aberrant activation of Cyp17a1 and Lhcgr may lead to local androgen excess and disruption of the AMH–FSH–E2 axis, resulting in follicular arrest, endocrine imbalance, and accelerated ovarian decline. Thus, Cyp17a1 and Lhcgr may serve as novel predictive markers of ovarian aging.
Could this study identify key targets for the treatment of ovarian senescence? By analyzing the common differential proteins significantly enriched in inflammation and oxidative stress-related pathways in two models of aging mice, we obtained seven core genes (pbk, sdhd, Gsta3, Gstm6, Nlrp5, Nlrp4f, and Nlrp14), which may be novel therapeutic targets for eliminating the chronic inflammation and oxidative stress in the ovaries, improving the ovarian microenvironment, and slowing down the aging of the ovaries. targets to slow down ovarian aging. Aging is often accompanied by chronic low-grade inflammation and oxidative stress, such as increased IL-1α, IL-6, TNF and NF-κB activity, and other inflammatory factors in tissues that increase with age, and inhibition of NF-κB confers resistance to progeroid conditions [
62

PBK (PDZ-binding kinase) [

Meanwhile, PBK plays a key role in the proliferation, progression and metastasis of many malignant tumors [
64
–

23

Thus, PBK may be an important target for inhibiting ovarian senescence and combating ovarian cancer. We found that SDHD is also a tumor-related senescence target, and the three subunits of mitochondrial complex II (SDHB, SDHC, and SDHD) have been reported to be one of the most important susceptibility genes in neuroendocrine tumors, head and neck paragangliomas, and pheochromocytomas and paragangliomas [
68
,
69
], and dysfunction of the SDH complex leads to the accumulation of mitochondrial ROS, which induces an oxidative stress reaction that can promote cellular senescence and tumor growth [
70
–
72
]. Glutathione transferases (GSTs) are an important family of multifunctional enzymes (e.g., Gsta3, Gstm6), which play important roles in detoxification metabolism and antioxidant defense, and regulate the cellular response to oxidative damage, and it has been demonstrated in relevant literature that Gsta3 and Gstm6 are highly expressed in germ cells [
73
–
75
].Among the members of the NLRP family, NLRP5, NLRP14, and NLRP4F are considered to be maternal effector genes specifically expressed in oocytes [

Among them, mutations in NLRP5 (also known as MATER), a core component of the subcortical maternal complex (SCMC), have been shown to be closely associated with impaired oocyte maturation and early embryonic developmental arrest in human oocytes [

Our subsequent work will target these proteins for intervention, which is expected to improve the local chronic inflammatory microenvironment of the ovary, stabilize the intrinsic metabolic state and epigenetic program of oocytes, delay follicular depletion, and maintain and slow down the ovarian aging process. At the same time, targeting certain proteins is important for inhibiting the development of aging-related tumors.
Strengths and limitations
This study presents several strengths. First, by applying high-throughput proteomics combined with qPCR validation, we systematically profiled protein alterations associated with ovarian aging, thereby enhancing the reliability of our findings. Second, within a unified research framework, we compared chemotherapy-induced and naturally aged models, uncovering both shared and distinct molecular mechanisms. Finally, we identified several potential early predictors and candidate therapeutic targets, which provide valuable directions for further investigation.
Nevertheless, certain limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study primarily relies on mouse models, and species differences may limit direct translation to humans, necessitating validation in clinical samples. Second, our work thus far has focused on proteomic and transcriptional levels, lacking functional studies to clarify causal links between the identified molecules and ovarian aging. Third, some proposed biomarkers (CYP17A1 and LHCGR) have not yet been clinically established, while widely used markers such as AMH exhibit limited predictive accuracy. To address these gaps, future research will integrate clinical cohorts, functional experiments, and multi-omics approaches to comprehensively verify the mechanistic roles of candidate molecules and to evaluate their potential in early diagnosis and therapeutic intervention.
Conclusion
In this study, we compared the dissimilarity and homogeneity of natural aging and chemotherapeutic drug-induced pathological ovarian aging models by quantitative proteomics technology. Based on their heterogeneity, we analyzed the targets of the pathways that characterize the two types of ovarian senescence to provide evidence for the mechanistic understanding of different types of ovarian senescence and precision therapy; moreover, based on their homogeneity, we performed model evaluation to verify the reliability of the CTX-BUS-induced mouse ovarian senescence model, and further performed early target prediction and therapeutic target screening of the co-differentiated proteins, and we screened 2 potential early predictors (Cyp17a1, Lhcgr) and identified 7 core therapeutic targets (pbk, sdhd, Gsta3, Gstm6, Nlrp5, Nlrp4f and Nlrp14).
