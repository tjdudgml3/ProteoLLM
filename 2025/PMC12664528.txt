Introduction
Membranous nephropathy (MN) and minimal change disease (MCD) represent two distinct forms of nephrotic syndrome (NS) with differing pathological mechanisms and clinical presentations. Classic MN is characterized by subepithelial immune complex deposition, complement activation, and thickening of the glomerular basement membrane, whereas MCD typically presents with podocyte foot process effacement in the absence of immune deposits [
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]. While these entities are traditionally considered separate, emerging evidence suggests the existence of overlapping or hybrid forms that challenge conventional diagnostic boundaries.
Segmental MN (segMN), which exhibits segmental subepithelial electron‐dense deposits (EDD) but with some clinical features resembling MCD, represents one of the MN variants [
5
]. Additionally, an early‐stage MN termed ‘Stage 0 MN’ – characterized by sparse subepithelial EDD, limited subepithelial IgG deposition, and clinically mild proteinuria – has been identified as distinct from classic MN [
6
]. In contrast, some MCD cases demonstrate subtle punctate IgG deposition on podocytes, suggesting an antigen–antibody reaction similar to MN [
7
]. Subsequent studies identified nephrin as the target antigen in these cases [
8
]. However, Zhang
et al
proposed that punctate IgG deposits on podocytes may result from podocyte budding rather than an immune reaction [
9
]. These observations raise important questions: does a unique disease entity exist between MN and MCD, or are these simply variants of MCD or MN? Furthermore, the immunological drivers of this potential hybrid form remain unclear.
Our previous observations identified some cases of MCD with scattered subepithelial dense deposits under electron microscopy (EM) by re‐evaluation that did not meet the diagnostic criteria of MCD. Therefore, we conducted this study to explore the clinicopathological characteristics and pathogenesis of cases exhibiting clinical features resembling MCD but with scattered subepithelial deposits characteristic of MN.
Materials and Methods
Patients
Patients who were previously diagnosed with MCD between 2020 and 2024 underwent EM re‐examination at Hangzhou TCM Hospital affiliated to Zhejiang Chinese Medical University. Patients with occasional or scattered subepithelial electron‐dense deposits (defined as the presence of at least one or two distinct deposits) involving less than 25% of the capillary loops, as observed by EM, were included in the study as the experimental group. In addition, to reduce demographic bias, the control groups (classic MCD and PLA2R‐associated MN) were selected from the same patient database and matched to the study group (MCD‐like MN) for gender and age in a 1:2 ratio. All patients were diagnosed and treated at the same institution under consistent pathological and clinical criteria. Furthermore, patients who had received prior immunosuppressive therapy or had incomplete data were excluded to ensure homogeneity. This study was performed in line with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the ethics committee of Hangzhou TCM Hospital Affiliated to Zhejiang Chinese Medical University (2023KLL081).

Clinical and pathological data collection
The clinical data, including gender, age, renal lesion duration, 24‐h proteinuria (24hUP), albumin (ALB), serum creatinine (Scr), treatment, outcome, and duration of follow‐up, were collected. Treatment regimens were divided into conservative treatment (angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors/angiotensin receptor blockers) and immunosuppressive treatment (steroids and/or immunosuppressive agents). Treatment responses were defined as follows: complete remission (CR) was characterized by urinary protein excretion <0.3 g/day with normal serum albumin and creatinine levels; partial remission (PR) was defined as urinary protein <3.5 g/day (≥50% reduction from baseline) with serum albumin >30 g/l and stable serum creatinine; no remission (NR) was indicated by persistent proteinuria >3.5 g/day with <50% reduction or ≥30% decline in eGFR [
15
]. The pathological data, including global glomerulosclerosis (GS), segmental glomerulosclerosis (SS), interstitial fibrosis/tubular atrophy (IFTA) observed under light microscopy (LM) were collected. IgG deposition in podocytes and the IgG subclasses observed under immunofluorescence (IF) were also collected. Secondary causes including infectious diseases (hepatitis, tuberculosis, syphilis, HIV) were screened using serological tests and polymerase chain reaction; autoimmune diseases (connective tissue disease, chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy) were evaluated through autoantibody panels and imaging studies; and malignancies (solid tumors, lymphoplasmacytic proliferative disorders) were investigated with imaging studies and/or biopsy.
Detection of antigens in kidney tissue
Laser capture microdissection‐mass spectrometry (LCM‐MS)
For each case, 8‐μm thick sections of formalin‐fixed and paraffin‐embedded renal tissue were cut onto membrane glass slides (Leica No. 11600288, Mannheim, Germany), and micro‐dissected using laser capture techniques (Leica LMD7, Mannheim, Germany) with a total area of approximately 50,0000 μm
2
. MS raw data were queried with the Sequest search engine against human entries in the UniProtKB/Swiss‐Prot Human Protein database concatenated with the reverse decoy database in Proteome Discoverer software (version 2.4, Thermo Fisher Scientific GmbH, Bremen, Germany) [
16
]. The mass spectrometry counts (MSC) of target antigens were recorded and compared to the normal range detected in healthy controls.
Immunofluorescence double staining
Both frozen and paraffin sections were used to detect MN antigens including THSD7A, NELL1, CNTN1, neuron‐derived neurotrophic factor (NDNF), thrombospondin repeat containing 1 (THRA1), myeloperoxidase (MPO), Sema3b, early endosome antigen 1 (EEA1), and two podocyte antigens (nephrin and podocin) in different groups by using the IF double staining method. Frozen tissue sections were incubated with antibodies against nephrin and podocin. Sections from paraffin‐embedded tissue underwent heat‐induced antigen retrieval in citrate buffer for CNTN1, and THRA1. For NDNF and MPO, retrieval was done by heating in ethylene diamine tetraacetic acid (EDTA). In addition, THSD7A and NELL1 required pepsin digestion. Nephrin was detected using a primary polyclonal sheep anti‐human nephrin (R&D Systems; AF4269) incubation, followed by a secondary Alexa Fluor™ (AF) 568‐conjugated donkey anti‐sheep IgG (1:100, Invitrogen; A21099). Podocin was detected using a primary polyclonal rabbit anti‐human podocin (1:500, Millipore Sigma; P0372), followed by a secondary AF568‐conjugated donkey anti‐rabbit IgG (1:200, Invitrogen; A10042). MN antigens were incubated with primary antibodies: rabbit anti‐human NELL1/THSD7A/NDNF/MPO (Atlas Antibodies, HPA051535; Atlas Antibodies, HPA000923; LSBio, LS‐C168140; Maxim, RAB‐0379), goat anti‐human CNTN1 (R&D Systems; AF904), and mouse anti‐human THRA1 (R&D Systems; MAB2916), followed by incubation with secondary AF594‐conjugated anti‐rabbit, anti‐goat, and anti‐mouse IgG (1:200, Invitrogen). After the first antibody incubation, sections were co‐stained with FITC‐conjugated IgG (1:50, F0202, DAKO Corporation). Results were interpreted by two pathologists, and images were acquired using a STELLARIS 5 confocal scanning unit (Leica Microsystems CMS GmbH, Germany).
Detection of antibodies in serum
Antibodies of
MN
Time‐resolved fluorescence immunoassay (TRFIA) assay was applied to detect serum antibodies targeting MN antigens including M‐type phospholipase A2 receptor (PLA2R), thrombospondin type‐1 domain‐containing protein 7A (THSD7A), neural EGFL like 1 (NELL1), Contactin 1 (CNTN1), Semaphorin 3B (Sema3b) as previously reported [
17
,
18
,
19
]. Serum samples were pipetted into the antigen‐coated microtiter plates that had been prepared. After washing, europium‐labeled goat anti‐human IgG antibodies were added to the plates, and after rinsing, enhancement solution was added to the plates. Finally, the concentrations were analyzed using a TRFIA analyzer (TRF‐1000, Quaero Life Science, PR China).
Anti‐nephrin antibody analyzed by two assays
Both enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and cell‐based assay (CBA) were applied to detect anti‐nephrin antibody. (1) ELISA (ELK Biotech; ELK2043): according to the protocol, nephrin‐coated microplates were sequentially incubated with serum sample diluent buffer, biotinylated antibody diluent, and horseradish peroxidase diluent. Visualization was achieved by incubating with 3,3′,5,5′‐tetramethylbenzidine. The reaction was terminated with stop reagent, and the absorbance [optical density (OD)] was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. (2) CBA (ImmonoArt, MNDX09): according to the protocol, cells expressing nephrin were fixed, permeabilized, and blocked before incubation with diluted sera. The cells were subsequently treated with Alexa Fluor 594‐goat anti‐human IgG Fcγ (1:1000; Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Grove, PA, USA). The results were visualized under a fluorescence microscope (Leica, Solms, Germany).
Additional podocyte‐associated antibodies
Chemiluminescence immunoassay (CLIA) was used to detect antibodies binding to 10 antigens of podocytes (YYIS Medical Diagnostics, WLS‐BL‐1103‐2001‐G), according to the previous report: Aconitate hydratase 2 (Aco2), Caldesmon 1 (Cald1), Vinculin (Vcl), Lamin A/C (LMNA), Serine Arginine Rich Splicing Factor 9 (SRSF9), Peptidyl prolyl cis–trans isomerase D (Ppid), Capping actin protein of muscle Z‐line subunit beta (CAPZB), Proteasome Subunit Alpha type‐1 (PSMA1), Peroxiredoxin 1 (Prdx1), Annexin A2 (ANXA2) [
20
,
21
]. These antigens were coated onto a nitrocellulose membrane. Serum samples, biotinylated anti‐human IgG, and alkaline phosphatase‐labeled streptavidin complex were added according to the manufacturer's instructions. A purple line appeared at the respective antigen positions if specific autoantibodies were present in the sample.
Statistical analysis
Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normally distributed measurement data were represented by mean ± SD, while those not normally distributed were represented by median (P25, P75). For comparison between three groups where the samples were independent, normally distributed and had equal variances, one‐way ANOVA was adopted; LSD
t
‐test was adopted to compare two groups. Continuous variables lacking normal distribution and equal variance were compared via the Mann–Whitney
U
test in a non‐parametric test. Categorical variables are expressed as numbers (percentages), with comparisons by chi‐square or Fisher's exact test. Cox proportional hazards regression was performed to identify predictors of treatment‐induced CR. Kaplan–Meier (KM) analysis was used to plot curves of cumulative CR rate. All statistical tests were two‐sided, and a
p
< 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were processed with SPSS 25.0 statistical software.

Pathological characteristics of
MCD
‐like
MN
and comparison with the other two groups
Sixteen patients initially diagnosed with MCD but later found to have sparse and segmental subepithelial EDD were identified among 107 archival MCD cases. The EM features generally exhibited 2–10 subepithelial deposits per capillary loop, and capillaries with deposits accounted for 10–30% of the whole capillary loops (Figure
1A,B
). The quantity and area of deposits were significantly lower than observed in MN patients (
p
< 0.001). In most cases, GBM showed no thickening, a pattern similar to that seen in classic MCD patients, except in localized regions adjacent to the deposits (Tables
1
and
2
). Moreover, the GBM thickness surrounding deposits showed no significant difference compared to MN, suggesting a similar GBM response to deposits (
p
> 0.05). Other characteristics under EM did not show significant differences among the three groups. IF on frozen tissue revealed weak and dusty IgG deposits in podocytes in 62.5% of MCD‐like MN patients, a proportion significantly higher than that in the classic MCD group (
p
< 0.001). However, after antigen retrieval on paraffin tissue, the proportion decreased to 43.7%, and some of the dusty deposition patterns changed to segmental subepithelial patterns (Figure
1C,D
). IgG subclass testing was performed on 14 patients in the MCD‐like MN group. Among these, eight patients exhibited IgG subclass positivity in podocytes, all demonstrating identical IgG deposition patterns. All IgG subclass‐positive cases showed IgG1 deposits, with only one case displaying concurrent IgG4 and another case showing concurrent IgG3 deposits. In contrast, all MN cases demonstrated subepithelial deposits, with 100% positivity for IgG1, and the positive rates of IgG1 and IgG2 were significantly higher compared to the MCD‐like MN group (
p
< 0.01). Complement components including C3 and C1q were all negative in the MCD‐like MN group. LM parameters, including GS and SS were not observed in all MCD‐like MN cases, while significantly less IFTA was observed compared to the MN group (
p
< 0.001).

The target antigens of MN were detected in 84.6% of MCD‐like MN cases (11/13), even though with relatively low MSC (range from 5 to 20), whereas none were detected in the MCD group (Figure
2
). In the MCD‐like MN group, 46.2% of cases were detected with only one MN antigen, 30.8% showed two antigens, and 7.7% had three antigens. The antigen with the highest detection rate was THSD7A (46.2%), followed by EEA1 (38.5%), and CNTN1 (15.4%). Other antigens such as MPO, NELL1, Sema3b, VASN were only detected in one case each. Secondary causes were screened according to the positive MS findings. One case with CNTN1 detected was found to have had Ménière's disease 2 years ago, but no positive findings were detected by magnetic resonance imaging. An older patient (66 years) with THSD7A detected showed IgM kappa by immunofixation electrophoresis but without confirmed lymphoplasmacytic proliferative disorder or solid tumor. One patient with neuron‐derived neurotrophic factor (NDNF) detected showed a positive result on the T‐spot test, but without confirmed tuberculosis, and syphilis detection was negative. In addition, the patient with Sema3b detected was an adult without any findings of secondary causes. Furthermore, no elevated MSC values were detected for nephrin/podocin or other podocytopathy‐related target antigens (vinculin, ANXA2, PPID, SRSF9, LMNA) in MCD‐like MN cases, whereas a slightly elevated MSC of nephrin was observed in 1 of 4 (25%) classic MCD cases.

The antigens detected by MS were further verified through IF double staining. Some of the MN target antigens including NELL1, THSD7A, Sema3b, CNTN1, and EEA1 were positive along the capillary loops. NELL1 was completely co‐localized with IgG along the GBM when detected by antigen retrieval IF on paraffin‐embedded tissue, even though IgG showed dusty deposition in podocytes by IF on frozen tissue (Figure

Nephrin and podocin were performed by IF staining, and both of them were positive in all MCD‐like MN cases. However, in most cases, the distribution of nephrin and podocin was predominantly along the capillary loops with a linear pattern. Three cases exhibited a dusty pattern of nephrin deposition in podocytes; however, these deposits were not completely co‐localized with granular IgG deposits in the podocyte plasma (Figure
1G–I
). Although one case showed complete nephrin‐IgG co‐localization, it was ultimately excluded from our study because repeated examinations failed to demonstrate subepithelial deposits (Figure

Discussion
MN has been reported to have pathological variants [
5
,
6
]. Kudose
et al
described segMN, characterized by segmental distribution of subepithelial IgG immune complexes on IF (ranging from 25% to 75% average capillary involvement) and segmental EDD on EM [
22
]. Most segMN patients exhibited stable or improved renal function with either complete or partial remission of proteinuria [
23
]. Separately, Lerner
et al
proposed a ‘Stage 0 MN’ entity post‐kidney transplantation, defined by sparse subepithelial EDD on EM, weak subepithelial IgG deposition, and clinically mild proteinuria [
6

However, our study identified a cohort initially misdiagnosed with MCD that displayed clinicopathological features distinct from those variants of MN. Pathologically, all cases demonstrated sparse segmental subepithelial deposits, with more limited capillary EDD involvement than segMN and >50% showing cytoplasmic dusty IgG deposition – features distinctly differentiating it from segMN. In addition, the involved capillary loops exhibited a median of 1.5 deposits and a median deposit area of 2,600.8 nm
2
, which were significantly lower than those in classic MN, while MCD typically exhibits a complete absence of deposits, which could help practicing pathologists to distinguish MCD‐like MN from MCD and classic MN reproducibly. Clinically, our patients exhibited rapid disease onset and full‐blown NS, a presentation typically suggestive of classic MCD, which clearly differentiates them from stage 0 MN. This evidence supports classifying these cases as a novel MN subtype, which we propose to term ‘MCD‐like MN’ based on its hybrid features.
Compared to classic MN, MCD‐like MN demonstrated significantly lower IgG1/IgG2 deposition, absent C3/C1q, and milder IFTA, with normal LM findings (GS/SS), establishing it as a distinct clinicopathological entity. MN antigens were detectable in the majority of MCD‐like MN cases, even with comparatively low spectrometry counts observed. This indicates that MCD‐like MN shares the same pathogenesis with MN. However, PLA2R was negative in all MCD‐like MN patients, which suggests distinct pathogenetic mechanisms in this MN variant. Furthermore, we explored secondary causes according to target antigen detected via MS. Some cases presented with mild clinical manifestations or positive serum markers associated with certain secondary diseases, though none of these conditions were definitively confirmed. Besides, IF double staining demonstrated that NELL1 exhibited complete co‐localization with IgG along the GBM in paraffin‐embedded tissues, albeit with limited deposition. This finding indicates segmental MN, as confirmed by EM, even though IgG showed a cytoplasmic deposition pattern in podocytes in frozen tissues. We suspect the translocation of IgG deposition between different tissues might be due to antigen preservation differences or technical artifacts. The lack of fixation in frozen tissues increases cell membrane permeability, allowing antibodies to penetrate the cytoplasm more easily and amplify the cytoplasmic signal. In contrast, this process is blocked in paraffin‐embedded tissues due to fixation and subepithelial deposits sitting on GBM become more obvious. In addition, we hypothesize that the NELL1‐positive case could alternatively be interpreted as MCD superimposed on segmental MN, given that NELL1 fully co‐localized with IgG, even though the underlying mechanism remains unclear. This finding highlights the need for IgG staining on multiple tissue types to minimize MN misdiagnosis. Other MN target antigens displayed partial co‐localization with IgG or predominant distribution within the mesangial region, possibly due to reduced antigen exposure in paraffin‐embedded tissues. In addition, anti‐NELL1 and anti‐Sema3b antibodies were detected by TRAFIA in MCD‐like MN patients, consistent with the corresponding antigens identified in kidney tissue [
24
,
25
]. This finding confirms shared pathogenesis with MN [
26
]. These atypical manifestations suggest that MCD‐like MN may represent either a potential secondary form or an early developmental stage of MN.
MCD has been traditionally thought to be negative for immunoglobulins; however, in recent years, the dusty IgG deposition in podocytes was suggested as antibody‐mediated podocytopathy, and 52.6% of the patients experienced NS relapse [
7
,
27
]. The target antigens in these cases may include podocyte‐specific molecules such as nephrin, podocin and Kirrel1 [
7
,
28
]. Nevertheless, Ye
et al
identified 10 distinct anti‐podocyte antibodies in children who tested negative for the previously mentioned antibodies [
29
]. This type of antigen–antibody reaction did not induce subepithelial deposits, thus failing to account for the phenomenon observed in our study. Besides, Zhang
et al
demonstrated that the ‘floating’ granular IgG staining in MCD corresponds to budding ballooning clusters (BBC) of reactive foot processes within Bowman's capsule under EM [
9
]. This pattern differs from the antibody‐mediated podocytopathy or sparse subepithelial deposits observed in our study. In addition, some researchers have found that subsequent repeat biopsies revealed trace EDD or specific antibodies in cases initially diagnosed as MCD [
30
,
31
]. These findings led to diagnostic reclassification as early‐stage MN, particularly in anti‐PLA2R antibody‐positive patients. This evidence implies two potential scenarios: either a distinct MCD subcategory exists, or early‐stage membranous nephropathy with atypical pathological features was initially misdiagnosed. Therefore, podocytopathy may manifest not only as classic MCD and MN, but also present variants, special types, intermediate forms, and atypical lesions.
Compared with age‐matched classic MCD controls, our study cohort demonstrated a significantly higher prevalence of podocyte IgG deposition and more severe FPE on EM, potentially reflecting the distinct characteristics of classic MCD. In addition, we analyzed serum antibodies against podocyte antigens in the entire cohort [
7
,
8
,
29
]. Interestingly, we found no significant difference in serum anti‐nephrin antibody levels between MCD‐like MN and classic MCD. Moreover, other podocytopathy‐related antibodies were detected at much lower frequencies in MCD‐like MN compared to classic MCD. Therefore, we propose that MCD‐like MN is not an antibody‐mediated podocytopathy. Meanwhile, we performed double staining of IgG with intrinsic podocyte proteins (nephrin and podocin), with no case showing complete IgG‐nephrin co‐localization. These limited findings further support a pathogenic mechanism distinct from antibody‐mediated podocytopathy.
When comparing clinical characteristics with those of classic MN and MCD groups, disease course, 24hUP, and ALB levels showed closer resemblance to those of MCD patients, while serum Scr levels aligned more closely with MN patients. Although the follow‐up duration was shorter than that of the MCD group, the remission rate remained comparable. The favorable treatment response clinically suggests MCD characteristics, likely attributable to the brief duration of foot process injury. However, there were two cases with NS relapse and a treatment switch (from steroids to RTX), which suggests the potential complexity of podocyte injury that could not be easily observed morphologically. Therefore, we define a special category of MN with clinical MCD characteristics to enhance clinical vigilance, improve long‐term monitoring through follow‐up, and enable timely adjustment of therapeutic strategies. Long‐term follow‐up is essential to fully elucidate the disease course, and a longitudinal biopsy could be performed for any patient in our study cohort who exhibits a relapse of NS to clarify disease characteristics and progression.
Based on the experimental data above, we propose that MCD‐like MN represents a unique subtype or a variant of MN. Whether this entity progresses to classic MN requires confirmation through long‐term follow‐up observations and repeated biopsy procedures. Several limitations of this experiment must be acknowledged. Firstly, the sample size was insufficient, resulting in a lack of significant differences in some statistical outcomes. Secondly, the follow‐up period was relatively short, which might not reflect the precise outcome of this special disease entity. Finally, while the diagnostic criteria for MCD‐like MN were based on its intermediate pathological and clinical features, its molecular mechanisms remain unclear. These limitations suggest that multi‐center studies with larger sample sizes, long‐term follow‐up, and molecular research are needed to explore the characteristics and pathogenesis in the future.
In conclusion, this study identifies a distinct glomerular disease entity termed ‘MCD‐like MN,’ characterized by sparse subepithelial deposits, podocyte IgG cytoplasmic deposition, and short disease duration with a favorable response to immunosuppressive therapy. This condition represents a special variant of MN that bridges the clinicopathological features between MN and MCD.
