Introduction
Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a significant burden worldwide
1
. Most CRC cases are preventable and treatable when detected early. Thus, there is an emerging need for efficient screening and diagnostic approaches. Applications of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in CRC have transformed screening, diagnosis, and treatment, particularly with the introduction of digital pathology. Whole slide images (WSI) have emerged as a vital component of digital pathology, providing detailed visual information about architecture and cellular morphology. WSIs are high-resolution digital scans of histopathological tissue slides, enabling pathologists to examine specimens at various magnifications on a computer, such as traditional microscopy, but with additional capabilities, such as automation and algorithmic analyses.
Histopathological assessment is the gold standard for CRC diagnosis and prognosis. AI models, particularly Multiple Instance Learning
2
,
3
using WSIs, have demonstrated robust performance in diagnosing and predicting survival outcomes in CRC
4
–
7
. However, the complexities of CRC diagnosis extend beyond histopathology, necessitating the incorporation of additional data to capture the multifaceted nature of the disease, particularly for predicting survival or treatment response. Recent multimodal studies have shown that the performance of AI models on WSIs can be significantly improved by supplementing a larger clinical context with other modalities
2
,
3
. By integrating these modalities, multimodal approaches benefit from the capture of complementary information that no single modality can provide, thereby enhancing predictive performance, diagnostic accuracy, and personalised treatment decisions. This emphasises the importance of incorporating WSIs with other types of data not only to improve the accuracy, precision, and reliability of AI-driven prognostic and diagnostic models but also to better understand the heterogeneity observed in CRC
8
,
9
. Figure
1
illustrates the typical workflow of a multimodal approach. The process begins with extraction of the relevant features of the different modalities. Different fusion techniques, such as early, intermediate, and late, are often employed. In early fusion, raw data are fused followed by a feature extractor. In intermediate fusion, the fusion happens during model processing, and in late fusion, probabilities are calculated independently for each modality, and their outcomes are fused, followed by prediction.
Fig. 1
Typical workflow of the multimodal approaches in Colorectal cancer (CRC).
Despite the potential benefits of multimodal models, several challenges persist, including data heterogeneity, fusion techniques, model interpretability, and generalisability. This systematic review explores the current landscape of multimodal approaches that combine WSIs with other modalities, such as clinical information, genomics data, radiological scans, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and mass spectrometry imaging, focusing on their advantages in enhancing the diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment planning of CRC. By synthesising the evidence, this review aimed to identify recent advances in digital pathology and AI in CRC. Specifically, we aimed to review recent advancements in integrated multimodal models that combine histopathological WSIs with other modalities for the diagnosis, management, and treatment of CRC. A systematic review specifically on multimodal approaches using WSIs for CRC can identify the most effective methodologies, gaps in the existing body of evidence, and set priorities for future research. This targeted approach has a more direct impact on improving CRC outcomes. In this review, we focus on WSIs to critically appraise the histopathological and genetic heterogeneity represented in these images, which can be integrated with other modalities to improve the associated prediction tasks, such as survival or biomarker prediction.
Results
A total of 1601 studies were identified from Scopus, PubMed, IEEE Xplore, and Web of Science (Fig.

The eligible 22 multimodal studies

Among the reviewed studies, six were focused on biomarker prediction, highlighting their importance in precision medicine. Eight studies addressed survival prognosis, making it the most investigated task, while two studies dealt with WSI classification. Pathological staging and lymph node metastasis detection were explored in one study, which showed that these are less frequently studied tasks. Furthermore, three studies investigated therapy response or postoperative outcomes. Notably, one study addressed multiple downstream tasks including classification, retrieval, segmentation, and captioning, highlighting the versatility of multimodal models. Another study uniquely bridged survival, prognosis, and pathological staging, emphasising the potential of these approaches to simultaneously address multiple clinical objectives. Multimodal datasets
WSIs are digital representations of tissue slides created by scanning as described by Farahani et al.
32
. Staining plays a key role in visualising tissue components and can be broadly classified into haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and non-H&E techniques. H&E staining, which colours cell nuclei blue and cytoplasm pink, is commonly used to assess tissue morphology in most of the studies included in systematic reviews
10
. Non-H&E stains offer more targeted insights into tissue features. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was used to detect specific proteins such as Ki67, and was performed by Tan et al.
13

20

,

Feature extraction and fusion techniques
Feature extraction is the crucial step in computational pathology and medical imaging, enabling the transformation of raw data into meaningful representations that can be processed using machine-learning algorithms. Working with unlabelled or weakly labelled data, such as image- or slide-level labels, is commonly encountered in histopathology, where patch-level annotations are often unavailable. This step is particularly essential for WSI because of its high dimensionality and complexity. Specialised techniques are used to distil relevant patterns, including morphological and textural features, enabling the differentiation between benign and malignant regions. Effective feature extraction lays the foundation for the construction of accurate diagnostic and predictive models. Across the included studies, feature extraction techniques primarily relied on deep learning models tailored for different data modalities (Table

Feature fusion is primarily used to integrate information from different modalities to obtain a larger context and account for complementary information. While some feature fusion methods are used to reduce the dimensionality of data from different modalities, other fusion methods, such as concatenation, can increase dimensionality. In this systematic review, early, intermediate, and late fusion techniques were used (Table

However, with large histological images, early fusion involves extracting features using pretrained models before combining them with other modalities. The key aspect is that feature extraction during early fusion is not part of the model training process. On the other hand, intermediate fusion involves the fusion of features after the feature extraction stage, where the feature extraction is part of the model training process. A related concept in intermediate fusion is the use of a shared latent space, where features from different modalities are projected into a common representation space. This approach is often employed during joint training or pretraining, enabling the model to learn modality-invariant representations that capture cross-modal relationships—for instance, between histopathology images and genomic data. After pretraining, different fine-tuning approaches were applied to adapt the model to specific downstream tasks. Shared latent spaces are commonly used in vision-language models. In these models, both visual and textual data are projected into a shared latent space during the training process.

Contrastive alignment helps the model understand the relationships between modalities while keeping them separate during the later stages of the task. PathChat

An important yet often underreported aspect in multimodal fusion is the relative weighting of different modalities. Across the reviewed studies, most papers did not explicitly specify how much influence each modality had in the final prediction.
While multimodal AI models are designed to integrate information from multiple data sources, the relative contribution of each modality to the model prediction can vary for different predictive tasks. Depending on the clinical context, some modalities may carry more relevant or discriminative information than others. Different fusion strategies handle this aspect in distinct ways. In early and intermediate fusion approaches, the model learns from all modalities jointly, enabling it to capture complex interdependencies and adaptively assign varying weights to different modalities during training. In late fusion, modality weights can either be learned during training or manually specified by the developer. A notable advantage of late fusion is its flexibility in handling missing modalities. Since each modality has its own independent model, missing inputs can be accommodated by adjusting the corresponding weights.
Consequently, attribution methods can be used to quantify the contribution of each modality to the final prediction
21
. Such analyses offer a degree of interpretability, allowing to verify that the model is making predictions from clinically relevant modalities. Moreover, in exploratory or resource-sensitive settings, attribution studies can help identify dominant or redundant modalities. This could reveal, for example, that a combination of less expensive or non-invasive modalities may yield comparable performance to models using more costly or invasive data types, guiding the design of more efficient and accessible diagnostic tools.
Methods such as MCB pooling, attention mechanisms, cross-modal transformers, or gated fusion layers inherently allow the model to assign differential importance to each modality during training. These techniques dynamically modulate the contribution of each modality based on task-relevant context and learned feature interactions. For example, in transformer-based fusion, cross-attention blocks can focus more heavily on one modality when it carries stronger predictive signals, while gated units can suppress less informative features from another modality. In joint training setups, where a shared projection layer or fusion space is learned, the model optimises both modality-specific encoders and the fusion mechanism simultaneously, allowing it to learn adaptive weights based on gradients propagated through the shared task objective. Although this adaptive behaviour is often not explicitly quantified in the reviewed papers, interpretability tools such as attention heatmaps or modality ablation studies can offer insights into which modality dominates in specific clinical contexts.

Discussion
We reviewed 22 studies, highlighting the importance of incorporating multiple data modalities to improve the model performance in both classification and prognostication tasks. The results consistently demonstrated that integrating different modalities leads to significant improvements in model predictions. Our findings suggest that the combination of image and text data can enhance the model performance compared to the use of a single modality. WSIs provide a detailed view of the tissue architecture and cellular morphology, whereas clinical and genomic data offer complementary insights into patient-specific factors and molecular mechanisms. By merging these diverse data sources, multimodal models can capture the complex patterns and relationships that are often missed when relying on a single modality. This integration allows for more accurate classification of cancer subtypes, better prediction of patient outcomes, and improved decision-making in clinical settings. Qiu et al. reported a 14% improvement in MSI prediction accuracy when histopathological images were combined with clinical data and/or genomics, highligthing the added value of multimodal approaches. Similarly, other studies in our review showed that leveraging clinical data (e.g. patient demographics and medical history) alongside genomics (e.g. gene expression profiles and mutations) enriches the contextual understanding of histopathological features and enhances the accuracy and robustness of predictive models.
We excluded several studies that did not meet our inclusion criteria. Jiang et al.
7
developed a survival prediction model to predict risk stratification using the WSI of CRC patients worldwide. The model was validated using three external validation cohorts: TCGA, Marien Hospital in Dusseldorf, Germany cohort (DUSSEL), and a Molecular and Cellular Oncology study (MCO), which had clinical and pathological data
39
. This study used multimodal data but was excluded from the review because other modality data were used only in model validation and not in model development. Studies that combined different types of images such as combining PET and MRI, were also excluded
40
,
41
. Heijmen et al.
41
used diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) and susceptibility-weighted (T2*) MRI to predict the response to systemic treatment in patients with colorectal liver metastases. In another study, radiological data such as T2-weighted images (T2WIs), DWIs, and the portal venous phase of contrast-enhanced CT were used to predict perineural status in patients with rectal cancer
42
. The exclusion of these studies meant that we did not assess the potential benefits of combining multiple imaging modalities, which could also offer valuable insights into CRC prediction by providing different visual perspectives on tumour characteristics. Similarly, the foundation models were beyond the scope of this study. However, in the following section, we contrast our findings in the context of foundation models for CRC and beyond.
Multimodal approaches allow the integration of different data modalities to increase the robustness and accuracy of the model; however, several challenges have been highlighted
9
,
43
. Constructing large, comprehensive datasets that match multimodal data for each patient is resource-intensive and often infeasible. Many studies rely on single-institution cohorts, limiting external validation, and leaving the generalisability of these models underexplored. Additionally, missing or incomplete data across modalities complicate model training and reduce the overall effectiveness and reliability of multimodal approaches. Addressing these issues is crucial for advancing AI-driven cancer diagnosis and treatment planning. Despite the potential of multimodal AI approaches, the lack of digitalisation in pathology departments remains a significant barrier in collecting large-scale real-world data (RWD). Currently, only 4% of pathology slides are digitally read in the USA, limiting the availability of digitised WSIs necessary for multimodal studies. The ESMO GROW guidelines emphasise the importance of leveraging RWD to enhance model development and validation; however, incomplete digitisation hinders this progress
44
. Addressing this digital divide is essential to improve the generalisability and clinical utility of AI-driven cancer diagnostics.
Majority of studies in this review do not explicitly address the issue of temporal alignment. It often remains unclear whether the data modalities were collected concurrently or within clinically acceptable time frames to be considered temporally consistent. While many studies focused on integrating two or three modalities, where temporal consistency may be assumed, the lack of reporting limits the ability to assess this assumption. Moreover, this issue becomes more pronounced when datasets are aggregated across different institutions, where variations in clinical workflows and timing of data acquisition can introduce additional heterogeneity. Such variability can significantly impact the generalisability and robustness of the resulting models. In some cases, this may not be critical (e.g., when pairing static imaging with diagnostic reports created at the same encounter), but when combining modalities such as histopathological images and clinical outcomes or follow-up lab values, temporal misalignment may have significant implications. In some cases, modalities are collected simultaneously or near-simultaneously—for example, PET-CT scans or paired H&E and IHC stains from the same tissue block. However, more disparate modalities such as diagnostic imaging (e.g., CT or MRI) and follow-up molecular testing are often acquired at different points along the patient’s clinical trajectory. It can affect the biological validity of the fused features and may lead to data leakage or bias, especially in tasks such as survival prediction or treatment response modelling. Although methods for temporal alignment have been developed in other domains such as synchronising audio and video streams
45
, their translation to the complex and heterogeneous clinical data is still largely unexplored.
Another important but frequently overlooked aspect in multimodal learning is the relative weighting of different modalities during fusion. While many studies employ advanced fusion strategies, such as attention mechanisms, cross-modal projection layers, or pooling operations like MCB, that implicitly allow the model to adjust modality importance, few explicitly quantify or interpret these weights. Understanding which modality contributes most to a given predictive task is especially important in clinical contexts, where interpretability and trustworthiness are crucial. Future work should place greater emphasis on analysing modality dominance, either through ablation studies, attention visualisation, or learned gating mechanisms, to clarify how each data type influences the final prediction. Such transparency would enhance model interpretability and help guide data collection strategies in real-world clinical settings. These issues, together remain an open challenge in assessing robustness and real-world reliability of multimodal AI models.
Foundation models are trained on large-scale datasets and include a feature-extraction stage to derive meaningful representations from the data. These models can be fine-tuned for various downstream tasks, rendering them versatile for various applications. Self-supervised techniques, such as contrastive learning, play a critical role in these models, particularly in handling unlabelled data, which is a common challenge in histopathology. Although self-supervised learning (SSL) is a powerful approach, weakly supervised models can also be developed without SSL, for example, by using ResNet feature encoders. It is important to note that architectures like ResNet can also serve as backbone in SSL frameworks, thus, the distinction lies in the training paradigm rather than the architecture itself. These models can use slide-level labels even when patch-level labels are unavailable
46
. In a recent study, Neidlinger et al.
47
tested foundation models as feature extractors for weakly supervised computational pathology, using unseen data. Among these, the CONCH foundation model achieved the best performance. For the zero-shot classification without prompt ensembling, the model reached an AUR-OC of 0.90. The model’s performance improved when it used a prompt ensemble for zero-shot classification, achieving an AUR-OC of 0.98. This model, introduced by Lu et al.
20
, represents a visual-language foundation model that employs contrastive learning to align similar features from visual (e.g., whole-slide images) and textual (e.g., biomedical text and image captions) data. Separate text and image encoders were utilised to extract features in a self-supervised manner. Models such as CONCH are expected to replace traditional fusion approaches because of their improved ability to process and integrate multimodal data.
Although many foundation models incorporate multiple modalities, some focus exclusively on vision and are not multimodal in nature. Examples of visual-language foundation models in medicine include CONCH

Multimodal and multimodal foundation models differ significantly in their designs and purposes. Multimodal models are specifically built to manage multiple data modalities, often addressing single tasks by using fusion techniques to combine information from various sources. However, they frequently struggle with generalisation beyond their intended task. On the other hand, multimodal foundation models are pretrained on diverse large-scale datasets and provide broader generalisation capabilities. Once pretrained, foundation models can be fine-tuned for specific tasks, thereby demonstrating a more robust and versatile framework. Unlike multimodal models, which often fine-tune image and text encoders separately for tasks such as image caption alignment or cross-modal retrieval, multimodal foundation models jointly train both types of encoders on vast datasets (Fig.
4
). This creates a unified representation space that can efficiently address a wide range of tasks with minimal fine-tuning, thereby rendering traditional fusion techniques less relevant. There are also unimodal foundation models that focus on a single modality, such as images or text, and that are trained on large datasets specific to that modality.

While the potential of foundation models is acknowledged, particularly their ability to generalise across tasks and modalities due to large-scale pretraining, there are important limitations that must also be considered. Prospective validation and adaptability to rare diseases or underrepresented subgroups are key limitations
53
. Foundation models often rely on data distributions seen during pretraining, which may not adequately cover rare clinical scenarios. As a result, their performance may degrade when applied to tasks involving uncommon pathologies or populations that were not well-represented in the original training datasets. Additionally, these models can exhibit biases inherent to the datasets they were trained on, and may not be easily interpretable or adaptable without further fine-tuning, which itself may be infeasible in data-scarce settings.
Moreover, deploying such large models in real-world clinical workflows requires addressing issues related to workflow integration, governance, privacy, and explainability, especially when clinical decisions are at stake
34
,
54
,
55
. Future research should explore methods to adapt foundation models to low-resource medical domains, including few-shot learning, domain adaptation, and personalised fine-tuning strategies that maintain reliability despite limited data availability.
Integrating different modalities of data significantly enhances the predictive ability of AI models for CRC classification and prognosis. This review highlights the challenges of data heterogeneity, generalisability, and interpretability, prompting further research. Additionally, it is important to systematically explore fusion strategies while developing multimodal models to improve our understanding of their impact on downstream prediction tasks. For instance, iterative refinement of feature representations in intermediate fusion may offer further enhancements in predictive accuracy by better capturing the nuances of multimodal data interactions. In addition, there is a need to standardise the data fusion techniques and develop robust validation frameworks to ensure the clinical applicability of multimodal models. Despite these challenges, multimodal models have demonstrated potential applications in personalised treatment and risk stratification for CRC. By embracing multimodal data integration, researchers and clinicians can move closer to achieving more precise and personalised cancer care.
Methods
Search strategy
We conducted a comprehensive search across four key databases, PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and IEEE Xplore, to systematically review the studies published between January 2014 and August 2024. Our focus was on multimodal techniques that integrate WSIs with other modalities such as clinical, omics, biomarker, text, and radiology data. Therefore, we devised a comprehensive search strategy (Supplementary Note
1
) through an iterative process.
Study screening and selection
The studies identified through our search strategy were screened using title, abstract, and full-text review according to the PRISMA guidelines
56
(Supplementary Note
2
) using the Covidence web application, and the protocol was registered with PROSPERO (PROSPERO protocol number: 635831). We included original research studies and full conference papers involving adult patients (≥18 years old) diagnosed with CRC that evaluated multimodal approaches (combining at least two data modalities, such as WSI with genomics, clinical data, or radiology) for downstream tasks, such as biomarkers, cancer staging, and survival prediction. Eligible studies must compare these multimodal approaches to at least one of the following: unimodal approaches processing WSI or other modality data; separate models for each data type with subsequently combined outputs; or different multimodal approaches. Eligible studies must also report at least one outcome directly attributable to the multimodal method and compare it with traditional methods. For the purposes of this review, slides that are not stained with H&E are considered a different modality, and when combined with H&E, form a multimodal dataset.
We also included several study designs that focused on multimodal predictive modelling using WSIs. This included prospective and retrospective cohort studies. Only the original research studies were considered. Studies were excluded if they did not apply multimodal methods, were not focused on CRC, did not involve human specimens, did not report an outcome, were reviews, editorials, letters to the editor, or conference abstracts, published in languages other than English, and lacked a clearly defined population or outcome that cannot be directly attributed to the multimodal approach.
Data extraction and synthesis
Studies that met the inclusion criteria based on the design, population, exposure, outcomes, and types of studies were included in the review. Data were extracted from eligible studies to synthesise evidence to address the objectives of the study. Three authors (JJ, MS, and QG) independently extracted data using a standardised template. The template included details required to synthesise study characteristics, information on multimodal approaches, and performance metrics of the best-performing multimodal technique. The quality and bias of the eligible studies were further assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale for observational studies
57
(Supplementary Table
3
). Studies were then categorised based on their characteristics, such as the technique, dataset, modality, and performance of the model presented. Furthermore, we focus on synthesising various feature extraction techniques from different modalities and the fusion methods used to combine these features. Finally, various downstream tasks were identified, and appraised architectures were incorporated, from basic models to more advanced deep learning frameworks.
