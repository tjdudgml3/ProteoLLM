Introduction
Oxidation is one of the major causes of quality deterioration of feeds during processing and storage, inducing oxidative stress to animals after feeding (
Lykkesfeldt et al., 2007
). Interest in protein oxidation in food systems is rapidly growing (
Lund et al., 2011
), but the influence and mechanisms of protein oxidation in feeds, especially in soybean, fish, meat, and milk products which are rich in protein, are largely unknown. Biologically, protein oxidation occurs when proteins are directly oxidized by reactive oxygen species (ROS) (
Shacter, 2000
). During production, processing, and storage, feed ingredients are exposed to molecular oxygen, which can be converted to ROS, as demonstrated in food research (
Choe and Min, 2006
;
Davies, 2016
). The ROS-induced oxidation of protein causes changes to the primary, secondary, and tertiary structures of the protein. Since the integrity of protein structure determines its biological function, structural changes caused by oxidation may decrease the bioavailability of amino acid and compromise the digestibility of proteins by reducing the binding efficiency of enzymes, which negatively affects the nutritional values (
Lund et al., 2011
). Additionally, the secondary product of lipid peroxidation such as malondialdehyde (MDA) can lead to the oxidation of proteins (
Haberland et al., 1988
;
Wang et al., 2017
). Protein oxidation will not only compromise the nutritional value of feeds but also lead to the accumulation of more free radicals (
Shacter, 2000
;
Lund, 2008a
,b;
Tang et al., 2012a
). The presence of oxidized proteins in feeds can cause reduced growth performance, compromised antioxidant capacity, immune disorders, and poor gut health of animals (
Wu et al., 2014
;
Li et al., 2016
;
Yang et al., 2017a
, b;
Zhang et al., 2017
;
Lu et al., 2019
). Therefore, strategies to detect and mitigation protein oxidation are necessary to optimize feed ingredient quality, thereby supporting animal health, particularly gut health. To the best of our knowledge, the relevance of dietary protein oxidation to animal health has not been reviewed. Therefore, this review aims to comprehensively discuss the relevance of dietary protein oxidation to animal health, particularly gut health, based on current scientific literature.
Protein oxidation
Definition and inducing factors for protein oxidation
As early as 1906, the study of protein oxidation was carried out by Henry D. Dakin (1880-1952), and this study referred to the phenomenon of oxidative degradation of amino acids (
Dakin H. D., 1906
). A subsequent study, in 1958, demonstrated that free radicals caused the oxidation of proteins and the formation of carbonyl compounds (
Jayko and Garrison, 1958
). With the development of protein research, the literature summarized that protein oxidation is defined as the covalent modification of a protein that is induced either by the direct reactions with reactive oxygen species (ROS) or indirect reactions with secondary by-products of oxidative stress (
Shacter, 2000
).

, including a number of metabolites such as hydroxyl radical, hydrogen peroxide and lipid peroxidation products and environmental factors such as irradiation, metal, pollutants in the atmosphere, repeated freezing and thawing, and excessive heating. Additionally, ROS are simple by-products of normal metabolic processes and are ubiquitous in both animals and plants (
Covarrubias et al., 2008
;
You and Chan, 2015
). In view of the numerous causes of ROS, proteins in animals and plants have the potential to be oxidized. When oxidized animal and plant protein were used as feed ingredients, they had the potential to impair the health of animals.

Mechanisms of protein oxidation
Protein oxidation has been studied for decades in the development of biological diseases, and some basic theories have been established from these studies. In these studies, the mechanism of the oxidation process involved specific biomolecules. However, in this review, we will combine these mechanisms and summarize them from a chemical perspective.
The processes of protein oxidation have been reviewed extensively and are considered to be free-radical reactions (
Stadtman and Levine, 2003
). Briefly, protein oxidation is a complicated process that involves free radicals which are initiated, propagated and terminated by chain reactions. The initiation phase generates a small amount of reactive free radicals that can react with stable molecules to form new free radicals. These new radicals react with other proteins to generate yet more free radicals during the propagation phase. This often involves hydrogen abstraction or the addition of the radical to double bonds in protein backbones and amino acid side chains.
Protein backbone, as one of the main targets of free radicals, can be oxidized by several different mechanisms (
Garrison et al., 1962
;
Schuessler and Schilling, 1984
). The principle of the first mechanism is the reaction of radicals on the α-carbon of amino acids, resulting in the formation of α-carbon-centered radicals. In the absence of oxygen, two carbon-centered radicals can combine to form cross-linkage (
Fig. 1
A). However, with the exposure of oxygen, α-carbon-centered radicals will quickly react with oxygen to form peroxy radicals. Peroxy radicals are highly reactive and can readily be converted to the alkyl peroxide, which can lead to the formation of the alkoxyl radical and the conversion to the hydroxy derivative (
Fig. 1
B). The second mechanism is mediated by ROS which can also lead to the cleavage of the backbone. As discussed by
Garrison et al. (1962)
, the hydroxylated α-carbon can undergo peptide backbone cleavage by two different pathways: 1) a break-up at the N–C bond through the α-amidation pathway; and 2) a break-up at the C–C bond through the diamide pathway (
Fig. 2
A, B). The third mechanism is by the oxidation of glutamyl (
Fig. 3
A) and aspartyl (
Fig. 3
B) residues of proteins to cause peptide backbone cleavage, the formation of protein fragments, and the release of α-aminobutyric acid. Additionally, the β-scission of the side chain residue can add the radicals to the backbone of the protein, inducing the formation of the carbonyl (
Fig. 4
).

Under high-temperature conditions, the aforementioned chemical reactions in feed proteins occur more extensively. For instance, during the high-temperature storage of SBM, primarily Lys, Cys, Arg, and Met undergo oxidative modifications, including hydroxylation, carbonylation, carboxylation (
Wang et al., 2025
).
Wen et al. (2023)
also found that the Trp, His, Arg, and Lys are oxidatively modified after soybeans are heated. Furthermore, the contents of Thr, Ala, Met, Lys, and His show a linear decrease after SBM is heated, and the
in vitro
digestibility also decreased (
Lu et al., 2017
). In fact, protein oxidation is much more complicated than described above, especially when some highly active intermediate products formed during the reaction process can transfer free radicals to other unoxidized proteins and form different derivatives, resulting in further expanding oxidation.
Consequences of protein oxidation
According to the mechanisms of protein oxidation, it is easy to find that peptide backbone and amino acid residues of the protein are the main modification sites. A peptide bond joins amino acids to form the primary structure of the protein, which is the foundation for determining the secondary (e.g., α-helices and β-sheets) and the tertiary structures (overall three-dimensional structure). Modifications that alter the primary structure of a protein often change its charge and biological function (
Pelley, et al., 2011
). Therefore, modification of the primary structure by oxidation can cause alterations in the spatial conformation and function of the protein. The consequences of protein oxidation in inducing biological diseases will not be covered in detail here since they have been reviewed extensively elsewhere (
Stadtman, 1992
;
Berlett and Stadtman, 1997
;
Davies, 2016
). However, the protein oxidation of feed ingredients including plant-derived (e.g. cereals, oilseeds, and their products) and animal-derived (e.g. meat bone meal, and fish meal) feedstuff has not been thoroughly reviewed.
Based on the oxidation mechanisms outlined above, proteins in feed ingredients face multiple oxidative risks during processing and storage, compromising their nutritional function. The specific consequences for major ingredient types are discussed below.
Soybean and its products
Soybean contains a high quantity of protein and its amino acid composition is approximate to the composition of animal proteins (
De Marco et al., 2015
). Therefore, the products of soybean such as soybean meal and soy protein concentrate are widely used to replace animal protein in feed (
Zhang et al., 2013
;
Oliveira and Stein, 2016
). However, soybean and its products contain lipids which are sensitive to oxidation under both enzymatic conditions and nonenzymatic conditions. Similar to protein oxidation, lipid peroxidation can produce free radicals as well as lipid hydroperoxides and reactive aldehydes, which can react with protein, leading to protein oxidation (
Refsgaard, et al., 2000
). An
in vitro
study by
Chen et al. (2013a)
found that malondialdehyde (MDA), the product of lipid peroxidation, can cause the oxidation of the soy protein isolate (SPI), resulting in carbonyl group generation, Schiff-base formation and free amino groups loss. Similarly, another study showed that the oxidation of SPI induced by 2,2′-azobis can cause the generation of the carbonyl group, degradation of the free sulfhydryl group, formation of dityrosine and loss of
in vitro
digestibility (
Chen et al., 2013b
, c).
Oxidation of soy protein could be affected by feed processing. Heating is a necessary processing step form proving the nutritional value of soybeans because it destroys trypsin inhibitors and other anti-nutritional factors that may interfere with protein digestion (
Liener, 1994
;
Purushotham et al., 2007
). However, excessive heating may result in the destruction of AAs (
González-Vega et al., 2011
) and the formation of oxidation products of proteins (
Wu et al., 2014
).
Wang et al. (2025)
reported that protein oxidation occurs in soybean meal during storage, mainly due to the oxidation of cysteine, lysine, methionine, and arginine, which leads to a decrease in the total free amino acid content. At the same time, as the lysine and arginine are the enzymatic sites of trypsin, the modification of these two amino acids may be one of the main reasons for the reduced
in vitro
digestibility of SBM protein. The study of
Ren et al. (2018)
showed that protein oxidation caused by thermal treatment decreased the
in vitro
digestibility and antioxidant activity and induced partial unfolding and aggregation of protein molecules. A similar study reported that protein oxidation of SPI could be induced by heat which caused the increased protein carbonyl and decreased protein sulfhydryl of SPI (
Zhang et al., 2017
).
Meat bone meal and fish meal
Animal protein sources provide nutritional advantages over plant protein sources, especially for nursery piglets (
Maxwell and Carter, 2001
). Meat bone meal and fish meal, commonly used ingredients in the diets, are co-products of the food industry (
Hendriks et al., 2006
). Meat bone and fish meal are made up of several groups of proteins, lipids and metallic elements that are easily oxidized (
Hendriks et al., 2002
;
Zhou et al., 2004
). In addition, production of meat bone meal and fish meal is seasonal, therefore, those feed ingredients are often stored for extended periods of time (
Bragadóttir et al., 2004
;
Hendriks et al., 2006
). It is well known that prolonged storage leads to lipid oxidation of meat (
Bragadóttir et al., 2004
;
Chanadang et al., 2016
), resulting in by-product forming in proteins which makes them susceptible to oxidation (
Utrera et al., 2014
). Although the protein composition of meat bone meal differs from fresh meat, its processing (e.g., high-temperature sterilization, prolonged storage) may induce similar oxidation mechanisms. The studies on the protein oxidation of meat and bone meal and fish meal are limited, some information can be drawn from existing studies on the protein oxidation of meat. Studies have confirmed that the onset of lipid oxidation can take place faster than protein oxidation in the meat system, and that lipid-derived radicals and hydroperoxides such as MDA further promote protein oxidation (
Estévez et al., 2008
).
Wang et al. (2017)
have reported that protein oxidation induced by MDA results in changes to the β-sheets structure, α-helix, and random structures, which further affects gelling properties of myofibrillar proteins (MPs) in meat. A similar result was reported that MDA can react with the amino groups of MPs, producing strong intermolecular cross-links of the Schiff-base that promote the gelation (
Buttkus, 1967
). Studies have shown that MPs can be oxidized by an oxidizing lipid (hydroxyl radicals), leading to changes in the secondary and tertiary structure of MPs (
Xiong et al., 2009
). In order to guarantee the denaturation of hazardous agents in animal by-products, manufacturers must heat these products at 133°C for 20 min at high pressure (
Anon, 1997
). However, a study of protein oxidation in pork showed that heat treatment at 100°C for 10 and 30 min could cause protein oxidation and increase surface hydrophobicity, carbonyl, protein aggregate and Schiff bases (
Traore et al., 2012
). The high temperature resulted in a significant increase of carbonyls and Schiff bases as well as a notable loss of free thiols in fish fillets compared to the raw material (
Hu et al., 2017
). Additionally, irradiation as an effective technology for controlling pathogenic microorganisms in meat (
Zhang et al., 2013
), can also produce free radicals, which potentially cause the oxidation of proteins in meat products (
Jo and Ahn, 2000
). Studies in poultry meat and beef have shown that the consequences of protein oxidation induced by irradiation caused the formation of protein carbonyl (
Rowe et al., 2004
;
Xiao et al., 2011
).
Whey protein
Whey protein is a valuable by-product from the dairy manufacturing industry, and it can be used as a high-quality protein source in diets for weanling piglets (
Grinstead et al., 2000
;
Van Dijk et al., 2002
). Protein oxidation in whey protein is not always apparent due to the lower lipid concentration compared with oilseed protein isolates. However, in-feed whey protein has a higher potential for oxidation than pure whey protein because of the existence of other complicate components in feed such as animal fat, vegetable oil, and minerals (
Cui et al., 2012
). Indeed, a study on the interaction between whey proteins and lipids showed that polyunsaturated fatty acid stimulated the protein oxidation process, causing riboflavin and tryptophan degradation, and protein-lipid adduct formation (
Mestdagh et al., 2001
). In addition, the presence of ROS during the processing of whey-protein-containing products exacerbated the oxidation (
Kong et al., 2013
). Whey protein oxidation induced by the hydroxyl radical caused chemical and functional changes including carbonyls generation, sulfhydryls reduction, dityrosine increasing, and the changes of gelation, emulsification, and foaming (
Cui et al., 2012
).
Feng et al. (2015)
suggested that radical-induced oxidation of whey protein caused an increase in carbonyl and dityrosine, changes to the secondary structure, and a decrease in
in vitro
digestibility.
Animal blood by-products
As a high-quality protein source, blood meal has a high protein content. Due to the differences in animal species and preparation methods, the protein content is generally between 65 % and 85 % (
Tabinda et al., 2006
;
Liu et al., 2010
). Fresh blood is rich in divalent iron and ascorbic acid, which can produce hydroxyl free radicals by Fenton reaction in the presence of oxidants (
Amici et al., 1989
). Therefore, during improper processing and storage, the proteins in the blood can be oxidized by free radicals. Moreover, in order to eliminate pathogens in the blood, the fresh blood obtained from the slaughtered animals must be heated, sterilized, and dried at a high temperature (
Gatnau et al., 1989
). As mentioned above, high temperature is also a condition for protein oxidation.
Spray-dried plasma is another protein-rich product obtained from healthy animals. Blood is collected, treated with anticoagulant, and centrifuged to separate the red blood cells and the plasma (
Howell and Lawrie, 1983
). Subsequently, plasma is concentrated and spray-dried at 80°C to convert it to powder (
Howell and Lawrie, 1983
). To disrupt microbial reproduction, ultraviolet light irradiation can be used to decrease the formation of bacteria and viruses (
Blázquez et al., 2017
). However, ultraviolet light irradiation is one of the causes of protein oxidation (
Shacter, 2000
). This evidence shows that the protein of blood has the risk of being oxidized, and across diverse feed ingredients supports the development of a universal assessment method based on protein oxidatio
Detection method for protein oxidation
It is known that oxidation causes changes in the physicochemical properties and biological functions of protein. Consequently, monitoring protein oxidation has become critical in the feed industry for manufacturing, characterization, and quality control. In this section, we list some common methods for measuring protein oxidation in feed by detecting and quantifying by-products and structural changes due to oxidative modifications.
Spectrophotometric assay
The spectrophotometric assay is a common method for measuring protein oxidation in feeds. Protein carbonyls and sulfhydryl groups are two frequently used markers for this method.
Protein carbonlys
Protein carbonyls can be generated from oxidative cleavage of the protein backbone, oxidation of amino acid residues, or as by-products of lipid peroxidation (
Stadtman, 1992
;
Butterfield and Stadtman, 1997
) and Maillard reaction (
Berlett and Stadtman, 1997
;
Wondrak et al., 2000
). Hence, protein carbonyl content serves as the most commonly used marker for detection or quantification of protein oxidation by spectrophotometric assay. The reaction of the carbonyl group with 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (DNPH) leads to the formation of a stable dinitrophenyl hydrazone product, and the samples require protease pretreatment to expose carbonyls; free DNPH must be removed by ethanol-acetate washes. Which providing a convenient technique for detecting protein oxidation (
Levine et al., 1990
). Dinitrophenyl hydrazone product can be detected spectrophotometrically at an absorbance of 370 nm (
Reznick and Packer, 1994
). Even though the spectrophotometric method has many disadvantages such as large requirements of sample and labor, it is currently the preferred method for
in vitro
evaluation of carbonyl content because of its low cost. Therefore, this method has been widely used in the evaluation of protein oxidation in both food and feed ingredients. For instance, the results of soy protein oxidation induced by peroxide radicals showed that the final carbonyl content measured by the DNPH method was 10.14 nmol/mg protein, which was six times higher than the initial carbonyl content (
Wu et al., 2009
). Besides, a heat-induced protein oxidation study showed that the protein carbonyl content of soybean meal measured by the DNPH color reaction eventually increased to 13 nmol/mg protein after incubation, which was nearly twice that of raw soybean. Results of protein oxidation in meat also indicated that the spectrophotometric method for carbonyl determination can accurately evaluate the degree of protein oxidation (
Xiao et al., 2011
;
Berardo et al., 2015
).
Sulfhydryl groups
Sulphur amino acids such as methionine and cysteine can be found in most susceptible amino acid residues of protein products, and the oxidation of sulfur-containing amino acids does not generate carbonyl (
Barelli et al., 2008
). The oxidation of sulfur-containing amino acids leads to a series of complex reactions resulting in theformation of various oxidized products such as sulfenic acid, sulfinic acid and disulfide cross-links (
Biswas et al., 2006
). Ellman’s reagent is frequently used for spectrophotometric determination of loss of sulfhydryl groups in proteins from meat, dairy and soybean, which is relatively easy to measure as the compounds are water soluble (
Hawkins et al., 2009
). The reported losses of sulfhydryl groups in soy protein, dairy, and meat during radical reaction, heating, and storage vary greatly. An approximate 48 % loss of sulfhydryl groups was found in soy protein incubated with 2,2′-azobis (2-amidinopropane) dihydrochloride for 24 h by
Chen et al. (2013a)
, while an 84 % loss of sulfhydryl groups in soy protein incubated in the same condition for 24 h was observed by
Wu et al. (2009)
. This may be related to the initial state of soybeans, because fresh soybean protein is relatively less likely to lose its sulfhydryl groups (
Lu et al., 2019
). Regarding the meat study, it was reported that a only 6 % loss of the sulfhydryl groups was found in pork
Longissimus dorsi
stored in a high-oxygen package for 14 days (
Lund et al., 2007
), however, a 37 % loss of sulfhydryl groups in minced pork stored in the same atmosphere for 7 days was observed in another study (
Lund et al., 2008a
). As for heat-induced protein oxidation of the different soy products such as SPI and soybean meal, the results of the decrease in sulfhydryl groups after heating (100 °C for 8 h) varied between approximately 10-26 % of the initial concentration based on different studies (
Wu et al., 2014
;
Zhang et al., 2017
;
Lu et al., 2017
). These different results indicate that the reduction of sulfhydryl groups due to protein oxidations measured by spectrophotometric assay is inconsistent even with the same protein sources and conditions. According to the sulfhydryl group oxidation mechanism, the detection of sulfhydryl group loss is more suitable for evaluating the oxidation of sulfur-rich proteins.
Fluorescence spectroscopy
Fluorescence spectroscopy is a novel approach for measuring protein oxidation in feeds. Compared with spectrophotometry, the fluorescence is measured to reflect protein oxidation instead of absorptions and fluorescence spectroscopy is more sensitive and specific. Fluorescence of markers such as dityrosine and trytophan could be used to reflect protein oxidations (
Hassoun et al., 2019
).
Dityrosine
The oxidative C—C linkage of two L-tyrosine molecules, is a fluorescent cross-linker of protein, which can be used as a useful marker for protein oxidation (
Kim et al., 2012
). Dityrosine quantification is widely used in the evaluation of protein oxidation in pure soy protein and isolated milk proteins. However, for the detection of protein in meat systems, dityrosine has only been observed in a simulated meat model (
Bhoite-Solomon et al., 1992
;
Lund et al., 2008b
,
2011
) and the finding of its use on raw meat and meat products is still largely unknown. This is because in the complex food system, the excitation and emission bands of individual fluorophores can affect fluorescence of dityrosine, making it difficult to obtain accurate results (
Berezincor and Achilefu, 2010
). As a result, this method has limited applicability for complex feeds.
Tryptophan
Tryptophan has also been described as particularly susceptible to being oxidized (
Pattison et al., 2012
). In contrast to dityrosine, fluorescent assay of tryptophan is a sensitive technique that has been widely used for the evaluation of protein oxidation in meat (
Utrera et al., 2012
). However, the measurement of tryptophan fluorescence may also be inaccurate due to decreased solubility caused by protein cross-linking in the aqueous phase, which may make the results reflect only the decreased protein solubility (
Zainudin et al., 2019
).
Neumann’s alkylation method to quantify methionine sulfoxide
The two main oxidized products of methionine are methionine sulfoxide and methionine sulfone (
Toennies, 1938
). Methionine is very reactive to oxidation by methionine sulfoxide during food processing (
Cuq et al., 1978
). Recently, a comparative study of protein oxidation product concentrations showed that the amount of methionine sulfoxide is by far the highest among the products of protein oxidation, which is at least 14 times that of protein carbonyls (
Chen et al., 2019
). In food and feed processing, up to 70 % of methionine can be oxidized to methionine sulfoxide (
Puchala et al., 1994
;
Baxter et al., 2007
;
Chen et al., 2019
). Therefore, the quantification of methionine sulfoxide has the potential to replace protein carbonyls as a means for evaluating protein oxidation. Methionine sulfoxide can be quantified by Neumann’s alkylation method, which treats the samples with iodoacetic acid and performic acid (
Neumann, et al., 1962
). In addition, the amount of methionine sulfoxide can vary greatly in the same food formulation with different processing, indicating that processing conditions is the main factor for the oxidized modification of methionine (
Hellwig, 2020
).
Amino acids analysis
Almost all the amino acids such as cysteine, tyrosine, phenylalanine, tryptophan, histidine, proline, arginine, lysine and methionine are highly susceptible to oxidation (
Schoneich et al., 2006
). The oxidative modification of amino acid residues is highly dependent on the oxidation conditions for forming different oxidation products. Previous studies have shown that in meat and soybean products, the amino acid content decreases as the oxidation level increases (
Ganhao et al., 2010
;
Chen et al., 2013c
). However, the sensitivity of amino acids as an indicator reflecting protein oxidation could be debated due to results of previous research.
Chen et al. (2013b)
reported that in the process of soy protein oxidation, when the carbonyl content increased tenfold, the content of amino acids such as lysine decreased only 20 % of the initial lysine. Another similar report showed that during the oxidation of soy protein, when the carbonyl group increased by 1.5 times, the amino acid content did not change significantly (
Wu et al., 2014
). A whey protein study showed that the carbonyl content increased twice while the total amount of amino acids did not decrease significantly (
Wang et al., 2012
). Besides, the amino acid content of different feed ingredients and formula feeds varies greatly, and the amino acid content of the same ingredients can also vary with seasonal and geographical changes (
Karr-Lilienthal et al., 2005
). Therefore, it is difficult to establish an amino acid evaluation system for protein oxidation with a generally applicable standard.
Sodium dodecyl sulphate polyacrylamide gel (SDS-PAGE)
The fragmentation and cross-linking of proteins produced by oxidation can inevitably lead to changes in molecular weight distribution of protein. The SDS-PAGE method is an effective method for detecting protein fragmentation caused by oxidation with different molecular weight which is widely used in the detection of protein oxidation (
Duque-Estrada et al., 2020
). For measuring the molecular weight of soy protein, the subunits of β-conglycinin and globulin are commonly used as molecular ladders. However, recent studies showed that when protein oxidation was induced by free radicals in prolonged storage, the bands of soy protein did not change significantly (
Duque-Estrada et al., 2020
;). Myosin heavy chain (MHC) is a common marker for SDS-PAGE detection of meat products. Compared with the results of soy protein studies, the protein band of meat such as MHC was fragmented or aggregated under different oxidized conditions (
Zhang et al., 2016
). The inconsistency of these studies may be due to the relatively high lipids in the meat. This is because lipids are susceptible to peroxidation under oxidation induction, and the oxidized products can further combine with proteins to form larger molecules which are detectable by SDS-PAGE. The latest study showed that the 35 KDa protein in the protein band of extracted-SBM protein decreased after high temperature (35 °C) storage (
Wang et al., 2025
). Considering that phytic acid or other lipids are present in SBM or other protein ingredient, and that fiber has an effect on the protein band, different pretreatments are required to reduce interference. Thus, for the evaluation of protein oxidation, the SDS-PAGE method can be more widely used for protein evaluation and detection of protein-rich feeds.
Other methods
In addition to the assays described above, there are other ways to quantify carbonyl and dityrosine such as high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), mass spectrometry techniques (e.g., MALDI-TOF, LC-MS, and slot blotting. (
Fenaille et al., 2004
;
Chen et al., 2019
). Even though these assays have higher accuracy, their high dependence on equipment and professionals restricts their application in the feed industry.
As another marker of protein oxidation, advanced oxidation protein products (AOPPs) were widely used in biological disease detection. In the presence of iodide, AOPPs could be detected by UV absorption at 340 nm (
Witko-Sarsat et al., 1996
). However, AOPP measurements could be interfered with by the products of glycation, which exist in food and sharing the same wavelength of AOPPs (
Lo et al., 1994
). Therefore, this method has rarely been used for the determination of protein oxidation in food because of its bias.
However, currently, it is somewhat difficult to assess feed quality by detecting the degree of protein oxidation in feed. For feed enterprises, few feed enterprises are equipped with the means for detecting protein oxidation, which poses a risk of reduced aquaculture/livestock breeding efficiency. Furthermore, the detection methods for the carbonyl value of feed protein may involve unstable factors, making it impossible to standardize variations.
The effect of dietary protein oxidation on animals
Proteins are important ingredients of animal feed particularly for poultry, swine and fish aquaculture that play an indispensable role as the structural and functional components of living systems (
Bacou et al., 2021
). Protein is involved in the formation of all organs, muscles, hormones and biological fluids. Therefore, high-quality in-feed proteins are needed for maintaining growth and other physiological functions (
Boye et al., 2012
). Recently, protein oxidation in diets of animals has attracted considerable attention for the purpose of quality evaluation. However, proteins are targets for radical and nonradical species, which make them vulnerable to oxidation in processing, storage, and during transitions in gastrointestinal tracts (
Estévez and Luna, 2017
). Oxidation can reduce the nutritional value of protein due to the resultant carbonylation of peptide chains and amino acids and the decreased protein digestibility (
Kim et al., 2010
;
Utrera and Estévez, 2012
;
Youling et al., 2010
). When animals consume diets with protein that has been highly oxidized, the products of oxidation first enter the digestive system. The products of protein oxidation can confer the oxidation to the enterocytes of digestive organs, thus causing protein degradation, misfolding, and deposition which may lead to oxidative stress, making the animal’s redox state imbalanced and ultimately inducing a variety of compromises in the performance and health of animals (
Oke et al., 2024
).
Growth performance
Growth performance reflects the efficiency of the producer and the overall health status of the animal. The initial research on the effects of dietary protein oxidation on growth was conducted on mice (
Tang et al., 2012a
). This study found that heat-oxidized soy protein showed a higher protein carbonyl content with a level of 6 nmol/mg protein compared with those without oxidation. After the three weeks feeding stage, a significant decrease in feed intake and a notable decrease in body weight gain was found in the mice that were fed oxidized protein compared with the mice supplemented with unoxidized protein (
Tang et al., 2012, a
). Besides, the study of protein oxidation on livestock animals was first conducted in poultry. A study showed that dietary oxidized soy protein with a carbonyl content of 22.35 nmol/mg protein resulted in a significant weight loss by 7 % in the 21-day-old broilers (
Wu et al., 2014
). A similar result by
Zhang et al. (2017)
indicated that heat-induced oxidation of soy protein reduced the average daily gain of broilers for the first 21 days. However, other research on heat-induced dietary protein oxidation showed that soybean meal with a carbonyl content of 11.92 nmol/mg protein caused an increase in the feed conversion ratio for broilers aged 22 to 42 days only without affecting the growth performance of the starter chicks (
Lu et al., 2017
). Soybean meal stored at room temperature for 45 days had no significant effect on the growth performance of broilers either (
Wang et al., 2024
). The difference in these results may be caused by different degrees of protein oxidation although the reasons are unclear. Additionally, the dose-dependent damage of tyrosine oxidation was proven by an oral oxidized tyrosine trial in rats. After feeding rats oxidized tyrosine (O-Tyr) for 24 weeks, a significant decrease in body weight was found in rats fed 4 g/kg and 8g/kg O-Tyr without mortality, while a 2 g/kg O-Tyr supplementation did not affect the growth (
Li et al., 2016
). Moreover, a recent evaluation of the effects of oxidized milk on rats indicated that there were no significant differences in body weight among the groups in the first five weeks (
Li et al., 2019
). However, at the fifth week to the end of the experimental protocol, a significantly lower body weight gain was found in the group of rats fed oxidized milk powder (
Li et al., 2019
). So far, these studies have shown that the effect of dietary protein oxidation on animal growth appears to be a time-dependent effect rather than an acute toxicity effect. However, the effects of protein oxidation on farm animals with longer growth periods such as pigs, laying hens, beef cattle, and dairy cows, are currently lacking.
Oxidative stress
Oxidative stress is the imbalance between the free radicals and antioxidants in animal bodies. Animals are susceptible to oxidative stress especially if they intake oxidized feeds. Nowadays, oxidative stress has become a serious problem in compromising the health status of animals.
Causes of oxidation stress
In a healthy living system there is a steady production of ROS that is readily buffered by effective cellular defense systems, including glutathione as well as antioxidant enzymes: copper-zinc superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) (
Fritz and Petersen, 2011
). Moreover, ROS levels are tightly controlled by an inducible antioxidant program that responds to cellular stressors and is predominantly regulated by the transcription factor such as nuclear factor erythroid 2 (NF-E2)-related factor 2 (Nrf2) and its repressor protein Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1) (
DeNicola et al., 2011
). Oxidative stress occurs when the critical balance is disrupted due to the depletion of cellular antioxidants or excessive accumulation of ROS (
Ray et al., 2012
). Oxidative stress results in direct or indirect ROS-mediated damage of nucleic acids, proteins, and lipids. Among all, lipid peroxidation is particularly harmful for animals because it leads to facile propagation of free radical reactions (
Cejas et al., 2004
;
Stolc et al., 2025
). Because the interaction between lipids and proteins is strong, oxidative reaction products can be easily transferred from lipids to proteins (
Haberland et al., 1988
;
Kim et al., 1997
).
Studies on oxidation stress
Tang et al. (2012a)
found that oxidized soy protein consumption leads to a reduction in the activities of antioxidant enzymes such as GSH-Px and CAT in the plasma, pancreas, liver, duodenum, jejunum, brain and muscle in mice, and produces more ROS, MDA and protein carbonyl. Similarly, the ingestion of diets containing milk powder at different degrees of oxidation led to oxidative stress to the plasma, brain tissue, and liver of rats, where protein oxidation product deposits including protein carbonyl, dityrosine and AOPPs were formed (
Li et al., 2019
).
Li et al. (2016)
also reported that a 24-week feeding of O-Tyr enhanced aspartate aminotransferase and alanine aminotransferase activities, increased total bilirubin content and led to oxidative damage in rat liver. This study summarized that ROS generation together with MDA and AOPPs accumulation promotes hepatic fibrosis via the MAPK/TGF-β1 pathway. Regarding poultry, broilers receiving oxidized soy protein showed decreased glutathione peroxidase activity (GSH-Px) in serum, duodenum and jejunum (
Zhang et al., 2017
). The study concluded that oxidation products caused by depressed antioxidant capacity resulted in a redox imbalance of broilers. Similar results were obtained in an experiment on the effects of soybean meal protein oxidation on broilers (
Lu et al., 2019
). This study led to the conclusion that the total antioxidant capacity in the plasma and jejunum, and the total SOD and GSH-Px activities in the plasma, liver, and jejunum in broilers, could be impaired by oxidized protein. The gene expression of Nrf2 in the liver and jejunum was lower in broilers fed a diet containing the oxidized protein while the gene expression of Keap1 did not differ significantly. The above research shows that dietary protein oxidation could cause oxidative stress in the digestive systems, body fluids and other tissues and organs. Further studies on other animal species are needed, and investigation into the mechanisms of oxidative stress caused by dietary protein oxidation needed.
Immune system
Previously, many studies focused on the effects of lipid oxidation on the immune function of animals. For poultry, it was demonstrated that in-feed lipid oxidation could reduce effectiveness of the secretory IgA response and eventually impair the immune system of broilers. Since proteins and lipids share similar mechanisms of oxidation, it is worthwhile thinking about whether oxidized proteins can cause similar damage to the immune function of animals. The effects of oxidized soy protein on the immune function of broiler chickens were investigated by
Wu et al. (2014)
. In their study, oxidized protein supplementation diminished the weight of immune organs such as liver, spleen and bursa, and decreased the content of IgG in serum and duodenal mucosa of broilers. Interestingly, a significant increase was observed in the activities of the myeloperoxidase in serum and mucosa of broilers fed the oxidized protein. Immune function impaired by dietary protein oxidation was also noted by
Wu et al. (2014)
, showing that the duodenal IgG level of broilers fed the oxidized soy protein was reduced by 65.87 % compared with the control group. A recent study indicated that dietary meat oxidation increased serum LPS levels and downregulated tight junction-related genes in the intestine of mice (
Ge et al., 2020
). Presently, the published papers referring to the relationship between dietary protein oxidation and immune function in animals are very limited.
Gut health
Gut health is a comprehensive indicator for evaluating the status of animals, including effective digestion and absorption, the absence of gastrointestinal illness, normal and stable intestinal microbiome, effective immune status, and status of well-being (
Bischoff, 2011
).
Protein digestion
The digestion of protein in the gut of animals is the most likely process to be affected by protein oxidation. During the processing of protein oxidation, oxidants attack the primary structure of proteins, which can further distort the secondary and tertiary structure of well-packed proteins, and then eventually induce proteins to unfold to random conformations (
Davies and Delsignore, 1987
). Since the tertiary structure and intermolecular forces of protein are the basis of the catalytic cleft of the enzymatic activity (
Talbert and Goddard, 2012
;
Tavano, 2013
), their destruction by protein oxidation has the potential to affect protein digestibility. the susceptibility of enzyme-induced degradation of the oxidized protein is still unclear. In general, modestly oxidized proteins are easily digested by corresponding proteases because slight conformational changes can expose some buried peptide bonds for enzyme hydrolysis and increase degradation susceptibility (
Davies et al., 1987
;
Smuder et al., 2010
;
Zhao et al., 2020
). However, heavily oxidized proteins can lower digestibility, partly due to protein aggregation, which has resistance to enzymatic degradation compared to native protein forms (
Davies and Delsignore, 1987
).
Lu et al. (2017)
reported that, compared to the protein with a carbonyl content of 7.57 nmol/mg protein, moderately oxidized proteins with carbonyl contents of 7.71 and 8.58 nmol/mg protein increased the protein digestibility of broilers from 74.59 % to 76.20 % and 74.67 %, respectively. In contrast, protein carbonyl more than 11.92 nmol/mg significantly reduced the protein digestibility to 68.40 %. Similarly,
Chen et al. (2015)
demonstrated that with the deepening of the soy protein oxidation, the digestibility of broilers gradually decreased. However, oxidized soy protein with a carbonyl content of 6 nmol/mg protein significantly reduced the digestibility of dry matter and crude protein in mice (
Tang et al., 2012a
). These discrepancies may stem from oxidation degree's bidirectional impact on protein conformation: moderate oxidation exposes cleavage sites improving digestibility (
Lu et al., 2017
), while severe oxidation causes cross-linking/aggregation reducing digestibility (
Davies et al., 1987
), potentially explaining the variable growth responses.
Gut microbiota
For the effects of dietary protein oxidation on gut microbiota, previous research has shown that oxidized casein administration induced a decrease of intestinal
Lactobacillus
and increased intestinal
Escherichia coli
and
Enterococcus
in mice (
Yang et al., 2017a
). Oxidized amino acid ingestion changed many systemic metabolic processes, including reduced choline bioavailability, fat accumulation in the liver and renal dysfunction, changes in gut microbiota functions and inhibited energy metabolism (
Yang et al., 2017b
). High-oxidized pork intake reduced relative abundance of the
Akkermansia, Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium
, and
Desulfovibrio
and increased relative abundance of the pro-inflammatory bacteria
Escherichia-Shigella
and pathobiont
Mucispirillum
in the cecum of mice (
Ge et al., 2020
). To date, the effects of dietary protein oxidation on the gut microbiota in livestock are still largely unknown.
Intestinal antioxidant activity
The gastrointestinal (GI) tract is a key source of ROS; even under normal physiological conditions, ingested food such as macronutrients, micronutrients, and preservatives can generate ROS in the GI tract (
Cross et al., 1984
). Meawhile, natural antioxidant pathways and cellular antioxidants such as glutathione and thioredoxin can limit the generation of ROS and the amount can be maintained at a relatively low level. Moderate amounts of ROS have beneficial effects on several physiological processes in the gut including the balance of microbiota, damage repair, and signal transmission (
Bhattacharyya et al., 2014
). However, alterations of the balance between ROS production and the capacity to rapidly detoxify reactive intermediates can lead to oxidative stress. Previous studies have shown that the intestine is the main organ attacked by dietary oxidized protein. According to results obtained by
Tang et al. (2012a)
, after feeding mice the oxidized protein, ROS levels in the duodenum and jejunum in mice were 1.3 times higher than that of the control. Additionally, there was a significant decrease in total antioxidant capacity, GSH-Px, SOD, and CAT activity in the duodenum and jejunum in the oxidized protein group. Among all the reduced antioxidant indicators, the jejunum GSH-Px activity was the most affected, which was six times lower than control group. Similar results were found in a study where broilers receiving oxidized soy protein showed a 1.6 and 2 times decrease in GSH-Px activity in the duodenal and jejunal mucosa, respectively (
Zhang et al., 2017
).
In summary, the current research indicates that the oxidized protein compromises mainly the digestive function and antioxidant capacity of the gut. However, Current mechanisms are largely derived from rodent models; validation in livestock species regarding metabolic pathways of oxidized proteins is needed. Endpoints like growth inhibition, oxidative stress, and gut damage provide the biological basis for establishing protein oxidation threshold standards for feed quality assessment.
Potential effects of dietary protein oxidation on meat quality
Evidence shows that animals could be health-threatened by intaking a diet containing oxidized proteins which cause imbalance in antioxidants and ROS, causing the accumulation of ROS in the blood, liver, kidney, brain, and muscle (
Tang et al., 2012a
;
Li et al., 2016
;
Yang et al., 2017a
, b;
Lu et al., 2019
). This is especially a problem for muscle since it naturally contains the heme proteins myoglobin and hemoglobin with iron ions, which are sensitive to oxidation (
Lund et al., 2011
). When the oxidative potential of a muscle tissue system is suboptimal, this may cause an imbalance or instability in the functioning of muscle biological systems (
Bagchi and Puri, 1998
). As such, this may affect the reactive capacity of chemical components within muscle systems leading to the formation of free radicals and secondary oxidative substrates in the muscle of living animals as well as in postmortem muscle (
Dabbagh et al., 1994
;
Stephens et al., 1996
;
Brigelius-Flohe et al., 1999
). After slaughter, reactive oxygen species (ROS) exert a detrimental effect on meat quality through a sequential causal pathway: 1) Elevated ROS levels directly induce damage to a broad range of biological molecules in meat, encompassing DNA, lipids, and proteins (
Fridovich, 1998
). 2) Lipids are a primary target: during oxidative stress, ROS directly attack lipid components (
Cabiscol et al., 2000
). 3) The lipid peroxidation process degrades polyunsaturated fatty acids into various by-products (e.g., aldehydes) by generating additional free radicals, and these degradation products subsequently act on meat proteins (
Humphries and Szweda, 1998
). 4) The oxidative modifications occurring between meat proteins and lipids lead to structural alterations in these proteins, which ultimately result in meat quality deterioration.(
Tamarit et al., 1998
;
Giorgio et al., 2007
). Moreover, the main product of protein oxidation in muscle is protein carbonyl, which is an active compound that can participate in several chemical reactions that bring negative impacts on meat quality (
Estévez, 2011
). The formation of protein carbonyl in amino acid side chains contributes to impairment on the conformation of myofibrillar proteins leading to its denaturation and loss of functionality (
Carlin et al., 2006
). It has been demonstrated that protein carbonyl has potential effects on meat quality traits, such as decreased water-holding capacity (
Huff-Lonergan et al., 2005
;
Puolanne and Halonen, 2010
), destroyed texture (
Ganhão et al., 2010
), changed flavors, and reduced nutritional value (
Lund et al., 2011
). Therefore, dietary protein oxidation may affect edible tissues (e.g., muscle, mammary gland) via systemic circulation, potentially compromising animal-derived food quality.
Resolution strategies
In feed science and nutrition research, lipid oxidation has always been a hot topic owing to its apparent influence on the deterioration of processed oils, the meals of oil seeds. However, for decades protein oxidation has been disregarded as a major cause of food deterioration in spite of the fact that proteins play a major role in foods from nutritional, sensory, and technological points of view. In order to prevent protein oxidation in feed, several strategies could be applied. For example, improving storage conditions such as reducing light exposure and vacuum packing have been proposed to prevent protein oxidation (
Viuda-Martos et al., 2013
). Other strategies such as directly adding antioxidants to feed and regulating the diet formula to increase its antioxidant status are discussed below on the basis of the current literature.
Prevention during feed processing & storage
Optimize Storage
On one hand, since ultraviolet (UV) rays can accelerate the oxidative decomposition of nutrients such as proteins and vitamins in feed, it is necessary to reduce the impact of light. On the other hand, vacuum packaging is recommended, as this method can isolate oxygen in the air, fundamentally reducing the probability of oxidative reactions. It is particularly suitable for feed products with high protein content.
Control Processing
Parameters Temperature is a core control indicator. If the temperature is too high (e.g., exceeding 85 °C) during processes such as conditioning and pelleting in feed processing, it will destroy the spatial structure of proteins and increase the probability of oxidation. In addition, the processing duration can be adjusted based on the moisture content of raw materials to ensure that the moisture content of the feed is controlled below 12 % after processing, thereby extending its shelf life.
Dietary intervention
Exogenous antioxidants
Antioxidants, including synthetic antioxidants and natural antioxidants, are compounds that inhibit oxidation. Antioxidants can scavenge free radicals both
in vivo
and
in vitro
through the following mechanisms: (1) antioxidants directly scavenge active oxygen radicals by hydrogen supply; (2) antioxidants undergo a series of electron transfer reactions to form stable compounds and antioxidant-derived free radicals (
Wright et al., 2001
;
Alexandra and Alexandra L, 2022
). Therefore, the addition of antioxidants to feed provides an opportunity to reduce the risk of protein oxidation. At present, synthetic antioxidants are widely used in food processing to prevent food from oxidative deterioration and to extend shelf life. Commonly used synthetic antioxidants include tert‑butylhydroxyanisole (BHA), dibutylhydroxytoluene (BHT), tert‑butylhydroquinone (TBHQ), propyl gallate (PG), octyl gallate (OG), Nordihydroguaiaretic acid (NDGA) (
Carocho et al., 2013
). However, it is still controversial whether synthetic antioxidants pose a threat to animal health. The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) stipulates acceptable daily intakes (ADIs) for most synthetic antioxidants (
EFSA, 2004
;
2011
;
2012
). In addition, synthetic antioxidants are generally used to alleviate feed oxidation during prolonged storage rather than alleviating oxidative stress in animal organisms. Natural antioxidants include enzymes and non-enzyme antioxidants (
Seifried et al., 2007
). Non-enzymatic antioxidants can be divided into metabolic antioxidants and biological antioxidants. Metabolic antioxidants are endogenous antioxidants, which are produced by normal metabolic processes and include lipoic acid, glutathione, L-arginine, coenzyme Q10, melatonin, uric acid, bilirubin, metal chelation proteins, and transport proteins (
Dröge, 2002
;
Willcox et al., 2004
).
Tang et al. (2012a)
demonstrated that α-lipoic acid supplementation reversed growth depression in broilers fed oxidized soy protein. Biological antioxidants are exogenous and are compounds that the body cannot produce during normal metabolism and must be obtained from food, such as vitamins E and C, carotenoids, trace elements (selenium, manganese, and zinc), flavonoids, and ε−3 and ε−6 fatty acids (
Valko et al., 2007
). For example, replacing ethoxyquin with tea polyphenols and propyl gallate improved feed oxidative stability, hepatic antioxidant capacity in broilers, and gut health (
Xiao et al., 2024
). Biological antioxidants play an important role in assisting endogenous antioxidants to relieve oxidative stress in living systems (
Willcox et al., 2004
;
Donaldson, 2004
).
Antioxidants presented in the diet or added to the diet include vitamins C, vitamin E, phenolic compounds, carotenoids, selenium, and lipoic acid. Among them, lipoic acid has been shown to mitigate the negative effects of dietary protein oxidation on male mice (
Tang et al., 2012a
). Studies have shown that adding Vitamin E to diets can improve growth performance (
Chae et al., 2006
), antioxidant capacity (
Guo et al., 2001
), immune function (
Khan et al., 2012
), and meat quality of pork and poultry (
Jensen et al., 1998
), especially when the animal is under oxidative stress. According to the characteristics and physiological functions of VE, it may be a potential functional substance that regulates the damage of oxidized proteins to the animal.
Nutritional regulation
Since protein oxidation affects the intestinal function of animals, the nutritional regulation of gut health will be the focus of future research. Nutritional strategies have been developed to improve gut health by 1) enhancing the immune response; 2) reducing the burden of pathogenic bacteria; 3) stimulating the colonization of beneficial microbiota; and 4) stimulating digestion (
Lange et al., 2010
;
Dong et al., 2024
). The studies described above have shown that dietary protein oxidation causes the loss of animal digestive function, the propagation of pathogenic bacteria, and the decline of immune function. Therefore, nutritional strategies should first address these negative consequences. In general, nutritional strategies include the addition of functional additives, the application of high-quality ingredients and the improvement of feed formulation. In order to improve the digestive capacity of animals, the addition of proteases could be effective. Protein hydrolysis can be reduced by oxidation (
Davies et al., 1987
;
Smuder et al., 2010
), and proteases are known to improve protein hydrolysis even in the presence of anti-nutritional factors (
Dosković et al., 2013
). Moreover, exogenous proteases reduced undigested dietary and endogenous protein flow to the hindgut, reduced inflammatory effects associated with proteinaceous antinutrients, and improved gut tensile strength and tight junction integrity (
Cowieson and Roos, 2016
). Therefore, proteases can improve the stability of the intestinal tract by reducing the substrate for putrefactive organisms and enhancing the ability of the intestine to resist the invasion of harmful bacteria (
Cowieson and Kluenter, 2019
). There are naturally inherent components such as mycotoxins, phytates, tannins in legumes and cereals, gossypol in cottonseed protein products, trypsin inhibitors and haemagglutinins in legumes, lectins, and glucosinolates in feed raw materials, which may interfere with nutrients digestion and utilization, gut health, and growth performance of animals (
Sarwar Gilani et al., 2012
). Appropriate addition of protease can improve the digestibility of oxidized proteins, thereby reducing the flow of undigested proteins into the hindgut and thus lowering the risk of microbial dysbiosis. Thus, high-quality feed ingredients are the basis for the health of animals, and the controlling of feed quality and characteristics is still a key factor. In addition, when designing the feed formula, nutritionists should not only balance the amino acid profile, crude protein levels or the energy content of the diet but also consider other key parameters. For example, dietary fibers and buffering capacity are two factors that closely relate to raw materials in the final feed and have a tremendous impact on the fermentation in the gut, pH of the stomach, feed digestibility, and gut development (
Levic et al., 2005
;
Jha et al., 2019
). In all, in order to cope with protein oxidation, the use of nutritional regulation to improve the health status of animals is a potential method to be considered.
Conclusion and perspectives
Protein oxidation induced by ROS can cause modification of backbones and side chains of proteins. These modifications lead to structural changes in primary, secondary, and tertiary structures of proteins, and these structural changes can induce conformational and functional modifications of proteins. In the protein and lipid systems such as formulated feed, interactions between protein and lipid oxidation can lead to further oxidation in a concomitant and a reciprocal manner. Moreover, in animals, oxidative stress can be induced through oxidized proteins in the feeds, resulting in the deterioration of growth, redox state, immune function, and gut health. The relationship between oxidation of feed protein and meat quality and mitigation mechanism is shown in
Fig. 7
. Therefore, the control of in-feed protein oxidation is crucial for the feed and animal breeding industry. Although there are various indexes to evaluate the degree of protein oxidation, there is no complete evaluation system for protein oxidation in feed production. Therefore, there should be a limit on protein oxidation when formulating diets for animals to ensure that oxidation in excess does not occur. It is also critical to develop rapid and accurate screening of protein oxidation for routine testing in feed production. Moreover, more nutritional strategies are needed to minimize the effects of dietary protein oxidation on animals. For example, Utilize omics technologies to identify novel biomarkers (e.g., plasma dityrosine)"; Develop targeted antioxidants (e.g., nano-encapsulated VE) for gut-specific delivery; Integrate AI to predict protein oxidation risk in feed formulations.
