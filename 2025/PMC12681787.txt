Introduction
As an indispensable energy resource in modern society, petroleum has played a crucial role in driving economic development through its extraction and utilization. However, the process of oil extraction and utilization inevitably generates a significant amount of petroleum pollutants, which have caused severe environmental pollution and damage. In recent years, the discharge of non-compliant oil production wastewater has emerged as a prominent issue in oilfield environmental protection. This wastewater contains a variety of heterogeneous hydrocarbon mixtures, including alkanes, aromatic polycyclic hydrocarbons (PAHs), resins, and asphaltenes,
1
among others. The composition and physical properties of petroleum vary significantly due to differences in reservoir sources. While petroleum serves as a key raw material for multiple industries, the pollution problems caused by leaks are becoming increasingly prominent.
2
With the continuous iteration and upgrading of pollution control technologies, chemical remediation methods, though renowned for their high efficiency, often come with the risk of secondary pollution.

In contrast, microbial remediation technology, with its environmentally friendly and sustainable advantages,

In-depth exploration of the degradation potential of microorganisms in nature and understanding and applying the mechanisms of microbial gene action will provide more promising ecological solutions for the remediation of oil pollution. Among numerous microorganisms,
Pseudomonas stutzeri
shows great application potential due to its wide environmental adaptability and genetic diversity.
Pseudomonas stutzeri
, a bacterium widely distributed in nature and belonging to the genus
Pseudomonas

8
Meanwhile,
Pseudomonas stutzeri
, known for its high physiological characteristics and genetic levels, has at least 23 genomic variants reported
9
as of July 2025. This is attributed to their high rates of gene mutation, translocation, and recombination when adapting to different natural environments.
10
Previous studies on the composition, distribution, and genomic type allocation of
Pseudomonas stutzeri
detected in oil reservoirs have indicated that
Pseudomonas stutzeri
may form new genomic types in special environments.
11
The application of microbial remediation technology in controlling petroleum pollution offers significant advantages. Firstly, microbial remediation technology can utilize the metabolic activity of microorganisms to decompose petroleum pollutants into harmless substances, thereby purifying the polluted environment. Secondly, this technology is environmentally friendly and will not cause secondary pollution to environmental media such as soil and water bodies. Additionally, microbial remediation technology has relatively low costs, is easy to operate, and is convenient to implement and manage. Therefore, the application prospects of microbial remediation technology in controlling petroleum pollution are broad.
Pseudomonas stutzeri
, a microorganism with wide environmental adaptability, has significant potential in controlling petroleum pollution. It can survive and reproduce in various harsh environments and has a strong metabolic capacity, capable of decomposing a variety of petroleum pollutants. Moreover,
Pseudomonas stutzeri
also has the ability to produce biosurfactants, which helps to increase the bioavailability of petroleum pollutants and thereby enhance the effect of microbial remediation. Therefore, in-depth research on the genomic characteristics and metabolic mechanisms of
Pseudomonas stutzeri
is of great significance for the development of efficient microbial repair technologies.

The research found that both strains of bacteria demonstrated excellent growth capabilities under conditions where different hydrocarbons were the sole carbon sources, with a NaCl concentration of 1–10 % and a pH range of 6–10, showing strong environmental adaptability. Genomic comparative analysis indicates that the 1W1-1A strain is more abundant in aromatic hydrocarbon degradation genes than DW2-1A, which is consistent with its performance in the growth curve experiment where aromatic hydrocarbons are the sole carbon source. Additionally, through the analysis of the genomes of 457
Pseudomonas stutzeri
strains reported globally, it was found that strains carrying alkane degradation genes (such as alkB) and aromatic hydrocarbon degradation genes (such as bamA, nahG, etc.) are widely distributed worldwide, and strains in different environments have unique combinations of hydrocarbon degradation genes. The differences in the geographical distribution of these genes reflect the adaptive evolution of the strains to the environment, providing an important basis for the study of microbial ecological adaptability and functional evolution. This research not only provides scientific guidance for the development of efficient bioremediation technologies for petroleum pollution but also lays a solid foundation for the industrial applications of microorganisms, such as enhanced oil recovery (MEOR) and environmental remediation of petroleum pollution.

Materials and methods
2.1
Sampling and isolation of petroleum-degrading bacteria
Water samples were collected from oil wells in Dagang Oilfield, Tianjin, China, and stored at 4℃. Incubate and isolate petroleum-degrading bacteria using sterile crude oil as the culture medium. The cultures were spread on LB AGAR plates containing 5.0 g/L yeast extract, 10.0 g/L NaCl, 10.0 g/L peptone and 20.0 g/L AGAR, and monoclonal colonies were selected. Two of the strains (1W1-1A and DW2-1A) were identified as
Pseudomonas stutzeri
on the MALID-TOF-MS strain identification mass spectrometer. Strains identified as pure bacteria by MALID-TOF-MS mass spectrometer were cultured in LB medium. Genomic DNA extraction was carried out using QI Aamp DNA Mini Kit as per the manufacturer's instructions. The whole genomes of strains 1 W1-1A and DW2-1A were sequenced using the Illumina Hiseq4000 platform and Pacific Biosciences (PacBio) RSII sequencing technology. The TruSeq DNA sample preparation kit was used to prepare the 500 bp inserted Illumina end-to-end sequencing library, and the 10 kb template library was prepared for the PacBio RSII SMRT sequencing platform. 16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) was amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using the following primers: 27F(5 '-AGA GTT TGA TCC TGG CTC AG-3′) and 1492R (5 '-ggt tac CTT GTT acg act t-3′).
2.2
Phylogenetic tree construction
The 16S rRNA gene sequences were analyzed using the BLASTN program against the NCBI nucleotide collection (nr/nt) database. Sequence alignment was performed using CLUSTALW, and a neighbor-joining phylogenetic tree was constructed based on the Tamura-Nei model using MEGA11 software.
2.3
Morphological observation of
Pseudomonas stutzeri
strains
The cell morphology of
Pseudomonas stutzeri
strains 1W1-1A and DW2-1A was examined using a scanning electron microscope (Quanta 200, FEI Co., USA).
2.4
Whole-genome sequencing
The strains were cultured in LB medium at 37 °C under aerobic conditions. Genomic DNA was extracted using the method described by Marmur and Doty (1962). DNA concentration was measured using a NanoDrop™ 1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Sequencing was performed using the Illumina HiSeq 2000 platform with a paired-end sequencing strategy.
2.5
Sequence assembly and annotation
A total of 10,252,784 paired-end reads (1,281,598,000 bp) were obtained from the Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform, corresponding to 260-fold genome coverage. The PacBio platform generated 45,177 polymerase reads (1,514,998,013 bp). The raw data were assembled into a single contig using SMRT Analysis v2.1. Gene prediction was performed using the Glimmer software program. tRNA and rRNA genes were identified using tRNAscan-SE and RNAmmer, respectively. Functional annotation was carried out using BLASTP against the NR, KEGG, and COG databases, and metabolic pathways were determined using the KEGG Automatic Annotation Server (KAAS).

Growth curve determination
The growth of

2.9
Global distribution of hydrocarbon-degrading genes
A total of 457 whole-genome sequencing results of
Pseudomonas stutzeri
from various regions around the world were downloaded from NCBI. Prokaa was used for batch annotation, resulting in 457 annotated whole-genome sequences. Batch processing scripts were written to screen for strains carrying the alkane and aromatic hydrocarbon degradation genes
alkB, antA, antB, bamA, bphA, catA, nahD, nahE, nahG, phnA, tmoA, and xylA
. A Python program was developed to extract and match the strain numbers with their countries of origin, and to tally the reported whole-genome counts of this bacterium for each country. An R script was written to map the global distribution of each strain and the distribution of
Pseudomonas stutzeri
strains carrying the aforementioned hydrocarbon-degrading genes on a world map. Another R script was written to match the strain sources with the Prokaa annotation files, and to summarize the characteristics of hydrocarbon-degrading gene carriage in
Pseudomonas stutzeri
strains isolated from various environments, including the East China Sea, open waters of estuaries, metal − contaminated soil, plastic − contaminated soil, mineral oil − contaminated soil, seafloor volcanoes, toluene − contaminated soil, wastewater, and wood. Additionally, a heatmap of gene-environment distribution was generated.

Crude oil is a mixture of hydrocarbons of various molecular weights. It has been discovered that many bacteria in nature can degrade crude oil
30
and use petroleum as the sole carbon source for growth. The
Pseudomonas stutzeri 1W1-1A and DW2-1A can utilize alkanes and aromatic hydrocarbons from crude oil as carbon sources.
The genomic annotation results are shown in
Table 1
. The genes involved in energy metabolism of strains 1W1-1A and DW2-1A are shown in
Table 2
. Their genomes contain the alkB gene, encoding the key enzyme alkane 1-monooxygenase for alkane degradation. Additionally, flagellar genes (e.g., fli, flh, flg, mot) enable bacterial movement to the oil–water interface, facilitating efficient hydrocarbon degradation. Many other petroleum-degrading bacteria also have a set of genes related to flagellar assembly, and it is believed that these genes can also be beneficial to the emulsification and degradation of hydrocarbons in crude oil.
31
The genomes of the two strains also contain genes related to the synthesis of biosurfactants, such as the epsE and epsF genes encoding glycosyltransferases, the rmlC gene encoding DTDP-4-dehydrated rhamnose 3, 5-episomerase, and the rmlD gene encoding DTDP-4-dehydrated rhamnose reductase. These enzymes are involved in the synthesis of rhamnolipids, a class of glycolipids that act as bacterial surfactants by reducing surface tension, critical micelle concentration and interfacial tension, and increasing the emulsification and solubility of hydrocarbons in mixtures such as crude oil.
32
These genes reflect the metabolic characteristics of strains 1W1-1A and DW2-1A, which can degrade macromolecular and heteroatomic compounds such as naphthalene, chloroalkane, and alkanesulfonic acid while preserving alkanes, and have the potential for MEOR applications.
The genomes of these Pseudomonas stutzeri strains contain diverse metabolic genes, including those for nitrate reduction (e.g., nar, nap), nitrite reduction (nir), and hydrocarbon degradation. Key genes such as frmA and adhC enable the breakdown of naphthalene, chloroalkanes, and other aromatic compounds, while ssuD supports alkane sulfonate metabolism. This genetic repertoire allows the strains to utilize crude oil components as carbon sources and electron donors, even in nutrient-limited, anoxic reservoirs. Additionally, both strains possess complete flagellar and fimbrial synthesis genes, facilitating motility and chemotaxis. Notably, strain 1W1-1A exhibits enhanced aromatic hydrocarbon degradation capabilities due to unique genes like bphI and bphJ, which mediate ring-opening of benzene derivatives, and gene clusters such as pca and ben. These traits, supported by experimental data, indicate that 1W1-1A is particularly effective in degrading and utilizing complex aromatic hydrocarbons. The bphJ gene encoding phenol dehydrating enzyme, as well as numerous genes in gene clusters such as pca and ben, combined with the results of surface tension measurement data in
Fig. 3
and
Table 1
, all demonstrate that strain 1W1-1A has a stronger effect on the degradation and utilization of aromatic hydrocarbons.

Conclusions
This study conducted whole-genome sequencing on two strains of
Pseudomonas stutzeri
, namely 1W1-1A and DW2-1A, isolated from the Dagang Oilfield, revealing their rich hydrocarbon-degrading genes and providing significant genetic resources for the remediation of petroleum-contaminated environments. The research found that both strains exhibited robust growth capabilities under conditions where different hydrocarbons served as the sole carbon source, in the presence of 1-8 % NaCl, and across a pH range of 6 to 10, demonstrating strong environmental adaptability. Comparative genomic analysis indicated that strain 1W1-1A possessed a more abundant set of aromatic hydrocarbon-degrading genes than DW2-1A, which was consistent with its performance in growth curve experiments using aromatic hydrocarbons as the sole carbon source. Moreover, through the analysis of the genomes of 457 reported strains of
Pseudomonas stutzeri
from around the world, it was found that strains carrying alkane-degrading genes (such as
alkB
) and aromatic hydrocarbon-degrading genes (such as
bamA
and
nahG
) are widely distributed globally. Strains from different environments were found to possess unique combinations of hydrocarbon-degrading genes. The geographical distribution differences of these genes reflect the adaptive evolution of the strains to their environments, providing important evidence for the study of microbial ecological adaptation and functional evolution. This study not only offers scientific guidance for the development of efficient bioremediation technologies for petroleum contamination but also lays a solid foundation for microbial industrial applications, such as enhancing microbial oil recovery (MEOR) and the remediation of petroleum-contaminated environments.
