1
Introduction
Myasthenia gravis (MG) is a rare autoimmune disorder characterized by fluctuating muscle weakness and fatigue. Clinical heterogeneity is reflected in differences in antibody status, age at onset, thymic pathology, and disease severity [
1
]. Most patients (~85%) are acetylcholine receptor antibody seropositive (AChR+), while others are seropositive for muscle‐specific tyrosine kinase (MuSK) or low‐density lipoprotein receptor‐related protein 4 (LRP4) antibodies. MG subgroups based on age at onset include early‐onset MG (EOMG; 18–50 years), late‐onset MG (LOMG; 51–65 years) [
1
], and very late‐onset MG (VLOMG; > 65 years). AChR+ EOMG often affects women and is typically associated with thymic hyperplasia, while LOMG and VLOMG [
2
] more often affect men and usually lack thymic pathology.
Although antibody testing remains central to MG diagnosis, it does not reflect disease activity or treatment response [
3
] and antibodies remain absent in a subset of patients. Current disease monitoring relies largely on clinical evaluations and subjective scales of muscle fatigability, which are insufficient to guide individualized treatment [
4
]. Consequently, there is a growing need for objective, disease‐specific biomarkers that can support longitudinal disease monitoring, patient stratification, and evaluation of therapeutic response.
Recent biomarker research in MG has explored both targeted and untargeted proteomic approaches. Traditional mass spectrometry‐based proteomics enables broad protein discovery but often lacks sensitivity for low‐abundance proteins and requires extensive sample preparation. In contrast, the proximity extension assay (PEA), as used in the Olink platform, offers high sensitivity and specificity for predefined panels of proteins, particularly those involved in inflammation and immune regulation. While PEA is not discovery‐based, it allows robust quantification across large cohorts and is well suited for biomarker validation.
A recent study combining mass‐spectrometry‐based techniques with machine learning identified inter‐alpha‐trypsin inhibitor heavy chain (ITIH3), as a potential serum biomarker correlating with disease severity in AChR+ MG [
5
]. Other proteins reported to be elevated in MG include serum neurofilament light chain [
6
] and calprotectin [
7
], though these are not specific to MG and may reflect general neuroinflammation.
Previous PEA‐based studies have identified serum proteins such as CCL28, TNFSF14, 4E‐BP1, TGF‐α, and ST1A1 that distinguish AChR+ MG from healthy individuals, and others, such as IL18R1, CXCL17 and CCL11, associated with late‐onset MG and immunosuppressive therapy [
8
,
9
]. However, these markers are not necessarily specific to MG and may reflect general autoimmune or inflammatory processes. Therefore, these proteins require validation in larger, independent cohorts and comparison with other autoimmune neurological diseases.
In this multicenter study, we aimed to validate serum inflammatory proteins as potential biomarkers for MG, with a focus on their disease specificity and relevance for future treatment monitoring. We compared MG patients with controls and two disease controls, multiple sclerosis (MS) and chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (CIDP), to contextualize findings within broader neuroimmunological conditions. Secondary aims included evaluating the impact of immunosuppressive treatment and clinical subgroups on protein profiles.
2
Methods
2.1
Study Design, Power Calculations, and Patient Cohorts
Power calculations based on previous data for a t‐test performed in R indicated that a sample size of ~150 MG patients, 150 controls, and 150 neuroimmune disease controls (MS and CIDP) would provide ≥ 80% power to validate 21 differentially expressed biomarkers after Bonferroni correction (
p
< 0.0024). This sample size was sufficient to detect effect sizes similar to those observed for top markers in prior data [
8
]. For this validation cohort, serum was obtained from 200 AChR+ MG patients, 192 age‐ and sex‐matched healthy blood donors (referred to as “controls”), and 144 disease controls (93 MS and 51 CIDP). MG sera were collected from Uppsala University Hospital and Charité—Universitätsmedizin Berlin. All MG patients had confirmed AChR antibodies and objective fatigable weakness. Diagnosis was further supported by abnormal neuromuscular transmission on repetitive nerve stimulation and/or single‐fiber electromyography when available. MG subgroups were defined by age at onset (EOMG, LOMG, VLOMG) and severity using the MGFA classification (Table
1
). Clinical data included demographics, disease history, treatment, and follow‐up status. The MGFA class was available for all MG patients, whereas the MG‐ADL scores were available for 184 patients and the QMG scores for 103 patients.

10
] and included only typical presentations.
2.2
Sample Handling and Proteomic Analysis
Venous blood was collected in additive‐free tubes, allowed to clot, centrifuged, and serum aliquots were stored at −80°C. Haemolyzed and lipemic samples were excluded. Proteomic profiling was performed using the Olink Target 96 Inflammation panel, measuring 92 proteins. Samples were randomized and processed at the Clinical Biomarkers Facility, Uppsala University, with the group identity blinded. Quality control excluded 11 samples, yielding 200 MG, 93 MS, 51 CIDP, and 192 controls for final analysis.
The proximity extension assay (PEA) was performed per the manufacturer's protocol. NPX values (log
2
scale) were calculated from Ct values normalized to internal controls [
11
,
12
]. NPX values below the detection limit were retained due to potential biological relevance.
2.3
Statistical Analysis
Logistic regression was used to compare groups, with and without adjustment for age and sex. Bonferroni correction was applied for multiple comparisons (threshold
p
< 0.000543 for 92 tests;
p
< 0.00135 for 37 pre‐specified markers). All comparisons resulting in
p
‐values below the Bonferroni‐adjusted threshold were considered statistically
significant
, whereas proteins with unadjusted
p
‐values < 0.05, but not meeting the Bonferroni‐corrected threshold, were considered
nominally significant
. Since the NPX values are log
2
transformed, an increase of one unit in odds ratio (OR) represents doubling of the biomarker on a linear scale. The c‐statistic estimates the probability of correctly discriminating between two different groups; the higher the value, the better the discrimination.
Proportional odds logistic regression analysis assessed MG subgroups using the ‘lrm’ function in the R package ‘rms’. PCA and data visualization were performed with GraphPad version 10.4 and R version 4.4.2.
2.4
Feature Selection by Machine Learning
Random Forest and Boruta algorithms were used for biomarker selection. Random Forest used bootstrapped ensembles and permutation importance. Boruta introduced permuted shadow features and identified confirmed markers based on importance scores across 200 iterations, implemented via the Boruta R package [
13
].
3
Results
3.1
A Distinct Inflammatory Protein Biomarker Profile Was Validated to Distinguish AChR+ MG From Controls
This cohort included serum from 200 AChR+ MG patients [(75 males (37.5%), median age: 59 years, IQR 43.0–70.0; Table
1
)] and 192 matched controls [(93 males (48.4%), median age: 52 years (IQR 42.3–61.0))]. MG subtypes included EOMG (52.0%), LOMG (27.5%), and VLOMG (20.5%). The median MG disease duration was 4.0 years (IQR: 2.0–12.0), and 78.5% received immunosuppressive therapy, including 30 (19.1%) treated with newer biologics (Table
1
).
External validation of a previously identified 37‐protein‐panel [
5
] revealed 14 biomarkers significantly altered in MG versus controls in both unadjusted (Model 0) and age‐and sex‐adjusted (Model 1) logistic regression models (Bonferroni‐ threshold
p
< 0.00135), including AXIN1, IL7, ST1A1, IL10, CASP‐8, TNFSF14, HGF, STAMBP, IL6, IL8, SIRT2, CCL20, CDCP1 and ENRAGE (Table
2
, Table
S1
and Figure
1A,B
). Five additional proteins showed significance only in the unadjusted analysis (FGF‐5, FGF‐21, FGF‐23, MCP‐3, CD244).

3.3
Shared Biomarkers Across Autoimmune Neurological Disorders (MG, CIDP, and MS)
Comparison with controls identified 13 proteins significantly altered in MS and 20 in CIDP, confirmed by both logistic regression models and Boruta analysis (Figure
S1A,B
and Table
S3
). Most protein levels were lower in MS than in CIDP, with some exceptions (Figure
S1C
). Odds ratios for all comparisons are visualized in Figure
3G
.
Seven proteins, CASP‐8, FGF‐23, IL6, IL8, SIRT2, STAMBP, and TNFSF14, were common to all three disorders in the unadjusted model (Figure
3D
). After adjustment, five (IL6, IL8, SIRT2, STAMBP, and TNFSF14) remained significant (Figure
3E
). Integration with Boruta analysis identified four robust shared biomarkers, IL6, IL8, STAMBP, and TNFSF14 (Figure
3F
).
Further analysis revealed overlap between MG and MS (AXIN1, IL7, SIRT2, and ST1A1) and between MG and CIDP (ADA, CASP‐8, HGF, IL10, EN‐RAGE, CDCP1, and OPG). CD40 was specific to MS and CIDP compared to controls, but not to MG (Figure
3C,F
).

3.5
15‐Protein‐Inflammatory Signature Correlated With MG Disease Severity
Disease severity, defined as MGFA class, was categorized for 185 patients at the sampling time: 29 in remission, 31 with ocular MG (MGFA class I), 110 with mild generalized disease (MGFA class II), and 15 with moderate generalized disease (MGFA class III). Ordinal regression analysis identified 15 proteins nominally significantly associated with disease severity (< 0.05). PCA of severity classification highlighted 15 proteins distinguishing severity subgroups (Figure

and Table

Discussion
This study provides a comprehensive comparison of serum inflammatory proteins across three neuroimmune conditions, offering valuable insight into shared and distinct inflammatory profiles. While AChR+ MG is an antibody‐mediated disorder, MS is primarily driven by T lymphocytes, with the contribution from B cells and antibodies (e.g., oligoclonal bands), and CIDP is both a B‐ and T‐cell‐mediated inflammatory disorder. Given their neuroinflammatory nature, these diseases expectedly showed certain overlapping protein alterations compared to controls. However, distinct expression patterns emerged: CIDP exhibited the highest inflammatory protein levels, despite immunoglobulin therapy, followed by MG, while MS displayed the lowest levels overall. This may reflect the different anatomical sites of primary pathology, peripheral nervous system (PNS) demyelination in CIDP, neuromuscular junction pathology and peripheral inflammation in MG [
14
], primarily reflected by serum autoimmunity, and CNS demyelination in MS. Another potential explanation is that the MS patients were treatment‐naive, whereas most CIDP patients had continuous treatment with immunoglobulins.
The top 10 proteins distinguishing MG from controls included AXIN1, IL7, ST1A1, IL10, CX3CL1, IL6, TNFSF14, HGF, IL‐10RB, and CASP‐8. Among these, AXIN1, a regulator of AMPK and mTOR signaling, is highly expressed in skeletal muscle [
15
], yet its role in MG remains unexplored. AXIN1 suppresses Wnt/β‐catenin signaling, which is crucial for myogenic differentiation [
16
]. Notably, TGF‐β‐mediated suppression of AXIN1 enhances Wnt signaling, a pathway implicated in MG [
17
].
AChR+ MG is strongly linked to thymic hyperplasia, making thymectomy a recommended treatment. IL7, a key MG‐associated cytokine, is critical for early thymocyte development [
18
]. Unlike IL6, which increases with age in both normal and MG thymuses, IL7 expression remains stable throughout life [
19
]. Dysregulated IL7/IL7Rα signaling has been implicated in MG, particularly in type B thymomas [
20
].
Several previously identified MG biomarkers, ST1A1, IL10, FGF‐23, TNFSF14, and IL6 [
8
], were validated in this study. Notably, ST1A1, CASP‐8, and AXIN1 interact within T cell regulatory pathways [
21
], possibly explaining their altered levels in MS. Despite their distinct autoimmune mechanisms, MG and MS may share an increased co‐morbidity risk, particularly in EOMG [
22
], where impaired T regulatory (Treg) cell function has been implicated in both diseases.
IL6 seems to be an “overlap” marker for MG, MS, and CIDP, and it significantly differed in all these disorders compared to controls. Experimental autoimmune models for MG (EAMG) and MS (EAE) have demonstrated the disease‐modifying potential of targeting IL6, a cytokine strongly implicated in MG pathogenesis. IL6 receptor blockade in EAMG reduced muscle weakness and restored AChR antibodies, and T follicular helper (Tfh) cells, and plasma cells [
23
].
In a recently published clinical trial, treatment with satralizumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting IL6, resulted in clinical improvement in MG patients [
24
]. Clinically high IL6 levels correlate with MG disease severity [
25
]. TNFSF14 (LIGHT), which promotes IL6 production, is reduced in MS, potentially contributing to disease susceptibility [
26
]. The MG‐associated biomarker FGF‐23 [
8
,
27
] was also validated in the study, contrasting with MS, where FGF‐23 levels were lower, highlighting distinct inflammatory profiles. CASP‐8 emerged as a shared marker for MG and CIDP, underscoring apoptosis in antigen‐presenting cells as a potential common pathological mechanism [
28
]. CIDP exhibits features of both humoral and cell‐mediated autoimmunity, including inflammation at lesion sites, response to immunomodulatory treatment, and the presence of autoantibodies against myelin antigens [
29
]. Apoptosis, particularly activation‐induced cell death via the Fas–FasLigand pathway, is essential for eliminating autoreactive T cells and maintaining immune tolerance [
30
].
CASP‐8 plays a key role in this pathway, activating caspase‐3 to trigger apoptosis. Elevated CASP‐8 levels have been observed in EAMG mice following treatment with a dual altered peptide ligand, correlating with disease reversal [
31
]. Additionally, impaired Fas function has been linked to autoimmunity, with CIDP patients exhibiting reduced Fas‐induced T cell apoptosis, leading to persistent autoreactive T cell responses [
32
]. Hepatocyte growth factor (HGF) was significantly elevated in MG and CIDP, consistent with previous findings [
8
,
33
]. HGF has anti‐inflammatory properties, including suppression of IL6 production, and has been associated with improved steroid treatment responses in CIDP [
34
].
This study identified specific novel MG‐associated proteins, including CCL20, CDCP1, CSF‐1, CX3CL1, and IL‐10RB when three diseases were individually compared to controls. Regarding MG severity classification, FGF‐23, IL‐24, CXCL9, CDCP1, and OPG were the top five distinguishing proteins, with significantly higher levels in mild and moderate generalized MG compared to remission and ocular MG. This suggests a distinct inflammatory profile associated with disease progression, where these biomarkers may reflect heightened immune activation and inflammation in more severe MG subtypes. The CC chemokine ligand 20 (CCL20) has only one known receptor, CC chemokine receptor 6 (CCR6), to drive Th17‐mediated inflammation, a key pathway in autoimmunity [
25
,
35
,
36
]. CDCP1, a ligand for CD6, contributes to autoimmune inflammation. CSF‐1 and CX3CL1 regulate macrophage activity, influencing muscle physiology, and inflammation. Notably, CX3CL1 expression is upregulated in skeletal muscle cells upon TNF‐α stimulation, further linking inflammation to muscle involvement in MG [
37
]. CDCP1 was also significantly higher in patients with mild and moderate generalized MG than in those in remission and with ocular MG. Further, HGF and FGF‐23 both showed significant positive correlations with MG‐ADL and QMG scores, supporting their potential relevance as biomarkers of disease severity in MG.
AChR+ MG is subclassified based on age at onset, phenotype (ocular vs. generalized), and disease severity. This study identified distinct protein signatures across these subgroups. Several proteins, including CD40, CDCP1, CST5, Flt3L, GDNF, IL‐15RA, and osteoprotegerin (OPG), increased with age, clearly distinguishing late‐onset MG (LOMG) and very late‐onset MG (VLOMG) from EOMG. Although OPG showed the highest odds ratio for MG onset, consistent with previous findings [
8
], this effect was neutralized when adjusting for age and sex. OPG, a regulator of bone metabolism, increases with age and is influenced by sex hormones [
38
]. The rising incidence of LOMG and VLOMG may reflect an age‐related shift toward a pro‐inflammatory cytokine profile, termed “inflamm‐aging
”
[
37
]. Conversely, TRANCE and TWEAK levels declined with age in MG. TRANCE, a TNF superfamily cytokine crucial for immune tolerance, was significantly reduced in immunosuppressed MG patients, likely due to the high prevalence of immunosuppression in LOMG and VLOMG; although TRANCE was still significant for immunosuppression status in the logistic regression when adjusted for sex and age. TRANCE binds OPG with high affinity [
39
], suggesting a regulatory interaction that may underlie OPG's age‐related increase in MG.
Immunosuppressive treatment in MG, often adapted from MS therapies, affects shared inflammatory pathways. TRANCE and CD6 were the most differentially expressed proteins in immunosuppressed MG patients. TRANCE reduction may reflect the age‐related increase in OPG due to high immunosuppression rates in older MG patients. TNFRSF9 (CD137), another TNF super family member and a key T cell co‐stimulatory molecule, was the most significantly downregulated protein in MG patients receiving novel biologics. Its stimulation of antigen‐presenting cells promotes monocyte and macrophage proliferation, resulting in pro‐inflammatory cytokine secretion [
40
]. TNFRSF9 blockade dampens Th1/Tc1 responses in EAE, mirroring the impact of newer immunosuppressants in MG. Autoimmune expansion of CD8+ and CD4+ T cells correlates with increased CD137 expression, while reduced Treg function lowers CD137 levels, potentially reflecting the effects of biologic therapy. CD6, a novel MG‐specific protein, was significantly reduced with immunosuppression. CD6 blockade decreases Th1/Th17 cell numbers and enhances NK cell cytotoxicity [
41
], suggesting a mechanistic link to MG immunotherapy.
Several factors should be considered when interpreting our findings. The MG cohort was heterogeneous in treatment and disease duration, and most patients received immunosuppressive therapy, which may have influenced serum protein levels. Differences in treatment exposure across comparator groups (e.g., IVIG in CIDP, untreated MS) may also have introduced bias. The cross‐sectional design limits causal inference, and biomarker rankings from regression or machine learning carry some uncertainty. Although the Bonferroni correction applied to adjust for multiplicity was appropriately stringent for one outcome, the inclusion of multiple outcomes and subgroup analyses inherently increases the risk of spurious results. Although the identified proteins are not specific to MG and may reflect broader immune activation, their consistent association with disease status suggests potential utility as biomarkers for disease monitoring rather than diagnosis. Nonetheless, the study's strengths include a large, well‐characterized cohort, the inclusion of relevant disease controls, and the use of a high‐sensitivity proteomics platform.
In conclusion, this study validates distinct serum inflammatory protein profiles in MG, including both disease‐specific markers and proteins shared with MS and CIDP, reflecting overlapping and unique immune signatures. The identification of MG subgroup‐specific biomarkers and treatment‐associated protein changes provides new insights into disease pathogenesis and reveals potential targets for therapeutic monitoring. These findings advance the development of objective, blood‐based biomarkers for MG, with implications for diagnosis, stratification, and personalized treatment. Future studies are needed to elucidate the functional roles of these proteins in disease progression and therapeutic response.
