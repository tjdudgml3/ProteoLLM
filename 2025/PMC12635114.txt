Introduction
Alpha-thalassemia major (homozygous alpha-thalassemia-1) or hemoglobin (Hb) Bart’s disease is the most severe form of thalassemia, characterized by the absence of alpha-globin chain production
1
–
5
. The frequency of thalassemia mutations is present in up to 5.0% of the population in Southeast Asia, leading to a high prevalence of the homozygous mutation (-SEA/-SEA)
6
, which causes Hb Bart’s disease. Fetuses with this condition develop hydrops fetalis and normally die before or shortly after birth. Therefore, early identification, diagnosis, and management are key for the prevention of subsequent complications.
Currently, prenatal screening for Hb Bart’s involves Hb typing and assessing the mean corpuscular volume (MCV), dichlorophenolindophenol (DCIP), and osmotic fragility (OF)
5
,
7
,
8
. Any couple at risk of their fetus having Hb Bart’s hydrops fetalis is counseled to perform an invasive diagnostic test (chorionic villus sampling, amniocentesis, or cordocentesis) or the use of a noninvasive technique, such as ultrasonography, to evaluate the cardiothoracic ratio (CTR), middle cerebral artery peak systolic velocity (MCA-PSV), or placental thickness
5
,
8
. Late in the first trimester (12–15 weeks of gestation), a CTR of > 0.5 has the highest predictive performance for the identification of Hb Bart’s hydrops fetalis with 90% sensitivity, 97.2% specificity, 90% positive predictive value (PPV), and 97.4% negative predictive value (NPV)
5
. A high MCA-PSV (> 1.5 multiple of median [MoM]) and a thick placenta (> 18 mm) in the first trimester show a limited capacity to predict Hb Bart’s hydrops fetalis (MCA-PSV: 17.6% sensitivity, 96.7% specificity, 66.7% PPV, and 76.1% NPV; placental thickness: 72.9% sensitivity at 31.2% false positive rate)
5
. Maternal blood biochemical markers (e.g., pregnancy-associated plasma protein A [PAPP-A] and placental growth factor) and prenatal cell-free fetal DNA have been shown to be poor predictors of Hb Bart’s hydrops fetalis; thus, they are not routinely used in a clinical setting
5
,
9
–
14
. The use of ultrasound is relatively limited due to its operator- and equipment-dependent nature. Hence, there is a need to identify Hb Bart’s biomarkers that can be detected using noninvasive methods.
Maternal plasma proteins biomarkers have been shown to play roles in the pathogenesis of the great obstetrical syndromes, including preeclampsia and fetal death
15
–
22
. Until recently, soluble plasma proteins mediated regulatory activities via autocrine, paracrine, and endocrine signaling pathways by directly binding to cell surface receptors
23
–
28
. However, the identification of extracellular vesicles (EVs) and their role in intercellular signaling has shed new light on molecular communication, including the maternal–fetal dialogue, which is now considered a complex phenomenon that involves EVs mediating the cross-talk between the feto-placental unit and the mother
23
–
28
. EVs are lipid bilayer-enclosed nanoparticles that are secreted by cells into the extracellular space
29
,
30
. They can carry proteins and nucleic acids
31
with angiogenic/anti-angiogenic, immunoregulatory, growth regulatory, and other properties
32
,
33
and deliver them to other specific cell types. Hence, these intercellular messengers are more efficient than the communication system that relies on the release of free molecules into the extracellular space. EVs can alter a cell’s physiology by delivering their cargo through an intracellular delivery mechanism. They can also release their cargo in the vicinity of target cells to create a high extracellular concentration of a specific messenger molecule, even with a small number of released molecules
34
,
35
. In pregnancy, the placenta is known to secrete a large number of EVs into the maternal circulation; placental EVs are shed from the syncytiotrophoblast into the intervillous space and then flushed via the uterine veins into the maternal circulation
36
. The placental EVs can be confirmed to have a placental origin by detecting the placental alkaline phosphatase (PLAP) protein marker.
In Hb Bart’s hydrops fetalis, the placental pathology exhibits obvious vascular alterations associated with placental hypoxia (i.e., increased number of villous vessels, thickened vascular endothelium, and more branching of vessels)
37
. The development of hydropic villi, which reduces the intervillous space, further exacerbates placental and fetal hypoxia
37
–
39
. Previous studies have demonstrated that hypoxia stimulates the release of EVs
40
and influences EVs’ contents as well as their biological activities, such as protein and miRNA, against target cells
40
–
42
. Therefore, we hypothesized that the placental hypoxia observed in fetuses with Hb Bart’s stimulates the release of placenta-derived EVs.
In this study, we aimed to characterize the (1) placenta-derived EVs in the maternal plasma; and (2) proteomic profiles of maternal plasma EVs in women with Hb Bart’s fetuses. The findings of this study will serve as a foundation for developing a noninvasive method for detecting Hb Bart’s disease.

Determination of the PLAP levels in the isolated EVs
PLAP is a membrane-bound glycoprotein primarily expressed in the placenta during pregnancy. Since EVs share plasma membranes with their original cells and tissues, we measured the PLAP levels in the EVs isolated from the participants to identify placenta-derived EVs and examine their use as an indicator of placental pathology or injury (Fig.
4
). The amount of PLAP per EV was significantly higher in the women with fetuses with Hb Bart’s with hydrops (Group 2) compared to that in the women in the gestational age-matched normal pregnancy group (Group 1). The women with fetuses with Hb Bart’s without hydrops also had a non-significant trend of high PLAP levels per EV. The women in the disease control group (Group 4) seemed to have the highest PLAP levels per EV, which suggests that their placental pathologies (preeclampsia and FGR) resulted in more protein leakage than the placental edema and hypoxia present in the women with fetuses with Hb Bart’s hydrops. The women with fetuses with hydrops fetalis due to non-Hb Bart’s causes (Group 5) had significantly lower PLAP levels per EV than the gestational age-matched normal controls (Fig.
4
).

The reactome analysis showed that the 16 proteins are associated with alterations in the innate immune system and the axis of toll-like receptors (TLRs), myeloid differentiation factor 88 (MyD88), interleukin-1 receptor-associated kinase 4 (IRAK4), and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)

Discussion
Principal findings
This study determines the levels of PLAP per EV and the proteomic profiles of maternal plasma EVs from women with Hb Bart’s fetuses. We found that: (1) the EVs from the women with fetuses with Hb Bart’s with hydrops were smaller than those from the women in the normal and disease control groups; (2) Bart fetuses with hydropic features had higher PLAP levels per EV particle than gestational age-matched normal pregnancies; although, PLAP levels per EV particle tended to be highest in the placental associated complications groups; (3) Bart fetuses without hydropic features had a non-significant trend of high PLAP-EV levels compared to gestational age-matched normal pregnancy group; (4) fetuses with hydrops due to non-Hb Bart’s causes had lower PLAP levels per EV particle compared to gestational age-matched normal pregnancies; and (5) The proteomic analysis of the maternal plasma EVs revealed that the proteins that were differentially expressed in the EVs are involved in aberrant immune responses, pro-inflammatory cytokine regulation mediated by the TLRs/MyD88/IRAK4/MAPK axis, and vascular injuries associated with the pathophysiology of Hb Bart’s hydrops fetalis. Based on these findings, we hypothesized that the number of PLAP-containing EVs and their proteomic profiles are related to the degree of placental hypoxia.
Prenatal identification of Hb Bart’s hydrops fetalis
Currently, sonographic and maternal serum biomarkers are evaluated during screening for fetuses with Hb Bart’s hydrops in at-risk pregnancies. Common sonographic markers include the CTR and MCA-PSV
5
,
39
. To date, only the CTR accurately predicts fetal Hb Bart’s disease as early as 12–15 weeks of gestation, with a detection rate of 75–100% at 90–100% specificity
5
,
39
. The detection rate of the MCA-PSV (≥ 1.5 MoM) is approximately 64–85% at 16–22 weeks of gestation, with 98–100% specificity
5
,
39
. Other sonographic signs, including nuchal translucency, placental thickness, liver length, and splenic circumference, have limited value in the early identification of fetal Hb Bart’s disease
5
,
39
. Recently, Harn-A-Morn et al. demonstrated that serial ultrasound screening (using the CTR and MCA-PSV) for the identification of Hb Bart’s disease during pregnancy, beginning in the first trimester and continuing every 2–4 weeks until 24 weeks, has a sensitivity of 100% at a 10.9% false positive rate for detecting pre-hydropic signs
8
. They also showed that the mean gestational age at the time of Hb Bart’s diagnosis was 15.5 ± 2.6 weeks of gestation. Therefore, our national standard prenatal care program involves the use of sonographic markers in routine screening for Hb Bart’s disease in all at-risk pregnancies. The main limitations of ultrasonography are that it requires specific equipment and is operator-dependent, which means additional training is necessary.
Several maternal serum biomarkers, such as free beta-human chorionic gonadotropin, inhibin-A, PAPP-A, alpha-fetoprotein, unconjugated estriol, and angiogenic factors (soluble fms-like tyrosine kinase and placental growth factor), have poor predictive value for the diagnosis of Hb Bart’s disease
5
,
39
. In addition, the use of non-invasive prenatal testing for the identification of fetuses with Hb Bart’s is still challenging. According to a recent study involving 878 cases at risk of fetal Hb Bart’s disease, the sensitivity and specificity of cell-free fetal DNA for detecting fetal Hb Bart’s disease as early as 11–13 weeks of gestation were 98.98% and 96.06%, respectively
46
. However, the high costs, complex techniques, and lack of validity associated with noninvasive testing for cell-free fetal DNA make it inappropriate for use in routine clinical practice.
Placenta-derived EVs in the plasma of women with fetuses with Hb Bart’s fetuses
In this study, we isolated EVs using the combinatory methods of stepwise centrifugation, ultrafiltration, and qEV size exclusion chromatography
47
–
50
and then validated the presence of EVs in the isolates using NTA, TEM, and Western blot analysis (Figs.
1
,
2
,
3
) following the ISEV guidelines
43
. Then, we measured the amount of PLAP per EV and used SWATH proteomics to detect and compare the EV proteins present in the EVs from the different groups. To our knowledge, this is the first report of maternal plasma EVs containing PLAP in women with fetuses with Hb Bart’s fetuses.
PLAP is uniquely produced by the syncytiotrophoblast
51
, and EVs containing PLAP are only found during pregnancy
52
,
53
. Our results show that the amount of PLAP per EV was significantly higher in women with fetuses with Hb Bart’s hydrops compared to those with gestational age-matched normal pregnancies (Fig.
4
). The women with fetuses with hydrops due to non-Hb Bart’s causes had significantly lower levels of PLAP per EV than those with gestational age-matched normal pregnancies. However, the amount of PLAP per EV in the women with placenta-associated complications was highest among all groups. This suggests that the amount of PLAP in EVs may depend on the degree of placental pathology or hypoxia. Further studies with larger cohorts are needed to investigate the usefulness of tracking PLAP-containing EVs in women with various pregnancy complications.
Limited studies have examined the role of EVs in thalassemia
54
–
56
, and no studies have evaluated the role(s) of EVs in Hb Bart’s disease. Placenta-derived EVs are thought to promote maternal immune tolerance of the fetal allograft as they can suppress maternal T-cell signaling
52
. In normal pregnancies, placenta-derived EVs have been shown to modulate the function of the maternal endothelium to promote trophoblast migration, angiogenesis, and spiral artery remodeling
57
. However, when inflammatory or pathological conditions occur during pregnancy (i.e., obesity, hypoxia, and high blood sugar), a high number of circulating EVs can induce the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines from endothelial cells, which leads to perturbation of physiological functions
57
–
59
. Together, these findings suggest that the content and effects of placenta-derived EVs depend on the physiological and pathological conditions of pregnant women.
Several lines of evidence indicate that placental hypoxia occurs in women with fetuses with Hb Bart’s disease. First, the placenta has an increased number of immature intermediate villi, which persist despite advancing gestational age; a phenomenon referred to as “generalized delayed villous maturation”
37
,
60
. The immature intermediate villi have large diameters, and their predominance leads to narrowing of the intervillous space, which impedes fetoplacental oxygen and nutrition transfer. Second, villous edema is present, as well as numerous cytotrophoblastic cells that cover the villous stroma
60
. Third, an increased number of stromal cells at the periphery beneath the trophoblast layer, or peripheral villous stromal hypercellularity
60
, is a sign of placental adaptation to chronic hypoxia. In addition, morphometric studies have shown a branching vascular pattern, which is associated with placental hypoxia
60
. This change is thought to be responsible for placental enlargement, which compromises the blood flow from the uterine distension and the generally diminished intervillous space. Finally, the dramatically reduced capacity of Hb Bart’s molecules to extract oxygen from the intervillous space also contributes to placental hypoxia. The observation that the levels of PLAP per EV were highest in the women with placenta-associated complications is consistent with the findings of previous studies
59
,
61
–
63
. It also indicates that the presence of placental hypoxia may stimulate the placenta to release EVs into the maternal circulation.
The women with fetuses with Hb Bart’s without hydrops exhibited a non-significant trend of high PLAP levels per EV, which indicates that they had fewer hemodynamic changes or less placental hypoxia than those with fetuses with Hb Bart’s with hydrops. Interestingly, the women with fetuses with hydrops due to non-Hb Bart’s causes had lower PLAP levels per EV than those with gestational age-matched normal pregnancies. The hydrops in these fetuses was due to non-immune causes: cardiac disease (n = 1), Down syndrome (n = 1), and unknown causes (n = 3). These cases had no fetal anemia, as shown by normal MCV-PSV values. While the pathophysiology of hydrops fetalis is not completely understood, evidence suggests that it is mainly caused by developmental defects in the microcirculation and lymphatic system, decreased ventricular filling, or increased central venous pressure resulting from increased right heart pressure or obstructed lymphatic drainage
64
. The presence and degree of placental hypoxia in hydropic fetuses due to non-Bart causes are currently unknown.
Proteomic profiling of plasma EVs from women with Hb Bart’s fetuses
Our proteomic analysis of the plasma EVs showed that 16 proteins were differentially expressed in the EVs from the different groups (Fig.
5
). These proteins were found to be involved in the fibrinogen complex, fibrin clot formation, and integrin signaling. Among the 16 differentially expressed EV proteins, hnRNPA2B1 showed the greatest difference between women who had fetuses with Hb Bart’s hydrops and those with normal pregnancies or placenta-associated complications.
A2B1 is a member of the hnRNPAB subfamily. It consists of two structurally homologous proteins (hnRNPA2 and hnRNPB1), which have strong sequence correlation and a conserved domain structure; hnRNPB1 has 12 additional amino acids at the N terminus. Like other hnRNPs, A2B1 is an RNA-binding protein. It forms a complex with heterogeneous nuclear RNA (hnRNA) and affects the localization, shearing, stability, translation, and other biochemical functions of RNA
65
. hnRNPs are associated with pre-mRNA in the nucleus and appear to influence its processing and other aspects of mRNA metabolism and transport. They are expressed in several tissues, such as the placenta, ovaries, musculoskeletal tissues, endocrine organs, and gastrointestinal tract
66
. Notably, hnRNPs are differentially expressed in the placentas of women with gestational diabetes mellitus and may play a role in regulating the occurrence and development of gestational diabetes mellitus
66
. Based on our results, we concluded that the women with fetuses with Hb Bart’s hydrops experienced placental hypoxia and that this triggered the release of A2B1 from early in gestation. Indeed, there are four proteins (immunoglobulin lambda-like polypeptide 5, fibrinogen alpha chain, fibrinogen beta chain, and apolipoprotein B-100) that are significantly upregulated in the placenta-associated complications group compared to normal pregnancy and the Bart group (with and without hydrops). However, these proteins are not significantly different between Bart with and without hydrops. Such proteins are involved in inflammation, coagulation, and lipid transportation
67
–
69
. These observations are consistent with previous studies reporting that patients with preeclampsia and FGR had higher concentrations of such proteins
70
–
72
and also imply that placenta-associated complications may have a different pathway for the release of PLAP-containing EVs from Hb Bart’s disease. Larger studies are required to validate our findings.
Clinical and research implications
Ultrasound parameters and soluble maternal serum biomarkers have been proposed as tools for identifying Hb Bart’s in fetuses. However, there is still a lack of sensitive methods for effective early screening. In addition, the detection of ultrasound parameters requires standardized procedures and training. This study revealed that the level of PLAP per EV was higher in women with fetuses with Hb Bart’s hydrops and that 16 proteins—including hnRNPA2B1—were differentially present in EVs from women with fetuses with Hb Bart’s hydrops compared to women with normal pregnancies and placenta-associated complications. Therefore, measuring the total amounts of maternal plasma EVs, PLAP-containing EVs, and the differentially expressed EV proteins might have potential value as a non-invasive screening test for the identification of fetuses with Hb Bart’s hydrops. Given that preparing and analyzing EVs and evaluating their contents require expertise, future studies should focus on developing user-friendly tools for preparing and analyzing EVs in clinical laboratories.
Strengths and limitations
In terms of its strengths, this is the first study to determine the levels of PLAP per EV and the proteomic profiles of plasma EVs from women with fetuses with Hb Bart’s disease. Another strength is that we included groups of women with a range of pregnancy conditions, including a disease control group and hydrops from non-Bart’s causes. In addition, we used a definitive invasive prenatal diagnostic test to confirm that all the patients in the normal pregnancy group were carrying fetuses without Hb Bart’s. We also separated the EVs using published methods and following international standards
43
. The main limitation of our study is related to the small sample size. To overcome this limitation, future studies with larger populations are required to validate our results.
Conclusions
The EVs from the women who have fetuses with Hb Bart’s with hydrops are smaller than those from the women in the normal and disease control groups. Women with fetuses with Hb Bart’s hydrops have higher PLAP levels per EV than those with normal pregnancies. In addition, they suggest that several proteins are differentially present in the EVs of women with fetuses with Hb Bart’s hydrops and that these proteins play roles in innate immunity, the TLRs/MyD88/IRAK4/MAPK axis, and vascular injury. The assessment of placenta-derived EVs might have potential value as a non-invasive screening test for the identification of fetuses with Hb Bart’s hydrops. Future larger studies are required to validate these findings.
Materials and methods
Study population
This prospective cohort study included pregnant women at 11–13 weeks of gestation onwards who attended antenatal care clinics between November 2021 and November 2023. Participants were recruited at Ramathibodi Hospital and Siriraj Hospital, both in Bangkok, Thailand. In Thailand, all pregnant women (and their partners, if available) are required to undergo thalassemia screening during their first prenatal visit. Standard thalassemia screening includes Hb typing and assessing MCV, DCIP, OF, and alpha- or beta-thalassemia mutations in some cases. Couples at risk of their fetus having Hb Bart’s were counseled to support the performance of diagnostic tests or a noninvasive follow-up method, such as ultrasonography. The gestational age of the patient was used to determine the diagnostic testing method. For example, chorionic villous sampling and amniocentesis were performed at 11–13 and 16–18 weeks of gestation, respectively, to collect samples for fetal DNA testing, while cordocentesis was performed at > 18 weeks of gestation to collect samples for Hb typing
5
,
7
,
8
.
Women aged ≥ 18 years and at 11–13 weeks of gestation onward were eligible for inclusion. Women who were unable to give informed consent, whose fetuses showed a severe major abnormality, and whose fetuses were at risk of having Hb Bart’s without prenatal diagnostic test confirmation were excluded from the study.
All patients provided written informed consent prior to sample collection. The use of clinical databases and biological samples was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of Mahidol University, Thailand, with IRB No. COA.MURA2021/439 (Ramathibodi Hospital) and COA.No.SI 840/2021 (Siriraj Hospital). The study was approved by an appropriate institution, and all procedures were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations of Mahidol University.
A total of 39 patients were included in the study. We stratified the participants into five groups: 1) normal pregnancies (Group 1; n = 19; control group); 2) women who had fetuses with Hb Bart’s hydrops (Group 2; n = 4); 3) women who had fetuses with Hb Bart’s without hydrops (Group 3; n = 7); 4) placenta-associated conditions group (e.g., preeclampsia or FGR) (Group 4; n = 4; also called “disease control group”); and 5) women who had fetuses with hydrops fetalis due to non-Hb Bart’s causes (Group 5; n = 5).
All women in Group 1 (the control group) delivered at term without maternal or neonatal complications, and their fetuses were of an appropriate weight for their gestational age. Women in Groups 2, 3, and 5 were matched with women in Group 1 based on gestational age at venipuncture and maternal characteristics (i.e., age, race, parity, and body mass index). Women in Group 4 were gestational age-matched with women in Group 2 because most of the women in Group 2 had a diagnosis of a placental complication during mid-late gestation, which is comparable to the gestational age at the time of diagnosis of Hb Bart’s hydrops fetalis.
Data on the participants’ obstetric and delivery outcomes, including the mode of delivery and maternal and neonatal outcomes, were obtained from computerized records and reviewed by dedicated researchers.
Clinical definitions
Hydrops fetalis is characterized by abnormal interstitial fluid collection in two or more compartments of the fetal body (i.e., the peritoneal cavity, pleura, and pericardium) or fluid accumulation in one site and anasarca
73
–
80
. Preeclampsia was defined as systolic blood pressure (BP) ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic BP ≥ 90 mmHg on at least two occasions measured 4 h apart in previously normotensive women, accompanied by one or more of the following new-onset conditions at or after 20 weeks of gestation: proteinuria, evidence of other maternal organ dysfunction (e.g., acute kidney injury), liver involvement, elevated liver enzymes, neurological complications, hematologic complications, or uteroplacental dysfunction
81
. FGR was defined as estimated fetal weight (EFW) or abdominal circumference (AC) below the third percentile or EFW or AC below the tenth percentile combined with abnormal Doppler findings or a decrease in growth percentiles
82
. In all cases with FGR, we confirmed that the neonatal birth weight was below the third percentile. Uncomplicated or normal pregnancy was defined as a live birth at or after 37 weeks of gestation, with the fetus being an appropriate weight for gestational age without any complications.
EV isolation
Maternal blood was collected in an ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid tube and immediately transported to the laboratory. EVs were isolated from the plasma via stepwise centrifugation, ultrafiltration, and size exclusion chromatography, as previously described
47
–
50
. Briefly, 500 µL of plasma was diluted with 500 µL of filtered phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), centrifuged at 3,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C to remove cell debris, and centrifuged at 12,000 g for 20 min at 4 °C to remove large particles and protein aggregates. The supernatant was filtered through a 100-kDa cut-off centrifugal filter to remove soluble proteins < 100 kDa and to reduce the sample volume (from 1 mL to 500 µL) before being subjected to size exclusion chromatography (qEVoriginal/35 nm series, Izon Science Limited, Christchurch, New Zealand). The EV fractions were collected, pooled, and concentrated using a 3-kDa cut-off centrifugal filter to produce 100 µL samples of isolated EVs.
EV characterization
Nanoparticle tracking analysis
A ViewSizer 3000 nanoparticle tracking analyzer (Horiba, Indiana, USA) was used to determine the sizes and quantities of the isolated EVs. For this, a 1 µL sample was diluted in filtered PBS (1:2000), and then 1 mL was transferred to a cuvette with a magnetic stirrer to capture 25 video images. The acquisition parameters were set as follows: blue laser (445 nm) at 210 mW, green laser (520 nm) at 12 mW, red laser (635 nm) at 8 mW, pulse duration (B/G/R) of 15/15/15 ms, 5 s stirring time, stirring speed of 1,400 rpm, wait time of 3 s, 300 frames/video, and 25 videos. The Multispectral Particle Tracking Analysis software V.2.12 calculated the EV size and number by comparing the test and blank samples (e.g., filtered PBS only).
Transmission electron microscope (TEM)
TEM was used to visualize and determine the morphology of intact EVs. For this, 10 µL of an EV sample was aliquoted onto Parafilm, and the grid was placed face down on the sample for 10 min at room temperature. The grid face was washed twice in PBS for 1 min. After fixing with 2.5% (v/v) glutaraldehyde for 5 min, the grid face was washed twice for 1 min in PBS. The grid face was stained with 2% (w/v) uranyl acetate for 1 min and then air dried for 10 min. The EVs on the grid face were imaged via TEM.
Western blotting of EV protein markers
EV markers were detected via western blotting. Briefly, isolated EVs (20 µL) were lysed in Laemmli buffer (62.5 mM Tris–HCl, 10% glycerol, 2% SDS, and 2.5% beta-mercaptoethanol at pH 6.8) with sonication. After heating at 95 °C for 5 min, Bradford’s assay was used to measure the EV proteins. EV and plasma samples containing 10 µg of total protein were subjected to 10% SDS-PAGE, and the proteins were then transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane. Nonspecific binding sites on the membrane were blocked with 5% skim milk/PBS at room temperature for 30 min. Then, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies (rabbit polyclonal anti-CD9 [ab223052, Abcam], rabbit polyclonal anti-HSP70 [ab79852, Abcam], and mouse monoclonal anti-CD63 [ab193349, Abcam]) diluted to 1:1,000 in 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA)/PBS at 4 °C overnight. The membranes were washed three times with 0.5% Tween 20 in PBS (PBST) for 5 min each. The membranes were then incubated with goat anti-mouse or anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibodies conjugated to horseradish peroxidase diluted to 1:2,000 in 3% skim milk/PBST at room temperature for 1 h. After washing with PBST, a peroxidase substrate for enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) was added, and the chemiluminescent signal was detected using an imaging detector (G: BOX, Syngene, Cambridge, UK).
PLAP EVs ELISA determination
A human PLAP sandwich ELISA kit (MBS289869, MyBioSource) was used to evaluate the PLAP levels in the isolated EVs. Briefly, PLAP at 1,000 pg/mL was subjected to serial two-fold dilutions to establish a set of standards (1000, 500, 250, 125, 62.5, 31.25, 15.625, and 0 pg/mL). Equal volumes (100 µL) of each recombinant protein standard and the EV samples (20 µL EVs plus 80 µL PBS) were added to wells (two technical replicates, seven biological replicates per group) and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. The solution was removed, and 100 μL of detection reagent A working solution was added to each well. After incubation at 37 °C for 1 h, the solution was removed, and the wells were washed three times by adding and aspirating 300 µL of wash buffer. Then, 100 µL of detection reagent B working solution was added to each well before the plate was incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. After thoroughly washing the wells five times, 90 µL of substrate solution was added to each well, and the wells were then incubated at 37 °C for 15 min in the dark. Then, 50 µL of stop solution was added, and the optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm.
The average duplicate OD values of the samples and standards were subtracted from those of the blank control (0 pg/mL PLAP). A standard curve was established by drawing a line of best fit through the standard data points using regression analysis (r
2
> 0.99). The protein concentration of each sample was then calculated using the resultant equation. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to determine the statistical significance of the results, with a
p
-value < 0.05 indicating significance. The PLAP levels were normalized according to the number of EVs in each sample.
SWATH proteomics
A SWATH proteomic analysis was performed as previously described in our studies
83
–
85
. Briefly, 50 µL of EVs were lysed in Laemmli buffer. The total protein was quantified using Bradford’s assay (Bio-Rad, Hercules, USA). For this, 50 µg of protein was reduced, alkylated, and digested using trypsin. After stopping the reaction with 5% formic acid in acetonitrile, the peptide solution was dried using a speed vac concentrator. The dried peptides were reconstituted in 0.1% formic acid before desalting using a C18 stage tip. The desalted peptides (2 µg in 2 µL) were injected into an Eksigent NanoLC-Ultra high-performance liquid chromatography system coupled to a TripleTOF 6600 + mass spectrometer (SCIEX, Toronto, Canada) set for information-dependent acquisition (IDA) and data-independent acquisition (DIA). The peptides were loaded onto a C18 column trap (Nano Trap RP-1, 3 μm 120 Å, 10 × 0.075 mm; Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, USA) at a flow rate of 3 μL/min of 0.1% formic acid in water for 10 min to desalt and concentrate the sample, which was then separated using a C18 analytical column (bioZen Peptide Polar C18 nanocolumn, 75 μm × 15 cm, particle size: 3 μm, 120 Å; Phenomenex). The flow rate was 300 nL/min, and the mobile phase gradient was as follows: 3–30% acetonitrile/0.1% formic acid for 60 min, 30–40% acetonitrile/0.1% formic acid for 10 min, 40–80% acetonitrile/0.1% formic acid for 2 min, 80% acetonitrile/0.1% formic acid for 6 min, 80–3% acetonitrile/0.1% formic acid for 2 min, and 3% acetonitrile/0.1% formic acid for 25 min. The eluate was ionized and sprayed into the mass spectrometer using an OptiFlow Turbo V Source (SCIEX). The ion source gas 1, ion source gas 2, and curtain gas were set at 19, 0, and 25 vendor arbitrary units, respectively. The interface heater temperature was 150 °C, and the ion spray voltage was 3.3 kV.
Mass spectrometry (MS) was performed in the positive ion mode at 3,500 cycles/105 min gradient elution. Each cycle included one time of flight (TOF) scan (250 ms accumulation time, 350–1250 m
/z
window with a charge state of + 2), followed by IDA of the 30 most intense ions. The minimum MS signal was set to 150 counts. The MS/MS scan was performed in high-sensitivity mode with a 50 ms accumulation time and 100 ppm mass tolerance. Former MS/MS candidate ions were excluded for 12 s after their first occurrence to reduce the redundancy of identified peptides. DIA was performed in the range of 350–1500 m
/z
using a predefined mass window of 7 m
/z
with an overlap of 1 m
/z
for 157 transmissible windows. The MS scan involved 2,044 cycles, with each cycle including one TOF–MS scan (50 ms accumulation time across 100–1,500 precursor mass range) for a total cycle time of 3.08 s. Spectra (100–1500 m
/z
) were acquired with the accumulation time set as 96 ms per SWATH window width. The resolution was 35,000 for the MS1 scan and 30,000 for the SWATH-MS2 scan. A rolling collision energy mode with a collision energy spread of 15 eV was applied. The IDA and DIA data (.
wiff
) were recorded using Analyst-TF software v.1.8 (AB SCIEX).
A total of 36 wiff files from the IDA experiments (six from Group 1, three from Group 2, four from Group 3, three from Group 4, and two from Group 5; two technical replicates per biological sample) were combined using Protein Pilot software v.5.0.2.0 (AB SCIEX) and used to search the Swiss-Prot
Homo sapiens
database (UniProtKB 2022_01), which contains 20,385 proteins. The search parameters were as follows: alkylation on cysteine by iodoacetamide, trypsin enzymatic digestion, one missed cleavage allowed, monoisotopic mass, and a 1% false discovery rate (FDR). The group file (the Protein Pilot search result) was loaded into SWATH Acquisition MicroApp v.2.0.1.2133 in PeakView software v.2.2 (SCIEX) to generate a spectral library. The maximum number of proteins was set at the number identified at a 1% global FDR from fit. Retention time alignment was performed using the high number of endogenous peptides in the chromatographic range. SWATH data extraction of the 36 DIA files was performed using SWATH Acquisition MicroApp software (SCIEX) and the following parameters: 5-min extraction window, 25 peptides/protein, six transitions/peptide, exclusion of shared peptides, peptide confidence > 99%, FDR < 1%, and extracted ion chromatogram width of 20 ppm. The SWATH extraction data, including the peptide and protein identities and quantities, were normalized using multiple linear regression (Marker View software version 1.3.1) and then exported to an Excel file for further analysis. The protein area in each group was analyzed using ANOVA, multiple comparisons (Tukey), and SPSS software (IBM SPSS version 18; Armonk, N.Y., USA). The functions of the significantly differentially expressed proteins were predicted via STRING protein–protein interaction pathway analysis (
https://string-db.org/

https://reactome.org/
