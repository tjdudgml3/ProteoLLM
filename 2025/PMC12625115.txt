Introduction
Heart failure (HF) is a multifaceted clinical syndrome characterized by symptoms and signs of pulmonary and systemic congestion, leading to a cascade of physiological and pathophysiological changes. It is affecting over 56 million people worldwide, with a high prevalence rate expected to rise due to aging populations and increasing rates of risk factors such as hypertension and diabetes mellitus [
1
]. Heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) accounts for a significant portion of HF cases [
2
]. Despite the advances in drug and device therapies for HFrEF over a period of four decades, the mortality rate of patients with HFrEF seems to be declining but still remains high [
3
–
5
]. The absence of timely disease progression monitoring and comprehensive clinical evaluation further compromises patient outcomes. Consequently, there is an urgent need for novel biomarkers that can improve risk stratification for high-risk cohorts, and guide personalized therapeutic strategies for heart failure patients.
HF is the consequence of the interaction between cardiac overload or injury, subsequently activates specific pathways such as neurohormones, metabolism, and inflammation. The levels of circulating angiotensin II, aldosterone, catecholamines, and natriuretic peptide are all altered in HF, which are associated with increased mortality rate. These biomarkers have therefore emerged as valuable tools and main therapeutic targets for HF [
6
]. – [
7
] Accordingly, a systematic search for circulating biomarkers reflecting pathophysiological processes in HF, like neurohormonal activation and myocardial remodeling, is a promising strategy for discovering new biomarkers and therapeutic targets.
Peptidomics is an emerging branch of proteomics that is based on investigating endogenously produced protein fragments generated in tissues or body fluid [
8
]. – [
9
] The peptide content in the blood reflects multisystem secretion and release dynamics and has significant medical and pharmaceutical potentials [
10
]. Blood peptidomics have been extensively studied in the diagnosis, efficacy evaluation and prognosis of a variety of diseases [
11
–
15
]. Peptidomics-based profiling of circulating peptides hold promises for elucidating the molecular mechanisms involved in HF progression. Both B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) and N-Terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) are well-described diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers for HF [
16
–
19
] as well as subclinical heart and vessel diseases [
20
]. – [
21
] Additionally, serum levels of the carboxy-terminal propeptide of procollagen type I (PIP) is related to the extent of left ventricular fibrosis, and could serve as a screening and diagnosis tool for myocardial remodeling in patients with arterial hypertension [
22
]. Circulating PIP levels may also reflect myocardial fibrosis in dilated cardiomyopathy patients [
23
]. The Procollagen type III N-terminal propeptide (PIIINP) level is associated with incident HF with preserved ejection fraction in a population free of overt cardiovascular disease [
24
]. These findings suggest that a comprehensive understanding of the relationship between serum peptides and HF progression may uncover novel pathways and enhance our understanding of the complex pathophysiology of HF.
Therefore, we employed a peptidomics approach to investigate serum peptides in patients with HF who experienced all-cause death within one year, as well as in those without events and healthy controls (HCs), to comprehensively characterize peptide profile changes associated with different HF outcomes.
Materials and methods
Study design and participants
We performed a nested case-control study on patients from the BIOMS-POAHF (A Registry Study of Biomarkers in Progression of Acute Heart Failure) trial (ClinicalTrials. gov registration no.
NCT04108182
) [
25
]. Between January 2018 and August 2018, 135 patients with diagnosed acute HF were admitted to Anzhen Hospital of Capital Medical University (Beijing, China) (Figure S1). Acute HF was defined as rapid or gradual onset of signs and symptoms of HF resulting in unplanned hospitalization, including new-onset acute HF or decompensation of chronic HF [
16
].
We identified 27 patients with all-cause death occurred during 1 year after initial hospitalization (event group). For every such case, one control patient was randomly matched by propensity score from patients with acute HF who survived during follow- up using the MatchIt package in R. These control participants were matched to the case participants by age and sex. We ultimately selected 27 control participants (non-event group) for whom follow-up information was available (Figure S1). 27 Healthy controls (HCs) were also enrolled at Anzhen Hospital and matched for age and sex (Figure S1). We performed serum peptidomics in the 54 HF patients and 27 HCs to evaluate the peptide profile changes and identify potential biomarkers of HF outcome-related classifier. The study design of the study is shown in Fig.
1
.

Study design. HF, heart failure; HCs, healthy controls
All participants provided written informed consent. The study protocol was performed in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and it was approved by the ethics committees or institutional review boards of Beijing Anzhen Hospital.
Serum collection and storage
Serum samples were collected from all patients on admission. Serum samples were drawn into coagulation-promoting tubes and centrifuged at 2000 g for 10 min within 1 h of collection. All serum samples were stored in aliquots at −80 °C.
Serum peptidomics analysis
Peptide extraction
400uL of 50mM NH
4
HCO
3
(SCRC, 20002760, China) was added to the empty ultrafiltration tube, centrifuged at 10000Xg for 5 min, and the solution at the bottom of the collection tube was discarded. Serum samples containing protease inhibitors (Sigma-Aldrich, 5892791001, USA) were added to the equilibrated 30KD ultrafiltration tube, centrifuged at 10000Xg for 20 min, and smaller than 30 kDa peptides was used in the following steps. The solution was washed twice with 100ul of 50mM NH
4
HCO
3
containing protease inhibitors centrifuged at 10000Xg for 20 min, and the liquid was retained. The samples in the tubes were dried in a vacuum centrifuge concentrator (Thermo, SPD2010-230). The dried sample was dissolved by adding 100ul of 0.1% (v/v) formic acid buffer. The C18 desalting gun tip was activated with 100% acetonitrile (J.T.Baker, 9829-03, USA) buffer. Salt was removed with 100ul of 0.1% formic acid buffer and aspirated more than five times. The desalted peptides were dissolved by repeated blowing in 20ul of 45% to 50% acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid buffer. The samples were evaporated to dryness in a vacuum concentration centrifuge (Thermo, SPD2010-230).
LC-MS/MS analysis
LC-MS/MS analysis was performed on a Q Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) that was coupled to Easy nLC (Proxeon Biosystems, now Thermo Fisher Scientific). The peptides were loaded onto a reverse phase trap column (Thermo Scientific Acclaim PepMap100, 100 μm*2 cm, nanoViper C18) connected to the C18-reversed phase analytical column (Thermo Scientific Easy Column, 10 cm long, 75 μm inner diameter, 3 μm resin) in buffer A (0.1% Formic acid in water) and separated with a linear gradient of buffer B (84% acetonitrile and 0.1% Formic acid) at a flow rate of 300 nl/min. The mass spectrometer was operated in positive ion mode. The scanning range of the parent ion is 200–3000 m/z. Automatic gain control (AGC) target was set to 1e6, and maximum inject time to 50 ms. Dynamic exclusion duration was 30.0 s. Survey scans were acquired at a resolution of 70,000 at m/z 200 and resolution for HCD spectra was set to 15,000 at m/z 200, and isolation width was 1.5 m/z. Normalized collision energy was 30 eV and the underfill ratio, which specifies the minimum percentage of the target value likely to be reached at maximum fill time, was defined as 0.1%. We accepted a minimum signal-to-noise ratio (S/N) of 3:1 for the identification of peptides. The instrument was run with peptide recognition mode enabled.
Identification and quantitation of peptides
The MS raw data for each sample were combined and searched using the PEAKS Studio software (Version 8.5) for identification and quantitation analysis. Related parameters and instructions are as follows: The maximum deviation of Precursor Mass is 10 ppm, the deviation of Fragment Mass is within 0.02 Da, the FDR of peptide is ≤ 1% and − 10logP is ≥ 20, and open search was conducted. Dynamic modifications included Amidation, Oxidation (M, Methionine), Phosphorylation (S, Serine; T, Threonine; Y, Tyrosine), Acetylation (K, Lysine), and up to two dynamic modifications per peptide were allowed.
Statistical analysis
Clinical variables were summarized by median (interquartile range) for continuous variables and by N (percentage) for categorical variables. Comparisons between 2 groups were performed by using the χ
2
test (or Fisher exact test when any expected count was < 5 for a 2 × 2 table) for categoric variables and the 2-tailed Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables. A value of
P
< 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.
The peptide data were first log
2
-transformed for analysis, and the missing values were imputed with one-tenth of the minimal measured value. Differential expression analysis between the two groups was performed using Wilcoxon rank sum test. Significant differentially expressed peptides (DEPs) of each comparison was selected (p value < 0.05, |log2FoldChange(log2FC)|>1) and submitted to the “clusterProfiler” R package for enrichment analysis in KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) database. Only pathway categories with an Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR)-corrected P-value less than 0.05 were regarded as significant. STRING online network systems (
https://cn.string-db.org/
, version 12.0) were used for Protein–Protein Interaction (PPI) analysis. Cytoscape software (3.10.3, cytoHubba plugin) [
26
] was employed to identify Hub proteins. Hierarchical clustering method was used to screen peptides with the same changing trend in the serum of HCs, non-event, and event groups. The Orthogonal Partial Least Squares Discrimination Analysis (OPLS-DA) model was applied to maximize identification of differences in peptide profiles between groups.
Predictive biomarker selection was performed using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression. The LASSO model was implemented through the “glmnet” R package with optimal penalty parameter (λ) determination via 10-fold cross-validation. Multivariable logistic regression was used for constructing clinical model, 5 FIBA-combined peptides classifier model and 5 FIBA-combined peptides plus clinical factors model. Comprehensive validation via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis for discriminative ability and calibration plots for prediction-to-observation agreement. Nomogram construction using the “rms” package for clinical utility visualization. BIOPEP-UWM database [
27
] (
https://biochemia.uwm.edu.pl/biopep-uwm/
) offered possible bioactivities of identified 5 peptides. We searched online protease mapping database ExPASy Peptide Cutter (
https://web.expasy.org/peptide_cutter/
) for possible protease and cleavage sites.
All analyses were performed using SPSS version 24.0 (IBM Corporation), and R version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).
Detailed information on the inclusion and exclusion criteria of acute HF patients, and study endpoint are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Differential serum peptides between patients with HF and HCs
A total of 4126 peptides were identified from serum samples of HF patients and HCs, of which 3026 were quantified in ≥ 30% of samples in at least one of the 3 groups (Table S2). The OPLS-DA model showed significant a separation of these peptides between the HF and HCs groups (Fig.
2
A). We mapped 1343 of these peptides to 323 proteins with displayed altered abundance in patients with HF (
P
value < 0.05 and |log2FC)|>1), including 1052 downregulated and 291 upregulated peptides in the death group depicted in heat map format (Fig.
2
B and Table S3). FIBA-derived peptides were noted to be the most pronounced changes in abundance among all DEPs in comparisons between nonevent HF vs. HCs and also event HF vs. HCs (Fig.
2
C, red triangles represent FIBA-derived peptides). KEGG analysis of these 323 proteins revealed the most significantly enriched pathways including complement and coagulation cascades, platelet activation, focal adhesion, regulation of actin cytoskeleton, and tight junction (Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted
P
< 0.05) (Fig.
3
).

Differential serum peptides between patients who died and survived within 1 year
After evaluating the DEPs between HCs and HF samples, we then shifted our analysis to distinguish the peptide differences between patients who died and those who survived within 1 year. The OPLS-DA model also showed significant separation of all peptides between the event and non-event groups (Fig.
4
A). Our further analysis identified 215 peptides mapping to 88 proteins that were differentially expressed between these 2 groups (
P
value < 0.05 and |log2FC)|>1), including 125 downregulated and 90 upregulated peptides in the event group depicted in heat map format (Fig.
4
B and Table S4). KEGG pathway analysis showed that these 88 proteins were mainly enriched in complement and coagulation cascades, platelet activation, and focal adhesion terms (Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted
P
< 0.05), which are consistent with the significantly altered pathways in HF (Fig.
3
). Next, the PPI network derived from 88 peptide precursor proteins were comprised of 85 nodes and 361 interaction edges in Figure S2A. The network exhibited an average node degree of 8.49 and an average local clustering coefficient of 0.547. The PPI enrichment p-value was < 1.0e-16. FGA (Fibrinogen Alpha Chain) ranked first in the top 10 hub proteins identified by Cytoscape software cytoHubba plugin with Maximum Clique Centrality (MMC) method. (Figure S2B and S2C)

Key peptides associated with HF outcome
We then sought to explore the peptides profiles that changed in different HF outcome groups. By the cutoff of P-value < 0.05 and |log2FC)|>1, a total of 56 peptides showed significant changes among the 3 groups (Fig.
5
A and Table S5). Hierarchical clustering analysis was employed to classify the 56 peptides with the same change trend, resulting in 3 clusters with different expression patterns across the 3 groups (Fig.
5
B and Table S6). Most peptides (38/56) in cluster 3 showed a significant downward trend as the disease development. Meanwhile, there were 14 peptides classified in cluster 1, which presented a significant upward trend. 4 peptides classified in cluster 2 showed a low level in the non-event group, which gradually increased in the event group (Fig.
5
B).

Firstly, ROC analysis was performed on the 52 peptides (clusters 1 and 3) with the monotonic changing trend across 3 disease stages, and their area under the curve (AUC) values showed significant efficiency in the comparison between the HF patients and the HCs, especially eight of them had better performance (AUC>0.9), which could be used to screen HF from the general healthy population, with a better screening efficiency (Table
2.
). We also evaluated the ability of the 52 peptides to distinguish between patients in the event and non-event groups and identified eight peptides with significant classification ability (AUC>0.7, Table

Outcome classifier development based on key peptides
According to the logistics regression analysis (Table S7 and S8), each of the five peptides showed significant or close to significant predictive value in the multivariate analysis after adjusting LVEF, SBP and cTnI level. In addition, five FIBA peptides model was still statistically significant associated with event group in the multivariate analysis (Odds Ratio [OR] = 11.54, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 3.32–80.16; P value = 0.002), indicating the key predictive role of 5 FIBA-derived peptides.

We further explored the clinical correlation between FIBA risk score and clinical indicators. As shown in Figure S3, 5 FIBA peptides model showed significantly negative correlation with LVEF and SBP levels, indicating poor left ventricular systolic function. No significant correlation was observed in creatinine and hs-CRP, implying its independent role beyond renal function and inflammation level.
In subgroup analysis, higher risk score was associated with higher risk of 1-year all-cause death in patients with or without different comorbidities and medication adherences. This indicates that the 5 FIBA model had similar effects on patients in different subgroups, thereby supporting the application potential of this risk stratification tool in a wide range of populations. (Table S9)
Based on predicted bioactivities of five FIBA-derived peptides offered by BIOPEP-UWM database, they are mainly involved in HF-related pathophysiological processes, including ACE inhibition, dipeptidyl peptidase IV inhibition and neprilysin inhibition, some of which have already advanced into key therapeutic targets in heart failure and diabetes treatment. Discussion
This is the first study to perform comprehensive peptidomics in HCs and HF patients who died or survived within 1 year to screen for changes in the dynamic spectrum of serum peptides. We further provided insights into possible outcome-related biological pathways at the peptide level. Additionally, we demonstrated that peptidomics is a promising tool with strong predictive capability in identifying patients at risk for worsening heart failure. Multiple peptides, especially FIBA-derived peptides, showed significantly effective classification ability in the comparison of HF vs. HC, and event vs. non-event groups. The combination of FIBA-derived peptides demonstrated superior risk stratification performance compared to the conventional clinical indicators.
Peptidomics was chosen over proteomics for this study due to its ability to provide insights into the functional state of proteins. Given the ability of peptides to diffuse passively across endothelial barriers, their investigation in serum provides valuable biological information and may enable defining clinically useful biomarkers. Less focus has been placed on the improved capability to detect and quantify small bioactive peptides, even though the exact sequences of the peptide species produced can have important biological implications, such as BNP, insulin and glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) [
28
]. In addition, the analytical pipelines of peptidomics do not require enzymatic digestion of the sample, thus reducing the complexity of sample preparation [
29
]. Due to more smaller molecules, the structure of peptides is easier to rebuild and simpler for artificial synthesis and chemosynthesis. Clinically, peptides are gaining popularity as innovative therapeutics. To date there are more than 60 peptide drugs approved and more than 150 in clinical development [
30
].
After screening and model development, we found that the DEPs are mainly enriched in the complement and blood coagulation cascade pathway both in HF vs. HCs and event vs. non-event groups. The complement and coagulation systems form a tightly interconnected and complex network in the circulation. Through mutual activation and regulation, they jointly modulate immune responses and maintain tissue homeostasis. Disruption of any element in these pathways may result in pathological conditions and contribute to the clinical features of multiple diseases [
31
]. Other than our research, Ann et al. applied adapter-based proteomic methods and found that complement and coagulation cascade processes are activated during the development of HF [
32
]. Similarly, our study offers more supportive information of complement and coagulation cascade changes from the peptides level.
Fibrinogen is a large glycoprotein composed of three distinct pairs of polypeptide chains, known as two Aα(encoded by FGA), two Bβ(encoded by FGB), and two γ (encoded by FGG) chains [
33
]. Fibrinogen is a key determinant in the regulation of coagulation, hemostasis, and inflammation, and has been recognized as a risk factor for cardiovascular disease [
34
]. – [
35
] The hemostatic and fibrinolytic systems are classically recognized for their role in preserving vascular integrity. Key components such as fibrinogen and the proteases that regulate its deposition and clearance—including prothrombin and plasminogen—play critical roles in modulating both acute and reparative inflammatory responses. These processes significantly influence the progression of tissue injury, remodeling, and repair. Fibrinogen, a downstream substrate of thrombin, is among the most potent mediators of the inflammatory response within the coagulation cascade [
36
]. More importantly, fibrinogen facilitates platelet aggregation [
37
], and promotes the inflammatory activation of vascular smooth muscle cells [
38
] and endothelial cells [
39
], underscoring its multiple roles in hemostasis and inflammation—both of which are key processes in the development of cardiovascular diseases. In addition to affecting blood viscosity by altering plasma viscosity and erythrocyte aggregation, growing experimental evidence suggests that fibrinogen alters vascular reactivity and impairs endothelial cell layer integrity by binding to its endothelial cell membrane receptors and activating signaling mechanisms [
40
]. Additionally, Bryce Kerlin et al. [
41
] provided direct evidence that hyperfibrinogenemia function independently or interactively to modulate the severity and/or progression of vascular diseases using a transgenic mouse model of hyperfibrinogenemia. What’s more, Lassé M et al. conducted cohorts study showed that FGA may independently predict cardiovascular events occurring within three years in asymptomatic, low-to-moderate risk individuals (median age = 70 years, 80% male) (HR = 1.84) [
42
]. Also, previous studies have shown that drug therapy could regulate FGA and FGG levels to improve coagulation function and play a role in the treatment of coronary heart disease [
43
]– [
44
], suggesting fibrinogen is more likely a driver than a bystander in the pathophysiological process of heart failure.
Based on our analysis, we found that the peptides exhibiting the most significant changes were predominantly associated with blood coagulation, particularly those derived from fibrinogen alpha chain, which accounted for the largest proportion. To discriminate between the patients in event group and in non-event group, these 5 FIBA-derived peptides model achieved an AUC of 0.903, which was better than that of clinical model (ΔAUC = 0.051). Overall, changes in the coagulation system and FIBA- derived peptides may reflect the HF pathophysiological changes. While BNP is indeed a key biomarker in heart failure severity [
45
], the FIBA-derived peptides associated with the coagulation and complement cascade pathways may reflect more complex interactions and systemic responses that occur in the later stages of heart failure. These peptides may capture additional pathophysiological changes that BNP does not fully encompass. Predicted bioactivity of identified peptides may participate in HF-related hemostatic processes, regulating ACE, renin and neprilysin, which can influence outcomes in heart failure patients. Our results indicate that these peptides provide complementary information that enhances risk stratification beyond what is offered by clinical information alone. This finding is supported by statistical analyses demonstrating their superior discriminative ability in our study cohort. It is expected to provide ideas for risk stratification strategies in heart failure patients.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study using a serum peptidomics approach to improve the understanding of the pathophysiology of HF and its association with clinical outcomes in patients with HF. The results of this study showed that serum peptidomics provide insights into a dynamic disease-specific proteolytic degradation pattern related to HF clinical outcome, which would open new perspectives for translational research. The findings in this study also further suggested that the complement and blood coagulation cascade are involved in the decompensation process of HF. They may be potential therapeutic targets that may provide additive therapeutic interventions for patients with HF.
Our study had certain limitations. First, this was a single-center, nested, case-control study that manifested a potential selection bias in that circulating peptides levels were detected in only 27 non-event patients. We assumed that this limitation did not greatly affect our results because there were no significant differences in baseline characteristics between the 27 non-event patients and 81 non-event patients (Table S16). By propensity score matching, we enrolled non-event group and healthy controls with matched age and gender. Nevertheless, there still exists the potential for residual and unmeasured confounding factors. Second, potential overfitting risk for the classifier and variabilities among peptides may have impacts on the robustness of the conclusion. In the future, we will continue this study to verify our findings in other larger cohorts. Third, our study included a sicker subpopulation of acute HF patients with reduced EF, significantly elevated BNP levels, mild-to-moderate renal dysfunction, and a previous history of hospitalization due to HF assessed at the timepoint of admission. Finally, further experiments are required to verify the role of the key peptides.
Conclusions
In summary, this study expands our understanding of the molecular underpinnings of HF risk stratification at the peptide level and highlights the dysregulated complements and coagulation cascades during disease. Key peptides, particularly those derived from FIBA, may aid in predicting the 1-year all-cause mortality in patients with HF.
