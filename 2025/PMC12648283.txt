Introduction
The field of cancer immunotherapy has
advanced significantly in
recent years, through the development of immune checkpoint inhibitors
and adoptive CAR-T cell therapies.
,
These approaches
were developed to enhance antitumor immunity, demonstrating substantial
therapeutic benefit in various cancers, including melanoma, lung cancer,
and hematological malignancies.
However,
despite progress in cancer immunotherapy, it remains limited for effective
treatments for patients with pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC).
PDAC is the third leading cause of cancer-related death in the United
States, which accounts for over 90% of all pancreatic cancer cases
and is notoriously resistant to therapeutic interventions.
,
The 5 year overall survival (OS) for PDAC remains at around 11%,
in part because of its advanced stage at presentation and a high propensity
for recurrence following conventional systemic therapies.
This resistance is primarily attributed to the
tumor’s inherent heterogeneity and distinctive tumor microenvironment
(TME),
,
which complicates the effectiveness of these
therapies and necessitates strategies to overcome these barriers.
Arginase 1 (Arg1) positive monocytes, indicative of the M2 subtype
of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), have been shown to increase
in abundance as PDAC progresses.
This
enrichment suggests that both early- and late-stage PDACs display
distinct immunosuppressive profiles, with M2-type TAMs playing a crucial
role in shaping an immunosuppressive microenvironment that promotes
tumor progression and contributes to therapeutic resistance. PDAC
tumors are known to be immunologically “cold” with a
high density of immunosuppressive TAMs and minimal NK and T cell infiltration.
,
A major barrier to immunotherapy is, therefore, not only activating
these immune cells but also ensuring their recruitment and infiltration
into the tumor core. The crosstalk between TAMs and NK cells within
the TME is critical yet poorly understood, and it may serve as a key
checkpoint governing NK-cell infiltration.
Prior research indicates that the abundance of infiltrating TAMs
can mediate resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and
adoptive T cell immunotherapy.
,
Overcoming the barriers
to effectively polarize TAMs remains a significant challenge in countering
the immunosuppressive TME, which is essential for enhancing the efficacy
of conventional chemotherapy. Thus, therapeutic approaches that specifically
target TAMs and modulate the local tumor immune microenvironment hold
considerable promise for improving chemotherapy outcomes in PDAC.
Among recent advances in cancer immunotherapy, single-stranded
RNA origami (ssRNAOG) technology shows great promise for modulating
TAMs. Several studies have demonstrated that ssRNAOG can significantly
enhance antitumor efficacy, primarily by activating innate immune
cells like macrophages.
−
The programmability of nucleic acids allows for the
creation of synthetic RNA nanostructures with custom sizes, shapes,
and surface modifications, such as ssRNAOG, which has shown significant
promise as a therapeutic agent, immune adjuvant, and potent immunomodulator.
,
Continued investigation into their underlying mechanisms is essential
for further expanding their biological applications in the field.
Recent studies have demonstrated that ssRNAOGs, designed with distinct
edge lengths, shapes, and geometries, exhibit remarkable biostability
and significantly enhance the activation of macrophages via interaction
with Toll-like receptor 3 (TLR3). This interaction is critical, as
TLR3 is a key pattern recognition receptor that senses viral-like
dsRNA to initiate a potent TRIF-dependent innate immune response.
−
In our earlier studies, we found that ssRNAOG effectively prompts
innate immune responses through the TLR3 pathway, activating dendritic
cells, NK cells, and CD8+ T cells, forming the basis of an effective
antitumor immunity in a murine peritoneal colon cancer model.
This finding indicates the ssRNAOG’s
capacity to harness multiple facets of the immune system against tumors.
Furthermore, integrating ssRNAOG with the chemotherapeutic agent 5-fluorouracil
(5-FU) has also been shown to significantly enhance antitumor effects
in a PDAC mouse model, suggesting a potential synergy between TAM-reprogramming
and conventional chemotherapy.
However,
while the immunostimulatory potential of ssRNAOG is established, its
specific mechanisms within the complex and highly immunosuppressive
TME of PDAC remain poorly understood.
TME is a complex and dynamic
system composed of tumor cells, immune
cells, stromal cells, and extracellular matrix components that influence
tumor progression and therapeutic responses.
,
Tumor-associated macrophages often adopt an immunosuppressive M2-like
phenotype that supports tumor growth and metastasis in the TME. Recent
studies have shown that reprogramming these macrophages to a proinflammatory
M1 phenotype can enhance their ability to present antigens and secrete
cytokines that activate other immune cells, thereby promoting a more
antitumor response.
We hypothesize that
ssRNAOGs enhance antitumor efficacy by activating the innate immune
response of macrophages within the TME. In this study, we demonstrate
that ssRNAOG is preferentially internalized by macrophage, where it
induces the reprogramming of macrophages toward an M1-like phenotype.
Beyond simple repolarization, we further reveal a novel downstream
mechanism: ssRNAOG-activated macrophages significantly upregulated
MHC class I expression, which in turn leads to the enhanced recruitment
of NK cells to the TME. When combined with the chemotherapeutic agent
5-FU, this comprehensive immune modulation results in a synergistic
antitumor effect in pancreatic cancer models. Our transcriptomic and
proteomic analyses confirm that this synergy is driven by broad activation
of antitumor pathways, including NF-κB and STAT1 signaling,
which orchestrate both the M1 macrophage polarization and the MHC
class I-mediated NK cell crosstalk. These findings establish that
reprogramming the TME with ssRNAOG is a potent strategy to enhance
immune recognition and response and synergize with conventional chemotherapy,
thereby leveraging the immune system’s natural ability to target
and eliminate tumor cells.
Results
Rapid Macrophage Internalization of ssRNAOG in Both 2D and 3D
Coculture Systems
Macrophages are important mediators of
tumor progression and antitumor function, and their behavior is broadly
influenced by different microenvironmental stimuli.
To investigate the molecular basis of the internalization
of ssRNAOG in both macrophage and tumor cells simultaneously in the
TME, we assessed the uptake efficiency of ssRNAOG in vitro using a
coculture system of pancreatic cancer MIA PaCa-2 cells and macrophage
RAW264.7 cells. AlexaFluor488 (AF488)-labeled ssRNAOG molecules (ssRNAOG-AF488)
were synthesized through in vitro transcription and subsequently self-assembled
into a rectangle-shaped nanostructure via a straightforward annealing
process in PBS buffer, as previously described.
The integrity of the assembled ssRNAOG was characterized
by using gel electrophoresis and atomic force microscopy (AFM) (
Figure S1
). Coculture cells were treated with
ssRNAOG-AF488 at the concentration of 5 μg/mL for 4 and 16 h.
As shown in
Figure
A, ssRNAOG-AF488 were mainly internalized by RAW264.7 cells within
4 and 16 h. In contrast, only a minimal amount of ssRNAOG was taken
up by the pancreatic MIA PaCa-2 cells. To examine the distribution
and localization of ssRNAOG in coculture cells, we added LysoTracker
Deep Red dye to label cellular lysosomes. We observed that most of
the ssRNAOG colocalized with lysosomes and endosomes in the RAW264.7
macrophages. As predominant cell types in the solid tumor stroma,
macrophages and fibroblasts play crucial roles in facilitating tumor
progression and offer essential potential for target therapies.
,
To explore ssRNAOG uptake in the context of cellular heterogeneity
and plasticity within the TME, we established a 3D tumor spheroid
model composed of MIA PaCa-2-iRFP cells, fibroblasts (NIH3T3-GFP),
and CellTracker Blue-labeled RAW264.7 macrophages, designed to mimic
the characteristics of the natural TME.
,
Using confocal
microscopy, we found that the majority of ssRNAOG-AF568 was preferentially
internalized by macrophages, with minimal uptake by either cancer
cells or fibroblasts after 4 h of treatment (
Figure
B,C). These results demonstrate that macrophages
preferentially internalize ssRNAOG compared to other cell types in
the TME. The colocalization of ssRNAOG with endosomes and lysosomes
in macrophages suggests that the endosome is mainly involved in processing
ssRNAOG, which is critical for triggering TLR-mediated signaling pathways.
This highlights the importance of macrophages as critical effector
cells in the modulation of the immune response within the TME.

The Combination of ssRNAOG and 5-FU Reduces Tumor Cell Colony
Formation in Coculture Cells
Our previous studies demonstrated
that ssRNAOG nanostructures function as potent TLR3 agonists, effectively
stimulating antitumor immunity.
,
To assess tumor cell
proliferation, we utilized the colony formation assay (CFA) to determine
the antitumor capacity of macrophages activated by ssRNAOG within
a coculture environment. Both 5-FU and ssRNAOG were evaluated to inhibit
tumor cell growth in vitro using CFA. As shown in
Figures
D,E, and
S2
the area of cell colonies was quantified to assess the
proliferation inhibition by ImageJ/Fiji software after treating coculture
cells for 10 days with varying concentrations of 5-FU or ssRNAOG.
The data showed a significant decrease in cell colony growth following
treatments, with the extent of inhibition increasing in a concentration-dependent
manner. This confirms the dose-dependent effects of 5-FU and ssRNAOG
treatments in suppressing tumor cell proliferation. To further evaluate
the synergistic effect of 5-FU and ssRNAOG on tumor inhibition, we
selected the lowest doses of both agents for individual and combined
treatment. Results revealed that the combination of these minimal
concentrations remarkably inhibited the formation of MIA PaCa-2 pancreatic
cancer cell colonies in contrast to the less effective individual
treatment. This indicates that the combination of 5-FU and ssRNAOG
exerts a significant antiproliferative effect on coculture cells (
Figure
F).

ssRNAOG-Treated TAMs Recruit NK Cells to Pancreatic Cancer Cells
via MHC Class I Recognition in a Three-Cell Coculture System
NK cells are cytotoxic lymphocytes that can recognize stressed cells
and mediate their spontaneous killing. A key characteristic of NK
cell recognition is the education and licensing by binding to MHC
class I molecules via inhibitory receptors, leading NK cells to preferentially
kill MHC class I-deficient tumor cells.
Crosstalk between NK cells and other immune cells plays a crucial
role in the development of both innate and adaptive immune responses.
Notably, interactions between NK cells and macrophages
are vital for anti-infection and antitumor responses.
As shown in
Figure
, the ssRNAOG treatment led to the overexpression of MHC class
I molecules on macrophages. In contrast, the MIA PaCa-2 pancreatic
cancer cell line expresses low levels of MHC class I,
even in the presence of a high concentration of ssRNAOG
(
Figure S10
), making it more susceptible
to NK cell-mediated killing. Previous in vivo studies demonstrated
that NK cell depletion completely abrogated the tumor growth-inhibitory
effects of ssRNAOG activity.
Therefore,
we hypothesize that ssRNAOG-treated macrophages could induce NK cell
recruitment and infiltration into the tumor microenvironment via MHC
I recognition. This recruitment process is a known prerequisite for
effective NK cell-mediated cytolysis of immunologically “cold”
tumor cells.
To test this hypothesis, we first cocultured NK92
cells with RAW264.7 cells treated with or without ssRNAOG. Compared
with macrophage without RNA origami treatment, macrophage with ssRNAOG
treatment showed obvious NK cell recruitment, indicating the interaction
between ssRNAOG-treated macrophage and NK cells. We then cocultured
pancreatic cancer cells with macrophages (with or without ssRNAOG)
and NK cells. Consistent with our previous observations, ssRNAOG-treated
macrophages recruited more NK cells into the pancreatic cancer cells
(
Figure
A). Statistical
analysis revealed that ssRNAOG increased the level of NK cell binding
by around 2-fold (
Figure
B). Taken together, these findings suggest that ssRNAOG enhances
the interaction between tumor-associated macrophages and NK cells,
facilitating NK cell recruitment to the tumor microenvironment and
establishing a critical prerequisite for the cytolysis of MHC class
I-deficient tumor cells (
Figure
C).

The Combination of ssRNAOG and 5-FU Reduces PDAC Tumor Burden
and Alters the Tumor Microenvironment
Previous investigations
have revealed that ssRNAOG induced significant tumor suppression or
regression by engaging NK and CD8+ T cells, enhancing both innate
and adaptive antitumor immunity.
The
role of ssRNAOG as a potent TLR3 agonist can elicit strong immune
response and suppress tumor growth for cancer immunotherapy. We hypothesized
this response would depend on TAM regulating immune cell recruitment
and antigen presentation, both of which are necessary components in
promoting NK cell- and CD8 T cell-mediated antitumor immunity. To
specifically isolate the role of the innate immune system and assesses
the efficacy of its antitumor immunity response from the combination
of both ssRNAOG and 5-FU, BALB/c nude mice, which are T cell-deficient,
were implanted subcutaneously with the MIA PaCa-2-iRFP pancreatic
cell line (
Figure S11
), which allows for
monitoring of tumor growth.
,
At 4 weeks postimplantation,
mice were randomized into four distinct treatment groups, with the
average tumor burden measured at approximately 0.2 cm
3
.
The mice received intratumoral injections of 5-FU, ssRNAOG, ssRNAOG
+ 5-FU, or PBS. Tumor growth was tracked by iRFP signal and caliper
measurements in all experimental groups (
Figure S12
). The groups treated with PBS, 5-FU, and ssRNAOG alone
exhibited consistent tumor growth, reaching the experimental end point
around day 42. By contrast, in the ssRNAOG + 5-FU combination therapy
group, a significant reduction in tumor mass was observed by the study’s
end point. Two mice in the combination treatment group showed complete
tumor regression, indicating the synergistic efficacy of the dual
treatment compared with the other groups (
Figure
A,B). We next performed survival analysis
for the four treatment groups. The experimental end point was defined
as a tumor size of 2 cm
3
across all groups, and survival
analysis was monitored throughout the study. Mice in the combination
treatment group exhibited a significantly longer survival duration,
with a 60% survival rate observed at 42 days post-treatment compared
with the other three treatment groups (
Figure
C). Beyond extended survival, mice in all
experimental groups showed stable behavior and maintained normal body
weight for 7 weeks following the initiation of therapy.

the combination therapy of ssRNAOG and 5-FU resulted in a significant
reduction in Arg1+ macrophages and Ki67+ tumor cells. This result
indicates that the combined treatment shifted the macrophage population
toward an M1-like phenotype within the TME, promoting an antitumor
immune response.
Discussion
TAMs are a significant component of leukocytic
infiltration of
tumors and have served as a paradigm for cancer-related inflammation.
Macrophages are a double-edged sword in the
TME, exhibiting either protumor (M2-like) or antitumor (M1-like) phenotypes.
M2-like macrophages promote tumor progression by facilitating immunosuppression
and angiogenesis and supporting cancer stem cells,
−
whereas M1-like
macrophages elicit strong antitumor response. This is characterized
by the release of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-12 and TNF-α,
which are crucial for activating cytotoxic T cells and NK cells, enhancing
immune surveillance against tumors.
Consistent
with this, the presence of M1 macrophage is associated with improved
patient outcomes and tumor regression.
,
The plasticity
of macrophages is influenced by the local cytokine environment, contributing
to immune surveillance and tumor eradication by releasing proinflammatory
cytokines and activation of cytotoxic mechanisms. M1 macrophages are
essential in presenting antigens to enhance tumor-targeting immune
responses by internalizing and phagocytosing pathogens and cancer
cells. Their secretion of cytokines orchestrates immune surveillance
and facilitates the clearance of cancer cells.
,
Our results showed that the preferential internalization of
ssRNAOG
by macrophages in both 2D and 3D coculture systems underscores an
important aspect of the dynamic interplay between the TME and immune-modulatory
therapies. In cocultures, fluorescently labeled ssRNAOG was rapidly
internalized by RAW264.7 macrophages but not pancreatic cancer cells
or fibroblasts, suggesting a high propensity for macrophage uptake.
The colocalization of ssRNAOG with lysosomal and endosomal markers
indicates internalization via the endocytic pathway. This selective
uptake, even within a heterogeneous 3D spheroid model, reinforces
the potential of ssRNAOG to specifically target immune cells without
being sequestered by tumor or stromal cells. These results are consistent

This selective uptake by macrophages reveals

The internalization
of ssRNAOG via the endosome–lysosome
system positions it to interact with TLRs, a central pathway for activating
innate immune signaling in macrophages.
,
ssRNAOG demonstrates
stability in acidic environments, allowing it to be continuously recognized
and interact with TLRs to enhance its immunostimulatory effect. Macrophages
utilize TLRs to detect viral components; specifically, TLR3 recognizes
dsRNA, while TLR7/8 detect ssRNA, both leading to the production of
proinflammatory cytokines.
Our transcriptomic
and proteomic data confirm that ssRNAOG treatment triggers this cascade,
leading to the activation of the key transcription factors NF-κB
and STAT1. The activation of these factors is essential for driving
macrophage polarization toward the antitumor M1 phenotype, which is
characterized by the production of cytokines like TNF-α and
IL-12 and enhanced cytotoxic activity against tumor cells. The preferential
accumulation of ssRNAOG in macrophages, together with TLR3 activation
in macrophages that induces proinflammatory cytokines, implies a potential
role in inducing M1-like macrophage polarization within the TME. If
ssRNAOG can drive macrophage polarization toward the M1 phenotype,
it could enhance the antitumor immunity while mitigating the protumorigenic
effects associated with M2-polarized macrophages. It is crucial for
regulating functional polarization through the activation of stimulus-specific
TF in macrophage transcription.
NF-κB,
a transcription factor, regulates the expression of genes involved
in inflammation, the immune response, and cell survival. The activation
of NF-κB via TLRs signal transduction in response to ssRNAOG
is essential for transforming macrophages into the M1 phenotype, known
for producing proinflammatory cytokines and exhibiting enhanced cytotoxic
activity against tumor cells. Genin et al.’s studies have demonstrated
that M1-type macrophage driven by NF-κB can effectively kill
tumor cells through the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines and
cytotoxic molecules.
Macrophages polarization
driven by NF-κB and STAT1 activation has significant implications
in antitumor and antimicrobial response through the production of
proinflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α and IL-12.
A particularly novel finding of our study
is the elucidation of
a downstream mechanism connecting this M1-like polarization to the
broader innate immune landscape. We demonstrated that ssRNAOG-activated
TAMs significantly upregulate MHC class I expression, which in turn
enhances the recruitment of NK cells. This positions ssRNAOG-reprogrammed
TAMs as a crucial bridge linking macrophage activation with NK cell
trafficking, two powerful arms of the innate immune system. The strategic
importance of this axis is amplified in the context of PDAC, which
is notoriously immunologically “cold” and often characterized
by poor T cell infiltration. Activating an antitumor response that
leverages NK cells therefore represents a highly promising therapeutic
strategy that is not solely reliant on adaptive T-cell immunity. A
T-cell-deficient model was therefore employed to clearly delineate
the efficacy driven by this innate immune axis. Further studies to
explore the interplay with the adaptive response in immunocompetent
models are currently underway. Crucially, this recruitment creates
a powerful antitumor scenario, as our data confirms the pancreatic
cancer cells exhibit low MHC-I expression, making them ideal targets
for elimination by the newly arrived NK cells. This enhanced NK cell
infiltration provides a compelling rationale for the observed synergy
with 5-FU; the chemotherapy may induce stress on tumor cells, making
them more vulnerable, while the ssRNAOG-TAM axis delivers the NK effector
cells necessary for their elimination. While there is evidence that
5-FU can directly shape the TAM to M1 phenotype and improve colorectal
cancer patient prognosis,
M2 TAMs are
potent mediators of chemoresistance to 5-FU, promoting cancer cell
survival pathways, epithelial–mesenchymal transition, and metastasis
that blunts chemotherapy efficacy.

In summary, this study demonstrates
that ssRNAOG is a potent immunomodulatory
agent capable of reprogramming the suppressive TME of pancreatic cancer.
By selectively targeting and activating TAMs toward a proinflammatory
M1 phenotype, ssRNAOG not only elicits direct antitumor effects but
also connects innate immune pathways through the novel upregulation
of MHC class I and subsequent NK cell recruitment. Crucially, this
comprehensive immune remodeling sensitizes PDAC to conventional chemotherapy,
demonstrating a powerful synergistic effect. Our findings present
a promising immunochemotherapy strategy that leverages RNA nanotechnology
to transform an immunosuppressive “cold” tumor into
an active antitumor battleground, offering a new avenue for treating
this notoriously difficult-to-treat malignancy.
