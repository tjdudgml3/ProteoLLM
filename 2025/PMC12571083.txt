INTRODUCTION
Platinum-based drugs (Pt drugs) play a crucial role in chemotherapy. For decades, Pt drugs have been first-line treatments for various cancers, including testicular, ovarian, bladder, and colorectal cancer (
1
,
2
). Cross-linking with DNA is generally considered as the key mechanism underlying the anticancer activity of Pt drugs. Pt drugs form Pt-DNA adducts that interfere with replication and transcription, triggering downstream responses that can lead to cell death (
3
–
5
). The most common Pt-DNA adducts are 1,2-intrastrand d(GpG) cross-links (
1
,
6
), in which platinum coordinates with the N7 atoms of adjacent guanines on the same DNA strand.
The investigation of Pt drug–DNA interactions is important for understanding drug action mechanisms and developing next-generation Pt drugs. The ability to sensitively detect Pt drug–DNA adducts is particularly critical, as studies have shown that less than 1% of intracellular Pt drugs ultimately bind to nuclear DNA (
7
,
8
). Cisplatin sequencing (cisplatin-seq) and damage sequencing (damage-seq) have been previously reported to detect cisplatin binding sites across the genome (
9
,
10
). These independent studies revealed that mitochondrial DNA is a major target of cisplatin and that GG dinucleotides density primarily determines the incidence of cross-linking. However, current methods have underestimated the DNA binding capacity of Pt drugs according to DNA platination levels measured by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) (
11
). Moreover, the precise understanding of Pt drug binding patterns in the nuclear genome remains unclear, likely because of its higher sequence complexity and limited detection sensitivity. This limitation is primarily attributed to high background noise and high detection limits, which can contaminate signals and compromise robustness (
12
). In addition, while existing methods have primarily focused on cisplatin, there is growing interest in other Pt drugs, including third-generation Pt drugs and promising Pt(IV) complexes (
13
). Therefore, developing a more sensitive and reliable method for investigating genome-wide Pt drug–DNA interactions is needed.
Here, we present Pt sequencing (Pt-seq) as a genome-wide method to detect DNA binding sites of various Pt drugs with single-base resolution. We identified the DNA binding sites of cisplatin, oxaliplatin, lobaplatin, and our previously developed Pt(IV) complex, Pt-Bio-I (
14
). By using an exo- and endonuclease treatment strategy, we notably enhanced the sensitivity to detect Pt drug binding sites and achieved a relatively high signal-to-noise ratio. Pt-seq also demonstrated reliable performance with DNA inputs as low as 500 ng. Using Pt-seq, we unexpectedly found tens to hundreds of binding clusters, with median lengths of 10 to 20 kb, that appear consistently across different Pt drugs. These binding clusters are mainly located in centromeric and ribosomal DNA (rDNA) regions. Moreover, comparative analysis between cisplatin-resistant and wild-type (WT) cells showed significantly reduced binding to these regions in resistant cells. In addition, by leveraging the base resolution of Pt-seq, we identified that cancer cell mutations can generate previously unidentified binding sites. We further found that treatment with ICR-191, an acridine orange compound known to induce G insertions at consecutive G sequences (
15
), enhanced cisplatin-DNA cross-linking and sensitized cells to cisplatin treatment. Together, this study not only provides a powerful tool to identify Pt drug binding sites but can also facilitate our understanding of the genome-wide effects of platinum-based chemotherapy.
RESULTS
High-sensitivity profiling of Pt-DNA adducts by Pt-seq
To detect Pt drug–DNA interactions with high sensitivity and signal-to-noise ratio, background noises should be avoided. We reasoned that background noises are mainly caused by nonspecific DNA recognition during immunoprecipitation (IP) and unligated DNA resulting from limited adaptor ligation efficiency during library preparation (
12
,
16
). To address these issues, we developed a method named Pt-seq to identify Pt drug DNA binding sites with two strategies to specifically reduce these background noises (
Fig. 1A
and fig. S1A). First, we used a restriction endonuclease to remove non–cross-linked background DNA. Inspired by the library dedication strategy used in single-cell dual-omics profiling techniques (
17
,
18
), we incorporated a restriction site into the 5′ end of the adaptor (table S1). This restriction site is present as double-stranded only in non–cross-linked DNA during primer extension but not in cross-linked DNA due to DNA polymerase stalling caused by Pt-DNA adducts (
19
). The restriction enzyme can specially recognize and cleave the adaptor of non–cross-linked DNA, thereby reducing background noises. Second, we used exonucleases to remove the unligated DNA. The exonuclease can degrade unligated DNA, while fully ligated DNA with 5′ and 3′ terminal phosphorothioate bonds is protected from exonuclease digestion (
20
,
21
) (table S1). As a result, Pt drug binding sites can be specifically and sensitively identified as stop sites with base resolution through reads mapping.

We evaluated the performance of our exo- and endonuclease treatment strategy using cross-linked and non–cross-linked model sequences. Compared with cisplatin-seq, the detection signals increased by 4.9- or 10.8-fold after treatment with exo- or endonuclease individually and by 15.9-fold after combined treatment (
Fig. 1B
and fig. S1, B and C). The stop sites precisely represent the position of cross-linking (fig. S1D). In genomic DNA from cisplatin-treated HeLa cells, Pt-seq obtained high-quality detection signals even with low DNA inputs (as low as 500 ng) (
Fig. 1C
), representing a notable improvement over cisplatin-seq (
Fig. 1C
and fig. S1E). Moreover, Pt-seq demonstrated high reproducibility across different technical replicates of cisplatin treatment, with their signals exhibited strong and stable (
R
> 0.9) correlations (
Fig. 1D
).
Then, we investigated the binding sites of other Pt drugs, including third-generation drugs with reduced side effects (
22
,
23
), oxaliplatin and lobaplatin, and our previously developed Pt-Bio-I (
Fig. 1E
) (
14
). Although the antibody was originally designed to recognize cisplatin–cross-linked DNA (
24
), we demonstrated its broad detection capability for lobaplatin– and oxaliplatin–cross-linked DNA (fig. S2, A to C) (
25
). Pt-seq successfully detected binding sites of these Pt drugs, with most of them showing 72 to 89% GG frequency at stop sites, while oxaliplatin ranged between 54 and 66% (
Fig. 1F
). Given that Pt-DNA adducts preferentially form at GG dinucleotides (
4
–
6
), the high frequency of GG at stop sites highlights the specificity and fidelity of Pt-seq. The stable correlation of signals detected at overlapped binding sites between biological replicates further supported the reliability of these binding sites (
Fig. 1G
and fig. S2D). The robustness and performance of Pt-seq were also validated in HepG2 and MCF-7 cells, both showing GG frequencies exceeding 90% (fig. S2E). Compared to existing methods, our results showed that Pt-seq enhances both detection sensitivity and the signal-to-noise ratio of Pt drug–DNA binding sites, representing an advancement in studying Pt drug–DNA interactions.
Pt drugs form nonrandom binding clusters within the genome
After validating the performance of Pt-seq, we systematically identified the DNA binding sites of the four different Pt drugs in HeLa cells (
Fig. 2A
). We found that these drugs generated varied numbers of binding sites across the whole genome (
Fig. 2B
), ranging from 1280 to 25,298 for the four Pt drugs. The number of binding sites varied slightly between biological replicates but remained comparable overall. Orthogonal absolute quantification of Pt in genomic DNA validated the relative ranking of DNA binding levels across different Pt drugs identified by Pt-seq (fig. S2F), although the antibody exhibited slightly higher enrichment efficiency for cisplatin–cross-linked DNA (fig. S2G). Notably, oxaliplatin exhibited the fewest binding sites among all tested Pt drugs, consistent with previous reports that it generates fewer adducts to DNA (
26
–
28
). Between nuclear and mitochondrial genome, we found that mitochondrial DNA was a major target of all Pt drugs, with little context preference (fig. S3, A and B). This is in good agreement with previous observations by cisplatin-seq and other independent studies as well (
10
,
29
) (fig. S3, C and D). While the mitochondrial genome is relatively small compared to the nuclear genome, the enrichment is believed to be caused by the high copy number of mitochondria within cells, the relatively higher permeability of mitochondrial membranes compared to nuclear membranes, and the lack of nucleotide excision repair in mitochondria (
30
,
31
).

Because of the limited sensitivity and reproducibility of previous cisplatin profiling methods, the binding patterns of Pt drugs in the nuclear genome remain incompletely understood. With the high-quality data by Pt-seq, we performed comprehensive analyses of binding sites in the nuclear genome. First, we compared nuclear binding sites with cisplatin-induced mutations (
32
,
33
); we found that while only a small fraction (0.7%, 11 of 1530) of single-base substitutions overlapped exactly with binding sites, a notable portion (27.3%, 418 of 1530) was located within 100 bp of a binding site (fig. S3E). Next, by visualizing binding density and examining the distance between adjacent binding sites, we found that Pt drug binding sites were notably closer to each other (approximately one to two order of magnitude in distance) compared to randomly distributed GG dinucleotide sites (
Fig. 2C
). This observation was modestly apparent for only oxaliplatin by previous data, likely due to their lower sensitivity (fig. S3F). Overall, around one-third to two-thirds of binding sites fall within clusters (fig. S3G). This result suggested that Pt drug binding sites tended to aggregate into clusters within the nuclear genome.
Then, we examined the feature of these binding clusters. We found that the median length of the clusters is 10 to 20 kb, with each cluster generally consisting of tens to several hundreds of drug binding sites. Overall, we identified 197, 166, 52, and 96 binding clusters for cisplatin, lobaplatin, oxaliplatin, and Pt-Bio-I, respectively. While the overlap of the precise binding sites is modest (~20%) among various Pt drugs, except in the case of oxaliplatin, the overlap of clusters is much higher, ranging from 68 to 99% (
Fig. 2, D and E
). The zoomed-in view of a representative cluster is shown in
Fig. 2F
, in which the fluctuating binding sites were tightly aggregated within the binding cluster. Hence, we concluded that Pt-DNA adducts tend to form clusters in the nuclear genome, which is a shared feature for all Pt drugs tested in the study.
Binding clusters are majorly located in centromeric and rDNA regions
We next attempted to understand the genomic feature of these binding clusters. We first analyzed the relationship between Pt drug binding clusters and histone modifications or chromatin accessibility. We found only slight colocalization between binding clusters and either assay for transposase-accessible chromatin with sequencing (ATAC-seq) or H2A.Z signals (fig. S4, A and B) and no obvious relationship with histone modifications H3K27me3 (fig. S4C). It suggested that the binding of Pt drugs is not greatly affected by chromatin states (
9
).
We then investigated potential relationship of Pt drug binding clusters with genomic elements. By visualizing the binding clusters and their density across the chromosomes, we observed notable aggregation and enrichment of drug binding in the centromeric regions (
Fig. 3A
). This clustering phenomenon was particularly evident for 14 of 23 chromosomes, and we also observed clustering on pericentromeric regions for chromosome 7 (chr7), chr21, and chr22. In addition, we observed an aggregation of binding clusters on the short arm of chr5, which was not found for other chromosomes. Moreover, we also observed enrichment of Pt drug binding in the telomeric regions, which could be attributed to the G-rich tandem repeat sequences characteristic of telomeres. Notably, it has been shown that telomere shortening and degradation can occur following cisplatin treatment (
34
).

Next, we conducted quantitative enrichment analysis of Pt drug binding clusters on genomic elements and observed a notable enrichment in satellite DNA (
35
,
36
) (a key component of centromeres) and rDNA regions [encoding ribosomal RNA (rRNA)], with no similar enrichment observed in other repetitive DNA elements (
Fig. 3B
and fig. S4, D and E). This enrichment appeared weaker in damage-seq data and was not observed in cisplatin-seq data, likely due to their limited sensitivity (fig. S4, F and G). The binding ratio at sites within centromeric and rDNA regions was significantly higher than that outside these regions (fig. S4H). We further quantified the proportion of binding sites located within the centromeric regions. We found that 64.1 to 74.5% of oxaliplatin binding sites were located within the centromeric regions, while for other Pt drugs, this proportion ranged from 28.7 to 39.8% (
Fig. 3C
), comprising different satellite classes and states (fig. S4, I and J). Furthermore, we observed a substantial colocalization of Pt drug binding sites with centromere protein A (CENP-A), a specialized histone H3 variant in the centromeric regions that is crucial for centromeric DNA repair and kinetochore assembly (
Fig. 3D
) (
37
).
The rDNA region is responsible for transcribing rRNA and further forming ribosomes, which is essential for translation. Previous studies have shown that Pt-DNA cross-links interfere with ribosomal gene transcription (
38
); our finding that Pt drug binding clusters are enriched in rDNA regions may provide an explanation. In support of this, we conducted Gene Ontology (GO) analysis on genes overlapped with the binding clusters and found that the enriched terms were significantly related to ribosome function and biogenesis in eukaryotes (
Fig. 3E
), emphasizing the role of drug binding clusters in interfering transcription of rRNAs.
Resistance reduces drug binding to centromeric and rDNA regions
Chemotherapy resistance is one of the most notable challenges in cancer treatment (
8
). A widely accepted mechanism underlying resistance to Pt drugs is the reduced uptake and intracellular accumulation of these drugs in cancer cells (
11
). To better understand the Pt drug–DNA interactions in resistant cells, we investigated the differential genomic binding patterns between cisplatin-resistant A549 cells and WT A549 cells. We found that the pattern of DNA binding signals could be distinguished from the two cell types (
Fig. 4A
), indicating different Pt-DNA interactions at the genome scale. We found that there was ~20% reduction in binding sites in resistant cells compared to the WT cells (
Fig. 4B
), supporting the reduction of cisplatin binding to DNA in the resistant cells, and the context preference of binding sites remained similar (fig. S5A). Further investigation of binding clusters demonstrated significantly decreased binding density within clusters in resistant cells (
Fig. 4C
). Moreover, we identified differentially binding regions and found that nearly all of them (128 of 129) exhibited hypo-binding in resistant cells (
Fig. 4D
and fig. S5B). In addition, 72.1% of the differentially binding regions were located within binding clusters. We found that differentially binding regions were notably enriched in rDNA regions across all gene features (
Fig. 4E
) and in noncoding RNA (ncRNA) among gene types (
Fig. 4F
). The fewer binding to rDNA regions is expected to reduce the interference with rDNA transcription. The density of binding sites within centromeric regions was also significantly decreased in resistant cells (
Fig. 4G
). To illustrate these findings, we present specific examples of reduced binding density in resistant cells at chr20 (264,539,949 to 264,791,103;
Fig. 4H
) and within the rDNA region (
Fig. 4I
).

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis revealed that resistance was associated with multiple pathways, including drug transport (influx/efflux) and DNA damage repair (fig. S5C). To functionally validate these pathways, we performed small interfering RNA (siRNA) knockdown of key genes (fig. S5, D to K). Notably, silencing
ABCC2
(encoding the efflux pump multidrug resistance-associated protein 2 (MRP2) and combined knockdown of
ERCC1
and
XPC
(key components of the nucleotide excision repair pathway) significantly resensitized resistant cells to cisplatin, resulting in ~1.45- and ~1.87-fold reductions in median inhibitory concentration (IC
50
), respectively (fig. S5, L to O). Subsequent Pt-seq analysis showed that these knockdowns increased the total number of Pt drug binding sites (fig. S5P) and restored the binding site density within clusters and centromeric regions (fig. S5, Q and R). Moreover, WT cells treated with a low dose of cisplatin (10 μM) mimicked the binding pattern observed in resistant cells treated with 50 μM, showing no significant difference in cluster or centromere binding density (fig. S5, S and T), highlighting the importance of intracellular drug accumulation in Pt-DNA interactions. Whether the preferential reduction in Pt drug binding to centromeric and rDNA regions contributes to drug resistance or even represents a chemotherapy resistance mechanism warrants further investigation.
Mutations in cancer cells induce previously unidentified binding sites
Gene mutations are recognized as key drivers in cancer progression and resistance (
39
). Tumor cells inherently have a high possibility of gene mutations, particularly single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) (
40
). While genetic variations in DNA damage and repair pathway have been shown to influence response to platinum-based chemotherapy (
39
), whether the presence of SNP directly affects Pt drug–DNA interaction is still unknown. In our investigation of Pt drug binding sites, we found that mutations in HeLa cells that introduced GG dinucleotides contributed to the generation of previously unidentified binding sites for Pt drugs. This finding was made possible by the sensitivity and base resolution of Pt-seq (fig. S6A showed relatively low detection results in cisplatin-seq), allowing us to address precise Pt drug binding sites. As an example,
Fig. 5A
showed an A-to-G SNP in HeLa cells compared to the reference genome, and this mutation resulted in the formation of a previously unidentified cisplatin binding site. Hence, we examined all stopped sites detected in cisplatin-, oxaliplatin-, lobaplatin-, and Pt-Bio-I–treated cells and found that 12.84, 4.08, 13.75, and 9.99% of the initially identified non-GG stop sites were mutated to GG sites, respectively, in both treated and untreated HeLa cells (
Fig. 5, B and C
, showed cisplatin as an example). Among all the mutated binding sites, the most common type of mutation for cisplatin-treated cells observed was the GA-to-GG mutation, accounting for ~32% of the changes. For the other drugs, the most common mutation type was also GA to GG, accounting for ~21, 24, and 25% in oxaliplatin, lobaplatin, and Pt-Bio-I, respectively. Hence, while genetic mutations are considered as risk factors for cancer therapy, our findings above also revealed an unexpected facet of how SNPs can increase Pt-DNA cross-linking.

Introducing binding sites to enhance Pt drug cross-linking
Considering that drug resistance caused reduced Pt drug binding and gene mutations showed potential to increase Pt drug–DNA interaction, we hypothesized that it might be feasible to introduce binding sites for Pt drugs and accordingly enhance cytotoxicity. We investigated the use of ICR-191 (fig. S6B), an acridine orange compound previously used as a mutagen that induces frameshift mutations and has shown anticancer activity (
15
). The most frequent mutation type induced by ICR-191 is G insertions at consecutive G sequences. Hence, we reasoned that ICR-191 could introduce more probability of Pt-DNA cross-linking by generating more mutation-induced binding sites (
Fig. 5D
). Chinese hamster ovary (CHO)/hypoxanthine-guanine phosphoribosyltransferase (HGPRT) assay is a well-established method for selectively enriching ICR-191–mutated cells (
41
,
42
). We thus established CHO-ICR cells, a CHO cell line with ICR-191–induced mutations, and confirmed successful mutagenesis (fig. S6, C to E). Then, we treated the cells with cisplatin and used Pt-seq to detect the binding sites. We found an increase in the total number of binding sites in CHO-ICR cells compared to CHO-WT cells (fig. S6, F and G). This suggests that mutations induced by ICR-191 were creating additional binding opportunities for cisplatin. Further analysis of the mutation peaks revealed that the number of binding sites in CHO-ICR cells was nearly three times that in WT cells (
Fig. 5E
). As an example, chr6: 65,768,650 to 65,768,664 contained a GGA insertion induced by ICR-191; at this insertion site, we observed distinct binding by cisplatin (
Fig. 5F
). This demonstrated that ICR-191–induced G insertions create previously unidentified cisplatin binding sites. Furthermore, we examined the efficacy of cisplatin in mutated CHO-ICR cells. We found that IC
50
of cisplatin in CHO-ICR cells was nearly half of that in CHO-WT cells under the same treatment conditions (
Fig. 5G
). We also established ICR-191–mutagenized A549 cisplatin-resistant cells and found a decreased IC
50
for cisplatin compared to the parental resistant cells (fig. S6H). These results demonstrate that ICR-191 sensitized cells to cisplatin treatment. Our findings suggest that mutagens could serve as a Pt drug sensitizer, amplifying the probability of Pt cross-linking by creating additional binding sites. This enhances the cytotoxicity of cisplatin and offers a potential strategy for further exploration of combinational chemotherapy.
DISCUSSION
In this study, we developed Pt-seq, a genome-wide method for profiling Pt drug–DNA interactions. By using exo- and endonuclease to minimize non–cross-linked background noise, Pt-seq achieves high detection sensitivity with relatively low input requirements. Our strategy could be extended to study other DNA-targeting agents or lesions that induce DNA polymerase stalling, such as pyrimidine dimers (
43
). Pt-seq can detect the genomic binding sites of various Pt drugs. In this study, we identified the genomic binding sites of cisplatin, oxaliplatin, lobaplatin, and Pt-Bio-I. It has been studied that despite forming fewer DNA adducts than cisplatin, oxaliplatin exhibits comparable cytotoxicity (
26
). In agreement with this knowledge, we found oxaliplatin has fewer DNA binding sites than cisplatin, yet oxaliplatin also exhibits a clustering binding pattern, similar to other Pt drugs. Considering the rising interest in Pt(IV) complexes, which aim to reduce side effects and enhance anticancer activity, we applied Pt-seq to profile the DNA binding sites of a previously developed Pt-Bio-I. Although Pt-Bio-I exhibited fewer binding sites compared to cisplatin and lobaplatin, it shared the same clustering binding pattern with other Pt drugs. This is reasonable, as Pt(IV) complexes are reduced to Pt(II) in cells and then become active to cross-link with DNA (
13
,
14
). Pt-seq could help evaluate Pt drug candidates by providing detailed binding profiles that determine their ability to form Pt-DNA adduct, thereby facilitating the development of novel and effective Pt drugs.
While previous studies such as cisplatin-seq have reported preferential binding of cisplatin to mitochondrial DNA (
10
), which is also observed by Pt-seq, the distribution of Pt-DNA interactions within the nuclear genome has remained unclear. Leveraging its high sensitivity, Pt-seq revealed that Pt drugs predominantly bind to centromeric and rDNA regions in the nuclear genome, with notably strong enrichment signals. Centromere is inherently vulnerable to DNA damage and is a hotspot of chromosomal instability, likely due to the high density of repetitive sequences (
44
–
46
). The preferential binding of Pt drugs to centromeric regions provides a previously unexplored angle to look into the hypersensitivity of cells in mitosis to cisplatin and the impact of Pt drugs on cell cycle arrest (
47
,
48
), given the critical role of centromere in chromosome segregation. The lack of efficient DNA repair in mitotic cells could underlie this binding preference (
49
). In addition, the binding to rDNA regions suggests the possibility of cytotoxicity through the disruption of ribosome biogenesis. For instance, the upstream binding factor (UBF), a key positive regulator of rRNA transcription, is known to have a strong affinity for cisplatin-DNA adducts (
50
). The binding of UBF to Pt-DNA adducts may interfere with DNA damage repair, leading to the accumulation of adducts and disruption of subsequent protein synthesis (
51
). Moreover, we found a significant reduction of drug binding in these regions in cisplatin-resistant cells, which could be partially restored by perturbing key resistance-associated genes such as
ABCC2
,
ERCC1
, and
XPC
. This reduction could be a downstream effect of decreased drug uptake and/or enhanced damage repair capacity (
25
); alternatively, it could also contribute to drug resistance. Last, it has been reported that platinum exposure can induce genomic mutations (
32
,
33
), yet we observed a low overlap between the exact binding sites and cisplatin-induced mutations. It is tempting to speculate that error-prone DNA repair or heterogeneous adduct distributions at the single-cell level may influence lesion-mutation relationships.
Gene mutations are recognized as critical drivers of cancer initiation and progression, and they can significantly influence prognosis and drug resistance (
39
). Unexpectedly, we found that cancer cell mutations can create previously unidentified Pt drug binding sites; for instance, 12.84% of non-GG stop sites in cisplatin-treated cells were mutated to GG dinucleotides and created these previously unidentified binding sites. This observation led to our hypothesis that the use of mutagens may enhance drug binding and thereby sensitize cancer cells to Pt drugs. Specifically, using ICR-191 to induce G insertions in cells, we indeed increased cisplatin binding sites and observed increased sensitivity to cisplatin treatment. ICR-191 serves as a proof-of-concept agent to validate the rationale of enhancing Pt-DNA interactions. However, its inherent mutagenicity and cytotoxicity limit its clinical applicability. To further explore the therapeutic potential of this strategy, future efforts should focus on developing tumor-specific delivery systems or targeted prodrugs that minimize systemic toxicity and enhance therapeutic efficacy. It is tempting to speculate that potential previously unexplored combination therapy of Pt drugs and clinically relevant compounds capable of generating mutations could enhance anticancer activity.
Several limitations of our study should be considered. First, our analysis was primarily based on cell lines, which may not fully reflect the complexity of in vivo tumor environments. In addition, the relationship between these binding patterns and cellular and even clinical outcomes requires further exploration. Last, while Pt-seq provides valuable insights into the genomic binding patterns of Pt drugs, the impact of other factors, such as the integration of transcriptomics and proteomics, remains an area of active investigation.
MATERIALS AND METHODS
Cell culture and drug treatment
HeLa, HepG2, MCF-7, and CHO cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% GlutaMAX, and 0.5% penicillin/streptomycin at 37°C under 5% CO
2
. A549 and A549-resistant cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 under same condition; medium for A549-resistant cells were additionally supplemented with cisplatin (0.5 μg/ml) to keep resistance. CHO and A549-resistant cells were obtained from iCell Biosciences Inc., Shanghai.
For drug treatment experiments, cells were seeded in 10-cm dishes and grown to ~80% confluency. Then, the complete medium was replaced with fresh medium containing 50 μM different drugs: cisplatin (Sigma-Aldrich, C2210000), oxaliplatin (Sigma-Aldrich, Y0000271), lobaplatin (MedChemExpress, HY-105930), Pt-Bio-I (synthesized in-house), or dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). Cells were harvested after 12 hours of treatment, except for siRNA-treated cells, which were harvested after 24 hours.
Model sequence preparation
Cisplatin, oxaliplatin, lobaplatin, and AgNO
3
(Sigma-Aldrich, 85229) were dissolved and mixed in room temperature at 1000 rpm for 6 hours protected from light. Reaction products were centrifuged at 14,800 rpm for 5 min to discard AgCl. The supernatant was obtained as activated Pt drugs. Activated Pt drugs (2 mM) and up-strand of cross-link model sequences (table S1) were reacted in 10 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.5) at 1000 rpm for 12 hours at 37°C protected from light. The up-strand of cross-linked model sequences were purified with Oligo Clean & Concentrator Kit (Zymo Research, D4060). Each up-strand of model sequences were annealed with their bottom-strand respectively and exonuclease I [New England Biolabs (NEB), M0293] were used to digest unannealed single-strand DNA. The model sequences were purified with DNA Clean & Concentrator 5 (Zymo Research, D4013).
Pt sequencing
Genomic DNAs were extracted using the Universal Genomic DNA Kit (CWBIO, CW2298) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, 500 to 1000 ng of genomic DNA was fragmented by Covaris S220 Focused-ultrasonicator (Covaris Inc.) to generate DNA fragments with a medium size of 300 bp. DNA fragments with 1 ng of model sequences were performed for library preparation using the End Repair/dA-Tailing Module (NEB, E7546). Adaptors with modifications used in Pt-seq were synthesized by Sangon Biotech, Shanghai and annealed by gradual cooling process. Ligation Module (NEB, E7595) was used to perform restriction site–contained adaptor ligation. Then, DNA fragments were denatured and placed on ice. Nonimmunoprecipitated DNA was saved as input and stored at −20°C. In general, 1 μg of anti-Pt–modified DNA antibodies (Abcam, ab103261) were added into IP buffer and incubated with the processed DNA fragments overnight at 4°C with gentle rotation. Dynabeads Protein G (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 10004D) were prewashed and blocked with 0.1% bovine serum albumin/phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and then incubated with samples for 2 hours at 4°C with gentle rotation (
52
). The immunoprecipitated DNA-antibody-Dynabeads complexes were washed three times with IP buffer. The immunoprecipitated DNA was eluted by Proteinase K (NEB, P8107S) with freshly made elution buffer at 56°C for 2 hours and then purified. Then, unligated DNA was removed by RecJf (NEB, M0264) and exonuclease I (NEB, M0293). Then, the primer extension was performed using Q5 High Fidelity DNA Polymerase (NEB, M0544), and redundant primer was removed by exonuclease I (NEB, M0293). DNA fragments were denatured and placed on ice. Second adaptor ligation was performed on single-strand DNA samples using the T4 DNA Ligase (NEB, M0202) with polyethylene glycol, molecular weight 8000, at 16°C for overnight. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification was performed using Q5 High Fidelity DNA Polymerase (NEB, M0544), and DNA was amplified for 2 cycles initially. The PCR products were cleaned up with 1.2× AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter). Restriction endonuclease digestion was performed using Sbf I (NEB, R3642) at 37°C for 1 hour and followed by cleanup with 1.2× AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter). A second round of 6 to 8 cycles of PCR amplification was performed to obtain adequate libraries, and then the second-time restriction endonuclease digestion was performed to minimize non–cross-linked DNA. Final library was cleaned up with 1.2× AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter), and the concentration was measured using the Qubit 4.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with the Equalbit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Vazyme, EQ121). Size distribution was assessed using the Agilent 4150 TapeStation System. Libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000, MGISEQ-2000, or MGISEQ-T7 platform to generate 150-bp paired-end reads.
Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry
Quantification of platinum in genomic DNA. Cells were treated with 20 μM cisplatin for 6 hours, then collected, and washed three times with PBS to remove extracellular drug. Genomic DNA was then extracted using the Universal Genomic DNA Kit (CWBIO, CW2298) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA concentration was determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. For platinum quantification, purified genomic DNA samples reconstituted in Milli-Q water to a final volume of 2 ml. Platinum content was quantified using ICP-MS.
RNA sequencing
Total RNA of A549 WT and A549 cisplatin-resistant cells were extracted using the Total RNA Isolation Kit (Vazyme, RC112) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. rRNA was depleted using the rRNA Depletion Kit (TransGen Biotech, KD101), followed by library construction using the AccuNext Stranded RNA-seq Library Kit (Accurate Biotechnology, AG12504) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Final library concentration was measured using the Qubit 4.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with the Equalbit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Vazyme, EQ121). Size distribution was assessed using Agilent 4150 TapeStation System. Libraries were sequenced on MGISEQ-T7 platform to generate 150-bp paired-end reads.
siRNA knockdown
A549-resistant cells were seeded in 24-well plates and transfected with siRNA using the Lipofectamine RNAiMAX Transfection Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 13778150) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Knockdown efficiency was assessed 24 hours posttransfection by quantitative reverse transcription PCR. siRNAs were purchased from MedChemExpress and Beijing Xianghong Biotechnology Co. Ltd.
CHO/HGPRT assay
The CHO/HGPRT gene mutation assay is used to evaluate mutagenicity by measuring loss-of-function mutations in the

Normally, HGPRT enzyme activity converts 6-thioguanine (6-TG) into toxic nucleotides that kill WT cells. Mutagen-induced HGPRT mutations confer 6-TG resistance, allowing mutant cells to survive in selective media. Cells were treated with THMG (1%, v/v) for 24 hours, followed by THG (1%, v/v) for 48 hours to reduce background mutations. For mutation analysis, 5 × 10

5

Targeted deep sequencing
Total RNA of CHO cells was extracted by the Total RNA Isolation Kit (Vazyme, RC112), and then reverse transcription was performed using First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme, R312). The cDNA was diluted and amplified with primers specially designed for

Cell viability assay
Cell viability was assessed using Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; TargetMol, USA, C0005) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were seeded in 96-well plates and treated with cisplatin or DMSO for 24 hours (CHO cells) or 72 hours (A549 cells) after attachment. For drug sensitivity analysis after gene knockdown, A549-resistant cells were transfected with siRNAs in 24-well plates as described above. After 24 hours of transfection, cells were harvested and reseeded into 96-well plates and treated with cisplatin or DMSO for 24 hours after attachment. Then, the medium was replaced with medium containing CCK-8 solution and incubated for 2 hours. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using BioTek Cytation5. Cell viability was calculated as the percentage relative to untreated control cells.
Pt-seq library alignment and data cleaning
All sequence reads were processed using Trim Galore (v0.6.10) with parameters -q 30 --phred33 --length 25 --stringency 3 --paired. Reads with Sbf I enzyme restriction sites in 5′ terminal were removed. Cleaned reads were then aligned to CRGh38 human genome (CHO samples were aligned to Crigri-PICRH-1.0 genome) using bowtie2 (v2.5.1), and reads aligned to forward strand and reverse strand were split by SAMtools view -F 276 (for forward strand) and -F 260 -f 16 (for reverse strand). Aligned reads with a mapping quality ≥40, or between 1 and 10, were kept using Sambamba (v1.0.0). For sample comparison, samples were all downsampled to the volume of sample with the lowest volume. Pearson correlation coefficient across samples was calculated by deepTools multiBigwigSummary and plotCorrelation commands, with a bin size of 5 kb.
RNA-seq data analysis
All sequence reads were first cleaned using Trim Galore (v0.6.10) with parameters -q 20 --stringency 1 -e 0.3 --length 35. Filtered reads were mapped to GRCh38 genome using STAR (v2.7.5c) with parameters --sjdbOverhang 149 --outSAMmultNmax 1 --seedPerWindowNmax 50. Duplication were removed using picard.jar MarkDuplicates command (v2.27.5). Count matrix was then calculated using featureCounts (v2.0.8). Gene expression value and differential gene analysis were conducted using DESeq2 package (v1.42.1) in R (v4.3.3).

Differentially binding region calling
All sites called in replicates from resistant cell lines and WT cell lines were collected. For each site’s position in the collection, binding states from all the samples were recorded, 0 for nonbinding and 1 for binding. This table was then used to call the differentially binding region using metilene (v0.2-8) with parameters -f 3 -c 2 -d 0. Regions with
P
≤ 0.05, a difference of ≥10%, and at least 10 sites were considered differentially binding regions.
Mutation site calling and mutation-prone region calling
DeepVariant (v1.6.1) (
54
) was used to identify significant mutation sites of samples with model type WGS. To call mutation-prone regions after ICR-191 treatment, all next-generation sequencing (NGS) reads with insertions causing the duplication of G’s or C’s were kept. Consequently, mutation-prone peaks were called using kept reads via macs3 (v3.0.1) callpeak with parameters -g mm -s 150 -B.
Target sequencing data analysis
Raw sequencing reads were first subjected to unique molecular identifier (UMI) extraction using UMI-tools extract, where UMIs with specific patterns were identified and separated from the read sequences. The extracted reads were then trimmed using Trim Galore to remove adapter sequences and low-quality bases, with stringent parameters including a quality score threshold of 30, a minimum read length of 25, and a stringency value of 3. Trimmed reads were aligned to the reference genome using the STAR aligner. STAR was configured to perform end-to-end alignment. Duplicate reads were subsequently removed on the basis of UMIs using UMI-tools dedup, which extracted UMIs from read IDs and deduplicated paired-end reads.
