Monoallelic mutations in
DNAJB11
have been shown to cause polycystic kidney disease (
1
). Compared to classical autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD) due to mutations of
PKD1
or
PKD2,
patients with DNAJB11-related kidney disease have smaller cysts, kidney size is not increased, and fibrosis is more prominent (
1
,
2
,
3
).
While the mode of inheritance for human DNAJB11-related kidney disease is autosomal dominant, mouse models have established that the disease is recessive at the cellular level (
4
,
5
). Heterozygous loss of
Dnajb11
does not lead to cystic disease in mice; only homozygous deletion of
Dnajb11
in renal tubular epithelial cells causes a cystic phenotype comparable to the human disease. Homozygous mutations of
DNAJB11
in humans have been described as a cause of Ivemark II syndrome, an embryonically lethal disorder with polycystic kidneys as well as liver and pancreas abnormalities (
6
). In mice, constitutive homozygous deletion of
Dnjab11
also leads to reduced viability during embryonic development and around birth (
4
,
5
). The cause for the embryonic and perinatal lethality is not known to date. Notably, a few homozygous
Dnajb11
−/−
mice, well below the expected Mendelian ratio, survive into adulthood and do not display obvious abnormalities besides the cystic kidney phenotype. This suggests that loss of DNAJB11 causes a developmental defect but is not required for survival after birth (
4
,
5
).
It has recently been demonstrated that homozygous loss of
Dnajb11
leads to impaired processing of the
PKD1
gene product Polycystin-1 (PC1), which represents a plausible molecular mechanism for DNAJB11-related kidney disease because loss of PC1 function is the most common cause for ADPKD (
1
,
4
,
5
). PC1 is a large transmembrane protein that undergoes autoproteolytic cleavage at its G protein-coupled receptor proteolytic site (GPS). The two resulting fragments—a large extracellular N-terminal fragment and a C-terminal fragment encompassing the 11 transmembrane domains—remain non-covalently attached (
7
). The physiological importance of PC1 cleavage is highlighted by the observation that mouse models expressing GPS cleavage-deficient variants of PC1 develop polycystic kidney disease (
8
,
9
).
The majority of gene products mutated in the different forms of cystic kidney disease localize to primary cilia (
10
). Yet, DNAJB11 is not the only protein localized to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), whose mutations lead to the development of polycystic kidney disease. Mutations in the genes
GANAB
,
ALG5
,
ALG8
, and
ALG9
encoding other ER proteins also cause ADPKD (
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
). Impaired PC1 biogenesis and maturation have been proposed as a common theme in ADPKD caused by mutations in ER-resident proteins. The underlying molecular mechanisms of cyst formation remain incompletely understood.
On the molecular level, DNAJB11 has been characterized as a member of the HSP40 co-chaperone family in the ER (
15
). It consists of three domains: an N-terminal J-Domain, which is common to all HSP40 chaperones and mediates their binding to HSP70-chaperones; a substrate-binding domain, and a C-terminal dimerization domain (
16
). DNAJB11 can assemble and function as a homotetramer or in a complex with other ER-resident proteins (
16
,
17
). It has been shown to interact with and thereby regulate the function of the HSP70-chaperone BiP (
18
,
19
). Interestingly, DNAJB11 itself does not possess an ER-retention motif. It has been shown to be secreted into the extracellular space, where it may contribute to extracellular proteostasis (
20
). Two highly homologous ER-resident proteins, stromal cell-derived factor 2 (SDF2) and stromal cell-derived factor 2 like 1 (SDF2L1), have been proposed to regulate DNAJB11 ER localization
via
a direct interaction (
17
,
21
).
Chaperones are known to form multi-protein complexes that assist folding and maturation of client proteins (
22
). However, it is currently unknown whether DNAJB11 requires other proteins to enable PC1 maturation and cleavage. We therefore performed an unbiased DNAJB11 interaction proteomics screen to gain new insights into PC1 cleavage and the molecular mechanisms of DNAJB11-related kidney disease. We identified SDF2 and SDF2L1 as the two strongest interacting partners of DNAJB11 in renal epithelial cells and confirmed these interactions
in vitro
and
in vivo.
Taking advantage of newly established knockout cell lines for the respective genes, we show a strong interdependence of DNAJB11 and SDF2/SDF2L1 expression. Furthermore, we show that simultaneous loss of SDF2 and SDF2L1 leads to a strong impairment of PC1 cleavage. This defect in PC1 cleavage is not explained by the reduction of intracellular DNAJB11 protein levels after loss of SDF2 and SDF2L1. Together, our results suggest that SDF2 or SDF2L1 are essential subunits of the DNAJB11 protein complex that is required for normal PC1 cleavage and function.

SDF2 and SDF2L1 require DNAJB11 for PC1 cleavage
To test whether SDF2 or SDF2L1 need DNAJB11 for PC1 cleavage, we expressed SDF2 and SDF2L1 in

Discussion
In this study we aimed at gaining new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying DNAJB11-related kidney disease. To test whether DNAJB11 interacts with proteins that are required for the cleavage and maturation of PC1, we performed an unbiased interaction proteomics screen. The screen was conducted with endogenous proteins without crosslinking to identify physiological interactors that might serve as subunits of DNAJB11. We identified 20 DNAJB11-interacting proteins with SDF2 and SDF2L1 as the most enriched co-purified proteins. We therefore chose to focus exclusively on these two proteins. But the interaction proteomic dataset generated here could serve as an interesting starting point for further investigations into the function of DNAJB11. PC1 was not detected as an interactor of DNAJB11 in our mass spectrometry dataset, likely due to its very low endogenous expression levels.
SDF2 and SDF2L1 are two highly homologous ER-resident proteins (68% homology and 78% similarity) (
24
,
25
,
26
). Both proteins contain C-terminal ER retention signals and three MIR domains that might implicate these proteins in O-glycosylation of proteins (
27
). Yet, to our knowledge, no direct evidence for such a function of SDF2 or SDF2L1 has been reported. Alterations in SDF2 and SDF2L1 expression levels have been described as risk factors affecting the prognosis in different types of cancer (
28
,
29
,
30
,
31
). SDF2L1 is upregulated upon ER stress (
25
,
32
). The precise molecular and cellular functions of SDF2 and SDF2L1 have remained incompletely understood.
In line with previous publications (
16
,
21
,
33
), our data confirm an interaction of DNAJB11 with SDF2 and SDF2L1 and show an interdependence of DNAJB11 and SDF2/SDF2L1 intracellular protein levels. The interaction of DNAJB11 – which does not contain an ER retention motif - with SDF2 or SDF2L1 regulates the ER localization and intracellular protein levels of DNAJB11. In the absence of SDF2 and SDF2L1, DNAJB11 is almost completely secreted into the extracellular space.
Vice versa
, we show that SDF2 and SDF2L1 protein levels are reduced after loss of DNAJB11. Since we could neither detect reduced
Sdf2
or
Sdf2l1
transcript levels nor increased SDF2 or SDF2L1 protein levels after lysosomal or proteasomal inhibition in
Dnajb11
KO cells, the mechanism behind this remains unclear and needs further investigation.
The pathophysiology of DNAJB11-related kidney disease has been shown to involve impaired PC1 processing (
1
,
4
,
5
). Our data show that concomitant knockout of
Sdf2
and
Sdf2l1
leads to the same impairment of PC1 GPS-cleavage as the knockout of
Dnajb11
. This comes as no surprise, considering the almost complete absence of intracellular DNAJB11 in
Sdf2; Sdf2l1
KO cells.
However, our study demonstrates that it is not only the absence of DNAJB11 in
Sdf2; Sdf2l1
double knockout cells that leads to impaired PC1 processing. Re-expression of DNAJB11 with an added ER retention motif in
Sdf2; Sdf2l1
double knockout cells leads to normal intracellular DNAJB11 protein levels, but PC1 cleavage is nevertheless as strongly impaired as in
Dnajb11 KO
and in
Sdf2; Sdf2l1
double knockout cells. Similarly, reconstitution of normal SDF2/SDF2L1 protein levels
via
retroviral expression in
Dnajb11
KO cells is also not sufficient to rescue impaired PC1 cleavage. Taken together, these data show that a complex of DNAJB11 and SDF2 or SDF2L1 is necessary for physiological PC1 cleavage suggesting that these proteins act as functional subunits of DNAJB11.
Single knockouts of
Sdf2
and
Sdf2l1
, in which intracellular DNAJB11 protein levels are at about 50% compared to WT, do not impair PC1 biogenesis. This is in line with the presumed pathophysiology of DNAJB11-related nephropathy: Homozygous loss of
Dnajb11
leads to cystic kidney disease. However, heterozygous loss of
Dnajb11
, with approximately 50% of DNAJB11 protein levels compared to WT, does not lead to a pathologic phenotype (
4
,
5
).
Furthermore, the absence of impaired PC1 cleavage in
Sdf2
and
Sdf2l1
single knockout cell lines implicates that SDF2 and SDF2L1 are redundant regarding their role in PC1 GPS-cleavage. This redundancy may also explain why no
SDF2
- or
SDF2L1
-related kidney disease has been reported in humans yet. DNAJB11-related kidney disease is a relatively rare disorder with a prevalence of about 1:10,000 (
2
,
34
). Our data from knockout cells implicate that only biallelic mutations of
Sdf2
as well as
Sdf2l1
would cause impaired PC1 processing, thus making the occurrence of a theoretical
Sdf2/Sdf2l1
-related kidney disease rather unlikely. It is conceivable, however, that variants in those genes act as genetic modifiers of ADPKD.
To our knowledge, no studies investigating
Sdf2
knockout mice or double knockouts of
Sdf2
and
Sdf2l1
have been reported. A mutation of
Sdf2l1
has been described as one of several mutations in the “Cohen diabetic rat” model, an inbred rat model of type II diabetes (
35
,
36
). While animals developed diabetic nephropathy after administration of a diabetogenic diet, no cystic kidney disease was observed (
37
).
In summary, our data establish SDF2 and SDF2L1 as functional subunits of the DNAJB11 co-chaperone, which are required for GPS-cleavage of PC1. Future studies should investigate whether the DNAJB11-SDF2/SDF2L1 complex also affects the processing of other client proteins and whether the loss of these proteins
in vivo
phenocopies the loss of DNAJB11.

Cells were harvested 5 days after epithelial confluency (IMCD3) or 24 h after transfection (HEK 293T). Samples were lysed in cold lysis buffer (1%Triton X-100, 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 50 mM NaCl, 50 mM NaF, 15 mM Na

Immunoprecipitation took place for 2 h on a rotating wheel at 4 °C. After 3 washing steps (each 10 min, 4 °C), proteins were eluted for 30 min at 42 °C with 2× Laemmli sample buffer and 100 mM DTT.

Mass spectrometry
Proteins bound to beads were eluted in lysis buffer (5% SDS, 50 mM triethyl ammonium bicarbonate (TEAB), pH 7.5) and incubated at 95 °C for 10 min. Samples were centrifuged at 13,000
g
for 8 min and the supernatant used in the following steps. Proteins were reduced using 5 mM tris (2-carboxyethyl) phosphine hydrochloride (TCEP) (Sigma; 75259) for 10 min at 95 °C and alkylated using 10 mM 2-iodoacetamide (Sigma; I1149) for 20 min at room temperature in the dark. The following steps were performed using S-Trap micro filters (Protifi, Huntington, NY) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, a final concentration of 1.2% phosphoric acid and six volumes of binding buffer (90% methanol; 100 mM TEAB; pH 7.1) were added. After gentle mixing, the protein solution was loaded to an S-Trap filter and spun at 2000 rpm for 0.5 to 1 min. The filter was washed three times using 150 μl of binding buffer. Sequencing-grade trypsin (Promega, 1:25 enzyme:protein ratio) diluted in 20 μl digestion buffer (50 mM TEAB) were added into the filter and digested at 47 °C for 1 h. To elute peptides, three step-wise buffers were applied: 1) 40 μl 50 mM TEAB, 2) 40 μl 0.2% formic acid in H
2
O, and 3) 50% acetonitrile and 0.2% formic acid in H
2
O. The peptide solutions were combined and dried in a SpeedVac.
800 ng of peptides were analyzed on a Q-Exactive Plus mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) coupled to an EASY-nLCTM 1000 UHPLC system (Thermo Scientific). The analytical column was self-packed with silica beads coated with C18 (Reprosil Pur C18-AQ, d = 3 Å) (Dr Maisch HPLC GmbH, Ammerbusch, Germany). For peptide separation, a linear gradient of increasing buffer B (0.1% formic acid in 80% acetonitrile, Fluka) was applied, ranging from 5 to 40% buffer B over the first 90 min and from 40 to 100% buffer B in the subsequent 30 min (120 min separating gradient length). Peptides were analyzed in data dependent acquisition mode (DDA). Survey scans were performed at 70,000 resolution, an AGC target of 3e6 and a maximum injection time of 50 ms followed by targeting the top 10 precursor ions for fragmentation scans at 17,500 resolution with 1.6 m/z isolation windows, an NCE of 30 and an dynamic exclusion time of 35 s. For all MS2 scans the intensity threshold was set to 1.3e5, the AGC to 1e4 and the maximum injection time to 80 ms.
Raw data were analyzed with MaxQuant (v 1.6.17.0) with the built-in Andromeda peptide search engine (
39
) allowing two missed cleavage sites, no variable modifications, and carbamidomethylation of cysteines as fixed modification. The precursor mass tolerance was 20 ppm (MS1) and the Fragment mass tolerance (MS/MS) was 0.02 Da. The match between runs option was selected. The Mouse-EBI-reference database was downloaded from
https://www.ebi.ac.uk/
on Feb 4, 2020. For label free quantification the MaxLFQ algorithm was applied using the standard settings. Only unique peptides were used for quantification.
Data were normalized on peptide level by equalizing the medians and
p
values were calculated using the MSstats package (v. 3.20.3) in R (v. 4.0.4) (
40
). Subsequently, protein LFQ intensities were log
2
-transformed for calculation of fold changes and
p
values. Three biological replicates were performed per condition.
