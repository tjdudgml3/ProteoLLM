Introduction
Macrophages reside in various tissues as essential sentinel cells and constitute the front line of host responses against pathogens and tissue trauma.
1
,
2
Their potent phagocytic capacity enables them to contain invading microorganisms and other foreign bodies and to act as mechanisms for clearing the dying cells and cellular debris. Subsequently, macrophages secrete a plethora of cytokines, chemokines, and other inflammatory mediators to elicit an inflammatory response and immune cell recruitment at the site of injured or infected tissue. They also process and present exogenous antigens to activate the adaptive immune system. Under physiological conditions, macrophage-mediated immunoregulation is a self-limiting process that leads to removal of pathogens or other foreign bodies, repair and functional recovery of the tissue, inflammation resolution, and return to homeostasis. However, because dysregulated macrophage functions can cause persistent inflammation, tissue damage, and disruption of homeostasis, they are implicated in the pathogenesis of a variety of diseases such as metabolic diseases, cancer, fibrosis, and asthma.
2
,
3
Macrophages are heterogeneous and capable of polarizing into distinct functional phenotypes, in response to the surrounding microenvironment.
1
,
4
Typically, macrophages have a range of polarization phenotypes (or a so-called spectrum of macrophage activation states) with 2 extreme opposite ends—M1 and M2.
4
,
5
The M1 state refers to classically activated macrophages with proinflammatory properties, and M2 macrophages are alternative activated cells with anti-inflammatory or reparative functions. M1 macrophages actively generate proinflammatory cytokines (eg IL-6), chemokines (eg chemokine C-X-C motif ligand 10 [Cxcl10]), and other inflammatory mediators (eg inducible nitric oxide synthase [iNOS]) and often partake in pathogen eradication, inflammatory responses, and antigen presentation.
1
,
4
,
6
In cell culture experiments, naïve (M0) macrophages can be induced into M1 macrophages by treatment with microbial products (eg LPS) and/or proinflammatory cytokines (eg IFN-γ).
7–9
M2 macrophages, on the other hand, highly express mannose receptor C-type 1 (Mrc1, also called Cd206), CD163 molecule (Cd163), and many other distinct proteins; they mainly contribute to tissue remodeling and repair, inflammation resolution, and homeostatic maintenance.
1
,
4
,
6
In cell culture studies, incubation of anti-inflammatory cytokines (eg IL-4 and IL-13) with M0 macrophages leads to conversion into M2 macrophages.
7–9
Identification of distinct macrophage phenotypes is important to understand the contributions of specific macrophage populations to health and diseases. They also have significant implications in disease diagnosis and evaluation of medication efficacy. Historically, genes expressing proinflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and other inflammatory mediators (eg
Il6
,
Il1b
,
Tnf
, and
Nos2
) are considered to be biomarkers of M1 macrophages, whereas the genes highly induced in M2 macrophages (eg
Arg1
,
Mrc1
,
Ccl17
, and
Chil3
) are regarded as M2 biomarkers.
4
,
10
In recent years, a number of new biomarkers of M1 and M2 macrophages have been identified through transcriptomic or proteomic approaches.
8
,
9
,
11
Most of these studies have focused on one or few macrophage types, but there are considerable differences between murine and human macrophages, immortal cancer cell lines and primary macrophages, and in vitro and in vivo macrophages.
A careful examination of the literature did not reveal any proteomic data about murine primary macrophages that undergo M1 or M2 polarization. Therefore, we treated murine naïve M0 primary macrophages with LPS to induce the M1 or M(LPS) state or with IL-4 to induce the M2 or M(IL-4) state. Quantitative proteomic analysis was conducted to obtain the proteomes of murine primary M0, M1, and M2 macrophages. Comparative analyses of these proteomes revealed the unique protein landscapes and primary functions of murine M1 and M2 macrophages. Plasma membrane proteins in the signature proteins of M1 and M2 macrophages were identified as new biomarkers. In addition, the protein signatures of murine primary macrophages were compared with those of polarized human THP-1 macrophages to elucidate the similarities and divergences between murine and human macrophages. In doing so, we identified new M1 biomarkers shared by human and mouse primary macrophages.
Materials and methods
Cell culture
Mouse bone marrow–derived macrophages (BMDMs) were prepared and polarized into M1 or M2 phenotypes as previously described.
7
In brief, bone marrow cells were extracted from tibia and femur bones of C57BL/6J mice. To differentiate these cells into macrophages, they were cultured for 7 d in growth medium (RPMI 1640 [Corning, Tewksbury, MA, USA] with 10% FBS [Atlanta Biologicals, Flowery Branch, GA, USA; S1150], 1 mM sodium pyruvate [Corning], 10 mM HEPES buffer [Corning], 2 mM L-glutamine [Corning], 50 µg/mL penicillin-streptomycin [Corning], 50 µM β-mercaptoethanol [MilliporeSigma, St Louis, MO, USA]) supplemented with 25% L929 cell-conditioned medium. Naïve BMDMs were treated with 10 ng/mL of LPS (InvivoGen, San Diego, CA, USA; tlrl-peklps) for 8 h to polarize them into the M1 or M(LPS) state or with 20 ng/mL of mouse IL-4 (PeproTech, Cranbury, NJ, USA) for 24 h to activate the M2 or M(IL-4) phenotype. For prolonged LPS treatment, naïve BMDMs were treated with 10 ng/mL of LPS for 3, 8, 16, 24, 40 and 48 h, followed by cell harvesting for immunoblot analysis.
Human monocyte-derived macrophages (MDMs) were prepared as previously described with slight modifications.
11
,
12
In brief, fresh blood from healthy donors (Research Blood Components, Boston, MA, USA) contained in Vacutainer blood collection tubes with sodium heparin (Becton Dickinson [BD], Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) was centrifuged at 1,600 × g for 20 min at room temperature. The buffy coat layer enriched with monocytes was collected and washed with PBS. After centrifugation at 300 × g for 10 min, the cell pellets were collected and differentiated into macrophages by culturing for 7 d in the growth medium supplemented with 80 ng/mL of recombinant human macrophage colony-stimulating factor (PeproTech). Naïve macrophages were treated with 10 ng/mL of LPS (InvivoGen) for 8 h to induce the M1 phenotype or with 20 ng/mL of human IL-4 (PeproTech) for 24 h to induce the M2 phenotype.
12
Quantitative proteomic analysis
Three biological replicates of M0, M1, and M2 BMDMs were independently prepared for quantitative proteomic analysis as previously described.
7
In brief, the cellular proteins were extracted using radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer, followed by reduction using 5 mM dithiothreitol, then alkylation with 15 mM iodoacetamide. A Calbiochem ProteoExtract protein precipitation kit (MilliporeSigma) was used to precipitate the proteins, which were incubated with trypsin to digest them. Resulting peptides were analyzed using Orbitrap Eclipse liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (MS) with a RSLCnano system (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA). Separation was achieved using a C18 nano column (ACQUITY UPLC M-class, Peptide CSH, 130A, 1.7 µm, 75 µm × 250 mm; Waters Corp, Milford, MA, USA) at 300 nL/min with a linear gradient from 3% to 22% B over 96 min; solvent B was 100% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid. Data-dependent MS1 spectra were acquired using a mass range of m/z 375 to 1500, resolution 120,000, automatic gain control (AGC) target of 4 × 10
5
with a maximum injection time of 50 ms. Data-dependent MS2 spectra were acquired by higher-energy collisional dissociation as a Top10 experiment with normalized collision energy set at 30%; AGC target was set to 5 × 10
4
, 15,000 resolution, with a maximum injection time of 54 ms. Dynamic exclusion was set at 60 s and the isolation window set to 0.7 m/z. Acquired mass spectra were analyzed in Proteome Discoverer (Thermo Fisher, version 2.4), and the subsequent peptide quantification was conducted as previously described.
7
The data were searched against the common contaminants database cRAP (116 entries,
www.theGPM.org
) and the UniProt mouse database (version 20221103, 55,311 sequences) using Mascot 2.6.2. A decoy database search was included to set the false discovery rate to 1% (high confidence), and peptides were validated by Percolator with a 0.01 posterior error probability threshold. Finally, a filter of a minimum of 2 unique peptides was applied to remove any false positive.
The peptides were quantified using the precursor abundance based on intensity and normalized using total peptide amount. The peptide group abundances were summed for each sample and the maximum sum for all files was determined. The normalization factor used was the factor of the sum of the sample and the maximum sum in all files.
Comparative analyses of proteome profiles
Signature proteins of M1 or M2 BMDMs were identified based on the following 2 criteria: (1) fold change (FC) of abundance >2 and (2)
P
value of the abundance ratio <0.05. The online platform Metascape (
https://metascape.org
) was utilized to identify statistically enriched terms of candidate proteins in gene ontology (GO) processes.
13
The platform also facilitated the visualization of densely connected network components within the protein–protein interaction network of candidate proteins, using the Molecular Complex Detection (MCODE) algorithm. Principal component analysis (PCA) and heatmap diagrams were generated using Metware Cloud (
https://cloud.metware.cn
) to illustrate the distributions of the proteome profiles. Volcano plots were created using ImageGP to demonstrate proteins with significant expression changes in M1 or M2 BMDMs compared to M0 BMDMs.
14
Immunoblot and quantitative PCR analyses
Immunoblot and quantitative PCR (qPCR) analyses were carried out as previously described.
15
Primary antibodies used in this study included antibodies against glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (Gapdh; Cell Signaling, Boston, MA, USA, 2118; 1:1,000); C-C motif chemokine ligand 5 (Ccl5; Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA, 12000-1-AP; 1:500); mouse C-type lectin domain family 4, member e (Clec4e; Novus Biologicals, Centennial, CO, USA, NBP2-27093SS; 1:250); human CLEC4E (Proteintech, 19376-1-AP; 1:1,000); C-type lectin domain family 7, member a (Clec7a; R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA, AF1756-SP; 1:2,000); mouse CD72 antigen (Cd72; R&D Systems, AF1279SP; 1:1,000); guanylate binding protein 2 (Gbp2; Proteintech, 11854-1-AP; 1:1,000); mouse aconitate decarboxylase 1 (Acod1; Cell Signaling, 19857T; 1:1,000); human ACOD1 (Cell Signaling, 77510T; 1:1,000); and neurochondrin (Ncdn; Proteintech, 13187-1-AP; 1:1000). If not specified, most of the antibodies recognized both human and mouse antigens. For qPCR analysis, the levels of relative mRNAs were determined by normalizing the gene of interest to the hypoxanthine guanine phosphoribosyl transferase (
Hprt
) gene in murine cells or the
HPRT
gene in human cells.
Flow cytometry and immunofluorescence analyses
Flow cytometry and immunofluorescence (IF) analyses were conducted as previously described.
16
,
17
For flow cytometry analysis, BMDMs were blocked with TruStain FcX (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) for 15 min in cold FACS buffer (PBS containing 0.5% FBS, 2 mM EDTA) and incubated with Clec4e-PE (SantaCruz, Dallas, TX, USA, sc-390806; 1:20) and Cd72-FITC antibodies (BD, 550966; 1:200). Alternatively, BMDMs were permeabilized in 1× permeabilization buffer (BioLegend) for 30 min after blocking, followed by incubation with iNOS-Alexa Fluor 488 (eBioscience, San Diego, CA, USA, 53-5920-80; 1:100) and Arg1-PE antibody (eBioscience, 12-3697-80; 1:40). After labeling, the cells were subjected to flow cytometry analysis using a DxP10 FACSort flow cytometer (BD) or LSRFortessa (BD), followed by data analysis using FlowJo software (BD). For CD14 IF, naïve human MDMs were stained with anti-human CD14-PE antibody (BioLegend, 301805; 1:20); DAPI was used to stain nuclei. Cell images were taken using a SP8 confocal microscope (Leica, Teaneck, NJ, USA).
Statistical analysis
The data were analyzed using Excel software, and a 2-tailed
t
-test was performed. For each cell culture experiment, BMDMs were cultured, stimulated, and harvested for specific experiments each week, which was considered as one replicate. Such experiments were repeated in different weeks to obtain 3 to 4 independent biological replicates (
n
= 3–4).
P
values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.
Results
Polarization of murine primary macrophages into M1 or M2 macrophages
Murine bone marrow cells from C57BL/6J mice were differentiated into naïve M0 primary macrophages. These M0 BMDMs were incubated with LPS or IL-4 to polarize into M1/M(LPS) or M2/M(IL-4) macrophages, respectively. Their polarization phenotypes were validated using qPCR, immunoblot analysis, and flow cytometry. The expressions of typical M1 biomarker genes, including
Nos2
,
Il1b
,
Il6
, and
Ccl5
, were significantly increased by the treatment with LPS but not IL-4 (
Fig. 1A
). Meanwhile, the M2 biomarker genes, such as
Arg1
,
Mgl2
, and
Clec7a
, were highly induced by IL-4, but not LPS (
Fig. 1B
). Immunoblot analysis confirmed that LPS treatment led to specific expression of M1 biomarker Ccl5 protein in M1 cells, whereas IL-4 induced the expression of M2 biomarker Clec7a protein in M2 cells (
Fig. 1C
). The M1 and M2 states were further verified using flow cytometry analysis. After LPS treatment, the majority of macrophages became positive for the M1 biomarker iNOS but negative for the M2 biomarker Arg1 (
Fig. 1D
). After IL-4 incubation, most macrophages were positive for Arg1 but negative for iNOS (
Fig. 1D
). Together, these results confirmed that LPS or IL-4 treatment successfully polarized naïve M0 murine BMDMs into M1 or M2 macrophages, respectively.

Discussion
In summary, the comparative quantitative proteomic analysis of differentially polarized murine primary macrophages revealed the protein landscapes of M0, M1/M(LPS), and M2/M(IL-4) macrophages. Further bioinformatic analysis indicated that the uniquely enriched proteins in each macrophage subtype critically contributed to the typical functions and pathways of M1 and M2 macrophages. Plasma membrane proteins Clec4e and Cd72 were identified as new biomarkers of murine M1 and M2 macrophages, respectively. Comparison of the proteomes of murine and human macrophages led to identification of shared new M1 biomarkers Gbp2/GBP2 and Acod1/ACOD1. In addition, the CLEC4E membrane protein was found as a new M1 biomarker for human primary macrophages.
Macrophages are highly heterogeneous cells with important roles in host defense, repair of injured tissue, and maintenance of homeostasis. Depending on the surrounding microenvironment, these cells are capable of promptly tailoring their functionalities and demonstrate a broad range of immunophenotypes with 2 extreme opposite ends—proinflammatory M1 and anti-inflammatory M2.
4
,
5
Because of extrinsic environmental complexity and intrinsic cellular heterogenicity, macrophage activation or polarization is considered as a multidimensional or spectral continuum.
4
,
26
Indeed, when human primary macrophages were stimulated with 28 different activating signals, such as LPS, IL-4, IFN-γ, IL-10, TNF, and different fatty acids, transcriptome-based network analysis revealed a spectrum model of macrophage activation, rather than a M1 versus M2 dualistic model.
20
M1 and M2 are still useful terms when it comes to describing the proinflammatory and anti-inflammatory ends of the activation spectrum. During bacterial or viral infection, the host favors the M1 spectrum of macrophage activation, which enhances the microbicidal activities of these cells and promotes them to secrete reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, cytokines, and chemokines to eradicate pathogens.
27
,
28
In contrast, during parasite infection, it is usually not possible for macrophages to engulf or destroy large extracellular invaders, such as helminths, due to their sizes. Accordingly, the M2 spectrum of macrophage activation is favored to repair tissue damage caused by parasites.
29
Under pathological conditions, macrophages are challenged with diverse local activating signals and thus demonstrate spectral activation with different immunophenotypes. Our comparative proteomic analyses of murine primary M0, M1, and M2 macrophages demonstrated the unique protein landscapes and signatures of the extreme proinflammatory and anti-inflammatory ends of macrophage activation (
Figs. 2
and
3
,
Tables S1–S5
). The signature proteins of M1 and M2 macrophage subtypes were engaged in distinct pathways and functionalities (
Fig. 4
), which offered the biochemical bases for specialized and distinctive functions of macrophage subtypes. These proteomic data of murine primary macrophages could serve as a valuable dataset for future macrophage research.
Because macrophages with different immunophenotypes play diverse yet crucial roles under a wide range of physiopathological conditions, identification of their activation or polarization states is key to understanding their unique functionalities and contributions to health and diseases, as well as disease diagnosis and progression and treatment efficacy assessment. Considerable efforts have been devoted to identifying the biomarkers of macrophage polarization. A collection of canonical M1 and M2 biomarkers has been summarized
4
,
30
and used widely in macrophage research. Transcriptional analysis of murine primary M0, M1, and M2 macrophages
8
identified a number of new polarization biomarkers—CD38 molecule (Cd38), G-protein coupled receptor (Gpr18), and formyl peptide receptor 2 (Fpr2)—as M1 biomarkers and early growth response protein 2 (Egr2) and myelocytomatosis oncogene (Myc) as M2 biomarkers. A subsequent proteomic analysis of macrophages differentiated from human THP-1 cells
11
indicated that 3 IFN-induced proteins with tetratricopeptide repeats (IFITs: IFIT1, IFIT2, and IFIT3) were uniquely enriched in M1 macrophages. A recent comparative proteomic analysis of macrophages differentiated from human THP-1 cells and mouse RAW264.7 cells
9
further confirmed that Ifit/IFIT1-3 were highly induced in M1 macrophages. In addition, cytidine/uridine monophosphate kinase 2 (Cmpk2/CMPK2), radical S-adenosyl methionine domain containing 2 (Rsad2/RSAD2), RNA sensor RIG-I (Rigi/RIGI, also known as Ddx58/DDX58), and DExH-box helicase 58 (Dhx58/DHX58) were identified as new M1 biomarkers for both human and mouse macrophages.
Our proteomic studies using murine primary M0, M1, and M2 macrophages verified the presence of many canonical biomarkers in polarized macrophages such as Il1b, Ccl5, and Marco for M1 biomarkers and Arg1, Mrc1, and Clec7a for M2 biomarkers (
Tables S6
and
S7
). In addition, our studies confirmed that levels of Ifit1-3, Cmpk2, Rsad2, and Ddx58 were markedly increased in murine M1 macrophages (
Fig. S5
,
Table S6
). Of note, our list of potential M1 biomarkers (
Table S6
) also included some type I IFN–stimulated proteins, such as Ifits, Cxcl10, Gbps, and Isg20,
31
,
32
which was probably due to autocrine IFN production.
33
Our results were in agreement with studies showing that over 100 transcripts were co-regulated by LPS and IFN-β.
34
Finally, we identified 2 plasma membrane proteins, Clec4e and Cd72, as new biomarkers for murine M1 and M2 macrophages, respectively (
Fig. 6
). Considering the apparent divergences between mouse and human polarized macrophages,
4
,
9
,
20
we further compared the proteomes of mouse primary macrophages with those of human macrophages differentiated from THP-1 cells. We identified Gbp2/GBP2 and Acod1/ACOD1 proteins as new biomarkers for both human and mouse primary M1 macrophages but could not find a robust new biomarker for M2 primary macrophages shared by human and mouse.
Among the newly identified biomarkers, the plasma membrane protein Clec4e is a pattern recognition receptor that recognizes specific glycolipids on the surface of certain fungi and mycobacteria.
35
,
36
An early report indicated that the transcript level of the
Clec4e
gene was increased in response to LPS treatment in mouse peritoneal macrophages and the RAW264.7 macrophage cell line.
37
A recent study
38
showed that Clec4e in macrophages and neutrophils mediates persistent inflammation in the transition from acute kidney injury to chronic kidney disease. Cd72 is a type II transmembrane glycoprotein belonging to the calcium-dependent lectin superfamily. It is a well-established negative modulator that regulates the signaling and responsiveness of B cells.
39
There were few reports about the role of Cd72 in macrophages. One study using single-cell transcriptomic analyses of immune cells in the heart identified a subpopulation of Cd72-positive macrophages, which aggravated heart injury in mice with transverse aortic constriction.
40
Acod1 is a mitochondrial enzyme responsible for catalyzing the production of itaconate from the tricarboxylic acid cycle.
41
,
42
This gene was identified in 1995 as an LPS-induced gene in RAW264.7 cells.
43
Further studies showed that the expression of the
Acod1
gene is very low in macrophages but becomes highly induced by pathogen-associated molecular patterns, invading pathogens, and proinflammatory cytokines. Functionally, Acod1 mediates itaconate production and metabolic rewiring, playing both pro- and anti-inflammatory roles in immunity and diseases.
41
,
42
Gbp2, a member of the GTPase superfamily, is induced by both type 1 (IFN-α and IFN-β) and type II (IFN-γ) interferons.
44
,
45
Gbp2 demonstrates broad antiviral activities against Zika, measles, influenza A, and other viruses, by suppressing furin-mediated cleavage of vial envelope proteins.
46
Gbp2 also serves as a cytosolic LPS sensor during intracellular bacterial infection. It forms a complex with LPS to promote caspase-4–mediated noncanonical inflammasome activation.
47
These previous studies have linked the new biomarkers we identified to immune functions. Also, it has been reported that transcript levels of the
Clec4e
,
Acod1
, and
Gbp2
genes were increased in response to treatment with LPS or INF-γ. However, their roles as M1 biomarkers have not been rigorously investigated. Our studies demonstrated that both mRNA and protein levels of the
Clec4e
gene were very low in both murine primary M0 and M2 macrophages but became highly induced in M1 macrophages (
Fig. 6
). Notably, although the previous proteomic analyses
9
,
11
of human macrophages differentiated from THP-1 cells did not identify CLEC4E as a M1 marker, we found that both mRNA and protein levels of the
CLEC4E
gene were specifically enhanced in human primary M1 macrophages, compared to M0 or M2 macrophages (
Fig. S8
). Therefore, we identified the plasma membrane protein Clec4e/CLEC4E as a new M1 biomarker for both murine and human primary macrophages. Its plasma membrane localization would greatly facilitate its labeling and detection under physiological and pathological conditions. In addition, we found that both mRNA and protein levels of the
Acod1
and
Gbp2
genes were uniquely elevated in both murine and human primary M1 macrophages, compared to their M0 and M2 counterparts (
Fig. 7
). Therefore, Acod1/ACOD1 and Gbp2/GBP2 were identified as new M1 biomarkers for both murine and human primary macrophages.
The functions of Cd72 in macrophages remain largely elusive. We found that the
Cd72
gene was exclusively upregulated at the transcriptional and translational level in murine M2 macrophages, compared to M0 or M1 macrophages (
Fig. 6
). Its plasma membrane localization would also facilitate its labeling and detection under different conditions. However, we did not detect its robust transcriptional increase in human primary M2 macrophages (
Fig. S8
). Therefore, Cd72 was identified as a new M2 biomarker for murine macrophages but not for human macrophages. We did not identify any robust M2 biomarkers shared by murine and human primary macrophages.
In summary, our comparative proteomic analyses, combined with molecular and biochemical validation, identified Clec4e/CLEC4E, Acod1/ACOD1, and Gbp2/GBP2 as robust new M1 biomarkers for both mouse and human primary macrophages, as well as Cd72 as a new M2 biomarker for mouse primary macrophages. The plasma membrane identities of Clec4e/CLEC4E and Cd72 make them readily labeled by imaging agents. Our discovery of these new M1 and M2 biomarkers provides powerful tools for discriminating macrophage subtypes and facilitating targeted regulation of macrophage functions under different physiopathological conditions. Therefore, these new biomarkers have potential broad applications in both basic research and clinical settings. In the future, further studies are warranted to validate these new biomarkers of macrophage subtypes in vivo.
