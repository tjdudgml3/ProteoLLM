Introduction
Accumulating evidence indicates that the eukaryotic translation process differs from that of prokaryotes in both kinetic and structural properties [
1
]. Compared to prokaryotes, the higher eukaryotic translation apparatus includes more voluminous 80S ribosomes and an increased number of translation factors. This allows for the adjustment of the speed of translation and its regulation according to the more complex needs of a higher eukaryotic cell. The role of one of the translation elongation factors, eEF1A*GTP, is to bind any aminoacyl–transfer RNA, but only release the correct one to the A site of the messenger RNA-programmed 80S ribosome [
2
]. Despite being assumed to function equivalently to its prokaryotic counterpart EF-Tu, eEF1A differs in its mechanism of GDP/GTP exchange [
3
] and exhibits a distinct mode of interaction with the guanine–nucleotide exchange factor [
4
]. In higher vertebrates, the tissue-specific paralogs eEF1A1 and eEF1A2, which share 92% sequence identity and 97% homology (Fig.
1A
and
B
), are expressed in a mutually exclusive manner and are encoded by distinct genes located on different chromosomes [
5
]. The precise reason for the existence of these two paralogs remains unclear. The proteins exhibit only subtle differences in functionality during translation, yet surprisingly, they show marked disparities in their involvement in several biological processes, including myogenesis [
6
], viral propagation [
7
,
8
], neurodegenerative diseases [
9
,
10
], atherosclerosis [
11
], and cancer [
12
]. Speculations suggest that these differences may arise from conformational disparities between these closely related proteins [
13
–
15
]. The paralogs exhibit slight differences in their relative affinities for GDP/GTP, nucleotide exchange factors, and RNA [
16
–
18
]. These variations may influence the different translation elongation rates observed in paralog-specific tissues [
19
,
20
]. Furthermore, eEF1A1 and eEF1A2 exhibit distinct abilities to interact with various protein partners, which is consistent with the idea that the structural elements responsible for cellular interactions in these two paralogs are likely organized differently [
15
,
17
,
21
–
23
]. However, whether this hypothesis holds true remains to be determined.

AlphaFold2 predicts similar arrangements of the three domains (D1, D2, and D3) in the tertiary structures of both eEF1A1 and eEF1A2 (Fig.
1C
). Earlier studies utilizing short-term molecular dynamics (MD) simulations and various physical measurements have not identified significant structural differences between these paralogs, aside from the observation that eEF1A1 exhibits increased surface hydrophobicity, melting capacity, and a greater tendency to oligomerize [
14
,
24
]. eEF1A1 has proven challenging to crystallize whereas the X-ray structure of eEF1A2 is known [
3
,
25
]. Interestingly, various cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM) datasets of eEF1A1 complexes with the ribosome have revealed variability in the conformations of ribosome-bound eEF1A1 [
26
–
28
], highlighting uncertainty regarding the structural arrangement of eEF1A1 even in its ribosome-bound state. Clearly, a deeper understanding of mammalian tissue-specific protein synthesis and the mechanisms underlying the distinct roles of eEF1A1 and eEF1A2 in nontranslational processes requires experimental elucidation of the structural disparities between these paralogous proteins, both in their ribosome-bound and free states. Clarifying the nature and molecular details of potential differences between the paralogs could enhance our understanding of their differential involvement in viral replication, neurodegenerative diseases, and cancer [
7
,
29
,
30
], paving the way for the development of paralog-specific therapies.
Using a combination of various biophysical approaches, we reveal that eEF1A2 exhibits a compact and stable structure compared to the dynamic structural behavior of eEF1A1. Microsecond range MD simulations suggested the more compact conformation of eEF1A2 and the structural heterogeneity of eEF1A1. Hydrogen–deuterium exchange mass spectrometry (HDX-MS) provided detailed, domain-specific mapping of the most significant differences between eEF1A1 and eEF1A2, as well as the structural heterogeneity of eEF1A1. Small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) data indicated that eEF1A1 forms an extended dimer in solution, linked via a D2–D2 interaction, with a portion of monomeric species also present. In contrast, the solution-state structure of eEF1A2 appeared monomeric and adopted a more compact conformation. Thus, we demonstrate that two paralogous proteins with 97% sequence similarity, both playing critical roles in translation, exhibit strikingly different dynamic properties. These differences lead to distinct conformations and capacities for higher-order structure formation, potentially providing a structural basis for their unique macromolecular interaction patterns in translation and beyond.
Materials and methods
Proteins
eEF1A1 and eEF1A2 were purified from rabbit liver and muscle, respectively, using a combination of anion exchange, cation exchange, and hydroxyapatite chromatography, following previously described protocols [
31
,
32
].
MD simulations
The initial conformations of human eEF1A1 and eEF1A2 were obtained using primary sequences from UniProt web server (
https://www.uniprot.org/
); entries
P68104
and
Q05639
, respectively) and model prediction with AlphaFold2 [
33
,
34
]. The protonation state of titratable moieties of eEF1A1 and eEF1A2 were calculated with the H++ web server (
http://newbiophysics.cs.vt.edu/H++/hppdetails.php
) [
35
], and the pH was set to 7.0. Acetyl and amide groups (NH
2
) were used to cap the N- and C- termini of each protein, respectively. Each protein was placed on a cubic box (12 × 12 × 12 nm) and was solvated with TIP3P water (50 878 molecules), 25 mM Tris (26 molecules), 1 mM GDP (1 molecule placed on the GDP binding site), 165 mM NaCl (172 NaCl molecules plus extra 9–10 Cl to neutralize the system charge), 5% glycerol (525 molecules), and 6 mM 2-mercaptoethanol (6 molecules). The parameters for eEF1A and all additional molecules were obtained from the CHARMM-GUI web server (
https://www.charmm-gui.org/
) [
36
,
37
].
All MD simulations were conducted using GROMACS 2021 simulation package [
38
,
39
] and the simulations were performed with the CHARMM36 [
40
,
41
] force field. Default MD simulations parameters for the CHARMM force field were used (
https://manual.gromacs.org/current/user-guide/force-fields.html
). All systems were minimized with the Steepest Descent algorithm for 50 000 steps or until the maximum force was <1000 kJ nm
-1
mol
-1
. Then, each system was equilibrated in NVT (constant temperature and volume) and NPT (constant temperature and pressure) conditions for 100 ps with 2 fs time step. During the NPT equilibration, the Berendsen barostat [
42
] was used to maintain the pressure constant at 1 bar with a time constant of 2 ps and compressibility of 4, 5 × 10
–5
bar
-1
in isotropic pressure coupling conditions, whereas during the production phase, the Parrinello–Rahman barostat [
43
] was used instead. For the short range nonbonded interactions, the Verlet integration scheme [
44
] and a cutoff of 1.2 nm were used. The temperature was kept constant along the simulations at 300 K with the v-rescale algorithm and a time constant of 0.1 ps [
45
]. Two temperature coupling groups were used for the systems, one for the protein and one for the solvent. The Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method was used for long range interactions [
46
], and the LINCS algorithm was used to constrain the bond lengths [
47
]. The production phase for each eEF1A1 and eEF1A2 lasted 2–4 μs, and four replica simulations were performed for each system. We performed additional simulations of eEF1A1, in which the initial conformation of the protein was obtained from different time frames of the original trajectories. The main goal was to increase the sampling of the opened conformations that were explored during the production phase of eEF1A1. The time step of integration during the production phase was 2 fs, and snapshots were saved each 100 ps. In summary, we performed eight trajectories of eEF1A1 and four trajectories of eEF1A2, with total simulation times of 30 and 10 μs, respectively.
HDX-MS measurements
Before HDX reactions, the purified eEF1A1-GDP and eEF1A2-GDP proteins were incubated with excess GTP or GDP to ensure that all protein preparations were in their respective GTP- or GDP-bound forms. As described before [
16
], to achieve complete charging with a nucleotide phosphate eEF1A1 was incubated on ice and eEF1A2 at 25°C for 1 h just before starting the HDX reactions. The following concentrations of elongation factors and nucleotide phosphate were used: 53.3 µM eEF1A1 was combined with 1.5 mM GxP, while 84.3 µM eEF1A2 was combined with 2.45 mM GxP, resulting in nucleotide excesses of 28- or 29-fold, respectively.
HDX reactions of both eEF1A paralogs with GDP and GTP were performed at six time points (10 s, 1 min, 5 min, 25 min, 2 h, 24 h), all repeated in four technical replicates; 5 μl aliquots of proteins were added to 45 μl of deuterated buffer (25 mM Tris–HCl, 150 NaCl, 5 mM 2-mercaptoethanol, containing 1 mM GDP or GTP, respectively, pD 7.6) at 20°C. Final concentrations of proteins and nucleotides in the deuteration reactions were as follows: eEF1A1*GxP (5.35 μM protein, 1.05 mM GxP, nucleotide excess—196 fold) and eEF1A2*GxP (8.43 μM protein, 1.609 mM GxP, nucleotide excess—191 fold).
The H/D exchange reactions were quenched by moving the exchange aliquots to precooled tubes (on ice) containing 10 μl of quenching buffer (2 M glycine, 4 M guanidine hydrochloride, 100 mM Tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP) in 99.99% D
2
O, pD 2.3). After quenching, samples were frozen immediately in liquid nitrogen and kept at −80°C until mass spectrometry (MS) measurement. Samples were thawed directly before measurement and injected manually onto the nano ACQUITY UPLC system equipped with HDX-MS Manager (Waters). Proteins were digested on 2.1 mm × 20 mm columns with immobilized Nepenthesin-2 (AffiPro), for 1.5 min at 20°C and eluted with 0.07% formic acid in water at a flow rate of 200 μl/min. Digested peptides were passed directly to the ACQUITY BEH C18 VanGuard pre-column from which they were eluted onto the reversed-phase ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (Waters) using a 6%–40% gradient of acetonitrile in 0.01% of formic acid at a flow rate of 90 μl/min at 0.5°C. Samples were measured on the SYNAPTG2 HDX-MS instrument (Waters) in IMS mode. The instrument parameters for MS detection were as follows: ESI—positive mode; capillary voltage—3 kV; sampling cone voltage—35 V; extraction cone voltage—3 V; source temperature—80°C; desolvation temperature—175°C; and desolvation gas flow 800 l/h. The eEF1A1 and eEF1A2 peptide lists were obtained using nondeuterated protein samples, processed as described above for HDX experiments, and measured in MS
E
mode. Peptides were identified using ProteinLynx Global Server Software (Waters). Sequence coverage in eEF1A1 is 82% and in eEF1A2 85.3%, similarly for GDP- and GTP-bound forms of the proteins.
The HDX-MS experiment was analyzed using DynamX 3.0 (Waters) software for calculations of the weighted average center mass of isotopic envelopes. The PLGS peptide list was filtered by minimum intensity criteria—3000 and minimal product per amino acid—0.3. All MS spectra were inspected manually. Final data analysis was carried out using the in-house HaDeX software [
48
] with fractional uptake values calculated against maximum theoretical uptake for each peptide. For deconvolution of the complex, split isotopic envelopes, corresponding to the differently exchanging species, an in-house procedure was developed, as described before [
49
]. Differential deuterium exchange of residues and the double envelopes split analysis results were mapped to the models of eEF1A1 and eEF1A2 generated by the AlphaFold2 algorithm [
33
] and visualized using Chimera program (UCSF Chimera)—a visualization system for exploratory research and analysis [
50
].
To study a potential negative effect of intrinsic GTPase activity of eEF1A1/eEF1A2 on the GTP level in HDX-MS experiments, chromatography on a Mono Q column was used. 15 μM eEF1A1 or 15 μM eEF1A2 were incubated with 3mM GTP at 4°C (eEF1A1) or 25°C (eEF1A2) for 60 min to achieve complete charging of the proteins with GTP [
16
]. Second, the mixtures were diluted 10-fold with 25 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5 and incubated for 0, 30, and 150 min at 20°C. Immediately after the end of each incubation time the samples (100 μl) were loaded on the Mono Q column, washed with the 25 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, buffer containing 30 mM NaCl, and eluted with a 30–400 mM NaCl gradient in the same buffer. The peak areas were determined using the Amersham FPLC-system ÄKTA device and calculated with the Unicorn software. At least three independent chromatographic runs at each time point were done for eEF1A1*GTP, eEF1A2*GTP, and free GTP.
SAXS measurements
The measurements of eEF1A1 and eEF1A2 proteins were carried out at a SAXS instrument NanoSTAR (Bruker AXS) optimized for solution scattering with a liquid metal jet source (Excillum). The instrument employs home-build scatterless slits [
51
] and it has an automated sample handler that transfer the samples into a reusable quartz cell, which is used for both sample and background buffers. Concentrations of the proteins were 1.7 mg/ml of eEF1A1 and 1.9 mg/ml of eEF1A2. The data were background subtracted and normalized to absolute scale using the scattering from a pure water sample employing the SUPERSAXS package [
52
]. The distance distribution functions were determined using GNOM from the ATSAS package [
53
].
The initial conformations of eEF1A1 and eEF1A2 obtained from our MD simulations served as templates to determine the three-dimensional domain structure of the proteins through conformational sampling using the BILBOMD approach [
54
]. Energy minimization and high-resolution refinement of the 3D models in a water environment were performed using the Yasara web server (
www.YASARA.org
). The quality of the models was evaluated using the MolProbity web server (
https://phenix-online.org/documentation/reference/molprobity_tool.html
). For trajectory visualization and graphical structure analysis, UCSF Chimera [
55
] and VMD [
56
] software were used. The quality of the fit between the models and the experimental SAXS profiles was calculated using home-written software [
57
] and FoXS [
58
]. The dimers for eEF1A1 were generated using the ClusPro 2.0 web server [
59
] and subsequently optimized with BILBOMD. The MultiFoXS server [
60
] was used for determining the distribution between monomers and dimers by uploading five monomer models and five dimer models. For the home-written software the monomer structure was taken as the monomer in the dimer structures, both structures had a hydration layer added, and the linear combination was fitted to the SAXS data, also optimizing a scale factor for the scattering of the hydration layer.
Results
MD simulation of eEF1A1 and eEF1A2
Even if AlphaFold2 predicts that the structure of eEF1A1 and eEF1A2 is almost identical (Fig.
1C
), existing experimental data suggest some structural differences between eEF1A1 and eEF1A2 [
14
]. To theoretically evaluate these possibilities, we conducted extensive MD simulations using the corresponding models. The RMSD calculations revealed that the majority of eEF1A1 trajectories (replicas 1, 3, and 4) deviated significantly from the protein’s initial conformation, as depicted in Fig.
2A
. Conversely, eEF1A2 trajectories preserved the initial structure (Fig.
2B
). In both cases, the AlphaFold2 predicted conformations of eEF1A1 and eEF1A2 were used as references for the RMSD calculation. Upon visualizing the actual trajectories, we found that conformations with an RMSD >0.6–0.7 nm corresponded to the protein’s opened conformations (Fig.
2C
). This observation strongly suggests that the eEF1A1 structure has a greater propensity to adopt more open conformation compared to eEF1A2. To enhance the sampling of eEF1A1’s opened conformations, we carried out six additional simulations of eEF1A1. Each simulation began at different time points of replica 1 and yielded similar results, as depicted in Fig.
2A
, right panel. Notably, we did not observe any instances of eEF1A1’s structure reclosing within the simulated time scales. The root mean square fluctuations (RMSF) calculations indicate that eEF1A1 is more flexible than eEF1A2 (Fig.
2D
). The sharp rise in the RMSF values of eEF1A1 reflects the conformational changes that are absent in the eEF1A2 simulations. eEF1A2 preserves the closed conformation throughout the trajectories and oscillates around the initial structure. In all simulations, the ends of the protein exhibit high flexibility.

Hydrogen–deuterium exchange measurements of eEF1A1 and eEF1A2
HDX-MS analysis indicates higher stability of eEF1A2 compared to eEF1A1
. HDX involves the isotopic exchange between a protein’s amide hydrogens and deuterium in a deuterated solution. This increases the protein’s mass, measurable by MS, providing insights into the protein’s structure and solvent accessibility. The levels of fractional deuterium uptake in different regions of eEF1A1-GDP and eEF1A2-GDP are shown in the heat-map (Fig.
4A
). Despite sharing over 92% amino acid sequence similarity, the paralogs exhibit significantly different structural dynamics, consistent with MD predictions showing a higher compactness of eEF1A2 compared to eEF1A1. Overlay of exchange levels measured after 1 min of incubation on AlphaFold structures of the paralogs are depicted in Fig.
4B
.

Regions of eEF1A1 affected by split are shown by overlay on its AlphaFold structure (Fig.
5B
). With one notable exception, all these regions localize to domains D2 and D3. The only region affected by split outside these domains is located in positions 139–146 of D1 domain, covering the sequence LAYTLGVK. This region of eEF1A1 shows differing HDX levels in the presence of GDP or GTP (see below). In D2 and D3, four nonsplit regions (291–299, 348–371, 379–395, 428–434) were observed, but these correspond to fast exchanging loops or peripheral regions which may exchange too fast for the split to be revealed. Core regions of both domains D2 and D3 are strongly affected by splitting. The splitting may arise either from the co-existence of alternative protein conformations, each exchanging at different rates due to local structural dynamics (EX2 regime), or from exchange occurring exclusively as a result of global unfolding events [
61
]. In the latter case (EX1 regime), the population divides into two subpopulations: one that has unfolded and exchanged, and another that remains unexchanged. In this scenario, the mass centers of the two envelopes—representing the exchanged and unexchanged subpopulations—should remain stable over the incubation period. However, the abundance of the exchanged form is expected to increase over time, at the expense of the unexchanged form. In contrast, if two structural variants are present in the solution before the exchange starts (EX2 regime), the peptide masses should increase through separate kinetic pathways, with no expected change in the relative abundance of the signals from both forms. Deconvolution of split isotopic envelope into two distinct envelopes was done by using in-house software tool developed for this purpose previously [

Supplementary Fig. S9A
.

The nexus is expected to undergo exchange only upon global unfolding when the D1 and D3 segments become separated. Apparently, the D1–D3 opening episodes are not frequent, i.e. after 25 min <50% of peptides from the EX1 region (see, for instance,
Supplementary Fig. S8F
for one of the D3 peptides) populate the “exchanged” state.
Split regions fulfilling the criteria of two states exchanging via the EX2 regime are shown in Fig.
6B
(yellow rectangle). Peptides from this group all map to D1–D2 linker and domain D2 core β-strands. Thus, this region is characterized by the presence of two alternative structural states.
The unexpectedly high abundance of the ‘fast’ exchanging form in domain D2 (
Supplementary Fig. S8C,D
) indicates that this region populates both a more compact form and a more dynamic one, with the β-sheet forming elements being less firmly fixed in the central conformation of the β-sheet. It means that, over a significant period of time, these elements are open to intermolecular interactions. Exposed to solvent, β-sheet elements of domain D2 may find their binding partners in another molecule which would lead to dimer formation. Consequently, the EX2 regime may reflect the dynamic equilibrium between a monomeric and dimeric forms of eEF1A1.
Thus, our data suggest that the in-solution structure of the eEF1A1 molecule may vary in two distinct ways: first, between a closed monomer conformation, where domains D1 and D3 interact, and an open conformation, where D1 is structurally uncoupled and distanced from D3; and second, between monomeric and dimeric forms, mediated by D2, with monomers also potentially adopting more open or more closed conformations, either free or within the dimer. Both variants are observed simultaneously, providing increased dynamics of the eEF1A1 molecule.

Discussion
In this study, we demonstrated that eEF1A1 exhibits higher structural dynamics compared to eEF1A2 and reveals splitting of the isotopic envelopes during HDX. Such a splitting has been observed previously, although it remains a relatively rare occurrence under native conditions. Examples include the detection of EX1 behavior in a series of twelve SH3 domain variants [
66
], the glycosyltransferase MshA [
67
], and the GFAP protein [
68
]. The latter exhibited mixed EX1 and EX2 kinetics in response to changes in solvent composition, likely due to a weakened dimerization interface, similar to the D2 behavior observed in the eEF1A1 variant described in this study. In the case of SH3 domains, different structural opening half-lives were found, ranging from one second to 1 h [
66
] similar to the slow opening observed here for the eEF1A1 D1–D3 interface. What is particularly noteworthy in our study is the remarkable difference in stability between eEF1A2 which is very stable under native-like conditions, and eEF1A1, despite both being highly homologous paralogs derived from natural sources. Within the eEF1A1 protein, we identified both mechanisms responsible for envelope splitting: the EX1 mechanism, which affects the D1–D3 interface, and the EX2 mechanism, which operates within the D2 domain. Importantly, SAXS data are consistent with the simultaneous presence of both monomeric and dimeric forms of eEF1A1 in solution, with the dimeric form linked through the D2 domain. This contrasts with the compact dimeric configuration of eEF1A2 observed in crystallographic studies [
69
,
70
]. Although SAXS data support the monomeric nature of eEF1A2 in solution, whether compact eEF1A2 dimers can form under specific cellular conditions remains to be determined.
The only minimal differences in HDX patterns between eEF1A1-GDP and eEF1A1-GTP, as well as between eEF1A2-GDP and eEF1A2-GTP, were observed, suggesting that the GDP- and authentic GTP-bound conformations of these proteins are largely similar. This challenges the prevailing view based on essential differences observed in X-ray structures of EF-Tu—the prokaryotic analogue of eEF1A—when bound to GDP versus a nonhydrolyzable GTP analogue [
71
]. Supporting evidence comes from X-ray structures of the yeast eEF1A–eEF1Bα complex, where replacing GDP with a nonhydrolyzable GTP analogue produced only minor structural changes [
72
]. Furthermore, recent studies suggest that in solution, the conformation of EF-Tu may remain unchanged, irrespective of whether a GTP analogue or GDP is bound [
64
]. One may suggest that nucleotide binding to eEF1A does not significantly alter the protein’s overall structure but instead introduces a destabilizing potential that facilitates subsequent conformational changes, which are induced and stabilized by its protein or RNA partners [
4
,
73
,
74
].
The nucleotide change affected the dynamics of the nucleotide-binding domain in eEF1A1 more than in eEF1A2, consistent with the inherently more dynamic structure of eEF1A1 compared to eEF1A2. The nucleotide-binding domain in eEF1A1 is more stable in the presence of GTP, whereas the corresponding domain in eEF1A2 is somewhat more stable in the presence of GDP. The finding aligns with previous data showing that eEF1A1 preferentially binds GTP over GDP, whereas eEF1A2 favors GDP over GTP, although the differences are small [
18
]. Such distinctions may be important for the precise regulation of eEF1A1 and eEF1A2 functions in contexts where both paralogs are present, such as in cancer cells or developing brain neurons [
75
,
76
]. The contrasting dynamics observed in this region between eEF1A1 and eEF1A2 suggests that the increased D1–D3 interface dynamics contributes to the faster factor-free nucleotide exchange observed previously in eEF1A1-GDP compared to eEF1A2-GDP [
16
,
18
]. This suggests that while the distortion of the D1–D3 interface can occur spontaneously in eEF1A1-GDP, in eEF1A2-GDP, the binding of factors that promote guanine–nucleotide exchange primarily triggers it. This data is consistent with the observation that eEF1A2 relies significantly more on nucleotide exchange factors than eEF1A1 [
16
].
Thirty
de novo
mutations of eEF1A2 were found associated with developmental and epileptic encephalopathies [
10
,
77
] while the mechanism of how these mutations, distributed all over the protein, induce common neuropathological effect is not known. HDX-MS analysis reveals that of the 28 mutation-prone residues in eEF1A2 covered by MS, only two are located in nonprotected regions (
Supplementary Table S1
). Notably, one of these, G70S, is positioned immediately adjacent to a highly stable peptide (exhibiting <10% exchange). This proximity suggests that the G70S substitution could also destabilize local protein structure. These findings indicate that
de novo
mutations of eEF1A2 may compromise the stability of structured regions within the protein, potentially reducing its half-life and impairing its functional performance. This mechanism may explain the similar pathological effects of distant mutations, which often occur in regions of eEF1A2 that are not functionally essential.
In conclusion, the reported functional variation between the highly similar translation elongation factor 1 paralogs, eEF1A1 and eEF1A2, is resolved through the discovery of their structural and dynamic differences. We demonstrate that the increased dynamic properties of eEF1A1, compared to eEF1A2, arise from the heterogeneity of its observed structural states. These include the physical decoupling of the D2 domain from the D1–D3 nexus, as well as the opening and closing of the D1–D3 interface, while eEF1A2 remains compact and stable. As a result, eEF1A1 can adopt a dimeric organization formed through D2–D2 interactions, whereas eEF1A2 appears to exist as a monomer in solution. The identification of the molecular basis for the structural dissimilarity between these 97% homologous proteins may underlie their paralog-specific nontranslational functions and distinct roles in the development of human diseases.
