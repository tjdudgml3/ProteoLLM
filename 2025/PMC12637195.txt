1
Introduction
Bipolar disorder (BD) is a psychiatric disorder in which patients experience depressive episodes characterized by symptoms such as depressed mood and diminished energy, along with manic or hypomanic episodes distinguished by hyperexcitability, hyperarousal, increased energy, and decreased need for sleep [
1
]. The worldwide prevalence of BD is 2.4% [
2
]. Additionally, BD is a significant contributor to disability in young individuals. Compared with the general population, the attempted suicide rate in persons with BD is 20–30 times higher [
3
], with the suicide rate reaching 2.6% (1.8% in men and 0.8% in women) [
4
]. BD frequently results in cognitive dysfunction, which significantly affects quality of life. The pathogenic characteristics of BD partly overlap with those of other psychiatric disorders. The first episode of BD may usually manifests as depression, with the duration of depressive episodes throughout the illness significantly exceeding that of manic or hyperactive episodes. Given that the diagnosis of BD typically relies on scales and clinician-conducted interviews, this frequently results in the underdiagnosis of both BD and unipolar depression (UD). The misdiagnosis of UD is prevalent in patients with BD [
5
]. Additionally, there is an issue of overdiagnosis of major depressive disorder (MDD) [
6
]. According to one study, only 65 of 176 (37%) adults discharged from the hospital following their initial manic episode fully recovered in terms of their symptoms and functions after 18 months [
7
]. In the first year of seeking a diagnosis, only 20% of patients who experience depressive episodes of BD receive an accurate diagnosis [
8
], often with a delay of ten years or more between symptom onset and an accurate diagnosis [
5
]. Early recognition and optimal treatment of BD are crucial because the treatment response tends to be more pronounced in the early stages.
Biomarkers can provide patients with objective and reproducible measurements of the objective features of the disease [
9
], thereby facilitating an early and precise diagnosis to achieve accurate and effective medical intervention. Candidate biomarkers of BD can be categorized into genetic, peripheral and neuroimaging [
10
]. Biomarkers aid in establishing objective diagnostic criteria for BD and offer guidance for personalized treatment. Numerous studies have been undertaken to identify BD biomarkers, encompassing genes (e.g.,
POLG
,
OGG1
, and
NDUFV2
complex genes [
11
]), transcription molecules (e.g., long non-coding RNA ROR (lncRNA-ROR) [
12
]), proteins (e.g., interleukin (IL)-4 [
13
]), metabolites (including a biomarker panel consisting of threonine, aspartate, γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA), 2-hydroxybutyric acid, serine, and mannose [
14
]), and neuroimaging studies (e.g., computed tomography of the anterior right cingulate gyrus [
15
]).
In this review, we describe the classification, characterization, and current diagnostic approaches for BD, and summarize the research on BD biomarkers in omics and neuroimaging. This review could help advance the development of biomarkers for BD and pave the way for the accurate identification and diagnosis of BD in clinical settings and personalized medicine.
2
Search method and inclusion criteria
Studies published between 2004 and 2024 (as of August 31) were identified using PubMed. A total of 6,242 studies included the terms “BD” and “diagnosis” in the title or abstract, and 779 studies included “BD” and “biomarkers”. The predetermined inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) research articles rather than reviews, (2) studies on BD, (3) studies aimed at diagnosis or biomarker development, (4) studies related to genes, transcription, proteins, metabolism, and neuroimaging, and (5) English-language publications. Based on these criteria, 110 studies were included. Among them, five were related to genes, 16 to transcription, 23 to proteins, 40 to metabolism, and 26 to neuroimaging.

3.2
Characteristics
BD is clinically characterized by bipolar mood episodes of mania or hypomania and depression. Manic symptoms feature a decreased need for sleep, elevated emotional energy, talkativeness, irritability, and overconfidence [
1
]. Hypomania is milder and shorter in duration than is mania. Manic episodes can lead to high-risk behaviors that may adversely affect, among other, careers, relationships, and property, things [
18
]. Depressive symptoms are characterized by an increased or decreased need for sleep, decreased energy, sadness, lowered self-esteem, and increased or decreased appetite [
19
].

BD I is the most severe manifestation of BD and is characterized by a pronounced elevated mood and manic symptoms. This disorder features abnormal periods of mood elevation, inflation, constant irritation for at least one week, or requirements for hospitalization [
20
]. Individuals experiencing mania typically encounter disruptions in thinking, cognition, and behavior, which are primarily attributable to the illness. Concurrently, behaviors such as a reduced need for sleep, uninhibited socialization, verbosity, and heightened risk-taking tendencies are observed. The individual's reduced need for sleep may lead to such high levels of energy and activity after only two or three h of sleep that they fail to consume adequate food and water, leading to exhaustion. Aggressive behaviors such as reckless driving, excessive alcohol consumption, use of illicit drugs, and gambling exacerbate the burden of BD. People with BD may endanger their lives because of these high-risk activities, but they rarely attempt suicide at this stage. The highest levels of disability and risk in individuals with BD occur during depressive episodes, which constitute the primary phase of the disorder. The depressive symptoms of BD closely resemble those of MDD, including depressed mood and lowered energy. During depressive episodes, patients experience low mood and sadness, which can lead to despair regarding the severity and depth of their illness and result in suicidal ideation. Notably, 79% of suicides occur during depressive episodes [
21
].
BD II is characterized by cyclical mood changes, including at least one major depressive episode and periods of elevated, irritable, and exaggerated mood known as hypomania. In contrast to the markedly elevated mood and disorganization of mania, hypomania exhibits a persistently elevated mood, but is milder and less disorganized. During the hypomanic phase, patients require less sleep; are less easily fatigued; display increased creativity, energy, and productivity; engage in more social activities; and may exhibit risk-taking behaviors. Depressive episodes in BD II resemble those in UD and BD I, last for most its course, and are a major source of distress in this group of patients. Although BD II may appear milder than BD I, it is associated with increased disability and a higher risk of death from suicide. Among individuals with BD, those with BD II have the highest risk for successful suicide [
3
].
4
Biomarkers
Biomarkers are objective indicators of disease states that can be externally measured in an accurate and reproducible manner [
9
] and are not limited to the patient's perceived symptoms of health or disease. They include diagnostic, surveillance, response, predictive, prognostic, safety, and risk biomarkers [
22
]. Biomarkers have wide-ranging applications across various fields, particularly in medicine, where they serve various purposes such as disease diagnosis, treatment selection, and efficacy monitoring [
23
]. Amid the heightened focus on health paradigms and the gradual ascendancy of personalized medicine, biomarkers have emerged as pivotal tools for tailoring individualized treatment regimens, thereby facilitating the realization of precision medicine. Rapid advances in omics and neuroimaging have expanded the scope of research to discover novel biomarkers.

4.1
Potential applications of biomarkers in diagnosis and treatment
Disease development generally comprises three stages: relatively healthy, pre-disease, and disease state. Treatment during the pre-disease state is more likely to result in reversal to a relatively healthy state [
24
]. Biomarkers are robust in distinguishing between the three stages of disease progression, expediting early disease detection, guiding the prescription of tailored interventions for precision medicine, and unraveling the intricacies of disease processes and treatment outcomes.
Clinical diagnostic methods for BD include self-assessment scales and structured interviews with clinicians to assess diagnosis and severity of the disorder. Direct interviews with patients and their family members are crucial for an accurate BD diagnosis, facilitating the discernment of the disorder's longitudinal course, which often diverges from cross-sectional interview results [
25
]. In the current diagnosis of BD, patient accounts heavily influence the diagnosis, and reliance on self-report scales makes the process highly subjective, hindering the accurate assessment of the severity of the disorder.
The reliability of diagnosing BD based on patients' apparent symptoms is controversial, and the absence of objective, precise, and definitive diagnostic biomarkers for pathophysiological processes complicates the accurate diagnosis of BD. Misdiagnosis may result in patients receiving inappropriate or inadequate treatments that not only fail to reduce the burden, symptoms, or impairments associated with the disease, but may also further destabilize the patient's mood. Patients are at risk of future difficulties in terms of employment, socialization, alcoholism, substance abuse, or suicide, which causes significant distress in their educational, occupational, and general lives.
The search for objective biomarkers for BD can not only enhance the accuracy of clinical diagnosis but also inform treatment strategies and prognosis assessment. Additionally, biomarkers help to more precisely differentiate between the BD I and BD II subtypes, enabling targeted and effective therapeutic strategies. Biomarker research provides a direction for elucidating the complex mechanisms underlying BD and offers critical insights for the development of innovative treatment strategies.

Biomarkers of BD
Given that omics employs a holistic approach to analyze the biomolecular composition of biological samples thoroughly, utilizing omics tools is crucial for investigating BD, particularly in endeavors aimed at developing novel diagnostic biomarkers [

In addition, neuroimaging techniques can be used to identify biomarkers of neural circuits that contribute to the diagnosis of BD by identifying abnormalities in white matter connectivity, gray matter, and other neural circuits.
In recent years, peripheral blood, cerebrospinal fluid, genomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, metabolomic, and brain imaging studies involving BD have rapidly increased, and although these studies have improved our understanding of this disorder, biomarkers for BD are not adequately summarized, and reliable biomarkers for BD in clinical practice are lacking. In this review, we provide a comprehensive summary of research pertaining to “bipolar disorder”, “biomarkers”, and “diagnostics”, categorizing them into five distinct groups: genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics, and neuroimaging.

Few studies have validated the obtained candidate biomarkers, while one study may be promising in terms of current application. Schumer et al. [
114
] examined activity and functional connectivity with approach-related emotions for the region-of-interest mask that supports emotion processing and regulation, and assessed lifetime manic/hypomanic and depression risks using Mood Spectrum Self-Report. In the discovery sample (with/without psychiatric diagnoses
n
= 36/78), elastic network regression modeling was used to identify neural variables associated with the risk of mania/hypomania and depression, and multivariate regression modeling was used determined the extent of significant associations between the selected variables and each risk measure. Multivariate regression models were subsequently used to examine whether the associations in the discovery sample were replicated in the two test samples (test sample 1: with/without psychiatric diagnoses
n
= 47/56; test sample 2: with/without psychiatric diagnoses
n
= 19/63). The findings showed that greater bilateral amygdala-left amygdala functional connectivity was associated with greater a risk of mania/hypomania and depression, and a greater bilateral ventral lateral prefrontal cortex-right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex functional connectivity and greater right caudate nucleus inactivation were associated with a greater risk of mania/hypomania and depression, respectively.
The study of biomarkers of BD in neuroimaging still needs to be further developed, with the current research being at a very early stage. One study that included patients who did not receive medication during the study considered the possible influence of medication-related factors [
119
]; this contributed to future studies controlling for confounding factors.
5
Discussion
5.1
Current research status
A review of candidate biomarkers (including omics and neuroimaging) for predicting the diagnosis of BD indicates that the vast majority of biomarkers are in the early stages of development and validation. The current BD biomarker studies have certain limitations.
First, most studies involve a limited sample size and encompass a narrow ethnic and geographic spectrum; thus, the identified biomarkers possess a restricted scope of application and lack clinical relevance and generalizability. Second, numerous confounding factors, including demographic characteristics, severity, patient's psychotropic medication usage, duration, comorbid psychiatric conditions, and diagnostic heterogeneity of mood disorders, could have influenced the study outcomes, potentially leading to biomarkers that inadequately reflect the underlying pathophysiological processes. Further studies are required to validate the currently identified biomarkers, and it is imperative to enroll participants from diverse regions, ethnicities, and age groups to ensure the broad applicability and credibility of these biomarkers for clinical use. Meanwhile, studies should incorporate case-control designs from the outset, with the aim of developing biomarkers for specific purposes (such as assessing susceptibility or predicting disease). Lastly, biomarker development necessitates both internal and external validation phases; however, most studies fail to replicate prior findings, and most biomarkers have yet to successfully undergo internal validation. Hence, it is crucial to employ appropriate methods for biomarker validation, such as cross-validation, resampling techniques, and validation using independent samples.

Although these candidate biomarkers have been validated either internally or externally, further studies are required to determine their clinical applications.
5.2
Trends in biomarker research
Owing to the impracticality of performing brain biopsies in individuals with mental illness, non-invasive and minimally invasive research methods are greatly needed in clinical practice. Neuroimaging is a robust and non-invasive tool that is both safe and reproducible, whereas body fluid collection methods are frequently non-invasive (e.g., urine) or minimally invasive (e.g., blood). Integrating measurement tools, neuroimaging techniques, and other emerging methodologies holds promise for the biological and behavioral differentiation of BD. Utilizing individual or combined strategies may enhance the sensitivity and specificity of biomarkers by integrating biological approaches beyond genetics and neuroimaging, such as transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics. Multi-omics research should be embraced more frequently, as multi-omics integration facilitates the discovery of relevant compounds via quantitative modeling, thereby enabling the targeting of specific biological pathways [
74
]. Thus, multidirectional studies that combine neuroimaging with fluid markers can serve as a powerful means for identifying BD biomarkers in the future.
EVs have emerge as a promising liquid biopsy technique in body fluid analysis. The ability to isolate distinct EV subpopulations by leveraging distinctive surface markers present in biofluids paves the way for gaining molecular insights into diverse tissues and organs [
140
]. One study found that differentially expressed complement proteins derived from extracellular vesicles are superior biomarkers for SCZ compared to their plasma counterparts [
141
], indicating the potential research value of biomarkers within extracellular vesicles. Consequently, the multidirectional integration of neuroimaging and body fluid analyses is anticipated to emerge as a future research trend, and it could represent a pivotal direction in BD diagnostic biomarker research. Non-invasive biomarkers, along with non-invasive or minimally invasive assays, are expected to undergo expansion and clinical utilization, concurrently facilitating the advancement of personalized treatments and novel interventions for BD.
Mendelian randomization (MR), which uses genetic variation to determine whether a causal effect exists between risk factors and outcomes [
142
], can reveal causal relationships between risk and etiological factors of BD [
143
] and is a powerful tool for studying BD. MR studies have identified dysregulated arachidonic acid synthesis [
144
] and oxidative stress [
145
] as potential pathogenic risk factors for BD. However, there is a substantial lack of MR studies on BD, suggesting this as an important direction for the development of BD biomarkers.
Lithium, a mood stabilizer, has proven effective in the treatment of BD [
146
] and the prevention of manic and depressive episodes [
147
]. Lithium is the first-line treatment for BD, and predicting the lithium response can assist in the diagnosis and prediction of BD before treatment and management after treatment, as well as further the understanding of BD [
148
]. A review of lithium response biomarkers in BD showed that no viable biomarkers for predicting lithium response currently exist [
149
]. This suggests that the development of biomarkers to predict lithium response may be relevant to the diagnosis of BD, and that studies to predict lithium reactivity need to be further developed. Furthermore, owing to the heterogeneity of studies, assessing the transdiagnostic nature of biomarkers is important for clinical application [
150
].
6
Conclusion
No diagnostic biomarkers for BD that can be used in a clinical setting exist, and further efforts are required to support research in this area. Multidisciplinary studies combining neuroimaging with fluid markers may be a future research direction, and noninvasive or minimally invasive assays are expected to become a prominent trend in research and application.
