1
Introduction
Neural stem cell (NSC) therapies may be a feasible alternative to pharmacotherapies for improving recovery after various neurodegenerative diseases (Cox
2018
; Weston and Sun
2018
). NSCs have an inherent ability to migrate to sites of pathology in the central nervous system (CNS) (Li et al.
2016
; Rockne et al.
2018
; Gutova et al.
2021
). Intrinsic migration, engraftment, and differentiation of NSCs to sites of brain inflammation promote an environment that fosters cellular regeneration by suppressing inflammation, delivering neurotrophic factors, reducing axonal injury, protecting against neuronal loss, and/or differentiating into mature neural‐lineage brain cells (Acharya, Martirosian, et al.
2015
; Acharya et al.
2014
; Acharya, Rosi, et al.
2015
; Amirbekyan, Adhikarla, et al.
2023
; Metz et al.
2013
). Stem cell‐based therapies currently under investigation for brain injury include NSCs, bone marrow‐derived mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), and umbilical cord‐derived MSCs (Zibara et al.
2019
; Haus et al.
2016
; van Velthoven et al.
2010
; Tajiri et al.
2014
; Donega et al.
2014
; Hong et al.
2011
). Endogenous NSCs have been shown to generate new neurones and glial cells in neurogenic regions of the adult brain, such as the subventricular zone (SVZ) and the dentate gyrus (DG) of the hippocampus (Shear et al.
2011
; Shihabuddin et al.
2000
; Santos et al.
2017
; Kerman et al.
2015
; Marchetto et al.
2010
). Following traumatic brain injury (TBI), increased cell proliferation has been observed in both the SVZ and DG, suggesting an intrinsic capacity of the brain to initiate repair and regeneration through activation of endogenous NSCs (Haus et al.
2016
; Tajiri et al.
2014
; Lemmens and Steinberg
2013
).
We utilize two human fetal NSC lines, LMNSC01 and LMNSC02, both genetically engineered to express the human
L‐MYC
gene, along with their secreted extracellular vesicles (LMNSC‐EVs) (Li et al.
2016
; Nakagawa and Yamanaka
2010
; Nakagawa et al.
2010
). Both lines were derived with appropriate clinical consent and exhibit a normal female karyotype, strong self‐renewal capacity, and the ability to differentiate into neurones, oligodendrocytes, and astrocytes in vitro and in vivo (Li et al.
2016
; Rockne et al.
2018
). LMNSC01 cells have demonstrated stable long‐term engraftment without tumorigenicity in the naïve mouse brain for up to 12 months (Rockne et al.
2018
). Furthermore, LMNSC01 cells migrate to sites of brain injury, suppress neuroinflammatory gene expression, and improve spatial learning and neuroprotection in rodent models of TBI (Gutova et al.
2021
; Haus et al.
2016
; Zhu et al.
2019
). This NSC‐based therapeutic approach represents a promising alternative for the treatment of chronic neurodegenerative conditions (Amirbekyan, Adhikarla, et al.
2023
).
As a novel means of inter‐neuronal communication, EVs appear to play critical roles in many physiological processes such as synapse growth and plasticity (Koh et al.
2020
). EVs released by local neurones and glial cells, such as oligodendrocytes, astrocytes, and microglia, participate in intercellular communication and in maintaining neuronal homeostasis in the nervous system, and can penetrate the blood brain barrier (BBB). EVs modulate and influence nervous system function, including neural development, repair, maintenance, and regeneration (Koh et al.
2020
; De Toro et al.
2015
; Huo et al.
2021
). NSC‐derived extracellular vesicles (EVs) have been shown to promote recovery from chemotherapy‐induced cognitive impairment ('chemobrain') by delivering neurotrophic molecules, modulating mRNA translation in postsynaptic regions, enhancing neuronal survival, improving neurotransmission, and increasing overall neuronal viability (Baulch et al.
2016
; Smith et al.
2020
). However, the clinical translation of NSC‐based therapies has been hindered by limited single‐cell‐level characterization of NSC heterogeneity and an incomplete understanding of how NSC‐derived EVs exert disease‐modifying effects, such as modulating neuroinflammation, enhancing synaptic repair, and promoting neuroregeneration in diverse pathological conditions. Integrating insights from NSC subpopulation diversity with analyses of NSC‐EV cargo offers a powerful approach to identify which cellular subsets contribute to specific EV contents. This strategy can reveal candidate RNAs and proteins within NSCs or their EVs that are relevant to disease pathophysiology or to molecular, structural, and cellular mechanisms of recovery (Smith et al.
2020
; Malik et al.
2014
; Yan et al.
2013
; Efthymiou et al.
2014
).
In this study, we conducted a comprehensive characterization of LMNSC cell lines and their EVs using single‐cell transcriptomics, proteomic profiling, and imaging. These integrated approaches provided detailed insights into the molecular composition and biological functions of both LMNSCs and their secreted EVs, supporting their suitability for clinical application in CNS disorders. We further demonstrated the neuroprotective properties of LMNSC‐EVs using a brain organoid model of methotrexate (MTX)‐induced neurotoxicity in vitro.
Our use of MTX models parallels the known aetiology of “chemobrain,” as MTX induces microglial activation, disrupts oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPCs) and astrocytes, and causes white matter damage, collectively contributing to tri‐glial dysfunction (Monje and Dietrich
2012
). Childhood acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (ALL) is the most common paediatric cancer, representing approximately 25% of all childhood cancers in the United States (Daetwyler et al.
2022
; van der Plas et al.
2021
). Advances in therapy over recent decades have dramatically improved survival rates; however, survivors remain at increased risk for neurocognitive impairments, largely attributable to the neurotoxic effects of MTX, a cornerstone of modern treatment regimens. All children with ALL receive intrathecal MTX as part of CNS prophylaxis to prevent leukemic involvement of the CNS. MTX exposure has been consistently linked to white matter injury and leukoencephalopathy on MRI, as well as to alterations in white matter microstructure and cortical volume (Bansal et al.
2025
; Cheung et al.
2016
). The transcriptomic data offers valuable insights into LMNSC and LMNSC‐EV biology and highlights molecular pathways relevant to neural function and disease, informing future therapeutic strategies for neurological conditions (Eirin et al.
2017
).
2
Methods
2.1
Cell Culture
LMNSC01 and LMNSC02 cells were cultured in T‐175 flasks using NeuroCult NS‐A Basal Medium (Human) (StemCell Technologies, Cat# 05750), supplemented with either NeuroCult NS‐A Proliferation Supplement (Human) (Cat# 05751) or NeuroCult NS‐A Differentiation Supplement (Human) (Cat# 05752). The medium was further supplemented with basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF, 10 ng/mL; Cat# 78003), epidermal growth factor (EGF, 10 ng/mL; Cat# 78006), and heparin (0.2 × 10⁴% w/v; Cat# 07980), and cells were maintained under physiological hypoxia (4% O
2
) at 37°C to prevent oxidative stress and minimize accumulation of mutations, as previously described (Li et al.
2016
; Rockne et al.
2018
). Briefly, fetal brain tissue (12–14 weeks of gestation) from elective abortions was obtained as discarded material through Advanced Bioscience Resources, under a City of Hope Institutional Review Board‐approved protocol (IRB #10079). All procedures involving the isolation and propagation of NSC were conducted in accordance with approved protocols (City of Hope SCRO #11002 and IBC #21043).
2.2
Proliferation and Differentiation of LMNSC01 and LMNSC02
LMNSC01 and LMNSC02 cells at passage 10 were seeded at a density of 9.5 × 10
3
cells/cm
2
in 24‐well plates and cultured under physiological hypoxia (4% O
2
) in either NeuroCult proliferation or differentiation medium. Brightfield imaging was performed on Day 6 using an Olympus IX71 microscope. Following imaging, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and processed for immunocytochemistry (ICC).
For ICC, cells were washed three times for 10 min with 1× TBS containing 0.1% Triton X‐100, then blocked with CAS Block (ThermoFisher, Cat# 008120). Primary antibodies were applied overnight at 4°C. Markers included stem cell markers SOX2 (Chemicon, Cat# ab5603; 1:300) and nestin (Millipore, Cat# ab5922; 1:200); neuronal markers MAP2 (Abcam, Cat# ab11267; 1:300), β‐tubulin III (BioLegend, Cat# 801202; 1:700), and NeuN (Invitrogen, Cat# 702022; 1:300); glial markers GFAP (Abcam, Cat# 53554; 1:500), SOX9 (R&D Systems, Cat# AF3075; 1:100), Olig1 (Invitrogen, Cat# PA5‐21613; 1:500), and Olig2 (Invitrogen, Cat# MA5‐15810; 1:200); and vimentin (Abcam, Cat# ab8069; 1:300).
Secondary antibodies were applied for 2 h at room temperature: Alexa Fluor 488 donkey anti‐rabbit (Invitrogen, Cat# A21206; 1:1000), Alexa Fluor 555 donkey anti‐mouse (Invitrogen, Cat# A21147; 1:1000), and Alexa Fluor 647 donkey anti‐goat (Invitrogen, Cat# A21447; 1:1000). After three additional washes with 1× TBS/0.1% Triton, cells were imaged using a Zeiss LSM900 confocal fluorescence microscope.
2.3
Single‐Cell RNA Sequencing of LMNSC01 and LMNSC02 Cells
LMNSC01 and LMNSC02 cells were dissociated from T75 cell culture flasks and counted, and a targeted recovery of 15,000 cells was calculated for each sample by loading a concentration of approximately 18,000 cells at passage 6 for both lines. Cells were immediately processed with the 10× Genomics Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3’ v3.1 (Dual Index) kit (10× Genomics, Pleasanton, CA). Samples were loaded, cDNA amplified, and a library constructed following the manufacturer's protocol. Library quality control was based on Agilent TapeStation 4200 HS D1000 screentapes (Agilent Technologies, Waldbronn, Germany). The multiplexed library pool was based on Agilent Tapestation 4200 HS D1000 and the Kapa Library Quantification Kit for Illumina platforms (Kapa BioSystems, Boston, MA) and sequenced at shallow depths on Illumina's iSeq 100 v2 flowcell for 28 × 10 × 10 × 90 cycles for estimated reads per cell. After demultiplexing, libraries were normalized based on their reads per cell. Rebalanced pool QC was based on Agilent Tapestation 4200 HS D1000 and Kapa Library Quantification Kit for Illumina platforms and high‐depth sequenced on Illumina's NovaSeq 6000 S4 v1.5 flow cell for 101 × 11 × 11 × 101 cycles.
2.4
Single‐Cell RNA Sequencing Data Analysis
FASTQ files generated from sequencing were analysed using 10× Genomics Cellranger Count (version 7.0) using the 10× default human reference to include introns. After Cellranger analysis, H5AD matrices from Cellranger were loaded into Scanpy for further analysis. All samples were combined into a single adata object and filtered using the following cut‐offs: cells with > 5% mitochondrial genes were removed, cells with < 10% ribosomal genes were removed, cells with > 0.1% haemoglobin genes were removed, and cells with < 200 genes were removed. The included genes were also filtered to include only genes found in more than 10 cells. Doublets were identified and removed using the Scrublet software package. Samples were integrated using SCVI using the 6000 most highly variable genes in the dataset. The SCVI integrated data set was then used for PCA analysis, nearest neighbour calculations, and UMAP generation. Clusters were called using the Leiden algorithm with a resolution of 0.3. Dot plots and UMAPs of known marker genes were then generated to show cell type characteristics of the identified clusters.
2.5
Flow Cytometry and Human Leukocyte Antigen (HLA) Analysis
LMNSC01 at passage 5 and LMNSC02 at passage 13 were stained and analysed for HLA class II protein expression using a previously described protocol (Peton et al.
2024
). Briefly, 2.0×10
5
cells were incubated with a primary antibody targeting HLA Class I or Class II. The following monoclonal antibodies were used to measure HLA expression: w6/32 (HLA Class I, Abcam, Cat# ab22432; 1:200), LGII‐612.14 (HLA Class II, Cell Signaling Technology, Cat# 68258; 1:100), L243 (HLA‐DR BD, Biosciences, Cat# 340857; 1:100), B‐K27 (HLA‐DQ, Cat# Abcam ab47342; 1:100), B7/21 (HLA‐DP, Abcam, Cat# ab20897: 1:100), and IgG1 (Isotype control, Abcam, Cat# ab172730; 1:100). Following a 30 min incubation at 4°C, the cells were centrifuged and washed 3 times in wash buffer (PBS with 2% FBS and 0.1% NaN
3
). The cells were then incubated with FITC‐conjugated anti‐mouse‐IgG secondary antibody (Jackson ImmunoResearch, Cat# 115‐096‐071; 1:200) and incubated for 20 min at 4°C. During this secondary incubation, lymphocyte samples were incubated with anti‐CD19‐PE (BD Biosciences; Cat# 340364; 1:50) to distinguish B‐cells. Following three additional washes with wash buffer, the stained cells were fixed with 2.5% formaldehyde in PBS and 7‐AAD staining solution (BD Biosciences, Cat# BDB559925). Cellular events were distinguished with FACSCanto II (BD Biosciences) using FACS Diva acquisition software (BD BioSciences).
2.6
Media Collection and Preparation for EV Isolation
LMNSC01 and LMNSC02 (cell passages 8 and 14) cells were cultured in T‐175 flasks in proliferation media at 4% O
2
and 37°C. When cells reached 80%–85% confluency, culture medium was replaced with fresh medium in a 30 mL/flask for a 48 h incubation, after which conditioned media was collected for EV isolation. This media was centrifuged at 500 ×
g
for 10 min to pellet any excess debris. Thirty millilitres of supernatant was buffer‐exchanged using a Vivaspin 100 kDa MWCO concentrator (Satorius, Cat# PN‐VS2042) and concentrated to 500 mL of media. EVs were isolated using the Izon‐qEV original column, 70 nm Gen 2 (IZON, Cat# ICO‐70‐12745). Particle counts, RNA concentration, and purity were determined upon EV isolation. Protein concentrations of isolated EVs were analysed using a modified BCA procedure of the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific, Cat# 23225), and values were measured in triplicate.
2.7
EV Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA)
The concentration and overall size distribution of EVs were measured using the NS300 NanoSight instrument (Malvern Panalytical). Isolated EVs were diluted 1:100 in 1 mL of Milli‐Q water and injected into a syringe. The samples were carefully injected into the chamber of the machine and stabilized using a syringe pump module (Harvard Apparatus, Cat# 98‐4730). The injected samples were heated to 25°C to stabilize the analysis. For each sample, three measurements were taken for a 60 s duration with 30 frames captured per second. The screen gain was set to 1, and the camera level was set at 14, depending on how visible the particles were in the instrument software. The number of particles was measured in triplicate and averaged in the NTA software after sample analysis.
2.8
RNA Extraction and Isolation From EVs
RNA was extracted and isolated from EVs using the MiRNeasy Serum/Plasma Kit (Qiagen, Cat# 217814) in accordance with the manufacturer's protocol (50 µL of EV elution was used for RNA isolation and quantification). Purified and isolated RNA samples were eluted in DNase‐RNase free water. The concentration and purity of RNA samples were measured using the NP80 NanoPhotometer instrument (Implen) by NanoDrop. The average A260/A280 ratios were used to determine the purity of the sample and to assess the presence of contaminants such as phenols, carbohydrates, and guanidine. Both RNA concentration and purity were measured in triplicate.
For the purpose of RNA‐seq, EVs were isolated using the exoEasy Maxi Kit (Qiagen, Cat# 76064) according to the manufacturer's protocol. RNA was directly isolated afterwards using the MiRNeasy Serum/Plasma Kit (Qiagen, Cat# 217814) in accordance with the manufacturer's recommendations. Prior to off‐column DNase treatment, RNA concentration was measured using an NP80 NanoPhotometer (Implen). Samples were DNase treated with DNase I digestion and heat inactivation, as described previously (Baulch et al.
2016
). Briefly, DNase I, 10× DNase I reaction buffer, and DNase‐RNase free water were added to the RNA sample, and samples were then incubated at 37°C for 30 min. Afterwards, DNase I was inactivated by adding 25 mM EDTA and reincubating the sample at 75°C for 10 min. DNase I‐treated samples were then cleaned on‐column using a modified protocol of the RNeasy MinElute Cleanup Kit (Qiagen, Cat# 74204), where miRNA was also purified.
2.9
Single Extracellular VEsicle Nanoscopy (SEVEN)
EVs from fresh conditioned cell media were characterized using SEVEN (Saftics et al.
2023
). Briefly, 0.75 µL of cell culture media was diluted to 150 µL in PBS supplemented with 0.025% Tween20. The samples were incubated overnight onto coverslips functionalized with anti‐tetraspanin antibodies (CD9, CD63, and CD81). Captured EVs were stained using anti‐tetraspanin antibodies (CD9, CD63, and CD81) conjugated to fluorescent dye Alexa Fluor 647 (AF647). Samples were fixed and immediately imaged using a Nikon N‐STORM microscope. Material, instrument, and protocol details were previously published (Saftics et al.
2023
). Images were processed using Nikon Elements software and analysed using Voronoi tessellation. For data where all EVs were assessed, logarithmic transformation was applied to obtain close to normal distribution, and then a two‐tailed
t
‐test was used to calculate
p
values. For data where average EV properties per ROI were assessed, Mann‐Whitney tests were used to determine
p
values.
2.10
Proteomic Analysis of NSC‐EVs
Similar to our previous preparations for RNA isolation, 30 mL of either LMNSC01 or LMNSC02 culture media was concentrated to 500 µL using 100 kDa centrifugal filters (Sartorius, Cat# VS2042). EVs were isolated by size exclusion chromatography using qEV 70 nm columns (IZON, Cat# ICO‐70). qEV columns were equilibrated with 15 mL of 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate buffer. 500 µL of concentrated media was passed through the column frit, followed by 15 mL of 50 mM of ammonium bicarbonate buffer. 7–10 fractions were collected, pooled, and further concentrated using 100 kDa centrifugal filters. EVs were lysed by tip sonication in a 2% deoxycholate lysis buffer in 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate containing 1× protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Cat#
PIA32961
, Thermo Scientific). Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 16,000 ×
g
at RT. Protein amount was quantified using the Pierce BCA assay, and 7 µg of protein per sample was processed using an in‐solution trypsin digestion as previously described (DiStefano et al.
2024
). MS data was acquired on the Vanquish Neo liquid chromatography system (ThermoFisher Scientific) coupled to a timsTOF HT mass spectrometer (Bruker). 500 ng peptides per sample were directly loaded on a 25 cm C18 column (PepSep XTREME, 150 µm ID, 1.5 µm particle size) and separated by a 27 min reverse phase chromatography gradient at a flow‐rate of 750 nL/min. MS data were acquired in diaPASEF mode using the following settings: dia‐PASEF scans over a mass range of 228.4–1178.4 m/z and mobility range (1/
K
0
) of 0.7–1.3 with a cycle time of 1.13 s and ramp time and accumulation time of 120 ms. The capillary voltage was set at 1600 V and the dry temperature at 180°C.
Raw spectra were searched in Spectronaut (v19.5) using directDIA against the human protein database (UP000005640, Swissprot). Protein digestion was set to trypsin and allowed for a maximum of 2 missed cleavages. Both the proteins and peptides were filtered for a false discovery rate (FDR) of < 1% for high‐confidence identifications. Methionine oxidation was set as a variable modification, and cysteine carbamidomethyl was set as a fixed modification. Cross‐run normalization and data imputation were turned off, while the remaining settings were kept at default in Spectronaut.
Protein abundances were normalized using variance stabilizing normalization (VSN) in R (v4.4.0). Proteins detected in at least 50% of the samples were subjected to unsupervised hierarchical clustering using Euclidean distance, implemented with the R package
pheatmap
. Statistical significance between LMNSC01‐EVs and LMNSC02‐EVs was assessed using Welch's
t
‐test, followed by Benjamini–Hochberg correction to control the FDR. Differential expression was considered significant at a
q
‐value < 0.05 and an absolute log
2
(fold change) > 1. Subsequently, gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed using HGNC gene symbols in the ToppFun module of ToppGene (Chen et al.
2009
), queried against the human reference database. The top 10 enriched biological process terms were selected based on FDR‐adjusted
p
values (Chen et al.
2009
; Yu et al.
2012
). The complete list of identified proteins in LMNSC01 and LMNSC02 was then compared to a previous study, which identified proteins in EVs from human iPSC‐derived NSCs (Upadhya et al.
2020
). Proteins unique to LMNSC01 and LMNS02 EVs, as well as those unique to the Upadhya EV dataset, were analysed using the enrichGO function from the clusterProfiler package in R. HGNC gene symbols were used as input, with annotations based on the human gene database (org.Hs.eg.db). The analysis applied a
p
value cutoff of 0.05, a Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple testing, and a
q
‐value cutoff of 0.05 (Lennon et al.
2019
; Leskoske et al.
2022
; Thieme et al.
2023
; Carna et al.
2023
). Proteins with a
q
‐value < 0.05 were selected for functional analysis using ingenuity pathway analysis (IPA) with the ingenuity knowledge base (2025) as the reference. Analyses were restricted to human proteins and filtered by tissue and cell line settings limited to tissues and primary cells. Log
2
fold‐change values were used to compute activation
z
‐scores. Canonical pathways were considered significantly enriched if they had a
p
value < 0.05 and an absolute
z
‐score greater than zero.
2.11
RNA‐Seq Analysis of NSC‐EVs
Each EV sample, derived from the same LMNSC01 (p5 and p19) and LMNSC02 (p9 and p20) batches that were used for scRNA sequencing, was brought up to 500 µL with ultrapure, distilled, DNase‐RNase free water prior to isolation. RNA was isolated from EVs using the Plasma/Serum Circulating and Exosomal RNA Purification Kit with on‐column DNase treatment (Norgen Biotek, Cat# 51000). The RiboGreen assay was utilized to determine RNA concentration (Invitrogen); High Sensitivity RNA ScreenTape was used to measure RNA quality (Agilent). Due to an excess amount of DNA found after genomic DNA tapestation, an off‐column DNase treatment was completed with the RNA Clean & Concentrator‐5 (Zymo Research, Cat# R1015).
Sequencing libraries were generated from RNA using the SMARTer Stranded Total RNA‐Seq Kit v3‐Pico Input Mammalian (Takara). Ribosomal RNA was depleted using Takara's ribogone technology, and single‐stranded DNA was generated with their template switching mechanism without the need for adapter ligation. cDNA fragments were purified using AMPure XP beads and then amplified using PCR. Libraries were characterized using D1000 high‐sensitivity screen tapes (Agilent) and quantified using qRT‐PCR (Roche, Cat# KK4824P). Equimolar pools were generated, quantified (Roche, Cat# KK4824P), and sequenced on the Novaseq6000.
Bulk RNA sequencing (RNA‐seq) data for LMNSC01 and LMNSC02 EVs were analysed using Illumina Partek Flow v12.1.0 bioinformatics software. Data was imported into Partek Flow, following processing according to the TruSeq Stranded Total RNA Library Prep Kit with Ribo‐Zero globin protocol. Pre‐alignment quality control (QA/QC) assessed the quality of raw reads, which were then mapped to the Homo sapiens genome (hg38) using STAR v2.7.8a, followed by transcript quantification and annotation based on Ensemble release v102.
Data normalization and differential gene expression (DGE) analysis were performed using DESeq2, identifying significantly differentially expressed genes (DEG) with |FC| > 1.5 and
p
value < 0.05. This analysis facilitated the generation of hierarchical clustering heatmaps, gene set enrichment, and pathway analysis. Qiagen IPA was conducted to investigate interactions among DEGs and identify enriched canonical pathways, calculated against a background gene set relevant to this study.
2.12
Single‐Cell RNA‐Seq Analysis of LMNSC01 and LMNSC02 NSCs
Single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐seq) data from LMNSC01 and LMNSC02 cell lines (two replicates each) were analysed using Illumina Partek Flow v12.1.0. Data processing followed the Illumina Single Cell 3′ v3 protocol, beginning with pre‐alignment quality control (QC) to assess overall cell quality. Quality filtering criteria included mitochondrial read content > 50%, dominant gene expression > 80%, and feature counts (low ≤ 250; high > 5000). Features with values ≤ 1.0 in ≥ 99.9% of cells were excluded from further analysis.
Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using 100 principal components with variance‐based feature contribution. Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) was then applied using the following parameters: neighbourhood size = 30, minimum distance = 0.3, distance metric = cosine, number of iterations = 0, random seed = 42, initialization = random, and input = 20 principal components. Normalization was performed using log
2
(CPM + 1) transformation.
DGE analysis was conducted using ANOVA, identifying genes with fold change (|FC|) > 2 and FDR < 0.001 between LMNSC01 and LMNSC02 lines. These results supported downstream analyses, including hierarchical clustering, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), and pathway analysis. Ingenuity pathway analysis (IPA; Qiagen) was also performed to explore DEGs and associated pathway networks, with enrichment scores calculated against a relevant background gene set.
2.13
LMNSC01 and LMNSC02 Organoids and Methotrexate Treatment
To generate organoids ('mini brains') from LMNSC cells, 83,000 LMNSC01 or LMNSC02 cells were resuspended in 25 µL of NeuroCult medium supplemented with EGF and FGF, then mixed with 500 µL of cold Matrigel (Ojeda et al.
2025
). Using a repeater pipette, 6 µL droplets were dispensed onto Parafilm squares placed in each well of a 6‐well plate. Droplets were incubated under hypoxic conditions (4% O
2
) for 3 h to allow gelation, then overlaid with NeuroCult medium. After 3 days, organoids were transferred to an orbital shaker set to 100 rpm. The media was changed weekly for the first 3 weeks and twice weekly thereafter.
On Day 45, organoids were treated with MTX at concentrations of 1 µM and 2 µM for 72 h to induce neurotoxicity. The in vitro MTX treatment protocol was adapted from Gibson et al., where MTX was used to activate microglia (Gibson et al.
2019
). MTX doses were experimentally optimized to induce damage across all three glial populations (neural, glial, OPC) in LMNSC organoids, as described previously (Gibson et al.
2019
; Gibson and Monje
2019
). Following MTX exposure, EVs derived from LMNSC01 or LMNSC02 cells were applied at 2 µg/mL for 24 h to the corresponding LMNSC01‐ or LMNSC02‐derived organoids. The therapeutic EV dose had been optimized in prior experiments. Organoids were harvested for immunofluorescence and flow cytometry analysis. On Day 49, organoids were fixed, washed, and blocked, then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. After additional washes, they were incubated with secondary antibodies and Hoechst stain overnight, followed by mounting with ProLong Gold Antifade Mountant. Imaging was performed using a Zeiss LSM‐900 confocal microscope and analysed with Zen software. Immunostaining included β‐tubulin III (1:500; primary) and Alexa Fluor 555 donkey anti‐mouse (1:1000; secondary).
2.14
Flow Cytometry of NSC Organoids
Organoids were harvested, transferred to tubes, and dissociated using enzyme, dispersion, and isolation solutions with gentle pipetting and brief incubations at 37°C. The resulting single‐cell suspensions were stained with Zombie Violet 450 to assess viability, then fixed, permeabilized, and stained with the following antibodies: NeuN PE, MAP2 AF610, β‐tubulin III PE‐Cy7, GFAP PerCP‐Cy5.5, O1 AF488, and SOX2 AF647. After staining, cells were washed and resuspended in FACS buffer prior to flow cytometric analysis using an Attune Flow Cytometer.
Data were analysed with FCS Express 7 software. Gating strategies included exclusion of debris, identification of singlets, and selection of viable cells. Mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was calculated for each marker, and the proportion of NeuN/β‐tubulin III double‐positive cells was quantified. Group comparisons of MFI values were performed using one‐way ANOVA.
3
Results
3.1
LMNSC01 and LMNSC02 Cell Proliferation and Differentiation In Vitro
We first assessed the ability of two donor‐derived foetal NSC lines immortalized with the L‐Myc gene, LMNSC01 and LMNSC02, to self‐renew and proliferate in in vitro cultures. Both plated NSC cell lines promptly adhered within 24 h after culturing in proliferation media. Initial seeding density was 10
4
/cm
2
, and NSCs remained viable for up to 6 days. After 24 h, cells were grown in either proliferation or differentiation media for 6 days. To visualize cell morphology and differentiation, we performed ICC analysis for NSC, neural, and glial markers after 6 days in proliferation or differentiation medium. Both LMNSC01 and LMNSC02 cells were stained for NSC markers (SOX2 and nestin), neural markers (β‐tubulin, MAP2 and NeuN), glial (GFAP), vimentin, OPC (OLIG1), and SOX9 biomarkers (Figure
1
and Figure
S1
). LMNSC01 differentiated on Day 6 with a robust increase in expression of neural markers (β‐tubulin III) (a2), while glial (GFAP) and oligodendrocyte markers (OLIG1) were minimally expressed (Figure
1a3,a4
). LMNSC01 cells were positive for vimentin on Day 6 in differentiation media (Figure
S1, a2
). LMNSC01 cells in proliferation media on Day 6 mostly expressed nestin (Figure
1a5
), GFAP, and Olig1 (Figure
1a7,a8
). LMNSC02 cells differentiated into neural lineage (Figure
1b2
), glial GFAP (Figure
1b3
), oligodendrocytes OLIG1 (Figure
1b4
), and vimentin (Figure
S1b2
). LMNSC02 cells in proliferation media were similar to LMNSC01 cells, expressing nestin (Figure
1a5,b5
) and SOX9 (Figure
S1a7,b7
). In summary, LMNSC01 and LMNSC02 samples showed similar differentiation and proliferation patterns, with LMNSC01 cells mostly differentiating into neural cell types and LMNSC02 differentiating into neural, glial, and OPCs.

3.2
Single‐Cell RNA‐Seq Analysis of LMNSC01 and LMNSC02 NSCs
To characterize cell populations and gene expression profiles of LMNSC cells, we conducted scRNA‐seq analysis. Our analysis revealed that both LMNSC01 and LMNSC02 cell lines (passages 6–9) were composed of cell populations consistently expressing NSC markers such as SOX2 and nestin, along with differentiation markers such as doublecortin (DCX) and vimentin. Notably, LMNSC01 cells were enriched in markers characteristic of excitatory neurones and some glial cells, indicating their potential for predominantly neural differentiation. In contrast, LMNSC02 cells exhibited enrichment for markers associated with inhibitory neurones, glial cells, and OPCs, suggesting distinct trilineage differentiation capacities of LMNSC02 cells. Based on our scRNA‐seq data, cell populations were assigned to eight distinct clusters, each representing specific neural cell types and states, highlighting the diversity within these lines (Figure
2A
). Cluster 1 predominantly contained mature astrocytes involved in CNS homeostasis and inflammation response, while Cluster 2 comprised a mixed population of astrocytes and OPCs, indicating transitional states between these lineages. Clusters 3 and 4 were enriched with neural progenitor cells (NPCs) and NSCs, displaying high proliferative activity and early lineage commitment markers. Cluster 5 presented a heterogeneous population of MSCs, NSCs, and reactive astrocytes, indicative of roles in neuroprotection and tissue repair. Clusters 6 through 8 contained neural crest‐derived cells, differentiated neurones, and neurone‐specific populations at various stages of development, highlighting differentiation potential and signalling activity within the neural microenvironment.

Our analysis revealed that LMNSC02 predominantly expresses clusters 1 and 5, with limited representation of clusters 7 and 4, highlighting its neural and astrocytic profiles (Figure
2A
, right). In contrast, LMNSC01 showed a broader and distinct expression pattern across clusters 2, 3, 4, 6, 7, and 8, including NSCs, OPCs, and differentiated neurones. ).
The heatmap of selected genes (Figure
2B
) illustrates transcriptional differences between LMNSC01 and LMNSC02 cell lines. Differential analysis highlighted distinct gene expression profiles, with LMNSC02 showing elevated levels of astrocytic and neuronal markers, and LMNSC01 exhibiting higher expression of genes associated with NSCs, OPCs, and differentiated neurones. These transcriptional differences underscore the heterogeneity and lineage potential of each cell line. This differential expression provides valuable insights into the cellular and molecular characteristics of each line.
GSEA and selected neuro‐related IPA revealed differences in selected pathways such as regulation of neurogenesis, neurone development, and differentiation, underscoring the transcriptional diversity and potential of these cell lines for studying neural differentiation and CNS injury responses (Figure
2C, D
). A closer examination of separate populations within these lines showed dynamic transcription factor expression patterns modulated by both systemic and local signals, controlling the quiescence, proliferation, and differentiation states of NSCs (Figure
S2A–C
). Both LMNSC01 and LMNSC02 express core neural stem or progenitor markers such as SOX2, NES, and DCX, indicating maintenance of a neural progenitor identity (Figure
S2A
). Differential patterns are observed for lineage‐associated markers: LMNSC01 shows stronger representation of SATB2, GAD1, RNF220, and SMOC1, suggesting neural lineage differentiation, including some astrocytic and oligodendrocyte populations (CNP, PDGFRA). LMNSC02 cells exhibit higher expression of NRGN, RBFOX3 (NeuN), and GAD1/2, genes associated with neuronal differentiation, including GABAergic identity. Additional genes such as SMOC1, PDGFRA, and SATB2 are differentially expressed and provide further insight into regional and subtype specification (Figure
S2
). Interestingly, single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐seq) analysis revealed that MKI67 (1.5‐fold) and SOX2 (1.3‐fold) were upregulated, while SOX9 (–1.1‐fold) and PCNA (–1.2‐fold) were downregulated in LMNSC02 cells compared to LMNSC01 cells, indicating differences in proliferation rate for LMNSC01 and LMNSC02 lines. These findings indicate that while both LMNSC lines retain stemness, they exhibit distinct molecular signatures reflective of divergent lineage potential and subtype bias, which may underlie functional differences in therapeutic applications.

3.6
Proteomics Analysis of LMNSC01‐ and LMNSC02‐EVs
To characterize the NSC‐EV proteome, we employed Data Independent Analysis Mass Spectrometry (DIA‐MS). LMNSC01 and LMNSC02 cell lines were cultured in different batches, producing five biological replicates per cell line (
n
= 5). DIA‐MS was then utilized to profile the EV cargo proteomes, offering comprehensive and high‐resolution analysis of protein content. This approach provided detailed insights into the unique proteomic profiles of the EVs derived from LMNSC01 and LMNSC02. The analysis revealed key proteins and pathways specific to each NSC‐EV type, enhancing our understanding of their distinct therapeutic potentials and how the EVs could contribute to neurological repair and neuroprotection.
Unsupervised clustering of proteomics data showed EV proteomes for LMNSC01 and LMNSC02 clustered by their parental cell line (Figure
5A
). We identified 2,207 proteins, with 92% of the identified proteins being shared between the EVs from the two cell lines (Figure
S5
). Only 34 and 13 proteins were uniquely identified by LMNSC01‐EVs and LMNSC02‐EVs, respectively (Figure
S5
).

3.7
Bulk RNA Sequencing of NSC‐EVs
To characterize the RNA content of EVs, we performed RNA‐seq on EVs isolated from LMNSC01 and LMNSC02 cell lines. This analysis provided detailed information on the mRNA and non‐coding RNA composition within the EVs. Next, we compared distinct transcriptional profiles of EVs from LMNSC01 and LMNSC02, revealing donor‐specific variations in DGE (Figure
6A
). These differences highlighted unique RNA cargo signatures associated with each NSC line, which could play a role in their respective therapeutic mechanisms. The variations in gene expression profiles emphasize the importance of understanding donor‐specific influences when considering EV‐based therapies for neurological applications (Chatterjee et al.
2024
). The volcano plot and heatmap illustrate significant transcriptional differences in EVs derived from LMNSC01 and LMNSC02 cell lines, highlighting donor‐specific heterogeneity (Figure
6A,B
and Figure
S11
). Genes such as CDDC9B, ORC3, ARHGAP11A, and KLF7 are highly expressed in LMNSC02 EVs, suggesting that these vesicles play distinct roles in cell cycle regulation and signalling pathways. Other genes, like NACA2, RASA1, and KRT15, also show increased expression in LMNSC02 NSCs, potentially indicating differences in cellular structure and vesicle formation processes. Additionally, the upregulation of TGFB2, SLC25A4, and NRP1 in LMNSC02 EVs points to variations in extracellular matrix interactions and metabolic functions between the two cell lines. This DGE profile underscores the functional diversity of EVs and suggests that the LMNSC01 and LMNSC02 lines may produce EVs with unique therapeutic and biological properties, influenced by their donor‐specific transcriptional signatures.

3.8
Analysis of DGE in LMNSCs and Their EVs
To investigate the gene expression profiles of LMNSC cell lines and their EV cargo, we conducted a comparative analysis between scRNA‐seq of LMNSC cells and bulk RNA‐seq of LMNSC‐derived EVs. This approach enabled us to identify both overlapping and distinct molecular components found within EVs released by LMNSC cells (Figure
7A
). The scRNA‐seq of LMNSC cell lines identified 8,880 gene and bulk RNA‐seq of LMNSC EVs identified 15,834 genes. Notably, 8,222 genes were shared among genomic datasets, suggesting a core set of molecular components present in both the cells and their EVs. Additionally, distinct elements were identified in each dataset: 658 genes were unique to the scRNA‐seq of LMNSCs, and 7,612 genes were unique to the bulk RNA‐seq of EVs.

NSC Organoids Demonstrate Cellular Recovery Post MTX Exposure Following Treatment With LMNSC‐EVs
To investigate the neuroprotective role of LMNSC‐EVs, we employed a 3D organoid model generated from LMNSC01 and LMNSC02 lines, cultured for 45 days under standardized conditions (Ojeda et al.
2025
). To quantify the cellular response to MTX and assess EV‐mediated recovery, immunohistochemistry (IHC) and flow cytometry analysis were performed, focusing on the expression of neural (Tuj1, MAP2, NeuN), astrocytic (GFAP), and oligodendrocytic (O1) markers (Figure
8A–I
). IHC analysis revealed a substantial reduction in neuronal (Tuj1+) cell populations following MTX exposure (Figure
8A–D
), confirming the neurotoxicity of MTX therapy on the brain tissue (Gibson et al.
2019
) (Figure
8E–G
). Notably, post‐MTX treatment, replacing media containing LMNSC01 or LMNSC02 EVs resulted in marked restoration of neuronal populations, with increased staining for neuronal differentiation markers Tuj1 and MAP2, indicative of neuroprotective effects (Figure
8E–I
and Figure
S7
).

). However, treatment with LMNSC01‐EVs post‐MTX exposure facilitated a notable recovery in SOX2, GFAP, TUJ1, and MAP2 populations, with expression levels of these markers nearly reaching those observed in untreated control organoids (Figure
8E,F
and Figure

). High dose MTX (HD‐MTX) exposure causes glial damage, particularly to OPCs, which cannot be rescued by LMNSC01‐EVs (Figure

). The lack of rescue in the oligodendroglial lineage likely reflects the high vulnerability of OPCs to MTX, as previously described by Gibson et al. (2019). Longer incubation periods or repeated rounds of EV treatment may be required to promote recovery in this population. Data from LMNSC01 organoids treated with LMNSC01‐EVs are presented in Figure

and Figure

, while LMNSC02 organoid data treated with their respective EVs are shown in Figures

and

. Notably, LMNSC02 organoids exhibited MTX‐induced neuronal (NeuN) loss that was rescued by LMNSC02‐EVs (Figure

). Together, these findings suggest that EVs derived from LMNSC01 and LMNSC02 provide substantial neuroprotection and enhance cellular recovery in MTX‐induced toxicity within in vitro organoid models. The capacity of LMNSC‐EVs to mitigate MTX‐induced reductions in neuronal, astrocytic, and oligodendrocyte markers highlights their therapeutic potential in facilitating recovery in models of MTX‐induced brain toxicity.
4
Discussion
Human NSCs are attractive candidates to help restore brain function through neuroprotection or cell replacement after brain trauma (Gutova et al.
2021
; Hade et al.
2021
). Our working hypothesis is that NSCs, through their intrinsic ability to migrate toward sites of brain inflammation and injury, promote a reparative microenvironment that facilitates tissue regeneration and functional recovery. Upon reaching damaged brain regions, NSCs are thought to exert multiple therapeutic effects through both cell‐intrinsic and paracrine mechanisms. These include the secretion of neurotrophic and growth factors (BDNF, GDNF, VEGF) that support neuronal survival, synaptic plasticity, and angiogenesis; the modulation of neuroinflammatory responses by downregulating pro‐inflammatory cytokines and upregulating anti‐inflammatory mediators; and the attenuation of secondary injury processes such as axonal degeneration and excitotoxicity (Hering and Shetty
2023
).
In addition to these neuroprotective actions, NSCs possess the capacity to differentiate into various mature neural lineage cells, including neurones, astrocytes, and oligodendrocytes, contributing directly to the replacement of lost or dysfunctional cells (Kline et al.
2016
). Furthermore, NSCs may influence the endogenous repair response by recruiting resident progenitor cells and enhancing neurogenesis in neurogenic niches such as the SVZ and hippocampal dentate gyrus (Kodali et al.
2019
; Leavitt et al.
2020
). Collectively, these multifaceted mechanisms suggest that NSC‐based therapies not only serve as cellular replacement strategies but also function as dynamic modulators of the brain regenerative landscape, offering a promising approach for treating diverse neurological disorders and injuries.
Supporting the feasibility of NSCs as a potential treatment option for CRCI, NSCs are currently being evaluated in clinical trials for repair of damage associated with stroke and multiple sclerosis (Gutova et al.
2021
; Kalladka et al.
2016
; Hou et al.
2016
; Fauzi et al.
2016
; Duncan et al.
2015
; Chau et al.
2014
; Bowden et al.
2013
; Al Fauzi et al.
2017
; Michailidou et al.
2014
; Meamar et al.
2016
; Harris and Sadiq
2015
; Amirbekyan, Cheng, et al.
2023
). Human NSC grafts have also been assessed as a cellular therapy for cell replacement in Pelizaeus–Merzbacher disease, a rare CNS leukodystrophy, and other neurodegenerative diseases (Marchetto et al.
2010
; Osorio et al.
2017
; Winner et al.
2011
; Vishwakarma et al.
2014
; Tuszynski et al.
1990
; Perry et al.
2010
; Lee et al.
2007
; Latchney and Eisch
2012
; Gincberg et al.
2012
; Feng and Gao
2012
; Bjorklund et al.
1983
). Allogeneic NSCs derived from human foetal brain tissue and immortalized using the C‐MYC gene were also successfully tested in a clinical trial for use after stroke, demonstrating initial safety in humans and a potential therapeutic effect (Reneuron Limited, UK;
NCT02117635
) (Kalladka et al.
2016
; Giorgetti et al.
2012
; Stroemer et al.
2009
; Katare et al.
2014
; Pollock et al.
2006
).
However, a major obstacle to the clinical translation of NSC‐based therapy is the lack of comprehensive functional characterization of NSCs and their EVs and their downstream targeted pathways that drive therapeutic benefit (Gutova et al.
2010
,
2008
,
2017
,
2015
). Our findings begin to address this gap by integrating scRNA‐seq, bulk RNA‐seq of EVs, and proteomic analyses to define the molecular signatures of two human NSC lines and their secreted vesicles. These data reveal both shared and line‐specific transcriptional and proteomic programmes, highlighting biological distinctiveness at the cellular level while demonstrating convergence at the level of EV cargo. Our proteomics data shows that LMNSC EVs are enriched for proteins involved in regulation of neuronal development and differentiation (Figure
6C
), which agrees with previous studies that have shown EVs to be actively involved in the process of neurogenesis (Ma et al.
2019
; Stronati et al.
2019
). EVs are also known to be actively involved in the body's immune response. Our proteomics and transcriptomics data showed an increase in IL‐4 signalling (Figure
7B
and
D
) that has been shown to alleviate neuroinflammation in a mouse model of multiple sclerosis (Casella et al.
2018
). Casella et al. showed that microglia‐derived EVs loaded with IL‐4 in the EV cargo and lactadherin on the surface can significantly reduce tissue damage in neuroinflammatory diseases. There is generally no linear or one‐to‐one correlation between mRNA and protein cargo in EVs; selective and functional molecular enrichment dominates over identity matching (Eirin et al.
2017
; Chen et al.
2022
).
Importantly, functional testing in brain organoid models of MTX‐induced toxicity confirmed the neuroprotective potential of LMNSC‐EVs, with evidence for lineage‐specific responses in neurones, astrocytes, and OPCs. Our proposed use of MTX models for our studies parallels known aetiologies of chemobrain, in that MTX causes microglial activation, disruption of OPCs and astrocytes, and white matter damage (tri‐glial dysfunction) (Rockne et al.
2018
). While other chemotherapies elicit elevated cytokine levels and oxidative stress, these may originate in microglial activation and therefore fall within our focus on creating a system of stem cell therapies alleviating chronic tri‐glia activation and disrupted neural function. The most prominent symptoms of microglial dysregulation arise following treatment with high doses of MTX (Kerman et al.
2015
; Bansal et al.
2025
). Given its broad clinical application and the ability of our proposed model to recapitulate human chemobrain characterized by tri‐glial dysfunction and white matter pathology, the MTX model represents an appropriate and translationally relevant system. The lack of rescue in the OPC lineage likely reflects the high vulnerability of OPCs to MTX, as described by Gibson et al. (
2019
). Longer incubation periods with LMNSC‐EVs or additional rounds of EV treatment may be required to promote recovery within this population. Together, these results provide a mechanistic framework linking NSC‐EV molecular profiles with functional outcomes and suggest that therapeutic optimization will require matching specific NSC lines or EV preparations to disease contexts. This approach advances the translational potential of NSC‐based strategies and supports the development of targeted EV therapies for CRCI and other neurological disorders.
In conclusion, our study supports the potential of LMNSC cells and their derived EVs as therapeutic agents for MTX‐induced CRCI and broader CNS injury applications. As our understanding of EV biology advances, LMNSC‐EVs have the potential to emerge as a viable modality for neurodegeneration, offering a versatile approach to treating a wide range of CNS disorders. Further investigation is essential to establish the therapeutic efficacy, safety, and practical application of LMNSC‐EVs in various preclinical studies that will pave the way for translation to clinical settings.
