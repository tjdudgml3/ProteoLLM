INTRODUCTION
Male infertility, affecting approximately 7% of men worldwide and accounting for approximately 50% of infertility cases in couples, is a complex biological issue.
1
2
Asthenoteratozoospermia, clinically defined by the co-occurrence of suboptimal sperm motility (<32% progressively motile sperm) and impaired morphology (<4% normal forms), is a leading cause of male infertility.
3
4
Morphological aberrations can be systematically categorized into three primary domains based on structural defects: primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD), oligo- or teratozoospermia, and multiple morphological abnormalities of the sperm flagella (MMAF). PCD is a genetically heterogeneous motile ciliopathy
5
that manifests clinically as impaired mucociliary clearance, leading to recurrent sinopulmonary infections (including bronchiectasis and chronic rhinosinusitis), conductive hearing loss, and neonatal respiratory distress requiring intensive care.
6
Most PCD-causing variants are found in genes encoding the structural components of axonemal dynein complexes, including outer dynein arms (ODA), inner dynein arms (IDA), and cytoplasmic preassembly machinery dynein axonemal assembly factors (DNAAFs) essential for dynein arm formation. Studies indicate that only 50%–75% of individuals with clinically diagnosed PCD receive a confirmed genetic diagnosis.
7
8
Pathogenic variants in cilia-related genes are increasingly recognized as significant contributors to male infertility, particularly in cases of asthenospermia or sperm structural defects. Although various mutations have been identified in male patients with infertility, further exploration of additional causative genes and their underlying mechanisms is required.
CFAP300
, known as cilia- and flagella-associated protein 300, is encoded by the
C11orf70
gene and contributes to the assembly of ODA and/or IDA in axonemal cross-sections.
3
9
10
11
12
13
14
Mutations in
CFAP300
may affect the transfer of these complexes to motile cilia and chaperone-mediated preassembly of dynein arms,
15
similar to dynein axonemal assembly factor 2 (
DNAAF2
/
KTU
) mutations.
16
17
Additionally, CFAP300 interacts with the cytoplasmic ODA/IDA assembly factor DNAAF2, demonstrating its role as a preassembly factor in PCD etiology.
10
Current studies consistently report complete sperm immobility (asthenozoospermia) in affected individuals.
12
18
However, systematic investigations into ultrastructural sperm defects associated with this genetic lesion are lacking. The mechanistic link between
CFAP300
mutations and aberrant sperm motility or ultrastructural flagellar defects remains poorly understood.
In this study, we identified a novel homozygous loss-of-function mutation in
CFAP300
in a male patient diagnosed with PCD and severe asthenozoospermia. The proband exhibited numerous sperm abnormalities and acrosomal malformations, along with completely immobilized sperm. To elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which
CFAP300
regulates spermatogenesis, tandem mass tag (TMT)-based quantitative proteomic profiling of patient-derived sperm samples was performed. The results revealed that
CFAP300
deficiency significantly downregulated essential spermatogenesis molecules, such as sperm flagellar 2 (SPEF2), solute carrier family 25 member 31 (SLC25A31), A-kinase anchoring protein 3 (AKAP3), and tetratricopeptide repeat domain 21A
(
TTC21A). Notably, the upregulation of transcription factor EB (TFEB) and mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase 1 (MAP2K1) suggested enhanced autophagy activation and protein phosphorylation signaling pathways. Our findings identify a novel homozygous
CFAP300
variant, provide the first evidence linking
CFAP300
mutation to abnormal sperm morphology and impaired acrosomal biogenesis, and elucidate the potential pathophysiologic mechanism of
CFAP300
’s role in spermatogenesis.
PARTICIPANTS AND METHODS
Clinical information of participants
A 28-year-old male with asthenozoospermia, born to consanguineous parents (first cousin marriage), visited The First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University (Hefei, China) for fertility treatment in November 2023. The patient had a 2-year history of primary infertility, while his spouse showed no evidence of female reproductive tract anomalies or endocrine dysfunction on a comprehensive gynecological evaluation. The proband underwent a complete diagnostic workup for assisted reproductive technology (ART), including blood and semen sample collection and computed tomography (CT) scanning. Semen analysis revealed severe asthenozoospermia (
Table 1
). Written informed consent was obtained from the proband prior to the commencement of the study. The control group is healthy individuals with normal sperm parameters but spouses with fertility problems, hereinafter referred to as the control. This study was approved by the Ethical Review Board of The First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University (Approval No. PJ2023-04-19). Permission to publish the data involved in the paper has been obtained. Each individual signed the informed consent.

Histological hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining
Semen samples from the proband and the control group were obtained through masturbation and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for over 48 h. Spermatozoa were smeared onto clean slides, washed with water for 5 min, and submerged in Harris hematoxylin (C0107-100 ml; Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and eosin (C0109; Beyotime) for 15–30 min each. After washing with clean water, the smears were dehydrated and permeabilized. The slides were then sealed with neutral resin and covered with coverslips. Images were captured using an upright fluorescence microscope (ZEISS, Oberkochen, Germany). Sperm morphology was analyzed, and the proportion of sperm with aberrant morphology was assessed by counting more than 300 sperms.
Electron microscopy evaluation
As previously reported,
19
sperm samples were washed with 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde (pH 6.9) at 4°C overnight. For scanning electron microscopy (SEM), samples were sputter-coated using a Cressington 108 Auto Sputter Carbon Coater (Cressington Scientific Instruments, Watford, UK) and observed using a ZEISS GeminiSEM300 instrument (ZEISS). For transmission electron microscopy (TEM), fixed spermatozoa were dehydrated using a gradient, embedded in EPON 812 epoxy resin (E8000; HEAD BIOTECHNOLOGY, Beijing, China) and sectioned (100 nm). Ultrathin (100 nm) sections were observed using a Talos L120C G2 TEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).
Immunofluorescence staining
Processed sperm samples were fixed onto slides and incubated at 4°C with primary antibodies against
CFAP300
(1:100; HPA038585; Sigma-Aldrich, Kalamazoo, MI, USA), anti-acetylated α-tubulin (1:400; T6793; Sigma-Aldrich), dynein axonemal intermediate chain 1 (DNAI1; 1:200; 12756-1-AP; Proteintech, Wuhan, China), dynein axonemal intermediate chain 2 (DNAI2; 1:200; 17533-1-AP; Proteintech), dynein axonemal light intermediate chain 1 (DNALI1; 1:200; 17601-1-AP; Proteintech), dynein axonemal heavy chain 6 (DNAH6; 1:200; 30073-1-AP; Proteintech), dynein axonemal assembly factor 1 (DNAAF1; 1:200; 30156-1-AP; Proteintech), and DNAAF2 (1:100; PA5-44697; Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA). The samples were then washed three times with 1× PBS and treated with secondary antibodies: anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 488 (1:500; 34106ES60; Yeasen Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) and anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 594 (1:500; 111–585-003; Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Grove, PA, USA). DNA was stained with Hoechst 33342 (1:1000; 62249; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Images were obtained using a laser scanning confocal microscope (ZEISS).
TMT proteomics analysis
Sperm samples from two healthy controls and two biological replicates from the proband were ultrasonically lysed using lysis buffer containing phosphatase inhibitors (ST637-10 g; Beyotime) and 1% protease inhibitors (P1070-25 mg; Beyotime). After centrifugation (twice, 10 min at 1350
g
, at 4°C) using refrigerated centrifuge (TGL-16M; BIORIDGE, Shanghai, China), the supernatant was collected, and the protein content was determined using a BCA kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The appropriate amount of protein was taken from each sample, separated using 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and visualized using coomassie brilliant blue G250 staining. Depending on the protein concentration, incubate the appropriate amount of protein in each sample with trypsin overnight. The peptides were desalted in a SOLA SPE 96-well plate (60309-002; Thermo Fisher Scientific
)
and analyzed using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) high-resolution mass spectrometry (timsTOF HT; Bruker, Bremen, Germany). PASER software (Bruker) combines all mass spectrometry data to complete the database search of data-independent acquisition (DIA) mass spectrometry data and protein DIA quantification. The database sequence file was obtained from UniProt-Homo sapiens (version 9606-2024.2.1;
https://www.uniprot.org/taxonomy/9606
, last accessed on August 27, 2024) in the FASTA format.
RESULTS
Identification of CFAP300 mutation in male with asthenozoospermia
To elucidate the mechanistic contribution of
CFAP300
to male reproductive pathology, we employed WES coupled with computational pathogenicity assessment, identifying a novel homozygous loss-of-function mutation in
CFAP300
(GenBank:
NM_001363505
) from an individual. The frameshift variant c.304delC was absent from the allele frequency and protein function prediction tools, including the 1000 Genomes Project_EAS (
https://www.internationalgenome.org/
, last accessed on December 13, 2024), ExAC Browser_EAS (
https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/
, last accessed on December 13, 2024), and SIFT (
http://blocks.fhcrc.org/sift/SIFT.html
, last accessed on December 13, 2024), as shown in
Supplementary Table 2
. The proband harboring the homozygous
CFAP300
loss-of-function mutation c.304delC (p.P102fs) belonged to a consanguineous family (
Figure 1a
and
1b
). Sanger sequencing of blood samples from the proband’s parents (III-1 and III-2) confirmed their heterozygous carrier status and the proband’s homozygous genotype, fulfilling the criteria for autosomal recessive inheritance. The proband presented with chronic (symptoms or evidence of infection lasting ≥4 weeks) and recurrent respiratory infections and clinical manifestations consistent with a mild PCD subtype without situs inversus. CT revealed bronchiectasis (
Figure 1c
). Multiple sequence alignments across species showed that the
CFAP300
mutation was highly conserved (
Figure 1d
). These findings suggested that the
CFAP300
mutation may be closely associated with the asthenozoospermia and PCD symptoms observed in the proband.

PCD phenotype identified in the proband with asthenoteratozoospermia
Following 24 months of regular unprotected intercourse (≥3 times weekly) without conception, the proband (a 28-year-old male) was diagnosed with primary infertility. While the semen quantity of the proband was comparable to that of healthy individuals (
Table 1
), the key finding was that up to 98% of spermatozoa exhibited flagellar abnormalities, and sperm motility was reduced to zero, indicating complete immobility. Sperm morphology was evaluated using H&E staining according to the World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines (6
th
edition).
21
Compared to the control, the proband’s sperm flagellar defects predominantly manifested as coiled tails (
Figure 1e
and
1f
).
Similar deformities were observed using SEM (
Figure 1g
). Compared with the normal control, the acrosome of the patient’s sperm exhibited a wavy shape and was detached from the sperm head (
Figure 2a
). To elucidate the effect of the
CFAP300
mutation, the expression and localization of
CFAP300
in human spermatozoa were investigated. Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that the sperm flagella of the proband with the
CFAP300
mutation lacked a
CFAP300
signal, in contrast to the intact presence of this signal along the entire sperm flagella in the control group (
Figure 2b
). Four types of abnormal sperm flagellar morphology were identified: short, coiled, absent, and bent. TEM analysis revealed ultrastructural alterations in the cross-sections of the proband’s sperm flagella. Unlike the control sperm flagella, which displayed the typical “9+2” microtubule arrangement (
Figure 2c
), most of the proband’s sperm flagella cross-sections harboring the
CFAP300
mutation lacked ODA and IDA. These ultrastructural observations suggest that the
CFAP300
mutation caused abnormalities in the IDA or ODA of sperm flagella, thereby affecting sperm motility and flagellar morphology.

Decreased levels of dynein axonemal arm components in CFAP300-deficient patient
To investigate the structural pathogenesis of sperm flagella associated with
CFAP300
deficiency, we performed TMT-based proteomic profiling to compare spermatozoa from the proband harboring a
CFAP300
mutation with those from matched healthy donors. Quantitative proteomic profiling identified 2972 proteins with significant alterations in the expression in spermatozoa carrying the
CFAP300
mutation, revealing differential expression profiles comprising 1594 significantly upregulated and 1378 significantly downregulated targets (
Figure 3a
). Profiling of spermatozoa from the proband with the
CFAP300
variant revealed a marked depletion of axonemal components essential for dynein arm assembly, including dynein axonemal heavy chain 17 (DNAH17), AKAP3, and glutamine rich 2 (QRICH2), as shown in
Figure 3b
. Immunostaining analyses of proband-derived spermatozoa using ODA- and IDA-related proteins, including DNAI1, DNAI2, DNALI1, DNAH7, and DNAAF1, further confirmed the downregulation of dynein arm assembly in spermatozoa from the proband (
Figure 3c
–
3i
). Consistent with the TEM results, acrosome deficiency and the absence of IDA and ODA were found in the most proband’s sperm flagella (
Figure 4
). Additionally, proteomic analysis revealed that the upregulated proteins were significantly enriched in three key proteostatic pathways: protein phosphorylation, ubiquitin-dependent protein catabolic processes, and autophagy, suggesting that the downregulation of axonemal proteins may be due to protein degradation mechanisms (
Supplementary Figure 1
).

Favorable intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) outcome in family with CFAP300 mutation
Following clinical examination and genetic counseling, the patient opted for ICSI treatment. The patient’s wife underwent ovulation induction. On the day of oocyte retrieval, 25 metaphase II oocytes were obtained. Nineteen embryos were fertilized by ICSI, resulting in 15 cleavage-stage embryos on day 3 and eight blastocysts on day 5 (
Table 2
). Subsequently, the couple achieved pregnancy after the transfer of one blastocyst embryo. These results indicate that ICSI is a viable option for infertile men with
CFAP300
loss-of-function mutations.

DISCUSSION
We identified a homozygous loss-of-function mutation in the
CFAP300
, gene associated with PCD, in a patient from a consanguineous family. The proband exhibited typical PCD features and bronchiectasis in both lungs, as confirmed by CT scanning. Morphological analyses using H&E staining and SEM revealed pronounced anomalies in sperm flagella. Comprehensive morphological analysis revealed that the
CFAP300
mutation is associated with chaperone-mediated preassembly of dynein arms. We identified potential interactions between
CFAP300
and inner or outer motor protein arm assembly factors, including DNAI1, DNAI2, DNAH6, DNALI1, and DNAAF1. Initially characterized as a cytoplasmic preassembly factor for axonemal dynein complexes in
Chlamydomonas
models,
CFAP300
has since been implicated in human ciliopathies through the identification of homozygous variants.
9
10
11
15
Consistent with prior studies,
10
individuals with
CFAP300
mutations exhibit a common PCD phenotype from birth, characterized by immotile respiratory cilia lacking IDA and ODA.
9
Our study suggests that the
CFAP300
loss-of-function mutation probably disrupts axonemal dynein assembly regulator. Although
CFAP300
mutations have been implicated in human PCD and male infertility, the precise molecular mechanisms through which
CFAP300
deficiency affects spermatogenesis remain unclear.
Population-specific genetic studies in Finnish PCD cohorts have identified
CFAP300
loss-of-function variants that are associated with characteristic respiratory manifestations. High-speed video microscopy analysis revealed stiff or static cilia.
15
Consistently, our infertile individual with
CFAP300
mutation also exhibited diminished sperm motility. Most men with PCD display complete or partial sperm immobility, a phenotype mechanistically linked to axonemal ultrastructural defects common to respiratory cilia and sperm flagella.
22
Strikingly, ultrastructural analysis of the proband harboring the
CFAP300
variant revealed multiple sperm flagellar defects and acrosomal malformations, representing previously unreported structural abnormalities in human cases. In previous studies, the
CFAP300
homolog FBB5 was identified as being tightly linked to intraflagellar transport (IFT)-related motor assembly proteins, including ODA16 (WDR69)
23
and ODA8,
24
which are predominantly cytoplasmic and present in trace amounts in the cilia/flagella matrix of
Chlamydomonas
and
Paramecium
.
9
Mammalian sperm tail formation requires IFT.
25
26
27
Some ODAs interact with IFT, facilitating flagellar maintenance by assuring the distribution of replacement ODAs along the flagellum.
28
Given the similarity between the ultrastructure of sperm flagella and cilia, we hypothesize that
CFAP300
mutation causes ODA deficiency, compromising sperm flagella maintenance via disrupted ODA-IFT interplay, thereby affecting the preservation of sperm flagellar morphology. Our findings collectively confirm abnormalities in sperm flagella and the lack of dynein arms in the patient with
CFAP300
mutation.
To elucidate the mechanistic basis of
CFAP300
-associated defects in human spermatogenesis, we performed TMT-based proteomic profiling of spermatozoa from the proband harboring the
CFAP300
variant and from healthy controls. These results confirmed that
CFAP300
deficiency led to the downregulation of several spermatogenesis-related proteins. Notably, key proteins involved in head elongation and acrosome formation, such as actin-like 7A (ACTL7A),
29
AKAP3,
30
sperm adhesion molecule 1 (SPAM1),
31
and zona pellucida-binding protein (ZPBP),
32
as well as those essential for flagellar biogenesis and assembly, such as DNAH17,
33
TTC21A,
34
QRICH2,
3
and SPEF2
35
were significantly downregulated in the proband compared to the control. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed a complete absence of DNAAF1 and DNAAF2 signals in spermatozoa from the
CFAP300
-variant proband, consistent with the TEM findings, indicating ODA defects. Previous mechanistic studies have demonstrated that DNAAF2 interacts functionally with CFAP300 through structural coordination,
10
acting as a molecular chaperone to regulate the cytoplasmic preassembly of dynein heavy chains.
17
Considering the early and late preassembly complexes consist of DNAAF2 and DNAAF1, respectively,
36
we suspect that
CFAP300
deficiency destabilizes these complexes, leading to the degradation of DNAAF1/DNAAF2 chaperone proteins via coordinated ubiquitin-dependent proteolysis and autophagy pathways. Interestingly, proteomic analysis of proband-derived spermatozoa revealed residual cytoplasm and accelerated proteolytic degradation, suggesting dysregulated proteostasis characterized by autophagy activation and protein phosphorylation. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis indicated the elevated levels of autophagy-associated proteins, such as unc-51 like autophagy activating kinase 1 (ULK1),
37
TFEB,
38
and phosphorylation-associated proteins, including MAP2K1
39
and large tumor-suppressor kinase 1 (LATS1).
40
Furthermore, proteomic profiling revealed a significant downregulation of proteins involved in mitochondrial ATP synthesis, including SLC25A31
41
and cation channel sperm associated 3 (CATSPER3).
42
This coordinated suppression of bioenergetic machinery components correlates with impaired mitochondrial membrane potential and decreased cellular ATP production. Our study provides experimental evidence of the molecular pathogenesis by which
CFAP300
dysfunction impairs spermatogenesis, ultimately contributing to male infertility.
Patients with PCD and infertility undergoing ICSI treatment often have predictable outcomes.
43
44
45
Given that all of the patient’s sperm were immotile, we recommended ICSI treatment, which yielded a favorable outcome. Our findings provide further evidence of the effectiveness of ICSI in treating individuals with
CFAP300
loss-of-function mutations.
In conclusion, we identified a
CFAP300
loss-of-function variant in an infertile proband from a consanguineous family, demonstrating aberrant sperm morphology and acrosomal malformation due to
CFAP300
deficiency. We elucidated the potential molecular mechanisms of
CFAP300
regulation in spermatogenesis. These findings enhance our understanding of this novel phenotype in patients with
CFAP300
mutations and provide mechanistic insights into
CFAP300
-associated asthenoteratozoospermia with acrosomal malformations. ICSI is an effective treatment for patients with
CFAP300
mutations and offers a promising approach for managing infertility in this patient population.
