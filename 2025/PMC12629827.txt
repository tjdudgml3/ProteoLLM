Introduction
Solid tumors are not just a collection of malignant cells—they are dysfunctional organs consisting of cancer cells and nonmalignant host cells together in a complex extracellular matrix (ECM).
1
,
2
,
3
The interplay between tumor cells and host cells in the tumor microenvironment (TME) shapes therapeutic responses and resistance.
1
However, conventional approaches in oncology drug development that use cultured cancer cell lines or subcutaneously implanted tumor cells fail to recapitulate the complexity of tumor cell-stromal cell and cell-ECM interactions.
4
Physiological models of tumor tissue that are compatible with drug screening are needed to discover drugs that target these complex interactions in TME.
Recently, we and others have established organotypic tumor slice model systems from mouse and primary human tumor samples that recapitulate the complexity of intratumor heterogeneity, preserve tissue architecture, and enable longitudinal biochemical and pharmacological studies.
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
However, the number of tissue slices that can be generated from a single tumor sample limits their use in high-throughput screenings. To address this challenge, we integrated these models with machine learning algorithms to computationally predict tumor responses to hundreds of drugs. As a proof of concept, we applied our integrated approach to identify drugs that specifically target the cancer TME in highly desmoplastic tumors.
We performed functional kinase inhibitor screens in mouse triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) cell lines and pancreatic cancer cell lines, both in monolayer (2D cultures) and in organotypic tumor slices (3D culture) from immune-competent tumors of the same origin. Using a polypharmacology-based machine-learning approach, we identified several drug candidates that significantly reduced the viability of tumor tissue slices but not the cancer cells in 2D cultures. While some of the drugs predicted to be effective on 3D tumors were previously known, we also discovered several drugs that specifically inhibited the viability of 3D tumors but not the cancer cells in 2D cultures. Notably, we validated the effectiveness of doramapimod in tissue slices from multiple desmoplastic tumors and
in vivo
mouse models. Mechanistic investigations showed that doramapimod, a known p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) inhibitor, also directly inhibits the discoidin domain-containing receptor (DDR1/2) in cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), leading to reduced production of ECM proteins, immune-suppressing chemokines, and growth-promoting factors. Both doramapimod treatment and genetic depletion of DDR1/2 and MAPK12 in CAFs hindered ECM production and stunted cancer cell growth in co-culture assays. Additionally, DDR1/2 and MAPK12 regulated ECM production by modulating GLI activity in CAFs, independent of the hedgehog signaling pathway. Targeting the DDR1/2-MAPK12-GLI pathway with doramapimod enhanced the effectiveness of chemotherapy and immunotherapy in tumor slices from patients and preclinical models. These results highlight the role of the non-canonical hedgehog pathway in CAF regulation and demonstrate the value of using 3D tissue models to identify critical targets within the TME.
Results
Comparison of 2D cultures and 3D tissue screens identifies drugs targeting the tumor microenvironment
We sought to identify drugs that specifically target the TME by conducting contrastive drug screenings on cells in monolayer (2D cultures) and tumor tissues (3D microtumors). These microtumors are prepared directly from fresh tumor specimens and retain multiple native cell types, including stromal and immune components, as well as the ECM and overall tissue architecture. Utilizing a machine learning-driven drug screening approach,
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
we carried out kinase inhibitor screenings on orthotopic, immune-competent mammary and pancreatic tumor tissues, along with cell lines of the same origin cultured in 2D (
Figure 1
A). We employed three well-established murine models, known for the substantial influence of the TME on cancer cell behavior and therapy response. These models include stroma-rich TNBC (E0771 and Py8119) and pancreatic cancer (KPC). Experimentally, we tested a panel of 32 computationally selected kinase inhibitors and assessed their impact on the viability of cancer cells in both 2D and microtumors. The collected phenotypic data were integrated with previously established kinase inhibitor profiles and elastic net regression modeling, an approach known as kinome regularization (KiR),
11
,
13
,
14
to predict responses to 428 kinase inhibitors (
Figure S1
;
Table S1
). KiR offers a major advantage by requiring only a small, well-characterized set of inhibitors that collectively target ∼90% of the kinome. It leverages the fact that while kinase inhibitors often have broad specificity, each has a distinct activity profile. Using elastic net regression, KiR links phenotypic outcomes (e.g., cell or tissue viability) to the activity of individual kinases, penalizing those with minimal contributions and highlighting key dependencies. This approach is particularly valuable when screening is performed in complex systems such as microtumor models, where throughput is limited, and large-scale testing is impractical. Once kinase dependencies are inferred, they can be used to computationally predict responses to ∼400 additional kinase inhibitors. We built a total of six KiR models: three using data from responses in 2D cell cultures and three using data from responses in micortumors. Model performance was evaluated using leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV), where the mean squared error (MSE) between predicted and observed drug responses was calculated. In LOOCV, the activity profiles of 31 drugs were used to train the model to predict the effect of the remaining drug on viability in 2D cultures or microtumors. The MSE between predicted and observed changes in viability provided an error score for each model. Overall, the models for both 2D cultures and 3D microtumors performed with at least 80% accuracy (
Figure S1
). The optimized models were then used to predict responses to 428 kinase inhibitors.

Doramapimod suppresses tumor growth across multiple cancer models
Since doramapimod’s role in cancer is not well established, we evaluated its effects in the TME using microtumors derived from multiple models, including murine tumors (KPC, 4T1, Py8119, and E0771 primary and lung metastases), a breast cancer patient-derived xenograft (PDX) (HCI-001), and human tumors (hepatocellular carcinoma and meningiomas) (
Figure 2
A). We found that treatment with doramapimod significantly reduced (
p
< 0.05) the viability of microtumors derived from desmoplastic tumors such as TNBC and pancreatic cancer. In contrast, its effect was limited in other tumor types such as HCC and meningiomas, which generally have less dense stroma (
Figure 2
A), suggesting that the mechanism of action of doramapimod may involve a desmoplastic stromal cell type. Analysis of doramapimod at 2.5 μM across 492 cancer cell lines from the DepMap data
23
showed minimal sensitivity, with only 11 lines exhibiting <50% residual growth and the most sensitive line retaining 37% viability (
Figure 2
B). This concentration is five times higher than in our microtumor and 2D assays, suggesting doramapimod primarily impacts microtumors viability via the TME rather than direct cancer cell toxicity.

Next, we evaluated doramapimod’s therapeutic potential
in vivo
using physiologically relevant mouse models. We tested its ability to suppress tumor growth in models of TNBC (E0771 and 4T1), pancreatic cancer (KPC), and a PDX model of TNBC (HCI-001) (
Figures 2
C–2E). First, we assessed the ability of systemically delivered doramapimod to reduce E0771 mammary tumors and KPC pancreatic tumors in mice. For establishing mammary TNBC tumors, we orthotopically injected E0771 cells into the mammary fat pads of female mice. Once the tumors reached a volume of 100 mm
3
, the mice were randomly divided into vehicle or doramapimod treatment groups. After a two-week treatment period, we found that doramapimod significantly reduced tumor growth by 57% and tumor weight by 26.0% compared to the vehicle control (
Figure 2
C). To establish pancreatic tumors, we orthotopically injected firefly luciferase-expressing KPC cells into the pancreata of male mice. One week after tumor implantation, we used an IVIS to evenly distribute mice with similar tumor burdens into control and doramapimod treatment groups. After two weeks of treatment, mice treated with doramapimod showed a significant reduction in tumor weight, with a 44% decrease compared to the control group (
Figure 2
D).
Previous studies have shown that doramapimod functions as an anti-inflammatory agent by inhibiting cytokines, such as interleukin (IL)-6 and IL-13, via p38 MAPK signaling.
24
,
25
,
26
To determine if its anti-tumor effects were due to systemic anti-inflammatory activity, we administered a low dose of (1 mg/kg) directly into the tumor site, minimizing systemic exposure. This approach was tested in both immunocompetent (4T1) and immunocompromised (HCI-001 PDX) mouse models. Intratumoral delivery of doramapimod significantly suppressed tumor growth in both models (
Figure 2
E). In 4T1 tumors, we observed a significant reduction in growth and a ∼2-fold decrease in final tumor weight. Similarly, localized treatment of HCI-001 PDX tumors led to a reduction in tumor volume to ∼63.6% of vehicle-treated controls. These findings suggest that doramapimod’s antitumor effects extend beyond systemic immune modulation and involve local mechanisms within the TME.

In desmoplastic tumors, CAFs are major contributors to ECM production. These fibroblasts are recruited and reprogrammed by cancer cells to promote tumor growth, in part by producing collagen-rich ECM.

Doramapimod targets DDR1/2 and MAPK12 kinases to regulate ECM in cancer-associated fibroblasts
To investigate the molecular mechanisms of doramapimod-mediated ECM regulation in CAFs, we first aimed to identify its direct protein targets. We performed an
in vitro
kinase assay using the HotSpot radiometric filter-binding method
30

Table S6

MAPK14
) with 0.2% of residual activity, or 99.8% inhibition, p38β (
MAPK9
) with 19% residual activity, p38γ (
MAPK12
) with 15.4% residual activity, consistent with previous reports.
16
,
31
Unexpectedly, we also found potent inhibition of kinases not previously linked to doramapimod, including discoidin domain receptor 1 (DDR1) (0.1% residual activity, indicating 99.9% inhibition), DDR2 (3.9% residual activity, or 96.1% inhibition), JNK2 (
MAPK9
) (7.8% residual activity), and EphrinA7 (1.6% residual activity) (
Figure 4
A). Since EphrinA7 is not expressed in CAFs, it was excluded from further analysis. These findings led us to hypothesize that doramapimod’s effects on CAFs may be driven by inhibition of p38 MAPKs, JNK2, and DDR1/2 kinases. To validate this, we used silencing RNAs to deplete the levels of these kinases in breast cancer-derived CAFs (
Figure S3
) and measured the expression of CAF markers (
Figure 4
B). Knockdown of DDR1 or MAPK12 significantly reduced ACTA2, a myofibroblast-like CAF marker,
32
while silencing DDR1, DDR2, or MAPK12 reduced CXCL12 expression, associated with inflammatory-type CAFs.
32
,
33
Notably, knockdown of DDR2 increased ACTA2, suggesting a potential compensatory effect. Both DDR1 and DDR2 are receptor tyrosine kinases that serve as collagen receptors,
34

Figure 4

DDR1/2-MAPK12 signaling axis is important for CAF-mediated cancer cell growth
CAFs interact with neighboring cells in their TME through direct contact and paracrine signaling.
35
Previous studies have shown that CAFs can promote cancer cell growth in serum-free conditions.
35
,
36
To test whether CAFs from breast and pancreatic tumors support cancer cell growth and whether doramapimod can disrupt this, we performed co-culture experiments. We found that 4T1 breast cancer cells failed to proliferate in serum-free conditions when cultured alone, but grew when co-cultured with breast-derived CAFs, indicating that CAFs promote cancer cell growth through both contact and secreted factors (
Figure S3
). Notably, doramapimod treatment reduced 4T1 proliferation in the presence of CAFs in a dose-dependent manner. Similar results were observed with MDA-MB-231 human TNBC cells co-cultured with fibroblasts (
Figure S3
), suggesting that doramapimod disrupts CAF-mediated support of cancer cell growth.
Next, we investigated which doramapimod-targeted kinases contribute to CAF-mediated cancer cell growth. We depleted levels of six kinases identified as doramapimod targets (
Figure 4
A) in breast cancer-derived CAFs and evaluated their ability to support breast cancer cell growth under serum deprivation conditions. Depletion of DDR1, DDR2, and MAPK12 (p38γ) significantly reduced CAF-mediated support of cancer cell growth, while silencing the other kinases had no effect (
Figure 4
F). These results suggest that doramapimod’s impact on microtumor viability and
in vivo
responses is likely driven by its inhibition of the DDR1/2-MAPK12 signaling axis.
DDR1 and DDR2 are collagen-activated tyrosine kinase receptors that signal through several downstream effectors, including Src, Ras/Raf/MEK/Erk, PI3K/Akt, and Jak2 kinases.
37
,
38
However, it’s less clear whether DDR1 and DDR2 can directly activate p38 MAPK kinase. Our data indicate that overexpressing DDR1 or DDR2 in untransformed fibroblasts substantially increases p38 MAPK phosphorylation, suggesting that p38 MAPK activation is a downstream effect of DDR1 and DDR2 (
Figure 4
G). These findings suggest that doramapimod disrupts CAF-mediated support of cancer cell growth by inhibiting DDR1, DDR2, and MAPK12 (
Figure 4
H), highlighting their key role in ECM remodeling and tumor-stroma interactions.
GLI1, a hedgehog pathway transcription regulator, controls the expression of ECM genes downstream of the DDR1/2-MAPK12 axis
Our data suggest that DDR1/2-MAPK12 plays an important role in regulating ECM genes in CAFs. However, the mechanisms by which these receptors and kinases regulate ECM genes and support cancer cell growth and which transcription factors are involved remain unclear. To further elucidate the mechanistic details of this pathway, we examined the overlap of genes that are downregulated in response to the knockdown of DDR1/2 and MAPK12 in CAFs. We identified an overlap of 7 genes that are commonly reduced by at least 2-fold due to the knockdown of DDR1/2 and MAPK12 (

GLI1 is a transcription factor involved in the hedgehog signaling pathway, crucial for embryonic development and tumor progression.
39
Upon activation, GLI1 translocates to the nucleus where it binds to DNA, initiating the transcription of genes that regulate cell proliferation, and differentiation.
39

As our data show that GLI1, a transcription factor in the hedgehog signaling pathway, can be activated by DDR1/2 kinases, we next explored potential cross-talk between the canonical hedgehog pathway and DDR1/2 kinases. We stimulated breast CAFs with Sonic hedgehog (SHH) ligand. We found that SHH treatment of breast CAFs led to an expected increase in the expression of
GLI1

We then investigated whether the targets of doramapimod, DDR1/2, and MAPK12, are downstream of SHH. We found that activation of the SHH pathway did not significantly increase p38 MAPK phosphorylation, which was already elevated at baseline. However, SHH stimulation led to increased (1.5- to 2-fold) mRNA expression of
DDR1
and
DDR2
in breast CAFs (

Next, we investigated the role of GLI1 in CAF-mediated growth of cancer cells. We depleted GLI1 levels in breast cancer-derived CAFs and assessed their ability to support breast cancer cell growth under serum deprivation as described previously. We observed that GLI1 depletion significantly hindered the ability of CAFs to enhance the proliferation of 4T1 cells, while the knockdown of a scramble control did not have this effect (
Figure 5

Figure 5

Figure 5

Discussion
The TME is a mixed ensemble of various cell types and signaling molecules. Targeting key signaling pathways in stromal cells could significantly enhance our understanding and modeling of complex cancer cell-host cell interactions, and open possibilities for therapeutic interventions. However, discovering targets specific to host cells remains challenging. Here, we describe a powerful approach of integrating organotypic tissue slice culture system, phenotypic measurements, and machine learning-based modeling that aims to overcome these limitations and provide a physiologically relevant TME to test and predict drug response. We demonstrated our approach using mouse and human microtumors and discovered numerous drugs that specifically inhibited the viability of microtumors while having no effect on cancer cells in 2D culture. Comparing all three models we screened, we identified 12 drugs that specifically reduce microtumors viability, drugs that might be overlooked by traditional screens using cultured cancer cell lines. We validated drugs predicted by KiR in multiple tumor models, both
ex vivo
and
in vivo
, and conducted a detailed investigation into the action mechanism of doramapimod. Thus, our findings demonstrate that KiR-based modeling can swiftly and accurately predict drug responses in microtumors, identifying drugs that target the TME effectively.
Using doramapimod as a chemical probe, we uncovered a previously unrecognized DDR1/2-MAPK12-GLI1 signaling axis that regulates cancer-stroma interactions. Transcriptomic and proteomic profiling of doramapimod-treated tumors revealed that it alters ECM composition in CAFs without affecting their proliferation. Mechanistically, doramapimod targets DDR1/2 and MAPK12, kinases critical for pro-tumorigenic ECM production in CAFs. DDR1/2, activated by collagen and overexpressed in CAFs.
45
,
46
,
47
,
48
, promotes MAPK12 activation and nuclear localization, enhancing GLI1 activity, a transcription factor linked to hedgehog signaling. This pathway drives ECM and growth factor expression that supports tumor progression. Doramapimod treatment impaired CAF function, reduced cancer cell growth in co-culture, and suppressed tumor growth
in vivo
. These findings highlight a previously unrecognized role of DDR1/2-MAPK12-GLI1 and demonstrate the utility of functional screening to dissect tumor-host interactions within the TME.
Clinical trials have shown that hedgehog inhibitors targeting SMO have failed to improve survival in pancreatic cancer.
49
This newly discovered non-canonical hedgehog pathway may sustain pro-tumorigenic CAF functions despite SMO inhibition. Targeting GLI1 or upstream regulators like DDR1/2-MAPK12 may therefore be more effective. Doramapimod, which inhibits both DDR1/2 and MAPK12, suppressed CAF-derived ECM and inflammatory signals, enhanced immune activation, and increased CD8
+
T cell infiltration.
In vivo
, doramapimod improved responses to anti-PD-L1 therapy in TNBC and potentiated gemcitabine efficacy in a KPC pancreatic model. Previously published single-cell RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis
50
of spontaneously arising KPC pancreatic tumors (
Figure S6
) revealed that both DDR1 and DDR2 are expressed in fibroblast populations, but in distinct CAF subtypes. Specifically, DDR1 is enriched in antigen-presenting CAFs, whereas DDR2 is predominantly expressed in inflammatory CAFs. Thus, a dual-targeting agent like doramapimod, which inhibits both DDR1 and DDR2, may be particularly effective by broadly suppressing functionally diverse CAF subtypes. Together, these findings support DDR1/2-MAPK12-GLI1 as a therapeutic axis for remodeling the TME and enhancing therapy response in desmoplastic tumors.
Current preclinical drug testing often fails to translate clinically due to a trade-off between throughput and physiological relevance. Organotypic tissue cultures address this by preserving native tumor architecture, ECM, and key stromal and immune components. Compared to organoids or 2D cultures, microtumors are faster to prepare, maintain critical cancer cell-microenvironment interactions, and yield more clinically relevant data. Our platform enables screening ∼30 drugs in patient-derived microtumors and uses computational modeling to predict responses to hundreds more, all within seven days, advancing precision oncology with greater speed and translational impact.
Limitations of the study
While this study highlights the value of combining kinase inhibitor-focused screening with microtumors models to uncover microenvironment-targeted therapies, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although microtumors offer greater physiological relevance than 2D cultures, they lack long-term immune competence, which limits their ability to fully capture immune-tumor interactions, particularly for therapies that depend on immune cell infiltration. Second, the machine learning approach used in this study was trained specifically on responses to kinase inhibitors, one of the largest class of anti-cancer drugs, but may not be applicable to identifying sensitivities to other drug classes. Finally, while we identified a novel DDR1/2-MAPK12-GLI1 signaling axis regulating CAF activity, the detailed molecular mechanisms and contributions of individual cell types within the complex TME warrant further investigation.

Experimental model and study participant details

Tissue slices and 3Dμtumors preparation and drug screening
Organotypic tumor slices were prepared from murine and human tumors as described previously.
5
,
53
Briefly, tumor slices were prepared from tumors extracted from tumor-bearing mice or human tumors using a Leica VT1200S vibratome (Leica Biosystems, Wetzlar, Germany). Fresh tumor tissues were shaped into 6 mm diameter cylinders using biopsy punches (Integra Miltex, York, PA, USA) and mounted using cyanoacrylate glue. Slices were immersed in ice-cold HBSS during preparation, with vibratome settings set at a blade angle of 18°, a cutting speed set of 0.14 mm/s (with a range of 0.10–0.25 mm/s), and a vibration amplitude of 2.0 mm. Human pancreatic tumors were prepared with a thickness of 250 μm and a diameter of 6 mm. To create μtumors, slices (400 μm thickness, 6 mm in diameter) were arranged in a monolayer on a plastic disc and sectioned using a McIlwain Tissue Chopper (Ted Pella, Redding, CA, USA). Following initial cuts, the disc was rotated 90°, and the process repeated. 3D μtumors were cultured in Williams’ Medium E containing 12 mM nicotinamide, 150 nM ascorbic acid, 2.25 mg/mL sodium bicarbonate, 20 mM HEPES, 50 mg/mL glucose, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 2 mM L-glutamine, 1% (v/v) ITS, 20 ng/mL EGF, 40 IU/mL penicillin and 40 μg/mL streptomycin.
Kinase inhibitor screening
3D μtumors were treated with Realtime-Glo MT cell viability reagent (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) at 1: 1000 dilution in 96-well plates. Following a 24-h incubation, luminescence signals were measured using a Biotek Synergy H4 microplate reader (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Wells with signal levels at least 10 times higher than the blank were selected for kinase inhibitor screening, with each inhibitor tested in duplicate or triplicate. 3D μtumors were maintained and real-time viability was measured for 3 to 7 days post-treatment. The viability was determined by calculating the ratio of the signal at the end of the study to the signals from the same tissue before treatment, which was then normalized to the value of vehicle control 3D μtumors set to 100%.
Kinome Regularization modeling
KiR models for three cancer cell lines and μtumors were generated as previously described.
9
,
11
Briefly, a panel of 427 kinase inhibitors had their pairwise effects on 298 human kinases profiled, resulting in a quantitative drug-target matrix. Each entry in this matrix is a percentage between 0 and 100, representing the residual activity of the kinases (as a percent of control, uninhibited activity) in the presence of each inhibitor. A subset of 25–30 kinase inhibitors was tested on cultured cancer cells in 2D and in μtumors as described above. The outcome was a single response for each drug, representing the change in viability (as % DMSO control) at the profiled dose of the inhibitor (typically 500 nM).
The kinase inhibition profiles of each inhibitor and the quantitative responses to those inhibitors were used as explanatory and response variables, respectively, for elastic net regularized multiple linear regression models as described previously.
9
,
52
LOOCV was used to select the optimal value for the penalty scaling factor λ. Models were computed for 11 evenly spaced values of α (the relative weighting between LASSO and Ridge regularization) ranging from 0 to 1.0 inclusive. Model accuracy was assessed via the LOOCV error and the root-mean-squared error of the predictions for the tested inhibitors (

Sample preparation for LC-MS/MS
E0771 and KPC tumors from independent mice treated with doramapimod or vehicle control were lyophilized, ground, and subjected to mass spectrometry (Q Exactive HF Mass Spectrometer, Thermo Fisher) as described previously.
54
Fresh frozen samples were milled in liquid nitrogen with a mortar and pestle. Approximately 5 mg of each sample was processed by a stepwise extraction resulting in cellular and ECM fractions for each sample. Glass beads (1 mm) were used to mechanically agitate samples in a Bullet Blender (NextAdvance, Model BBX24) prior to all extraction steps. Samples were decellularized using 3 successive washes of 50mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 0.25% CHAPS, 25mM EDTA, 3M NaCl, 1X protease inhibitor (Halt Protease Inhibitor Cocktail, Thermo Scientific #78429). Glass beads (1 mm) were used to mechanically agitate samples in a Bullet Blender (NextAdvance) after each buffer addition, followed by vortexing at 4
0
C for 20 min. Samples were then spun for 20 min at 18,000 x g and the supernatant was collected and pooled (cellular fraction). ECM extraction was performed by reducing and alkylating decellularized tissue pellets before five 10-min rounds of in-solution digestion at 37
0
C with ultrasonication using a Bioruptor (Diagenode). The first round of digestion was performed in 4M urea, 10 mM ABC pH 8.0, 0.02% ProteaseMax (Promega #V207A) with 2 μg Lys-C (Wako #121–05063). The second round of digestion was performed by adding 0.2 μg Lys-C and repeating ultrasonication. Third and fourth rounds of digestion were performed by diluting to 0.6M urea with 10 mM ABC, 0.02% ProteaseMax and adding 1 μg of trypsin for each digest followed by 10 min of ultrasonication. Finally, a fifth round of overnight digestion at 37
0
C without ultrasonication was performed after the addition of 0.5 μg trypsin to each sample. Samples were then spun for 20 min at 18,000 x g and the supernatant was collected (ECM fraction). Protein concentration of each fraction for each sample was assessed by A660 Protein Assay (Pierce). Proteolytic digestion of cellular fractions was carried out according to the FASP protocol (Wisniewski et al. 2009, Nature
STAR Methods
) with 10 kDa molecular weight cutoff filters. Cellular samples were reduced, alkylated, and digested with trypsin (1:100) at 37
0
C for 14 h. Peptides were recovered from the filter using successive washes of 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate and 0.2% formic acid. Digested samples were desalted using Pierce C18 Spin Tips (Thermo Scientific #84850) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Samples were prepared for 4 LC-MS/MS injections. Final volume was adjusted to inject 6 μg of protein for each run.
MS acquisition
Global proteomics was carried out (
n
= 3 per group) on a Q Exactive HF mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled to an Easy-nLC 1000 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) through a nanoelectrospray LC-MS interface. Eight μL of each sample was injected into a 20 μL loop using the autosampler. The analytical column was then switched on-line at a maximum pressure of 700 bar over an in house-made 100 μm i.d. × 150 mm fused silica capillary packed with 2.7 μm CORTECS C18 resin (Waters; Milford, MA). After 10 min of sample loading, the flow rate was adjusted to 400 nL/min, and each sample was run on a 120-min linear gradient to separate the peptides. For cellular fractions, a linear gradient from 4 to 24% ACN in 0.1% formic acid was applied from minutes 0–100, followed by an increase to 90% ACN from minutes 100–120 to wash residual peptides from the column. The same LC method was utilized for ECM fractions but with a gradient from 4 to 18% ACN from minutes 0–100. LC mobile phase solvents consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (Buffer A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (Buffer B, Optima LC/MS, Fisher Scientific, Pittsburgh, PA). Data-dependent acquisition was performed using the instrument supplied Xcalibur (version 4.8) software. The mass spectrometer was operated in the positive ion mode. Each survey scan of m/z 300–1,800 was followed by higher-energy collision-induced dissociation (HCD) MS/MS of the 15 most intense precursor ions. Singly charged ions were excluded from HCD selection. Normalized collision energies of 30 eV were employed using helium as the collision gas.
MS data analysis
For global LC-MS/MS, raw files were directly loaded into Proteome Discoverer 2.4 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and searched against the SwissProt database using an in-house Mascot server (Version 2.7, Matrix Science). Mass tolerances were ±15ppm for MS peaks, and ±0.05 Da for MS/MS fragment ions. Trypsin-specific cleavage was used allowing for 1 missed cleavage. Methionine oxidation, proline hydroxylation, protein N-terminal acetylation, deamidation of asparagine and glutamine, and peptide N-terminal pyroglutamic acid formation were allowed as variable modifications while carbamidomethylation of cysteine was set as a fixed modification. Label-free quantification was performed using the Minora Feature detector for precursor peak intensity-based abundance. A protein threshold of 1% FDR was used to filter for high-confidence identifications.
