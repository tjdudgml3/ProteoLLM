Introduction
Salmonella enterica
serovar Typhimurium, hereinafter referred to as
S
. Typhimurium, is a Gram-negative bacterium capable of infecting both humans and animals [
1
]. This pathogen is responsible for over 10 million illnesses and 100,000 deaths per year worldwide [
2
]. It invades host cells, causing salmonellosis, which is a gastroenteritis that is significant foodborne intestinal illness [
1
]. Although this disease is typically self-limiting, it can progress to bacteremia and systemic disease, impacting around 200 million individuals worldwide and causing over 200,000 fatalities annually [
3
,
4
].
The pathogenesis of
S
. Typhimurium is heavily reliant on two distinct type III secretion systems (T3SSs), which are encoded on separate pathogenicity islands, SPI-1 and SPI-2, respectively [
5
]. The T3SS-1, encoded by SPI-1, enables bacterial invasion by translocating virulence proteins, known as effectors, into host cells [
6
]. This process induces ruffling of the actin cytoskeleton and polymerization/remodeling, thereby enhancing bacterial uptake into non-phagocytic cells. The T3SS-2, encoded by SPI-2, facilitates the movement of
S
. Typhimurium across epithelial cells and aids in its replication and survival within macrophages [
7
]. A series of studies have indicated that the virulence of
S
. Typhimurium also depends on its flagella. These flagella guide the bacteria toward behaviors such as near-surface swimming and swarming, which enable them to colonize new surfaces and spread effectively [
8
,
9
]. Beyond aiding in motility, flagella also modulate key processes such as bacterial adhesion, biofilm formation, and interactions with the host’s immune system [
10–12
]. Consequently, variations in the recognition of these bacterial virulence factors can significantly affect the course of infections. As a result, hosts have evolved a range of strategies to either evade or modulate specific recognition, thereby adapting to the presence of these virulence factors.
In response to negative environmental stimuli,
S
. Typhimurium has become increasingly resistant to many antimicrobial agents [
13
]. Biofilm formation is one of the most critical factors contributing to antibiotic resistance, with biofilms being up to 1000 times more resistant to antibiotics compared to their planktonic state [
14
]. These biofilms provide a wide range of benefits to
Salmonella
, facilitating attachment and biofilm formation within the gastrointestinal tract during chronic human infections. According to the National Institutes of Health, 80% of all human infections are related to biofilms [
15
,
16
]. Infections caused by biofilm-forming bacteria require a substantially more intensive combination of antibiotics. Antimicrobial resistance can even cause 10 million deaths annually in the human population worldwide by 2050 according to the developed models. Indeed, when
Salmonella
becomes resistant to these medications, it poses a threat to public health and socioeconomic well-being [
17
].
HslV, known as heat shock protein locus V, is a core component of the HslUV protease complex [
18
]. Identified as a peptidase, HslV can degrade a few hydrophobic peptides and misfolded proteins slowly
in vitro
, but its activity can be significantly enhanced by binding with HslU, which activates it several times over [
19
,
20
]. Although numerous previous studies have elucidated the structure of HslV and its functions in de-folding, transporting and degrading substrates [
21
], our understanding of HslV remains limited. Proteomics studies have revealed that the protein levels of HslV, along with certain T3SS-1 effectors, are markedly elevated in
S
. Typhimurium after it invades host cells [
22–24
]. These reports indicated that the HslV can be involved in bacterial virulence of
S
. Typhimurium under natural infection conditions. Furthermore, heat shock proteins could impair the flagella motility, biofilm formation, cell adhesion, and cellular invasion of
S
. Typhimurium, and also inhibit its ability to induce inflammation in mice [
25
,
26
]. Thus, HslV has the potential to influence biofilm formation and T3SS-1 virulence gene expression.
To understand the physiological and pathological effects of HslV in
S
. Typhimurium, we investigated its roles during infection in cellular and mouse infection models, and compared the proteomes of the ∆
hslV
strain with its parental wild-type strain using mass spectrometry (MS)-based comparative proteomics. We observed the downregulation of certain T3SS-1 effectors and the upregulation of Lon in the ∆
hslV
strain, and found that HslV can indirectly enhance the expression of virulence genes by suppressing the expression of the
lon
gene in
S
. Typhimurium. Notably, the motility, proliferation and biofilm formation of the ∆
hslV
strain were significantly suppressed, which alleviated the inflammatory and lethal effects of
S
. Typhimurium in mice. Our study suggests that HslV might interplay with Lon to control the activity of T3SS-1 virulence genes, offering a potential target for therapeutic intervention and for combating antibiotic resistance in
S
. Typhimurium infections.
Materials and methods
Bacterial strains, plasmids, and growth conditions
The bacterial strains and plasmids used in this study are listed in Table S1.
Salmonella enterica
serovar Typhimurium SL1344 was used as the wild-type (WT) strain in this study.
Salmonella
strains were routinely grown overnight to stationary phase at 37°C in Luria – Bertani (LB) medium. Then, using the overnight cultures for inoculation, the strains were diluted 1:20 into fresh LB broth supplemented with 0.3 mol/L NaCl to stimulate bacterial invasion. Antibiotics were added at the following final concentrations: 30 μg/mL streptomycin (Sm), 50 μg/mL kanamycin (Km), and 100 μg/mL ampicillin (Amp).
Genome engineering of bacterial strains
The primers used for gene mutation and plasmid construction in this study are also listed in Table S2. Using WT
S
. Typhimurium SL1344 as the parent strain, the ∆
hslV
, ∆
lon
and ∆
hslV
∆
lon
strains were constructed with the λ-Red recombination system [
27
]. The complemented strain of the ∆
hslV
(Δ
hslV
+pHslV) was generated by cloning the
hslV
gene into the pTrc99a vector for transient expression.
Salmonella
strains with a 3×Flag tag inserted at the N-terminus of Lon were constructed using the standard homologous recombination method with a temperature-sensitive plasmid, pHSG415. All constructed strains were confirmed by both PCR amplification and targeted Sanger sequencing.
Cell adhesion, invasion, and proliferation assay
The methods and time points used in the experiment assay of cells infected with
S
. Typhimurium were referred to Liu
et al
[
23
]. Briefly, cells were incubated in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, Life Technologies, CA, USA), 100 μg/mL streptomycin, and 100 U/mL penicillin, under a controlled environment of 37°C and 5% CO
2
. 1 × 10
5
cells were seeded onto 6-well cell culture plates and infected with
Salmonella
strains at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 100 for 1 hour when cell monolayers reached 80% confluence. For the invasion assay, cells were washed with PBS 1-hour post-infection and then further incubated in DMEM containing 100 μg/mL gentamicin for another hour until lysis. For the proliferation assay, cells were washed with PBS 1-hour post-infection and then incubated with 100 μg/mL gentamicin for 1 hour, followed by a further incubation in DMEM containing 10 μg/mL gentamicin for an additional 4 hours until lysis. To isolate intracellular bacteria, cells were washed three times with Hanks’ buffered salt solution (HBSS) and subsequently lysed in a lysis buffer consisting of Tris-HCl (pH7.6) buffer containing 150 mM NaCl and 0.1% Triton X-100. The colony-forming units (CFU) of bacteria were counted by serial dilutions and plating of the bacterial suspension on LB agar plates.
Immunofluorescence
At specific time points post-infection, cells were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 20 minutes, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for an additional 20 minutes, and then blocked in 5% goat serum at room temperature for 1 hour. Subsequently, cells were incubated with the primary antibody against
Salmonella
(1:2000, Abcam) at 4°C overnight. This followed by incubation with the secondary antibody, fluorescein FITC-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG (H+L) (1:100, NEST) at room temperature for 1 hour. Finally, nuclei were labeled with Hoechst 33342 (1:1000, Beyotime Biotech Inc) at room temperature for 10 minutes and images were captured using a TCS-SP8 STED confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica, Heidelberg, Germany).
Proteomic sample preparation and LC–MS/MS analysis
The bacteria were harvested when the optical density at 600 nm reached 0.9 as mentioned previously [
23
]. After centrifugation at 10,000 ×
g
for 3 minutes, the bacterial pellets were washed three times with PBS. Subsequently, the pellets were resuspended in SDS-PAGE loading buffer and denatured by incubating at 100°C for 5 minutes. Protein samples were prefractionated by 10% SDS-PAGE and excised into 6 slices per sample, which were digested in-gel by sequencing-grade trypsin (Promega, USA). The peptide samples were vacuum-dried and then reconstituted in HPLC-grade water prior to LC–MS/MS analysis. A hybrid LTQ–Orbitrap Elite mass spectrometer (coupled with an EASY-nLC 1200 system, Thermo Scientific) was applied for label-free proteomics analyses.
Mass spectrometry data process
For the purpose of data analysis, MaxQuant (version 1.5.3.38;
http://maxquant.org/
) was utilized to generate peak lists from the raw files, employing a merged protein sequence database that consisted of
S
. Typhimurium LT2 (Taxon identifier: 99,287) and SL1344 (Taxon identifier: 216,597) sequences downloaded from UniProt (
www.uniprot.org
). The precursor mass tolerance was configured to 20 ppm, while the fragment mass tolerance was established at 0.8 Da. Trypsin was set as the digestive enzyme with two potential missed cleavages. The minimum ratio counts were selected as 1.5. The false discovery rate (FDR) of peptides and proteins was limited to less than 1%. Only proteins with a minimum of two identified peptides were deemed quantifiable.
P
-values were determined through paired Student’s
t
-test employing the Perseus and subsequently adjusted using multiple hypothesis testing procedures [
28
]. Proteins with
p
< 0.05 and average fold change > 1.5 were considered significantly regulated.
Selected reaction monitoring (SRM) analyses of HslV
Due to the lack of commercially available antibodies, we used SRM to quantify the relative abundance of HslV in the WT strain, the
hslV
gene knockout strain (∆
hslV
), and the complemented strain (∆
hslV
+pHslV). Bacterial samples were in-gel digested (a single gel slice without separation of proteins) and the resulting peptides were analyzed on a linear ion trap mass spectrometer (LTQ Velos, Thermo Scientific, USA) coupled with nano-LC (EASY-nLC II, Thermo Scientific, USA). To quantify HslV, the transitions were used: 594.18→502.05 (ALLENTELGAR). Corresponding peak areas for these transitions were obtained from Thermo Xcalibur 2.2 (Thermo Scientific, USA).
Mouse infections
Wild-type C57BL/6J mice, aged 6 to 8 weeks and weighing 16 to 18 grams, were obtained from the Peking University Health Science Center, randomly assigned to groups, and used for infection studies. To mimic natural infection by
S
. Typhimurium, mice were pretreated with 100 mg/mL streptomycin and subsequently orally treated with 200 μL of PBS containing 2 × 10
5
CFU (colony-forming units) of
Salmonella
strains or an equal volume of PBS as a control. For competitive infection, mice (5 mice per group) were injected i.p. with a 1:1 mix of either (∆
hslV
and WT) or (∆
hslV
+pHslV and WT) strains, and were euthanized on day 6 post-infection. The CI value was calculated as (CFU of ∆
hslV
strain inoculated) or (CFU of ∆
hslV
+pHslV strain inoculated) / (CFU of WT strain inoculated).
S
. Typhimurium can spread rapidly through the reticuloendothelial system, causing infections of the liver and spleen [
29
]. To determine bacterial numbers in the liver and spleen, mice were infected with each
Salmonella
strain individually, and organs were isolated and analyzed 6 days post-infection. The survival rate of the mice was monitored every 24 hours for 6 days, and the CFUs were counted and reported as CFU per gram of organ. All mouse experiments were conducted according to the relevant regulations and guidelines, and were approved by the animal ethics committee of Peking University Health Science Center in Beijing, China (LA2023129).
Histological analysis
Organ tissues were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 3 days, then embedded in paraffin and cut into 5-μm serial sections. H&E staining was performed and the samples were photographed and analyzed using a microscope (Olympus CX31, Japan) equipped with a digital camera. The images were assessed with the assistance of CaseViewer software. Histopathological analysis was revealed that bacterial cells were visualized using immunohistochemistry on paraformaldehyde-fixed tissue sections with
Salmonella
antibodies (1:200, Abcam).
Validation of proteomic results by Western blot
Salmonella
strains were harvested by centrifugation, suspended in SDS-PAGE loading buffer and subjected to SDS-PAGE analysis. Gel-separated bacterial proteins were further transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore Corp., MA, USA). Immunoblotting analyses were performed using primary antibodies specific for Flag (1:2000, Zen-bio),
Salmonella
(1:5000, Abcam), DnaK (1:5000, Abcam), and FliC (1:10,000, Abcam), followed by horseradish peroxidase (HRP) -conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000, Cwbio).
Swimming and swarming assay
Swimming and swarming assays were performed according to the illustrations by Kim and Surette [
30
]. Bacterial cells that had been pre-cultivated overnight in LB broth were diluted by a factor of 1:20 into fresh LB broth (0.3 mol/L NaCl) and subsequently incubated for an additional 3 hours. The OD
600
value of bacterial cultures was adjusted to 0.9. And then 2 μL of overnight cultures of the desired strains were inoculated at the center of soft-agar plates. To assess swimming motility, swimming plates containing 0.25% agar. The plates were then incubated at 37°C for approximately 8 hours. To evaluate swarming motility, swarming plates were prepared with 0.5% agar. Subsequently, these plates were incubated at 37°C for approximately 48 hours.
Transmission electron microscopy
The strains induced with 0.3 mol/L NaCl and reaching OD
600
= 0.9 were fixed with electron microscope fixative, followed by negative staining, and then preserved at 4°C. After discarding the bacterial pellets, the remaining pellets were resuspended in distilled water. Aliquots of 1 μL from these cultures were then applied to 200-mesh copper grids and negatively stained with 0.5% (
w/v
) aqueous uranyl acetate for 10 seconds, followed by drying for 10 minutes. Visualization of flagella was achieved using a JEM-1400 electron microscope, and subsequently, the average number of flagella per cell was computed.
Biofilm formation assay
To quantify the biofilm formation, 200 μL cultures were distributed into the wells of a 96-well microplate and incubated for 24 hours or 48 hours at 37°C. For qualitative examination of biofilm formation, 2 mL cultures were distributed into acrylic bottles and incubated for 24 hours or 48 hours at 37°C. After discarding the bacterial solution, the unbound bacteria in the wells were carefully washed with distilled water three times. Then, 0.1% crystal violet staining solution was added to each well, and the plates were incubated at room temperature for 20 minutes. After rinsing with distilled water and drying, the biofilm stained with crystal violet was dissolved in 95% ethanol, and its absorbance was determined at OD
570
.
RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis
Total RNA was extracted from both
S
. Typhimurium and mice using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA), and subsequently reverse transcribed into cDNA using the HiScript III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme, Jiangsu, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The quantitative PCR was performed with Roche 480 System (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) using the SYBR Green method. To test the reliability of the proteomic or RNA-sequencing data, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were selected and their expression levels were analyzed by RT-qPCR. The relative levels of mouse mRNA were normalized with GAPDH, while the mRNA levels of
S
. Typhimurium were normalized with 16S rRNA. The relative mRNA levels were analyzed using the 2
−ΔΔCt
method. The sequence information for all the primers used in this study is presented in Table. S2.
Statistical analysis
Data were collected and presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) from at least three independent tests. Analysis of the data was performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0, Origin 2021, and Cytoscape 3.9.1. Where necessary, two-tailed Student’s
t
-tests or one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s
post hoc
analysis were employed. A
p
-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

S
. Typhimurium, we constructed a
hslV

) and a complemented strain (∆

hslV

in vitro
(
Figure 1(C)
).

To investigate the role of HslV in the adhesion, invasion and proliferation of
S
. Typhimurium, we utilized three cell lines: the epithelial cell line Caco-2 (susceptible to
S
. Typhimurium), the epithelial cell line HeLa (a common model for infection), and the macrophage cell line RAW264.7. Our results showed that the adhesion ability of the ∆
hslV
strain was significantly impaired compared to that of the WT strain (
Figure 1(D)
). Moreover, competitive index (CI) assays demonstrated a significant defect in the competitive ability of the ∆
hslV
strain to invade both epithelial cells and macrophages (
Figure 1(E)
). In contrast, the competitive ability of the ∆
hslV
+pHslV strain was not significantly different from that of the WT strain in Caco-2 cells (
Figure 1(F)
). The invasion ability of the ∆
hslV
strain into Caco-2, HeLa, and RAW264.7 cells was also significantly decreased compared to that of the WT strain (
Figure 1(G,H)
). Consistently, automated fluorescence microscopy revealed consistent results with reduced bacteria of
∆hslV
strain in the HeLa cells than the WT strain (Fig. S1). We then assessed the proliferation of intracellular
Salmonella
at 6 hours post-infection in infected cells. The results showed that the number of intracellular ∆
hslV
bacteria was reduced within RAW264.7 macrophages (
Figure 1(I)
), as well as within Caco-2 and HeLa cells (
Figure 1(J)
), compared to the WT bacteria. Furthermore, the number of intracellular Δ
hslV
+pHslV bacteria was significantly higher than that of ∆
hslV
bacteria and was restored to at least half that of the WT bacteria in Caco-2 and HeLa cells (
Figure 1(I,J)
). The results indicated that HslV may promote bacterial proliferation through the epithelial cell pathway.
In short, these data indicate that HslV plays an important role in facilitating the adherence and invasion of
S
. Typhimurium during infection of epithelial cells.
HslV contributes to the virulence of
S
. Typhimurium
To further investigate the role of HslV in the virulence of
S
. Typhimurium, we infected C57BL/6J mice with both WT and
∆hslV
strains of
S
. Typhimurium at a 1:1 ratio and then measured the bacterial load in mouse tissues 6 days after infection. As expected, CI assays showed that the deletion of the
hslV
gene significantly impaired the competitive invasion of
S
. Typhimurium in the liver, spleen, and small intestine (
Figure 2(A)
), and reported the competitive ability of the ∆
hslV
+pHslV strain was significantly different from that of the WT strain in small intestine (
Figure 2(B)
). Subsequently, we infected mice with WT, ∆
hslV
, and ∆
hslV+
pHslV strains, respectively, and observed the survival of mice infected with either the WT or ∆
hslV+
pHslV strain began to die on the third day after infection. In contrast, mortality in the ∆
hslV
group started on the fifth day post-infection. By the sixth day, the survival rate of mice infected with the ∆
hslV
strain remained at 50%, whereas all mice infected with the WT strain had succumbed to the infection (
Figure 2(C)
). We observed weight loss in all groups beginning on the second day post-infection, and found that mice infected with the ∆
hslV
strain exhibited a less pronounced weight loss compared to those infected with the WT or ∆
hslV+
pHslV strains (
Figure 2(D)
). Correspondingly, we observed that the ∆
hslV
strain exhibited significantly reduced bacterial loads in the liver, kidney, spleen, small intestine, and colorectum of infected mice compared to the WT strain (
Figure 2(E)
). This decreased colonization ability was further supported by immunofluorescence imaging, which showed markedly less invasion into small intestinal tissue by the ∆
hslV
strain relative to the WT (
Figure 2(F,G)
). Moreover, H&E staining and histopathological analysis revealed that infection with the ∆
hslV
strain, compared to WT, resulted in neither significant intestinal shortening nor a markedly enhanced inflammatory response in the small intestine (
Figure 2(H,I)
), a finding that was consistently observed in the liver and spleen as well (Fig. S2 and S3). Meanwhile, we performed RNA sequencing on the small intestines of mice treated with these strains. KEGG pathway analysis indicated that the down-regulated genes in the small intestines of mice treated with the Δ
hslV
strain were predominantly enriched in lymphocyte-mediated immunity (Fig. S4A). We also found the mRNA levels of four pro-inflammatory cytokines genes (IL-1β, IL-6, IL-18, and TNF-α), selected from this pathway, were down-regulated. This revealed that the Δ
hslV
strain lacked the capability to trigger intestinal inflammation in mice (Fig. S4B).

Comprehensive proteome analysis reveals the biological functions of HslV in
S
. Typhimurium
Quantitative proteomic profiling was employed to investigate the mechanism of HslV in
S
. Typhimurium pathogenicity. In total, 1058 proteins were identified across three biological replicates. Among which, 24 up-regulated and 59 down-regulated proteins were identified in ∆
hslV
using a fold change cutoff of > 1.5 and
p
value < 0.05 in the protein-level volcano plot (
Figure 3(A)
and Table S1). One of the most striking changes observed was the remarkably downregulation of T3SS-1 effectors (e.g. SipA, SopB, SipC), and a markedly suppression of flagellar proteins, including FliA and FliC in the ∆
hslV
strains. Furthermore, the levels of several heat shock proteins (e.g. UspA, IbpB, ClpB, Lon) were significantly up-regulated (Table S3). KEGG pathway analysis showed that the differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were primarily enriched in the
Salmonella
infection pathway and other pathways associated with bacterial pathogenicity (
Figure 3(B)
). We clustered the proteins Lon, FliC, SipA, SopB, SipC, and SipD, which are associated with
Salmonella
infection pathway (
Figure 3(C)
). This analysis revealed changes in the expression of these functionally related proteins, which were further confirmed by Western blot analysis and TCA precipitation. Specifically, these methods confirmed the downregulation of FliC, SipA, and SipC and the upregulation of Lon in the ∆
hslV
strain (
Figure 3(D–F)
). To deeper understand the functional associations among DEPs, we constructed a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network, which resulted in a network of 81 genes (node genes) and 144 edges. The results revealed enrichment of these proteins in processes such as
Salmonella
infection and a mixture of function, including bacterial chemotaxis and the protein folding pathway composed of Lon and HslV (PPI enrichment
p
value < 1.0 e
−16
for both). Notably, Lon and HslV were predicted to be co-expressed with a co-expression score of 0.786, derived from GEO (Gene Expression Omnibus) microarray expression data by the STRING v12 database (
Figure 3(G)
).

Discussion
Studies from 2003 to 2017 indicate that
Salmonella
, which accounts for over 70% of bacterial food poisoning cases in China, was responsible for nearly a thousand foodborne disease outbreaks [
29
,
30
,
32
,
33
]. Currently, the threat to human health posed by bacterial pathogenicity and antibiotic resistance is increasing, underscoring the urgent need to identify new targets for combating infectious diseases and addressing antibiotic resistance. Here, we investigated the mechanistic basis of the T3SS virulence proteins in the clinically important pathogen
S
. Typhimurium, one of the most prevalent serotypes of
Salmonella
. We found that HslV enhances the transcription of T3SS-1 virulence factors by suppressing
lon
expression, which impromotes bacterial virulence. This supports prior studies, which indicated that HslV is upregulated in
S
. Typhimurium upon infection of epithelial cells [
22–24
]. We propose a model for the crosstalk among HslV, Lon, and T3SS-1 virulence proteins: knockout of
hslV
in
S
. Typhimurium upregulates Lon and downregulates flagellar proteins and T3SS-1 effectors. This in turn elicits defects in bacterial motility, flagellar assembly, and biofilm formation, which ultimately restraining adhesion to, invasion of, and intracellular survival within host cells (
Figure 6
). Our findings represent the first report on the combined effect of HslV and Lon on T3SS-1 virulence proteins.
The Lon protease regulates the pathogenicity of
S
. Typhimurium and negatively controls the expression of T3SS-1 virulence factors by degrading key regulatory proteins, HilC and HilD, thereby suppressing effector secretion [
34–39
]. In this study, the Δ
lon
strain exhibited enhanced biofilm formation (Fig. S5), increased invasion of HeLa cells (
Figure 5D
), and greater bacterial proliferation in a mouse model (
Figure 5E
), consistent with earlier studies on the Δ
lon
strain [
40
,
41
]. Western blot analysis revealed that the expression level of Lon was significantly upregulated in the Δ
hslV
strain compared to the WT strain, and this upregulation was further enhanced under heat shock stress (
Figure 5A
). These results are in accord with previous studies indicating that both HslUV and Lon modulate the stability of RpoH (σ32), an essential transcription factor for the heat shock response, thereby regulating the expression of heat shock proteins [
42
]. We found that ∆
lon
strain showed higher bacterial loads in the small intestine and colorectum compared to WT strain. In contrast, it has been reported that under oxidative stress conditions, deletion of the
lon
gene in
S
. Typhimurium promotes the release of host inflammatory factors, leading to premature bacterial clearance [
40
]. A possible explanation for this might be that Lon protease degrades abnormal proteins and its upregulation does not necessarily attenuate virulence. In future studies, we will investigate whether the observed effects are directly attributable to the absence of HslV.
Although studies have indicated structural and functional similarities between HslV and Lon, these proteases have typically been reported separately in the literature [
43
]. Even in cases where they have been compared, a direct functional connection between them has not been conclusively established. Our proteomic analysis revealed that the knockout of the
hslV
gene upregulates the expression of Lon and downregulates of T3SS-1 effectors (Table S1). Additionally, RT-qPCR results showed that the mRNA expression of the
lon
gene was also markedly increased in the Δ
hslV
strain. In contrast, no significant change was observed in the mRNA expression of the
hslV
gene in the Δ
lon
strain (
Figure 5B
). Further investigation demonstrated that the mRNA expression of the virulence genes (e.g.
sipA
,
sopB
,
sipC
) was significantly decreased in the Δ
hslV
strain relative to the WT strain, whereas their expression was notably increased in the Δ
lon
strain. In the Δ
hslV
Δ
lon
strain, the mRNA levels of these virulence factors were higher than those in the Δ
hslV
strain. These results suggesting that HslV may indirectly promote the expression of virulence genes by modulating the Lon expression (
Figure 5C
). Based on this mechanism, inhibiting HslV expression indirectly suppresses the secretion of
Salmonella
’s effector proteins, thereby reducing the bacterium’s ability to elicit inflammatory responses in mice. These results are consistent with the established view that the T3SS-1 of
S
. Typhimurium mediates invasion and inflammation in susceptible C57BL/6 mice [
44
]. However, it remains unclear whether HslV promotes the release of virulence proteins indirectly through Lon or directly through other mechanisms.
Biofilms are crucial for microbial survival and enhance resistance to host defense mechanisms [
15
]. Previous reports have shown that flagellar motility, involving FliC, was associated with a reduced ability to form biofilms and affected
Salmonella
’s motility behavior on host cell surfaces [
9
,
10
]. Biofilm formation enables
Salmonella
to establish long-term colonization at various sites within and outside the animal host, thereby enhancing bacterial survival and facilitating transmission [
16
]. In line with these findings, our results indicate that the Δ
hslV
strain has a reduced capacity to form biofilms under natural conditions and to colonize animal organs compared to the WT strain (
Figure 4C
). There is also evidence suggesting that HslV is involved in bacterial motility and biofilm formation [
45
]. Contrary to earlier findings, however, Δ
hslV
strain showed no altered biofilm production compared to the WT
Vibrio Cholerae
(
V. Cholerae
) [
31
]. We speculate that this might be due to the differences in amino acids and overall functions of HslV in
V. Cholerae
and
S
. Typhimurium. Because of alterations in biofilms that boosts antimicrobial resistance increase the risk of treatment failure [
46
]. We hypothesize that the knockout of HslV can contribute to reducing biofilm formation, thereby alleviating the lethal effects and resistance of
S
. Typhimurium. This highlights the broader impact of HslV on
S
. Typhimurium’s adaptive strategies.
Collectively, our data suggest that HslV is a critical factor that modulates the virulence of this bacterium. However, this study has several limitations: (1) Although HslV has been implicated in adhesion and invasion through the epithelial cell pathway in both cellular and murine infection models, its precise regulatory mechanism remains unclear. More comprehensive infection models are needed to further explore the underlying mechanisms. (2) While numerous studies have characterized Lon and Δ
lon
strains, our study did not systematically compare the Δ
lon
, Δ
hslV
, and Δ
hslV
Δ
lon
mutants for their effects on bacterial adhesion, motility, biofilm formation, and abnormal protein degradation. Such comparisons would help clarify the regulatory relationship between HslV and the
lon
gene. (3) The specific mechanisms by which HslV inhibits
lon
gene expression remain unknown. Future studies should employ techniques such as ChIP-qPCR and yeast two-hybrid assays to delineate the HslV-Lon regulatory network. (4) Although HslV plays an important role in intracellular protein degradation and virulence in pathogens, and represents an attractive antibiotic target due to its absence in human cells, development of HslV-targeted therapeutics faces challenges. Even attenuated HslV mutants retain cytotoxicity through their protein degradation activity. Thus, pharmacological targeting of HslV protease requires further extensive investigation. In summary, our findings delineate the HslV-Lon regulatory axis, advancing understanding of
S
. Typhimurium’s pathogenesis. This work establishes HslV as a potential therapeutic target and suggests novel intervention strategies against
Salmonella
infections.
