Introduction
The widespread production and use of synthetic
chemicals necessitate
rapid and reliable detection and identification of anthropogenic chemicals
in water resources such as groundwater, surface water, and drinking
water.
Chromatography coupled to high-resolution
mass spectrometry (HRMS) has become an important analytical tool for
environmental monitoring and risk assessment, enabling trace-level
detection of pharmaceuticals, pesticides, industrial chemicals, and
their transformation products (TPs).
Unlike
target screening, which is constrained by the availability of reference
standards, ChromatographyHRMS allows for broader chemical
coverage and supports the identification of contaminants of emerging
concern (CECs).
Over the past decade,
reversed-phase liquid chromatography (RP-LC)
coupled to HRMS has become the “gold standard” for environmental
nontarget screening (NTS), due to its large chemical space coverage,
accessibility, robustness, and “ease-of-use”. As a result,
the vast majority of environmental NTS studies rely on RP-LC- using
C
18
-based columns.
While RP-LC
provides good retention for nonpolar to moderately polar compounds,
it has limited ability to retain and separate highly polar and ionic
substances.
,
Recent studies have shown that restricting
analyses to RP-LC can
lead to knowledge gaps, particularly for compounds with log
D
pH7.4
< 0.
−
Of particular concern are highly polar and
mobile compounds, which are often persistent (so-called PM substances)
and thus capable of migrating from surface water into groundwater
and eventually into drinking water resources.
,
Furthermore,
commonly applied water purification steps (e.g., sorption to activated
carbon) are typically less effective in the removal of PM substances.
,
To address these limitations, several studies have introduced
alternative
chromatographic techniques better suited for polar compounds, including
hydrophilic interaction chromatography (HILIC),
,
supercritical fluid chromatography (SFC),
,
and
ion chromatography (IC),
,
all coupled to HRMS.
Although less established than RP-LC, these techniques enable the
detection of environmentally relevant compounds that are commonly
overlooked by RP-LC methods.
Each platform
has specific advantages and limitations. For example, HILIC is more
difficult to operate, more sensitive to operational parameters than
RP-LC.
HILIC and SFC typically do not
allow injection of water which is associated with the need of extra
sample preparation.
IC typically requires
high-salt eluents and suppressors and requires a net charge to achieve
separation (either anionic or cationic) while neutral highly polar
molecules can get lost.
Therefore, the
combination of multiple chromatographic platforms with complementary
retention profiles can improve the overall detection coverage.
,
,
While several studies have
paired two or three platforms (e.g.,
RP-LC with HILIC or SFC),
,
,
detailed and systematic comparisons of more than two chromatographic
methods are scarce which assess platform-specific strengths, overlaps,
and limitations in detecting a wide range of environmentally relevant
compounds.
The aim of this study
is to systematically compare the four most
widely used chromatographic platforms for environmental NTS of water
samples: RP-LC (including mixed mode or modified C
18
),
HILIC, SFC, and IC, each coupled to HRMS. A standard mixture of 127
compounds, covering log
D
pH7.4
of more
than 12 orders of magnitude, was used to assess compound detectability,
retention, peak shape, ionization efficiency, and adduct formation.
In addition, a subset of 30 highly polar analytes was spiked into
a groundwater matrix and enriched using vacuum evaporation concentration
(VEC) to evaluate platform performance in a realistic sample matrix.
A total of 12 standard operating methods were applied across four
laboratories, comprising 5 RP-LC, 3 (ZICc)­(i)­HILIC, 2 SFC, and 2 IC
methods, using five different HRMS instruments. Each chromatographic
platform was represented by at least two methods. We evaluate how
these platforms complement one another, identify their specific strengths
and limitations, and provide guidance on selecting and combining chromatographic
approaches to improve chemical space coverage in NTS workflows.

Data Evaluation
All vendor-specific MS raw files were
centroided prior to analysis, except for Agilent files, which were
already acquired in centroid mode. Files were converted to mzML format
using MSConvert.
Due to differences in
signal intensities between instrument types (arbitrary units, e.g.,
QTOF vs Orbitrap), baseline noise levels varied also between platforms.
To estimate appropriate intensity thresholds for each method, the
raw data overview tool in MZmine 4.2.0 was used. Noise histograms
were manually inspected to determine method-specific thresholds under
the assumption that low intensity values are the dominant contribution
in HRMS data. Therefore, an intensity value was chosen at the upper
tail of the noise distribution, where the frequency of low-intensity
peaks drops off sharply, indicating the transition from background
noise to potential signal (see
Figure S1
).
Initial feature detection was
performed using patRoon with standard
parameters, using the estimated noise levels as minimum intensity
thresholds for each platform (detailed parameters in
SI
).
To increase coverage and
reduce missed detections, features identified by both OpenMS
and XCMS
were combined.
,
Despite this, significant numbers of expected target features were
still not detected, likely due to differences in chromatographic signal
quality between platforms (e.g., peak width, baseline noise; see
Figure S2
and
Results and Discussion
).
To address this limitation and reduce false negatives, a
targeted
EIC-based workflow was implemented. This in-house Python-based approach
was applied uniformly for all data sets to extract compound-specific
EICs, independent of peak shape or feature boundaries. Similar EIC-based
methods have been successfully applied in previous studies where conventional
feature detection underperformed for noisy or irregular chromatograms.
,
,
The following workflow
was applied individually to each method
across all platforms, and the results were merged after processing.
Sample and blank pairs were loaded using the Python package
pyopenms
,
and for each compound
in the standard mixture, a complete EIC was generated using the exact
mass ± 2.5 mDa. Initially, EICs were extracted for [M + H]
+
and [M-H]
−
, depending on the ESI mode.
For compounds not detected in these primary forms, additional adducts
were considered: [M + Na]
+
, [M+K]
+
, and [M+NH
4
]
+
in ESI
+
; and [M+Cl]
−
, [M+Br]
−
, [M+COOH]
−
, and [M+CH
3
COOH]
−
in ESI
–
.
Peak detection was performed using
scipy
;
identifying local maxima
in each EIC that exceeded
5× the method-specific noise threshold (defined by method-specific
noise threshold in
Table
). The five highest peaks in each EIC were considered for
further evaluation. Peak areas were integrated at 3% of peak height
on either side of the apex. Full width at half-maximum (fwhm) was
also calculated using the same peak profile.
To increase confidence
in standard identification, isotope pattern
matching was performed. For each peak, the nearest MS
1
scan
at the retention time (RT) apex was used to extract the isotope cluster.
A mirror plot comparing the experimental and theoretical isotope patterns
was generated, and an isotope score was calculated following an approach
adapted from Pluskal et al. 2012.
For isotope matching,
m
/
z
values
falling outside ± 0.1 Da of theoretical [M+0] to [M+3] peaks
were discarded. Additionally, peaks with a chemical mass defect (MD)
differing by >20 mDa from the theoretical [M+0] were excluded to
reduce
interference from unrelated ions (still includes isotopes of C, S,
Cl, or Br). The closest remaining peaks to the theoretical isotope
masses were selected to calculate the isotope score and mass error
in ppm.
Signal-to-noise (S/N) ratio was estimated by dividing
the peak
height by the standard deviation of the EIC in a region spanning RT
+ 5 × fwhm to RT + 10 × fwhm. As this calculation depends
on the EIC extraction width, it should be regarded as an approximation.
All resulting figures and metadata were compiled into an interactive
HTML table for manual inspection (see
Figure S3
). Each EIC was visually reviewed for peak shape, isotope pattern
match, and, when available, MS
2
spectra with library MS
2
spectra. Peaks with S/N ratio <5 were excluded. In very
rare cases when more than one peak was present, the largest one was
chosen. The confirmed detections were compiled into curated tables
and were merged across platforms using the unique compound name as
a key. Despite the laborious manual inspection of all EICs (in duplicates),
some false negatives could remain for compounds forming adducts not
covered here. The Python source code of the EIC-based workflow is
available on Zenodo (
10.5281/zenodo.17062819
).
To ensure that only truly retained
compounds were considered, the
dead time (
t
0
) was estimated by identifying
the RT of the first eluting compounds. This was determined from
m
/
z
vs RT scatter plots, where a wide range
of masses (e.g.,
m
/
z
100 –
1000) eluted simultaneously after feature detection (examples in
Figure S4
). To confirm
t
0
, it was also calculated from column dimensions and flow rate
(
SI
). A compound was considered detectable
if its retention factor was
k
> 0.5, corresponding
to 1.5 ×
t
0
. Borderline compounds
close to
k
= 0.5 might be in- or excluded in case
of pH shifts.
For spiked groundwater samples, compounds eluting
in the dead volume
were retained, because the presence of matrix ensured environmental
relevance. In nontarget screening workflows, compounds are likewise
not discarded solely due to low retention if identification evidence
is strong enough (e.g., good quality MS/MS). Detectability was therefore
evaluated based on compound appearance, while acknowledging that matrix
effects differ between analytical platforms. This consideration is
particularly important for platforms that retain salts, as these can
cause significant matrix effects even at high retention times.
Statistical analyses were carried out in Python 3.9.13. For cluster
analysis, the cluster map method from
seaborn
(0.13.2) was applied (default parameters) to
the respective correlation matrix (RT, and intensity) based on the
Pearson correlation coefficient. For principal component analysis
(PCA), the binary matrix of detections was used as input.
Results
and Discussion
This study evaluated the performance of 12
chromatographic methods
across RP-LC, HILIC, SFC, and IC platforms using a standard mixture
of 127 environmentally relevant compounds, along with a subset of
30 highly polar compounds spiked into groundwater and enriched via
VEC. This data was used to perform an in-depth platform comparison
with respect to detection capabilities, retention profiles, platform
complementarity, and ionization efficiency.
Initial attempts
to apply conventional feature detection, combining
results from OpenMS
and XCMS,
resulted in a significant number of missed detections.
On average, 28% of expected compounds were not detected (7 methods
tested) when using fixed parameters (typical RP-LC settings), despite
being clearly visible in the raw data when examined manually using
the targeted EIC-based workflow. The problem was more pronounced on
SFC and HILIC showing slightly higher false negative rates than RP-LC
(
Figure S2
). This discrepancy is likely
because these feature detection algorithms were developed and optimized
for RP-LC-HRMS data making it more difficult to fine-tune them for
other platforms. Their default settings, especially for peak width
and shape, do not generalize well to data sets with distinct chromatographic
properties, such as much broader peaks. In addition to broader peak
shapes, IC and HILIC frequently showed baseline noise within peak
boundaries (also in the peak apex), which was likely a reason for
false negatives during feature detection as S/N thresholding might
reject even large peaks if they are noisy overall (
Figure S5
).
In several SFC and IC data sets, false negatives
corresponded to
lower-intensity peaks or unusually broad peak profiles (
Figure S6
). In these cases, peaks were potentially
excluded due to constraints in the algorithms, such as minimum peak
width or a fixed minimum S/N ratio. No single cause for these omissions
could be identified, but the issue was consistent in platforms with
more different chromatographic methods. Although false negatives are
rarely investigated, it is well-known that different feature detection
algorithms produce different results.
−
Parameter optimization
could, in principle, reduce false negatives. However, tuning 12 methods,
each with unique gradient profiles, column chemistries, eluent systems,
and instrument-specific behavior, is nontrivial. As the platform comparison
in this study focused on target-level performance, labor-intensive
and method-specific optimization was deliberately omitted. In this
study, minimizing such bias was critical for drawing meaningful conclusions.
Therefore, all data were preprocessed using a targeted EIC-based workflow
to ensure consistent and traceable evaluation between platforms, independent
of software heuristics.
Comparison of Individual Method Compound
Detectability
An evaluation of compound detectability was
performed between the
12 methods, where all compounds with a retention factor of
k
> 0.5 (which corresponds to a RT > 1.5 ×
t
0
) were considered detectable. In total, when
combining all methods, 125 of 127 compounds were detected, while folpet
and disulfoton (lacking ESI-active functional groups) remained undetected.
The majority of compounds were detected either as [M + H]
+
or [M-H]
−
(ranging from 58% (HILIC
Lab2, neg
) to 100%, median 93%), while in particular in ESI
–
on certain HILIC methods increased numbers of [M+COO]
−
adducts were observed, presumably because all used NH
4
FA as modifier (all adducts are shown in
Figure S7
). 1-Hydroxybenzotriazole was the only compound detected
by all methods (in at least one polarity). The number of compound
detections categorized by how many of the total 12 methods detected
each compound (from 1/12 to 12/12) followed an approximately normal
distribution (
Figure
a). Two compounds, acryloyldimethyltaurate and paracetamol, were
detected by 11 of 12 methods (undetected only by MM-LC
Lab3c
), while mecoprop, amitriptyline, and citalopram were detected by
10 methods. Generally, compounds detected by the majority of methods
(at least 7/12) either contained at least one nitrogen (amine group)
or were permanently charged (e.g., sulfonic acids) making them well
amenable to both ESI and a range of chromatographic conditions. Many
of these were typical (RP)­LC-MS analytes, known for their good ionizability
which were not detected by the IC methods, particularly due to poor
negative ESI ionization.

environmental samples, where matrix effects and sample preparation
often reduce coverage.

As qNTS gains attention, the interlaboratory

The observation that methods with different eluent compositions,
pH values, MS instruments, and even platform types still showed correlations
in the range of 0.7–0.9 suggests that qNTS models may become
transferable between platforms. However, this potential must be validated
using a representative range of compounds. In contrast, the two SFC
methods showed weak or no correlation with other platforms, indicating
distinct ionization behavior. This means that, for SFC, the prediction
of ionization efficiency is not inherently more difficult, but the
underlying processes differ from those in RP-LC. Consequently, models
developed are difficult to directly transfer to SFC, and SFC requires
its own dedicated modeling framework. Bieber et al. 2023 demonstrated
that while a qNTS model trained on RP-LC data could be applied to
SFC, performance improved significantly when trained directly on SFC
data.
This comparison shows that
cross-platform model transfer may be
feasible in some cases, particularly among LC-based methods, but larger
differences, especially with SFC, show the need for caution and may
limit the applicability of universal qNTS models. The correlation
approach itself has limitations: it only captures linear associations,
is more reliable for abundant features, and underrepresents low-intensity
compounds. While the observed correlations reveal consistent trends,
robust transferability between instruments or laboratories would require
normalization with internal standards and reference compounds.
Conclusions
This study provides a first comprehensive, compound-level evaluation
of four chromatographic platforms, RP-LC, HILIC, SFC, and IC, used
for environmental NTS of water samples. By systematically comparing
12 methods across 127 environmentally relevant compounds and spiked
groundwater samples, we demonstrate that no single platform is sufficient
to cover the full polarity and structural variability of target analytes.
RP-LC remains the most broadly applicable platform, particularly for
moderately polar and hydrophobic compounds. However, it lacks adequate
retention for highly polar and ionic substances, and this is where
complementary techniques such as SFC, HILIC, and IC are needed. SFC
uniquely bridges polar and nonpolar domains, while HILIC and IC are
essential for capturing highly polar contaminants, including problematic
PM substances. Importantly, each platform contributed unique detections,
demonstrating the need for platform complementarity to achieve full
chemical space coverage. The limited overlap in detections, combined
with platform-specific strengths, supports a clear recommendation:
at least two orthogonal methods should be employed in environmental
NTS workflows. This is especially critical when dealing with real-world
matrices, where matrix effects and sample preparation can significantly
reduce detectability. Beyond coverage, the study shows methodological
implications for data processing. Established RP-LC-centric workflows
underperform on platforms with broader peaks and noisier baselines,
such as HILIC and IC. Without specialized preprocessing, e.g., optimized
thresholds and feature detection settings, there is a great risk of
false negatives, particularly for polar analytes. Moreover, detection
of low-mass compounds, those with nonstandard adducts, or unusual
isotope patterns requires thoughtful adjustment of default processing
workflows. Finally, we show that interplatform correlation of retention
time is consistent, but ionization efficiency is more variable, especially
in SFC. This variability directly affects the transferability of qNTS
models. While model transfer may be feasible among LC-based platforms,
SFC may require platform-specific training due to distinct ionization
behavior. These results are key for the development of predictive
models in future high-throughput screening workflows. In conclusion,
this study provides a foundation for platform-aware NTS method development
and benchmarking. We recommend future work to expand compound sets
and real sample types, to better identify platform-specific blind
spots and further refine multiplatform strategies that maximize detection,
confidence, and quantification of CECs.
