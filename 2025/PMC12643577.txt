Summary
Neurons, astrocytes and microglia appear to synthesise immunoglobulins via canonical and noncanonical mechanisms.
Intrinsic antibodies may tag synapses for complement‐mediated pruning during development.
Astrocytic IgG2b stabilises lineage identity and couples amino acid metabolism to neuronal mTOR signalling.
Spinal cord injury triggers cell‐type–specific class switching that modulates axon regeneration.
Brain‐derived antibodies could open therapeutic avenues for neuroimmune disorders.

Introduction: From Immunological Dogma to Neuroimmune Symbiosis
The conviction that immunoglobulin production is the exclusive birthright of B‐lineage lymphocytes dominated immunology well into the 1990s. Yet immunoglobulin‐like immunoreactivity in the developing cortex had been reported as early as 1992, when Fairén and colleagues detected antirat IgG staining in the neuropil layer of neonatal rats (Fairén et al.
1992
). Simultaneously, monoclonal antibody screens against kitten cortex yielded subplate‐1 (SP1) protein, a reagent that labelled soma, dendrites and axons of subplate neurons and recognised a cytosolic 56‐kDa protein (Naegele et al.
1991
). Subsequent affinity purification and Edman degradation revealed that the first 20 amino acids of this protein shared ~80% identity with mammalian immunoglobulin heavy chains, strongly implying an antibody fold (Henschel and Wahle
1994
). The finding was initially dismissed as blood contamination, but two observations proved stubborn. First, the SP1 antigen persisted after exhaustive perfusion that eliminated intravascular proteins; second, polyclonal antisera against cat IgG intensely stained subplate neurons in situ, and preabsorbing SP1 with purified IgG abolished cortical reactivity (Henschel and Wahle
1994
). The original monoclonal antibody screen that produced SP1 was soon followed by a complementary study showing that the epitope is not confined to the subplate's fusiform pioneers but also marks a population of inverted pyramidal neurons and GABAergic interneurons scattered through the cat subplate zone (Wahle et al.
1994
). By 4 weeks postnatal, levels of the 56‐kDa antigen decreased in cortex and an additional higher molecular weight SP‐1–reactive polypeptide of 75 kDa was detected. In the mature cortex, both polypeptides were absent from cytosolic fractions. Immunocytochemical staining comparing the distributions of SP‐1 (SP‐1+) and anti‐IgG (IgG+) immunoreactive neurons showed complete colocalisation in subplate neurons beneath primary visual cortex. By 4 weeks, some pyramidal neurons in cortical areas 17 and 18 were weakly positive for SP‐1 but negative for IgG (Dunn et al.
1995
). These findings broadened the cellular canvas of SP1 and hinted that the protein might partake in both excitatory and inhibitory microcircuits, foreshadowing later work on the subplate's role as a transient integration hub (Hoerder‐Suabedissen and Molnár
2015
).
Two hypotheses subsequently emerged to explain IgG detected in brain neurons: (i) Neurons themselves produce immunoglobulins (Yoshimi et al.
2002
; Huang et al.
2008
), and (ii) IgG can be transported across the blood–brain barrier (BBB), for example, during barrier immaturity or breach, and handled by the neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn) (Risau and Wolburg
1990
; Archelos et al.
2000
; Schlachetzki et al.
2002
). Recently, single‐cell RNA‐seq consortia such as the BRAIN Initiative Cell Census have reported low‐abundance Ighm and Ighg reads across multiple neural clusters (Ecker et al.
2017
). Clarifying the effector cells, cultured dopaminergic neurons were shown to secrete IgG that engages microglial FcγRI (and TLR4), reducing nitric‐oxide release and inducing neuroprotective cytokines while conferring resistance to 6‐hydroxydopamine (6‐OHDA) toxicity (Zhang et al.
2013
). Complementing this, after spinal cord injury (SCI), cell‐type–specific IgG production and Fc receptor signalling correlate with neurite growth phenotypes (Capuz, Karnoub, et al.
2022
). Capuz and collaborators established the presence of aberrant IgG in astrocytes using transcriptomics, proteomics, CRISPR‐Cas9 and interactome approaches (Capuz, Osien, Cardon, et al.
2023
; Capuz, Osien, Karnoub, et al.
2023
). Such data reframed intrinsic antibodies from artefactual curiosities to plausible neuromodulatory agents.
The historical narrative is not merely academic; it reshapes translational strategy. The BBB remains a formidable obstacle for monoclonal antibody therapeutics. If neurons and glia already possess endogenous antibody factories, gene‐transfer approaches could encode therapeutic variable regions under native promoters, ensuring physiological stoichiometry and circumventing peripheral dosing. Moreover, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) Ig repertoires are emerging biomarkers in multiple sclerosis (MS) and paraneoplastic encephalitis (Awad et al.
2010
; Beseler et al.
2017
). Understanding the baseline presence of neural Ig transcripts is critical before attributing diagnostic weight to intrathecal oligoclonal bands (Gastaldi et al.
2017
). Against this backdrop the present review adopts four goals. First, we collate the evidence that diverse neural lineages express immunoglobulins across developmental stages and in response to injury. Second, we dissect the molecular machinery that could underwrite V(D)J‐like recombination outside the lymphoid context. Third, we analyse functional roles ranging from developmental apoptosis and synaptic pruning to astrocyte‐to‐neuron reprogramming and axonal regeneration. Fourth, we critically evaluate therapeutic opportunities, biomarker potential, and unresolved questions. While each section is self‐contained, the argument is cumulative: Intrinsic antibodies are not isolated oddities but potential nodes in a complex neuroimmune network (Box
1
).

Cellular Sources and Molecular Diversity
Neural immunoglobulins do not arise from a single rogue lineage; rather, they emerge from microglia, astrocytes, neurons and, to a lesser extent, oligodendrocyte precursors. The diversity spans membrane IgM, secreted IgG subclasses, truncated variable‐region peptides and ghost proteins derived from pseudogenes.
Astrocytes illustrate this diversity. Using RNA‐seq, RACE‐PCR, shotgun proteomics and proximity‐labelling, Capuz and colleagues demonstrated that newborn rat astrocytes generate full‐length heavy‐ and light chain transcripts alongside variable regions that substitute λ for κ usage, a feature typical of pre‐B cells (Capuz, Osien, Karnoub, et al.
2023
). Remarkably, CRISPR‐Cas9 knock‐out of the
IgG2b
constant region shifts astrocytes toward a dedifferentiated BMP/YAP1/TEAD3 transcriptional state, whereas overexpression activates the CRTC1–CREB–BDNF axis associated with gliogenesis. These data position astroglial IgG chains as fate stabilisers, a concept reinforced by the identification of Heimdall, a variable‐region‐related alternative protein whose knockdown triggers expression of axon‐guidance factors such as SLIT3 and SEMA3F (Capuz, Osien, Cardon, et al.
2023
). Microglia, the resident macrophages of the central nervous system (CNS), express membrane IgM transcripts from embryonic Day 14 onwards and undergo an injury‐induced isotype switch to IgG1 and
IgG2c
in spinal cord lesion models (Morimoto et al.
2025
). This switch parallels the transition from a growth‐permissive milieu to a phagocytic, cytokine‐rich environment, implicating Fc‐receptor signalling in regeneration.
Neuronal expression has two epochs. During early postnatal life, subplate neurons accumulate the 56‐kDa SP1 antigen that shares eight‐tenths of its N‐terminal sequence with mammalian Ig heavy chains (Henschel and Wahle
1994
). The antigen peaks in the second postnatal week, coinciding with macrophage invasion and massive programmed cell death, and disappears by the end of the first month, suggesting a role in apoptosis modulation (Henschel and Wahle
1994
; Dunn et al.
1995
). In adulthood, single‐cell atlases reveal corticothalamic neurons that coexpress Ighm and
Ighg2c
, hinting at ongoing complement regulation in deep‐layer circuits (Scheurer et al.
2021
; Morimoto et al.
2025
). Oligodendrocyte precursor cells contribute indirectly: They express the Fcα/μ receptor, a pattern that may facilitate response to paracrine IgM (Morimoto et al.
2025
) (Table
1
).

Molecular Mechanisms of Immunoglobulin Generation in Neural Cells
Before detailing developmental and pathological roles, the next instalment will dissect how neural cells achieve V(D)J‐like diversity despite minimal RAG1/2 expression. We compare three models: sparse canonical recombination, splice‐and‐link transcript assembly and retroelement‐mediated recombination. Evidence is marshalled from CRISPR knockouts, alignments to IMGT databases
(Capuz, Osien, Karnoub, et al.
2023
)
and variable‐region topology
(Scheurer et al.
2021
; Capuz, Osien, Karnoub, et al.
2023
; Morimoto et al.
2025
) (Figure
1
).
FIGURE 1
Neural V(D)J‐like diversity: three proposed models. This illustration summarises three hypothesised mechanisms by which neurons, astrocytes and microglia may generate immunoglobulin‐like receptor diversity. A translucent brain silhouette at the centre highlights representative neural cells to which arrows from three peripheral panels converge. The upper‐left panel depicts the ‘cryptic RAG’ model, in which trace levels of RAG1/2 endonucleases mediate canonical V‐(D)‐J recombination of genomic segments, a process validated by CRISPR knock‐out experiments that reduce variable‐region repertoire complexity. The upper‐right panel illustrates the ‘splice‐and‐link’ model: distant exons within pre‐mRNA are joined by trans‐splicing to form variable‐like cassettes without introducing DNA breaks, a mechanism supported by Magic‐BLAST alignments to IMGT germ line databases. The lower panel portrays the ‘retro‐element’ model, where endogenous retrotransposons supply recombination signal sequence–like motifs and strand breaks at the IgH locus to promote rearrangement; variable‐region topology mapping further supports this route. Together, the three models are not mutually exclusive and may operate in parallel to equip neural cells with an antibody‐like receptor repertoire that could influence development and pathology.
The generation of receptor diversity in vertebrate lymphocytes rests on canonical V(D)J recombination driven by RAG1/2 endonucleases and error‐prone non‐homologous end‐joining (NHEJ) (Schatz and Ji
2011
). Early work from
Baltimore's group
detected Rag1 transcripts in the murine CNS (Chun et al.
1991
), and subsequent studies reported RAG expression in zebrafish olfactory neurons and developing mouse brain (Jessen et al.
2001
; Feng et al.
2005
; Sun et al.
2007
).
It is essential to distinguish expression from recombination:
Low‐level RAG mRNA or protein does not by itself establish genomic V(D)J joining in neurons or glia. Supporting, but not yet definitive evidence for canonical rearrangement, comes from cultures prepared from SCID mice lacking mature B cells, where productive V(D)J‐like heavy‐chain transcripts and secreted IgG were detected alongside low‐abundance RAG1 protein (Huang et al.
2008
). Behavioural phenotypes in Rag1‐deficient rodents further imply non‐immune functions (McGowan et al.
2011
; Fang et al.
2013
). Low‐abundance Rag1 and Rag2 transcripts have been detected in embryonic microglia and neonatal astrocytes (Capuz, Osien, Karnoub, et al.
2023
), in adult corticothalamic neurons (Morimoto et al.
2025
) and in zebrafish olfactory sensory neurons (Jessen et al.
2001
; Feng et al.
2005
). RAG1‐deficient mice show impaired social recognition memory compared to mice wildtype for the RAG1 allele but also impair spatial learning and memory ability in rats (Fang et al.
2013
). RAG expression starts early in embryonic development. The expression of RAG‐1 persisted from E11 to P0 brain and was steadily increased from E11 to E19. Positive cells mainly appeared in the nucleus amygdalae, hypothalamus, thalamus and hippocampus at developmental stage. The expression began to appear in the ventricular zone (VZ) and intermediate zone (IZ) of the telencephalic vesicle and then gradually increased in the subventricular zone (SVZ), cortical plate (CP) and subcortical plate (SP) (Sun et al.
2007
).
3.1
Splice‐and‐Link RNA Assembly
An alternative explanation, first proposed after analysis of the 56‐kDa SP1 antigen, is that neural cells generate immunoglobulin‐like transcripts through RNA splicing events that join distant exons into V‐ and J‐like cassettes without requiring genomic rearrangement (Henschel and Wahle
1994
). Supporting evidence comes from 5′ RACE‐PCR of astrocytic κ‐chains that lack canonical RSS but exhibit precise exon–junction boundaries (Capuz, Osien, Cardon, et al.
2023
; Capuz, Osien, Karnoub, et al.
2023
). Long‐read sequencing in cultured astrocytes reveals multiexonic transcripts in which a variable‐like domain residing in intron 3 of Ighm is spliced directly to the first constant‐region exon (Cμ1). This splice‐and‐link process yields diversity through alternative exon choice rather than DNA breakage and mitigates genotoxic risk in long‐lived neurons.
3.2
Retroelement–Mediated Recombination
Retrotransposons can furnish recombination signal‐like motifs and strand breaks, potentially facilitating rearrangements at Ig loci in nonlymphoid cells. While this model remains speculative in the CNS, variable‐region topology and rearrangement‐like junctions observed in neural transcripts are consistent with such a contribution
(Capuz, Osien, Karnoub, et al.
2023
; Morimoto et al.
2025
).
Moreover, recent work further strengthens the cryptic‐RAG model while exposing an unexpectedly rich receptor landscape that can move, recycle and signal with intrinsically produced antibodies.
Huang et al. (
2008
)
cultured cortical neurons from severe‐combined‐immunodeficient (SCID) mice, animals devoid of mature B cells and still detected productive V‐(D)‐J heavy‐chain transcripts, an ≈70‐kDa membrane‐anchored IgG isoform and secretion‐competent full‐length IgG
(Huang et al.
2008
).
These findings remove the lingering objection that neuronal antibody signals reflect occult B‐cell contamination. Parallel immunoblotting identified RAG1 protein at very low abundance, aligning with the single‐molecule FISH data in primary astrocytes described earlier
.
If cryptic recombination does occur, the products must be trafficked through endoplasmic‐reticulum quality control, secretory vesicles, extracellular space and critically back across cellular barriers. A comprehensive immunohistochemical atlas of the human brain by Niu, Zhang, Sun et al. (
2011
) mapped the neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn) to cerebrovascular endothelium and ependymal lining, whereas high‐affinity FcγRI, inhibitory FcγRIIb and activating FcγRIIIa were distributed among cortical neurons, spinal ganglia, reactive astrocytes and microglia (Niu, Zhang, Guo, et al.
2011
; Niu, Zhang, Sun, et al.
2011
). Recently, such results have been validated. FcγRIIb and activating FcγRIIIa were identified in neurons and glial cells (Quan et al.
2009
; Okun et al.
2010
; Stamou et al.
2018
; Capuz, Karnoub, et al.
2022
). Taken together, these results suggest a closed, intracerebral antibody circuit in which intrinsic IgGs shuttle between parenchyma, perivascular space and interstitial fluid, engaging cell‐type–specific Fc portfolios along the way.
3.3
Post‐Translational Modification and Trafficking
Regardless of transcript origin, neural immunoglobulins must traverse the secretory pathway if they engage extracellular receptors. Proximal labelling experiments revealed that IgG2b is
N
‐glycosylated by the OST complex, addressed to vesicle membranes containing the ATPase complex and associates with LAT1/CD98hc
(Capuz, Osien, Karnoub, et al.
2023
).
Outside the cell, neural IgGs engage FcγRI, FcγRIII and FcRn on microglia and other cells, plausibly modulating phagocytosis and receptor trafficking
(Capuz, Karnoub, et al.
2022
; Morimoto et al.
2025
) (Figure
2
).

3.4
Integration of Canonical and Alternative Routes
The most parsimonious hypothesis is that neural lineages deploy a hybrid strategy. Sparse canonical RAG activity in progenitors could generate a limited scaffold of V‐to‐J joints, which, once established, amplifies via transcriptional or splicing diversity
(Capuz, Karnoub, et al.
2022
; Morimoto et al.
2025
).
Retroelements may further scramble or augment these transcripts in response to injury or stress. Such a system would produce an ‘innate‐like’ repertoire characterised by restricted diversity, minimal hypermutation and conserved binding pockets consistent with repeated detection of identical variable‐region motifs across species
.
4
Physiological Roles of Neural Immunoglobulins
4.1
Developmental Cell Death and Synaptic Pruning
The notion that subplate neurons orchestrate thalamocortical targeting gained decisive support from in vivo physiology: Ablation of SP1‐positive neurons in ferret and cat disrupts ocular‐dominance column formation and delays the onset of visually driven activity (Kanold et al.
2003
; Kanold and Shatz
2006
; Kanold
2009
; Kanold and Luhmann
2010
). Modern single‐cell atlases and evolutionary surveys now position subplate cells as a phylogenetically conserved ‘proto‐layer’ whose molecular repertoire, including the SP1 heavy‐chain‐like antigen, has diversified rather than disappeared (Hoerder‐Suabedissen and Molnár
2015
). The temporal coincidence of SP1 expression with complement tagging and microglial engulfment therefore frames SP1 not merely as a death‐flag but as a possible ligand or scaffold that links exuberant connectivity to activity‐dependent refinement. During early postnatal life the mammalian cortex undergoes exuberant synaptogenesis followed by activity‐dependent pruning. Subplate neurons, transient pioneers of thalamocortical circuitry, are culled in a wave of apoptosis between postnatal days 7 and 21 in rodents and felines. The SP1 antigen, a 56‐kDa heavy‐chain‐like protein, is upregulated precisely during this window (Naegele et al.
1991
; Henschel and Wahle
1994
). Immunocytochemistry reveals SP1‐positive blebs on axons targeted by microglial phagocytic cups (Dunn et al.
1995
). Complement C1q localises to the same neurites, and C1q knock‐out delays subplate clearance by 3 days. These observations support a two‐step model in which intrinsic antibody deposition flags neurites for C1q binding, accelerating complement‐mediated pruning (Stevens et al.
2007
) (Table
2
).

4.2
Circuit Maintenance and Plasticity in the Adult Brain
Stamou et al. (
2018
) provide functional evidence that Fc receptors can transduce signals in the developing brain. Using primary hippocampal neurons and astrocytes at postnatal day 5, cross‐linking with heat‐aggregated IgG evokes a fast intracellular Ca
2+
wave followed by ERK1/2 phosphorylation, responses abolished by FcγRI inhibition. Interferon‐γ exposure, which rises during perinatal infections, up‐regulated both FcγRI and FcγRIII mRNA, amplifying the Ca
2+
burst. These observations integrate with the SP1–complement axis: During the narrow window of subplate pruning, neurons not only decorate redundant synapses with an Ig‐like tag but also express receptors capable of reading Ig signals, thereby fine‐tuning activity‐dependent survival. In the mature CNS, deep‐layer VI corticothalamic neurons express Ighm and
Ighg2c
transcripts (Morimoto et al.
2025
).
In models of neuropathic pain induced by peripheral nerve injury,
biologic inhibition or genetic deletion of FcRn reduces
IgG accumulation
in lumbar dorsal root ganglia (DRG) neurons and in microglia of the lumbar dorsal horn, accompanied by analgesia (Montoyo et al.
2009
; Fiore et al.
2025
).
These findings indicate that FcRn shapes IgG residence time and distribution within somatosensory tissues; whether an analogous neuronal FcRn‐dependent pathway maintains synaptic receptor pools remains an open question rather than a demonstrated mechanism
. Astrocytes secrete
IgG2b
that adheres to LAT1–CD98hc heterodimers at the plasmalemma (Capuz, Osien, Karnoub, et al.
2023
). Pharmacological disruption of LAT1–IgG2b interaction derepresses YAP1, enhancing astrocyte proliferation after stab wound but impairing scar compaction. These
reciprocal
regulatory loops hint at a housekeeping role for neural Igs in balancing plasticity and stability (Capuz, Osien, Karnoub, et al.
2023
) (Table
2
).
4.3
Regeneration and Neural Repair
SCI heralds a dynamic shift in the microenvironment. Within 12 h, microglia up‐regulate IgG1 heavy chains, followed by
IgG2c
at 48 h (Capuz, Karnoub, et al.
2022
). Recombinant Fab fragments of the early IgG1 promote neurite extension of dorsal root ganglion neurons in vitro, whereas the late
IgG2c
fragments inhibit growth, an ‘antibody‐dependent neurite outgrowth modulation’ (ADNM) gradient reminiscent of developmental guidance molecules (Capuz, Karnoub, et al.
2022
). Neutralising FcγRIII blocks the inhibitory phase, underscoring receptor involvement. In vivo, intrathecal infusion of FcγRIII‐blocking peptides after thoracic contusion improves hind‐limb motor scores and enlarges serotonin‐positive axonal density caudal to the lesion. Collectively these findings place neural Igs alongside semaphorins and ephrins as dynamic regulators of growth (Capuz, Karnoub, et al.
2022
) (Table
2
).
4.4
Metabolic and Gliovascular Coupling
Beyond synapse and axon biology, neural immunoglobulins may influence energy metabolism. Astrocytic
IgG2b
cointernalises with LAT1, a branched chain amino acid transporter critical for supplying leucine to neurons. LAT1 knockdown reduces
IgG2b
surface retention and triggers mTORC1 inhibition in adjacent neurons, implicating an Ig–amino acid circuit that couples astrocyte metabolism to neuronal nutrient sensing (Capuz, Osien, Karnoub, et al.
2023
). Similarly, IgM–FcμR complexes on endothelial cells augment endothelial nitric‐oxide synthase activity, tuning local blood flow (Pivoriūnas and Verkhratsky
2021
; Stark et al.
2024
).
5
Pathological Implications of Intrinsic Neural Immunoglobulins
Neural immunoglobulins intersect a spectrum of CNS disorders, from classic autoimmunity to glioma. Because many disease‐associated antibodies are traditionally assumed to originate in peripheral B cells, disentangling the contribution of brain‐resident Ig synthesis is essential for the interpretation of serological tests, therapeutic targeting and biomarker development.
5.1
Autoimmune Encephalitides
The discovery of neuronal‐surface autoantibodies, against NMDA receptor (NMDAR), LGI1, CASPR2 and others, transformed neuroimmunology (Dalmau and Rosenfeld
2020
). Measurement of intrathecal IgG index often exceeds levels predicted by serum titres, implying local antibody production. Laser‐capture microdissection followed by V(D)J sequencing demonstrates clonally expanded IgG plasmablasts within parenchyma (Burgoon et al.
2005
; Kennedy et al.
2020
), while a second population of GFAP‐positive astrocytes harbours nonsomatically mutated IgG mRNA with recurrent variable regions (Capuz, Osien, Karnoub, et al.
2023
; Morimoto et al.
2025
). In anti‐NMDAR encephalitis, IgG1 from CSF reduces synaptic NMDAR density by cross‐linking and internalisation (Hughes et al.
2010
; Wang et al.
2024
).
Conversely, experimental manipulation indicates that endogenous IgM may buffer complement‐mediated injury,
suggesting a double‐edged sword (Narang et al.
2017
; Rahman et al.
2023
; Dalmau and Graus
2018
).
5.2
MS and Antibody‐Mediated Demyelination
Oligoclonal IgG bands in CSF remain a diagnostic hallmark of MS (Thompson et al.
2018
). Single‐nucleus RNA‐seq of chronic active lesions uncovers astrocyte clusters expressing Ighm and Ighg3, enriched for interferon signatures (Absinta et al.
2021
). The lesion rim, enriched in astrocytic
IgG2b
, is also an FcγRIIb‐high domain, favouring inhibitory signalling that dampens complement cascade; the demyelinated core, by contrast, is populated by FcγRIIIa‐positive microglia whose own
IgG2c
engages an activating pathway culminating in SYK and NF‐κB (Ulvestad et al.
1994
; Capuz, Osien, Karnoub, et al.
2023
; Yong
2022
; Watkins et al.
2016
).
5.3
Neurodevelopmental Vulnerability and Shared Complement Mechanisms
Detailed histological surveys of foetal cortex reveal that the subplate reaches maximal thickness and cellular heterogeneity, including SP1‐like immunoreactivity, during the late second trimester, precisely when many cortical malformations originate (Kostović et al.
2015
). Disruption of subplate architecture in genetic or environmental models of neuronal‐migration disorders often co‐occurs with altered complement deposition,
supporting a developmental model in which intrinsic immunoglobulin motifs and complement shape circuit refinement and vulnerability
.
We therefore emphasise a shared mechanistic principle, antibody/complement‐mediated synapse elimination, rather than a direct pathophysiological continuum with late‐life neurodegeneration
. Complement‐mediated synapse loss is also implicated in Alzheimer's disease (
ad
), but here, we cite it
as a conceptual parallel
where complement systems are redeployed in the aged or diseased brain (Hong et al.
2016
), not as evidence of a direct link from subplate biology to
ad
.
Dopaminergic neurons can secrete IgG that binds microglial FcγRI, shifting microglia toward an IL‐10–rich, neuroprotective phenotype in toxin models (
Zhang et al.
2013
). Removing that IgG (or disabling FcγR signalling) accelerates α‐synuclein–driven nigral loss
(Cao et al.
2010
).
Related Fc/complement pathways are observed after traumatic brain injury (TBI),
but these studies do not by themselves establish a Parkinson's‐specific mechanism (Mallah, Quanico, et al.
2019
; Alawieh et al.
2021
; Mallah, Zibara, et al.
2023
; Mantash et al.
2025
; Tansey et al.
2022
).
Accordingly, we avoid overextending these associations and frame them as hypotheses that require in vivo, cell‐type–specific manipulations
.
5.4
Psychiatric Disorders and Stress
Here, we use ‘isotype switch’ to denote a change in the dominant Ig class/subclass expressed by a given neural cell type (e.g., IgM → IgG2c in microglia after injury).
Although no study has yet
quantified cell‐specific isotype switching during chronic psychological stress
, chronic social‐defeat stress drives microglia‐dependent excessive pruning of cortical synapses (Wang et al.
2023
). Stamou's discovery that IFN‐γ boosts neuronal FcγRIII suggests a feed‐forward loop whereby early‐life inflammation could tilt the later balance of pruning toward overelimination (Stamou et al.
2018
).
Injury models provide additional mechanistic clues:
in a cortical injury paradigm, extravasated IgG engaging FcγRIII and CD9 promotes the emergence of synapse‐engulfing microglia in the thalamus and cognitive deficits (Matoba et al.
2024
).
Because this derives from tissue injury rather than psychosocial stress, we present it as a mechanistic bridge, not as direct evidence, for how IgG/Fc pathways might intersect with microglial pruning in stress‐related disorders
(Schafer et al.
2012
; Sekar et al.
2016
).
5.5
Therapeutic Opportunities and Biomarker Potential
5.5.1
Therapeutics
Endogenous neural immunoglobulins invite two broad therapeutic strategies. First,
ligand/receptor targeting
, in which selective FcγR modulators (e.g., FcγRIII inhibitors in SCI models) temper maladaptive pruning or growth arrest while sparing beneficial phagocytosis (Capuz, Karnoub, et al.
2022
). Second,
gene‐directed approaches
, in which viral vectors deliver defined variable regions under astrocyte‐ or neuron‐specific promoters to boost protective Ig motifs locally while minimising systemic exposure. Both avenues require careful safety assessment to avoid off‐target autoimmunity and to establish
cell‐type–specific necessity and sufficiency
in vivo.
5.5.2
Biomarkers
CSF Ig repertoires, oligoclonal bands and
cell‐type–enriched Ig transcripts
have diagnostic and prognostic promise in autoimmune encephalitis and MS; similarly,
changes in Ig subclass distribution
in injury or neurodegeneration could serve as dynamic markers of disease state or treatment response (Awad et al.
2010
; Beseler et al.
2017
; Gastaldi et al.
2017
). To unlock this potential, studies should couple single‐cell/spatial transcriptomics with proteomics and longitudinal CSF sampling to disentangle peripheral from CNS‐intrinsic sources.
6
Conclusion
Over little more than 25 years the field has journeyed from incredulity at a 56‐kDa subplate antigen to an appreciation of immunoglobulins as candidate endogenous mediators of neural development, circuit stability, injury response and disease. The evidence now spans modalities from single‐cell transcriptomics and top‐down proteomics to conditional genetics and clinical imaging. Neurons, astrocytes and microglia, each marshal distinct isotypes, deploy divergent synthetic pathways and pursue cell‐type–specific agendas, sometimes protective, sometimes injurious.
Yet, definitive, cell‐type–specific genetic tests of function remain limited, and several proposed roles rest on correlation
. Recognising neural immunoglobulins as integral components of
CNS biology
, not peripheral interlopers, reframes therapeutic strategy and sets a pragmatic agenda focused on rigorous gain‐ and loss‐of‐function experiments.
