NOVELTY AND RELEVANCE
What Is New?
The novelty of our findings lies in the identification of placenta-derived proteins in healthy pregnancies and preeclampsia using large-scale plasma proteomics combined with a unique in vivo sampling technique. This innovative methodology enables us to elucidate the release of proteins from the placenta, uncovering a range of previously unrecognized proteins.
What Is Relevant?
The identified placenta-derived proteins form a crucial foundation for a better pathophysiological understanding of pregnancy and preeclampsia, pointing toward novel placenta-derived biomarker candidates. As our knowledge of these proteins expands, they could significantly contribute to the ongoing quest for effective diagnostic and therapeutic strategies in managing preeclampsia.
Clinical/Pathophysiological Implications?
Placental dysfunction is the hallmark of preeclampsia. Currently, diagnostic and treatment options are limited to a few biomarkers, including the placenta-derived proteins PGF (placental growth factor) and sFLT1 (soluble fms-like tyrosine kinase 1). The discovery of novel placenta-derived proteins offers new possibilities for better phenotyping of preeclampsia, potential treatment targets and biomarkers. These proteins not only enhance our understanding of the disease mechanism but also pave the way for early diagnosis, facilitating timely interventions.
Preeclampsia is one of the leading causes of adverse pregnancy outcomes, affecting ≈2% to 4% of all pregnancies globally.
1
New-onset hypertension (≥140/90 mm Hg after 20 weeks of gestation) is a diagnostic criterion, accompanied by either maternal proteinuria, thrombocytopenia, renal insufficiency, impaired liver function, pulmonary edema, headache, visual disturbances, eclampsia, or fetal growth restriction.
2
The syndrome is often classified as early-onset preeclampsia (EOPE, <34 weeks) or late-onset preeclampsia (LOPE, ≥34 weeks) depending on gestational age at diagnosis.
3
The pathophysiology of preeclampsia is complex and not fully understood, but placental malperfusion is recognized as a key feature, leading to syncytiotrophoblast stress due to a combination of hypoxia, oxidative stress, and inflammatory and immune-related reactions.
4
–
6
These processes result in the release of placental substances into the maternal circulation, promoting systemic endothelial activation and the clinical manifestations of preeclampsia.
6
A milestone in the investigation of preeclampsia was the in vivo demonstration that the human placenta releases both the angiogenic placental growth factor (PGF) and the antiangiogenic sFLT1 (soluble fms-like tyrosine kinase 1).
7
–
9
Preeclampsia pregnancies are characterized by elevated circulating maternal sFLT1 levels and reduced PGF levels.
7
,
10
To date, sFLT1, PGF, and the sFLT1/PGF ratio remain the best-documented biomarkers for preeclampsia.
11
–
13
However, these markers do not fully capture the heterogeneity of the disease, and there is a significant knowledge gap regarding other placenta-derived proteins that may serve as biomarkers or provide insight into disease mechanisms.
14
Previous large-scale proteomic studies,
15
,
16
including our own studies,
17
,
18
have identified protein signatures associated with preeclampsia in the maternal circulation. However, these studies did not account for the tissue of origin, limiting their ability to identify placenta-specific signals. The human placenta is a transient and relatively inaccessible organ, making in vivo studies of placental protein release challenging. To address this, we previously demonstrated the feasibility of using the 4-vessel sampling method during cesarean delivery to identify the placental release of selected proteins linked to preeclampsia into the maternal circulation.
9
Furthermore, by combining this sampling method with large-scale protein quantification, we demonstrated that a substantial number of proteins are released from the placenta during healthy pregnancies.
19
,
20
In the present study, we aimed to (1) identify proteins released from the placenta into the maternal circulation in preeclampsia compared with healthy controls, (2) investigate the biological processes associated with these placenta-derived proteins using enrichment analysis, and (3) explore novel biomarker candidates among the placenta-derived proteins detectable in maternal circulation before the onset of clinical symptoms, using samples from a longitudinal cohort.
17
By identifying placenta-derived proteins in preeclampsia, this study contributes to a deeper understanding of the disease and may support the development of improved diagnostic tools.

Design and Study Population
The 4-vessel cohort recruited healthy pregnant participants and participants with preeclampsia who gave birth by cesarean section at Oslo University Hospital, Rikshospitalet, between October 2012 and June 2019. Exclusion criteria were preexisting comorbidities, medication (other than levothyroxine), diabetes treated with medications, pregnancy complications other than preeclampsia, and onset of labor before scheduled cesarean delivery. We sampled blood from the radial artery, antecubital vein, and uterine vein on the anterolateral surface of the uterus immediately before uterine incision.
21
Protein quantification was performed on a subsample of 75 of the 179 healthy participants. The subsample was selected based on the completeness of data according to the study protocol: (1) Plasma from 5 vessels, (2) Ultrasound measures on the maternal side of the placenta, (3) Placental homogenate, (4) Fetal ultrasound data, (5) Newborn caliper data collected at birth, in ranked order. All 37 participants with preeclampsia were also included, both EOPE (n=19) and LOPE (n=18) defined by cesarean section before or after 34 weeks’ gestation. All participants signed a written informed consent using a protocol approved by the Regional Committee for Medical and Health Research Ethics of Southeast Norway (S-07174a, 2419/2011, and 13885).
The prospective STORK cohort recruited 1031 healthy pregnant participants at Oslo University Hospital, Rikshospitalet, with uncomplicated singleton pregnancies during the first trimester in the time period between 2002 and 2008. Exclusion criteria were pregestational diabetes and severe chronic diseases (lung, cardiac, gastrointestinal, or renal). Fasting antecubital vein blood samples were taken at visit 1, 2, and 3 (gestational weeks 12–19, 20–26, and 28–34, respectively). Protein quantification was performed on blood samples collected at the 3 visits in a random subsample of 70 healthy participants and for all the 35 participants who were diagnosed with LOPE (birth ≥ gestational week 34). Participants signed a written informed consent, and the study was approved by the Regional Committee for Medical Research Ethics, Southern Norway, Oslo, Norway (reference number S-2014/224-0119a).
Body mass index (BMI) was calculated based on self-reported prepregnancy weight and height in the 4-vessel cohort, whereas in the STORK weight and height measured at the first visit were used. Maternal age and parity were collected from medical journals. Preeclampsia was diagnosed by blood pressure ≥140/90 mm Hg after 20 weeks of gestation and excessive proteinuria.
22
Excessive proteinuria was defined as a urinary total protein/creatinine ratio >30 or +1 on urine dipstick. The preeclampsia diagnosis was given based on the medical journals
International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems
, 10th version (ICD-10) coding in the STORK cohort, whereas preeclampsia participants were recruited based on blood pressure and proteinuria or the ICD-10 code in the medical journal in the 4-vessel cohort. The treatment strategies for preeclampsia were according to the current guidelines during the STORK study period
23
and the 4-vessel study period.
24
Antihypertensive medications were primarily labetalol and nifedipine. Delivery was ideally planned after 37 weeks of gestation, with careful consideration given to the clinical circumstances between 34 and 37 weeks. Before 34 weeks, we aimed to implement conservative management whenever possible to optimize outcomes for both the mother and fetus.
Handling of Blood Samples
Samples were transferred immediately to EDTA vacutainers, kept on ice, centrifuged within 30 minutes after collection (6 °C, 2500 g, 20 minutes), and stored at −80 °C. Due to practical challenges and time constraints, some vessels were not sampled (
Tables S1 and S2

Protein Quantification by SomaLogic
Proteins were quantified using 4979 unique DNA-based aptamers on the SomaScan assay 4.0 version from SomaLogic, Inc (Boulder, CO).
25
,
26
EDTA-plasma was mixed with aptamers, and only aptamer-protein complexes with specific binding were quantified by a fluorescence signal corresponding to the protein abundance (relative fluorescence units [RFU]). Median intra- and interassay coefficients of variation are ≈5%.
25
,
27
Counts of placenta-derived proteins and differential abundance tests refer to unique aptamers. Some proteins (like FLT1) were quantified by 2 different aptamers. More details are given in
Methods Text S1
. We report proteins using Entrez Gene symbols.

Preprocessing and Imputation of Data
Data processing and analyses were performed in R, version 4.1.3
31

The data set underwent SomaLogic standard preprocessing procedures including (1) hybridization normalization to control for variability in the readout of individual microarrays, (2) intraplate median signal normalization to account for differences in total signal between replicate control samples, (3) plate scaling to adjust for total signal variation from plate to plate, and (4) calibration to remove the variation between assay runs within and across experiments.
33
We did not use the Adaptive Normalization by Maximum Likelihood normalization to a healthy US population reference because our pregnant Scandinavian participants are likely to differ from the US population. All RFU values were log2 transformed.
Venoarterial differences for all proteins were calculated per patient on the maternal side of the placenta by log2 RFU uterine vein – log2 RFU radial artery (
Figure S4A and S4B

35

Gene Ontology Enrichment Analysis
We performed overrepresentation analyses to investigate enriched biological process gene ontologies for the placenta-derived proteins in each group (controls; 275 proteins and preeclampsia; 574 proteins). All the quantified proteins on the SomaScan platform were used as a background list (universe). We used the ClusterCompare function to compare enriched biological processes between the 2 groups and the enrichGO function to investigate enriched terms among the proteins released in preeclampsia uniquely using the ClusterProfiler (version 4.12.6) R package.
39
We used the simplify function (reduces redundancy of Gene Ontology terms) to improve interpretation. Enrichment was defined by a
q
value <0.05.
Identification of Placenta-Derived Biomarker Candidates in Preeclampsia
Using moderated
t
tests (limma R-package, version 3.50.0; script available at
https://github.com/trondmic/Omics_in_pregnancy_OUS/blob/main/Proteomics_PE_placenta/identify_placenta_derived_biom_cand.R

37

After identification of differential proteins based on
q

We compared the performance of the identified biomarker candidates with the known preeclampsia biomarkers FLT1 and the FLT1/PGF ratio (using the aptamer FLT1.8231.122). Corresponding receiver operating characteristics curves and area under the curve (AUC) were calculated as a measurement of model performance using the

40

Discussion
Previous studies have shown altered release of single proteins from the placenta into the maternal circulation in preeclampsia.
7
,
9
In this first of its kind human in vivo study, we combined the 4-vessel sampling method with the 5000-multiplex SomaScan platform to identify 620 placenta-derived proteins released into the maternal circulation in either preeclampsia, healthy controls or both. Using a prospective cohort, we identified promising biomarker candidates among these placenta-derived proteins at least 3 weeks, and on average >7 weeks, before delivery. We have previously identified HTRA1 and FAAH2 as important in multiprotein models using machine learning algorithms among 5000 circulating proteins in the same cohort.
17
Strikingly, this study showed that these proteins are placenta-derived and have comparable performance in single protein models with or without clinical variables as the established FLT1 and FLT1/PGF ratio. Gene ontology enrichment analyses of all placenta-derived proteins in preeclampsia (574 proteins) as compared with healthy (275 proteins) pregnancies, showed enrichment of several overlapping terms, but that terms such as response to oxidative stress, cellular response to inorganic substance, and cellular response to chemical stress were only present in preeclampsia, supporting that oxidative and cellular stress in the uteroplacental circulation is a key element of the disease.
42
The relatively large number of biological processes affected by the placenta-derived proteins in preeclampsia gives insights into the complexity of the disease and the wide range of biological processes affected. Future studies are needed to further explore the placenta-derived proteins’ potential role in preeclampsia pathogenesis.
The differences in circulating levels of placenta-derived proteins between preeclampsia and healthy controls in the longitudinal STORK cohort appeared to increase with gestation, potentially reflecting placental dysfunction and progressing preeclampsia pathophysiology. While 20 differential proteins were identified at visit 3, one and zero proteins were differential at visit 1 and 2. This finding is consistent with previous large-scale proteomic biomarker studies,
15
including our own,
17
,
18
although not all studies are in agreement.
16
Visualization of protein abundance showed that the EOPE cases in the 4-vessel cohort displayed the largest aberrations in placenta-derived proteins in the maternal circulation, compatible with the more severe placental dysfunction (
Figure S8
).
43
–
46
Reduced intravascular volume, due to endothelial dysfunction and capillary leakage, may also contribute to the increased protein abundance observed in preeclampsia.
47
Hemodynamic aberrations linked to preeclampsia, such as shear stress, may also trigger endothelial activation resulting in release of proteins (eg, inflammation and vasoactive proteins) from the endothelium.
48
Thus, the placenta may not be the sole source of all the identified proteins, as they may also be released from the endothelium.
While our former study showed that HTRA1 and FAAH2 were selected in multiprotein biomarker signatures by machine learning algorithms,
17
we here show that these proteins are placenta-derived and may serve as biomarkers also in either univariate models or in combination with clinical factors. HTRA1 is one of 4 secreted serine proteases involved in a variety of biological functions such as normal cell maintenance and growth, cell proliferation and regulation of apoptosis.
49
The HTRA proteins degrade misfolded and misplaced proteins or protect proteins from stress by acting as chaperones or proteases. HTRA1 is especially involved in extracellular matrix protein degradation,
49
and plays an important role in vascular health.
50
Abnormal levels of HTRA1 in placental tissue and maternal serum has been reported previously in preeclampsia,
51
–
55
although contradictory reports have also been published.
56
FAAH2 is a membrane bound enzyme degrading fatty acid amides that share 20% sequence identity with FAAH1.
57
FAAHs degrades bioactive lipids such as anandamide to arachidonic acid,
58
linking the endocannabinoid system to the eicosanoid metabolic pathway. Both the absence of differences in FAAH gene expression levels,
59
lower levels of FAAH
60
and higher gene expression levels of FAAH1
61
in placentas from preeclampsia have been reported. Notably, in agreement with higher FAAH2 in preeclampsia in our study which could reflect attenuated anandamide levels, lower levels of circulating anandamide were found in preeclampsia as compared with healthy pregnancies.
61
,
62
Due to the vasorelaxation effects of anandamide, attenuated anandamide levels could enhance spiral artery dysfunction in preeclampsia.
61
Also, FAAH inhibition in monocytes and macrophages, skewed these cells toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype, with reduced production of proinflammatory cytokines.
63
Other studies have also shown that FAAH inhibition suppresses inflammation,
64
suggesting elevated FAAH2 could augment inflammatory responses during preeclampsia progression.
Several glycoprotein INHBA isoforms and heterodimers were defined as placenta-derived in our model in accordance with former studies
15
,
16
,
65
–
67
and had altered circulating levels between healthy and preeclampsia pregnancies. Logistic regression showed AUCs ≥0.7 in the univariate models at visit 3 and AUCs >0.8 in the multivariate models including clinical variables. The sensitivity of Activin A and Inhibin A for prediction of preeclampsia was reported as limited (16% to 59%) in a former study,
66
although the sensitivity was better for EOPE (33% to 89%).
Interestingly, we observed placental release of several heat shock proteins only in preeclampsia, as well as elevated circulating levels of HSPA1A and HSPB6 before the onset of preeclampsia. Heat shock proteins serve as chaperones, assisting proper protein folding and preventing unspecific protein aggregation. Heat shock proteins are upregulated in response to hypoxia, oxidative stress and inflammation, as they also ameliorate proteotoxic stress.
68
,
69
Increased levels of heat shock proteins have previously been linked to preeclampsia as a consequence of placental hypoxia and oxidative stress,
70
,
71
with even higher levels in the hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, and low platelet count syndrome than in preeclampsia.
72
Elevated circulating abundance of 2 collagen alpha chain variants (COL28A1 and COL6A3)
73
and MFAP2
74
before onset of preeclampsia adheres with the extracellular matrix being subject to considerable remodeling during normal pregnancy, with aberrations in preeclampsia. GREM2 is a bone morphogenetic protein antagonist that has been linked to fertility,
75
,
76
with elevated placental expression in preeclampsia as compared with healthy pregnancies.
77
To the best of our knowledge CBX1, CRABP2, INSL4, KDELC2, MFAP2, PRSS22, ROR2, and SRP19 have not previously been linked to preeclampsia and are novel placenta-derived biomarker candidates.
Several of the placenta-derived proteins are considered intracellular or membrane bound proteins (such as histones and enzymes). In preeclampsia, these proteins may represent increased syncytial turnover due to apoptotic and necrotic processes in the dysfunctional placenta resulting in cellular, subcellular and molecular placental debris released into the maternal circulation.
78
,
79
Increased senescence of syncytiotrophoblasts, as a result of oxidative stress, accompanied by an altered secretory phenotype has also been suggested in preeclampsia.
80
A considerable strength of the current study is that it is the first human in vivo study to investigate proteins being released from the placenta into the maternal circulation in preeclampsia as compared with healthy pregnancies and that we were able to quantify ≈5000 proteins. Several transcriptomic studies have been performed on placental tissues in an effort to identify the placental secretome in preeclampsia.
81
–
83
These studies are limited by the fact that genes can be subjected to posttranscriptional and posttranslational modifications, such as alternative splicing, folding, transport, localization, degradation, and secretion.
83
Traditional proteomics have limited capacity to identify low abundance proteins. Contrary to this, the SomaLogic 5000-multiplex platform is characterized by high specificity and a wide dynamic range, making it superior to plasma mass spectrometry in terms of the number of proteins it can identify. Thus, the current analysis of human placenta-derived proteins in vivo represents an advancement toward identifying novel placental biomarkers that could be altered before the manifestation of preeclampsia and thereby aid earlier detection and improved diagnosis as well as novel therapeutic targets.
83
Furthermore, the identified placenta-derived biomarker candidates showed differential abundance in the longitudinal cohort well before the onset of preeclampsia since only 15% of the participants had developed hypertension at the time of the last blood sampling (visit 3).
Some limitations apply to the present study. The participants in the 4-vessel cohort were sampled at scheduled cesarean section, which may affect hemodynamics and blood supply to the placenta. We have used the term placenta-derived proteins being well aware that the uterine vein, from which samples were taken, also drains nonplacental tissues like the decidua and myometrium. Furthermore, the radial artery was used as a proxy for the uterine artery. The term placenta-derived is used for simplicity and refers to the experimental setup of our 4-vessel model. Before clinical translation, these findings need further investigation. The SomaLogic platform offers a distinct advantage by enabling the quantification of both low and high-abundance proteins, as well as previously undetected protein isoforms that may provide important biological insights. The SomaScan 7K platform has reported a cross-validation median and median absolute deviation of 4.5±1.5% for human proteins.

The results are primarily applicable for last trimester placentas since the samples were collected at the time of cesarean section. Our analyses did not include stratification into distinct subtypes of preeclampsia (based on placental pathology, severity nor timing such as early-onset versus late-onset) due to limitations in sample size and available data. Ideally, the 4-vessel study would have included healthy controls with similar gestational age as the EOPE cases, as gestational age may influence placental protein release. Conclusions
Using the 4-vessel sampling method and a platform quantifying ≈5000 proteins, we identified 275 and 574 proteins released into the maternal circulation in healthy pregnancies and preeclampsia, respectively. Among the 620 proteins classified as placenta-derived in either healthy or preeclampsia pregnancies, 21 proteins were identified as potential biomarker candidates for preeclampsia through tests of differential abundance. Notably, HTRA1 and FAAH2 demonstrated promising biomarker performance, with AUCs comparable to the FLT1/PGF ratio currently used in clinical obstetrics. Gene Ontology enrichment analyses further revealed that the placenta-derived proteins associated with preeclampsia were linked to oxidative stress and cellular turnover. The identified placenta-derived proteins will need additional investigation in novel studies, specifically addressing their biomarker capability and biological roles in preeclampsia pathophysiology.
Perspectives
This study provides the first comprehensive identification of placenta-derived proteins released into the maternal circulation in preeclampsia, utilizing a human in vivo model to quantify ≈5000 proteins in plasma samples. Notably, our findings confirm FLT1 and PGF as reliable biomarkers for preeclampsia. Given that placental dysfunction is at the core of preeclampsia pathophysiology, placenta-derived proteins may offer a more direct connection to the disease etiology and reflect the health and functionality of the placenta. Our analysis revealed that single protein models for FAAH2 and HTRA1 showed promising predictive capabilities for preeclampsia as early as gestational week 16, with performance comparable to the established FLT1/PGF ratio by week 31. Furthermore, 17 out of 21 placenta-derived proteins demonstrating differential abundance in our prospective cohort enhanced predictive accuracy beyond traditional maternal clinical variables (maternal age, BMI, and nulliparity). This underscores the potential of these placenta-derived proteins as biomarkers for preeclampsia.
The placenta-derived proteins identified herein will facilitate further studies, including the development of novel hypotheses related to preeclampsia pathophysiology, the exploration of these proteins’ biomarker capabilities longitudinally, and their integration into multimarker panels. Novel longitudinal studies may uncover patterns and interactions that are not apparent in cross-sectional analyses due to the dynamic nature of these proteins. These efforts may enhance clinical utility and predictive accuracy beyond what is currently achievable with the sFLT1/PGF ratio alone.
