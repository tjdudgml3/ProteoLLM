Introduction
Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is characterized by its aggressive nature, poor prognosis, and tendency to metastasize to the liver, brain, and lungs, thereby posing a significant threat to women’s health [
1
]. Chemotherapy remains the primary clinical treatment for TNBC, as it can enhance surgical success rates and achieve pathological remission [
2
]. However, patients with TNBC continue to face the risk of residual disease and recurrence, which limits the long-term survival benefits of treatment [
3
]. It is imperative to investigate novel mechanisms underlying TNBC progression to expand therapeutic targets and drugs.
The zinc finger protein (ZNF) family plays a pivotal role in regulating the expression of downstream genes and is involved in the processes of migration, apoptosis, proliferation, and metastasis of malignant tumor cells [
4
]. ZNF526 gene contains 13 zinc finger domains and encodes a Krüppel-type zinc finger protein involved in transcriptional regulation. The protein encoded by the ZNF526 gene consists of 679 amino acids and is ubiquitously expressed across both adult and fetal tissues. It is noteworthy that the expression levels are particularly elevated in the ovary, with subsequent high expression observed in the lung, pancreas, spleen, testis, spinal cord, and fetal liver [
5
]. Furthermore, mutations in the exons of the ZNF526 gene are strongly correlated with the development of Hodgkin lymphoma [
6
]. Bo Zhang [
7
] has highlighted the single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) rs3810151 (Val94Ala) within the ZNF526 gene as a significant locus associated with increased susceptibility to breast cancer. This finding underscores the potential role of ZNF526 in the genetic predisposition to this malignancy, warranting further exploration into its functional implications.
Cancer cells sustain uncontrolled proliferation through metabolic reprogramming, which fuels adaptation to the evolving tumor microenvironment by coordinating bioenergetic, biosynthetic, oncogenic signaling, and redox demands [
8
,
9
]. Central to this reprogramming is the serine-glycine-one‑carbon (SGOC) metabolic network. SGOC metabolic network couples serine-glycine metabolism with folate and methionine cycle-mediated one-carbon metabolism to fuel nucleotide biosynthesis, epigenetic modifications, and glutathione synthesis, thereby linking biomass production to redox homeostasis and promoting tumor progression [
10
]. Consequently, the frequent upregulation of SGOC enzymes in highly proliferative tumors establishes this pathway as a therapeutically actionable vulnerability [
11
–
13
]. Serine hydroxymethyltransferases (SHMT), the pivotal enzymes in SGOC metabolism, catalyze the reversible conversion of serine and tetrahydrofolate to glycine and 5,10-methylene tetrahydrofolate. SHMT enzymes include cytosolic isoform SHMT1 and mitochondrial isoform SHMT2 [
10
,
14
]. SHMT enzymes are critical for cancer cell survival, driving
de novo
nucleotide synthesis to sustain proliferation and maintaining redox homeostasis through antioxidant defenses, as evidenced in diverse malignancies [
15
,
16
]. For instance, in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), the loss of tumor suppressors LKB1 and KEAP1 converges to hyperactivate SHMT enzymes to satisfy the increased demand for one-carbon units necessary for antioxidant defence [
17
]. Recent evidence demonstrates that the epigenetic regulator KDM2B orchestrates oxidative stress resistance in TNBC specifically by amplifying the SGOC metabolic pathway [
18
].
Our study demonstrates that ZNF526 is aberrantly overexpressed in TNBC and strongly associated with adverse clinical outcomes. Mechanistically, ZNF526 orchestrates metabolic reprogramming of the SGOC network by transcriptionally upregulating SHMT1, thereby boosting intracellular glutathione (GSH) biosynthesis and scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS) to sustain tumor growth. These findings collectively establish the ZNF526/SHMT1/GSH signaling axis as a pivotal regulator of redox homeostasis and malignant progression in TNBC, highlighting its therapeutic potential for targeted intervention.
Methods and materials
Data collection and analysis
Expression data for ZNF526, along with relevant clinical information, were obtained from the TCGA pan-cancer dataset, both accessible through the UCSC Xena platform (
https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/
) and UALCAN (
https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/
index.html). A comparative analysis was subsequently conducted to evaluate the expression levels of ZNF526 in tumor tissues from the TCGA dataset against those in normal tissues obtained from both the TCGA databases. This analysis was performed using R software, version 4.0.3. Throughout this process, rigorous adherence to standardized procedures and methodologies was upheld to ensure the accuracy and objectivity of the obtained results. All data handling and analytical operations were executed with meticulous attention to detail, in accordance with the established norms and best practices recognized within the academic and research communities in the field of cancer genomics.
Patients
Breast cancer tissue samples were obtained from patients undergoing surgical resection at the Department of Breast Surgery, East Hospital, Tongji University. This study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of East Hospital, Tongji University. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians prior to sample collection. All procedures strictly adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and institutional ethical guidelines.
Cell culture
The MDA-MB-231, MDA-MB-468, BT20, HCC1806 cell lines were obtained from the Cell Resource Center at the Shanghai Academy of Biological Sciences, affiliated with the Chinese Academy of Sciences. MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-468 cell lines were maintained in L15 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and penicillin (50 U/ml)/streptomycin (50 µg/ml) at 37 °C in a humidified chamber under no CO
2
condition. BT20 and HCC1806 cell lines were maintained in MEM and RPMI medium respectively supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and penicillin (50 U/ml)/streptomycin (50 µg/ml) at 37 °C in a humidified chamber under 5% CO2 condition.
Plasmids, siRNA, and lenti-virus preparation
The human codon-optimized coding sequence (CDS) of wild-typed ZNF526 was synthesized and subsequently cloned into the PCDH-Puro lentiviral vector to generate the PCDH-Puro-ZNF526 plasmids. PCDH-ZNF526-V94A mutant plasmids were generated from PCDH-Puro-ZNF526 plasmids using the Q5
®
Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions(E0554S, New England Biolabs). For Luciferase reporter plasmids, related SHMT1 promoter region was synthesized and subcloned into pGL3.0-Basic plasmids. ZNF526-shRNA lentiviral plasmids were derived form PLKO.1-puro vector and the shRNA sequence was shown in Supplementary Table 1. The LentiCRISPR-V2-sgZNF526 and LentiCRISPR-V2- sgSHMT1 plasmids were constructed using the LentiCRISPR-V2 vector, with the sgRNA sequences detailed in Supplementary Table 1.
For lentivirus packaging, HEK293T cells (ATCC) were co-transfected with the transfer plasmids, the packaging plasmid psPAX2, and the envelope plasmid pMD2.G at a mass ratio of 1:1:1 using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen). The viral supernatant was collected at 48 and 72 h post-transfection, pooled, and filtered through a 0.45-µm filter. To establish stable cell lines, cells were seeded in 6-well plates and infected with lentiviral supernatant supplemented with 8 µg/mL polybrene (Sigma-Aldrich) for 24 h. After replacing the virus-containing medium with normal culture medium, the cells were cultured for an additional 24 h. Subsequently, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing 2.0 µg/mL puromycin (InvivoGen) for a 48-hour selection period. The resulting stable cells were maintained under 1 µg/mL puromycin selection and used for subsequent experiments.
For siRNA transfection, cells were transfected with either siRNAs targeting SHMT1 or a normal control siRNA (NC-siRNA) using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX Transfection Reagent (13778150, Invitrogen). All functional assays were performed 48 to 72 h after siRNA transfection. The siRNA sequence of SHMT1 was listed in Supplementary Table 1.
Animal experiments
Female C57BL/6 nude mice (6 weeks old) were obtained from Shanghai Model Organisms Center (Shanghai, China). A 100 µL suspension of tumor cells was injected into the mammary fat pad. Stable tumors formed 1-week post-injection. Mice were euthanized 2–4 weeks after tumor establishment. Serum and tumor tissues were collected under sterile conditions for downstream analysis. For SHIN1 treatment, after subcutaneous tumors were formed, mice were randomly divided into four groups for intraperitoneal injection three times per week with vehicle and SHIN1 (100 mg/kg) [
19
]. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Tongji University and complied with AAALAC International guidelines.
Cell growth assay
Approximately 1,000 to 2,000 cells were meticulously seeded into each well of a 96-well plate, with each well containing an approximate volume of 100 µl. Each experimental condition comprised 4 to 6 wells, and a control group was also included. The cells were then incubated at 37 °C in a humidified chamber for a related period of time. Following this, the medium in each well was carefully aspirated, and a new medium containing 10% CCK-8 solution(A311-01/02,Vazyme)was added to each well. The plate was subsequently incubated at 37 °C in a humidified chamber for 1–2 h. The optical density (OD) of each well was then accurately determined by using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) reader set to a wavelength of 450 nm(1410101, Thermo). The collected data underwent a thorough analysis to generate a proliferation curve by using Graphpad software.
Cells were either transfected with 20nM SHMT1-specific siRNA (si-SHMT1) or treated with 10nM SHIN1(HY-112066, MCE) for 72 h before CCK-8 detection. The concentrations of SGOC metabolic intermediates used in the experiments are as follows: N-acetylcysteine (NAC, 5 mM), purine nucleosides (adenosine and guanosine, 5 µM each), pyrimidine nucleosides (cytidine, thymidine, and uridine, 5 µM each), reduced L-glutathione (5 mM), hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂, 100µM), or S-adenosylmethionine (SAM, 100 µM). All nucleosides and H₂O₂ were sourced from Sigma-Aldrich; SAM was obtained from MedChemExpress (MCE).
Cell colony formation
Colony formation assays were performed by seeding cells in 6-well plates at optimized densities (800 cells/well for MDA-MB-231 and 2,000 cells/well for MDA-MB-468) and cultured for 12 days. Fresh medium was replaced every 3–4 days. Following medium removal, cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (BL539A, Biosharp) for 10–15 min, and washed again twice with PBS. Fixed cells were then stained with crystal violet solution (548629, Macklin) for 10–30 min, followed by extensive PBS washes. Cell clones were visualized and documented microscopically. Clone formation was quantified using Image J software (National Institutes of Health). Images were converted to 8-bit grayscale, and global intensity thresholds were optimized (Brightness/Contrast followed by Threshold function) to generate binary images highlighting regions of interest. Particle analysis (Analyze Particles module) was performed with empirically determined size thresholds based on cellular dimensions and a circularity range of 0.00–1.00 to encompass heterogeneous morphologies. The number of particles meeting validated size criteria was enumerated. Cloning efficiency was calculated as (number of colonies/number of seeded cells) * 100%.
Cell cycle analysis by flow cytometry
Cell cycle distribution was analyzed using propidium iodide (PI) staining coupled with flow cytometry. Briefly, approximately 1 * 10^6 cells were harvested, washed twice with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and fixed in 70% ethanol at 4 °C overnight. Fixed cells were then centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min, resuspended in 500 µL PBS containing 50 µg/mL PI (Sigma-Aldrich), 100 µg/mL RNase A (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 0.1% Triton X-100, and incubated in darkness at 37 °C for 30 min.
Cell samples were analyzed immediately using a BD FACS Canto II flow cytometer equipped with a 488-nm laser. PI fluorescence was collected through a 585/42 nm bandpass filter, with a minimum of 10,000 events recorded per sample. Cell cycle phase distribution was determined using Flow Jo v10.9 analysis software. All experiments were performed in triplicate and repeated in three independent biological replicates.
Annexin V/PI apoptosis assay
Apoptosis was quantified using the Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (BD Biosciences, Cat. #556547) according to manufacturer’s specifications. Briefly, 1 * 10^6 cells per condition were harvested, washed twice with ice-cold PBS, and suspended in 100 µL of 1× Binding Buffer. Cells were stained with 5 µL Annexin V-FITC and 5 µL propidium iodide (PI) working solution (50 µg/mL), followed by gentle vortexing and incubation for 15 min at 25 °C in darkness. Immediately prior to analysis, 400 µL of 1× Binding Buffer was added to each sample.
Flow cytometry was performed using a Beckman Coulter CytoFLEX within 1 h of staining. FITC fluorescence was detected through a 525/540 nm bandpass filter (FL1 channel), and PI through a 610/620 nm bandpass filter (FL3 channel). Viable cells, early apoptotic, late apoptotic and necrotic cells were distinguished using Flow Jo v10.9 software. Unstained cells, single-stained controls and Annexin V, and camptothecin-treated apoptotic cells (10 µM, 6 h) served as experimental controls.
GSH and GSSG measurement
The concentrations of glutathione (GSH) and oxidized glutathione (GSSG) in cells or mouse serum were detected by using the ELISA kit(KTB1600, KBT1610, Abbkine). All samples were repeated for 3 to 6 times.
GSH detection: Record the absorbance at 412 nm. Subtract the absorbance (A) of the blank wells from the absorbance (A) values measured for all other wells. That is, ΔA (standard) = A (standard) - A (blank), and ΔA (test) = A (test) - A (blank). Plot a standard curve with the concentration of the standard substance on the y-axis and ΔA (standard) on the x-axis. Substitute ΔA (test) into the equation of the standard curve to obtain the y value (µg/mL). The content of reduced glutathione (in µg/mL) is equal to this y value.
GSSG: The absorbance at 412 nm was recorded. The absorbance ΔA values measured for all wells were subtracted by the absorbance ΔA of the blank wells. That is, ΔΔA of the standard = ΔA of the standard - ΔA of the blank, and ΔΔA of the test = ΔA of the test - ΔA of the blank. With the concentration of the standard substance as the y-axis and ΔΔA of the standard as the x-axis, a standard curve was plotted. The ΔΔA of the test was then substituted into the equation to obtain the y value (µM), which represents the concentration of GSSG.
Untargeted metabolomics
Metabolites from cells were PBS-washed, extracted with ice-cold MeCN: MeOH (1:1, 800 µL), centrifuged (14,000 ×g, 20 min), and supernatants dried/resuspended in 100 µL MeCN: H₂O (1:1) for LC-MS with QC every fifth injection. Analysis used UHPLC-Q-TOF MS (Agilent 1290/Sciex TripleTOF 6600) with BEH Amide column (2.1 × 100 mm). Mobile phases: (A) 25 mM NH₄Ac/NH₄OH, (B) MeCN. Gradient: 95% B (0.5 min), 65% B (6.5 min), 40% B (7.5 min), 95% B (3 min re-equilibration). ESI: ±5500 V, 600 °C, Gas1/2 = 60, curtain = 30. Full-scan MS: 60–1000 Da (0.20 s); auto-MS/MS: 25–1000 Da (CE 35 ± 15 eV).
Raw data were converted to MzXML format using ProteoWizard and processed in XCMS with centWave algorithm parameters set to 10 ppm mass tolerance and 10–60 s peakwidth. Peak grouping applied a 5-second bandwidth and 0.025 m/z window. CAMERA-annotated features exhibiting over 50% non-zero values per group underwent sum normalization. Metabolite identification required mass accuracy below 10 ppm with MS/MS spectra matched against in-house standards. Multivariate analysis via ropls R package included PCA and OPLS-DA validated through 7-fold cross-validation and permutation testing. Significant features met dual thresholds of VIP greater than 1 and p-value under 0.05 from Student t-test with subsequent Pearson correlation analysis.
Detection of one carbon metabolites
HPLC-grade reagents: MeCN/MeOH (Merck), H₂O (Millipore), NH₄Ac (Sigma). Metabolite standards (Sigma/IsoReag) stored as 1 mg/mL MeOH stocks (−20 °C). Cell pellets resuspended in 100 µL H₂O; 50 µL aliquot mixed with 250 µL precooled MeOH (−20 °C), vortexed (2 min, 2500 rpm), three freeze-thaw cycles (liquid N₂ 5 min/ice 5 min). Centrifuged (12,000 rpm, 10 min, 4 °C); 200 µL supernatant incubated (−20 °C, 30 min), recentrifuged. Clarified supernatant (180 µL) filtered for LC-MS. ExionLC AD UPLC (SCIEX) coupled to QTRAP 6500+ (SCIEX) with ESI⁺. Column: Accucore 150 Amide HILIC (100 × 2.1 mm, 2.6 μm, 40 °C). Mobile phase: (A) 10 mM NH₄Ac in H₂O, (B) MeCN; flow 0.66 mL/min. Gradient: 95% B (0–1 min), linear to 50% B (1–6 min), hold 50% B (6–7.5 min), return to 95% B (7.6–10 min). Injection: 2 µL. ESI: 5500 V, 550 °C, curtain gas 35 psi. Quantification via metabolite-specific MRM with optimized DP/CE. PCA (prcomp R, unit variance scaling); HCA/heatmaps (pheatmap, Pearson correlation). OPLS-DA (MetaboAnalystR, mean-centered, 200 permutations): significant metabolites (VIP > 1 and |Log₂FC|>1). KEGG annotation; MSEA with hypergeometric test.
Immunohistochemistry (IHC)
Tumor tissue samples were paraffin-embedded and sectioned. The sections underwent histological processing, including deparaffinization, rehydration, antigen retrieval, and serum blocking, per established protocols. SHMT1-specific (1:200) and ZNF526-specific (1:100) antibodies were applied and incubated overnight. Target protein expression was assessed microscopically after DAB staining, with darker brown indicating higher expression. Positive rates were analyzed using Image software. In the tissue microarray context, the Pattern Quant module of Quant Center 2.3 software differentiated brown (positive) and blue areas. The Mask Area function accurately delineated the tissue regions. Histo Quant was then utilized at the lower threshold of the brown area to compute the area and grayscale values of positive regions, with optical density determined from these grayscale values.
Western blot and antibodies
Cell samples were lysed by using RIPA lysis buffer (P0013B, Beyotime Biotechnology) and Wb was performed. The detailed Wb procedure has been described previously [
20
]. The antibodies used are as follows: anti-ZNF526 (54686, ThermoFisher), anti-Cyclin D1 (60186-1-Ig, Proteintech), anti-P21 (10355-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-RB1(10048-2-Ig, Proteintech), anti-Phospho-RB1(84692-1-RR, Proteintech), anti-SHMT1 (A12489, Abclonal), anti-GAPDH (2118, Cell Signaling Technology), HRP, Goat Anti-Rabbit Ig(A21020,Abbkine). Images were captured by using a C-Digit chemiluminescent Western blot scanner (9120, LI-COR).
Quantitative real-time RT-PCR
Total RNA was extracted from cells using TRIZOL reagent (15596026CN, Invitrogen). Subsequently, 1 µg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the Reverse Transcription kit (15662ES, Yeasen). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using SYBR Green I Master Mix (11200ES, Yeasen) on an ABI 7500 instrument (Applied Biosystems). The relative mRNA expression levels of the target genes were normalized to the endogenous control GAPDH and calculated using the comparative 2^(-ΔΔCt) method. The sequences of all primers used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 1.
RNA-seq
Total RNA from biological replicates (
n
= 3) of ZNF526-overexpressing(ZNF526-OE) and vector control cells was isolated using TRIzol. RNA integrity (RIN > 8.0) was confirmed via Agilent Bioanalyzer RNA Nano chip. Strand-specific mRNA libraries (Illumina TruSeq Stranded mRNA Kit) were sequenced on NovaSeq 6000 (150 bp paired-end, 40 million reads/sample). Raw reads underwent quality control (FastQC), adapter trimming/quality filtering (Trimmomatic), and alignment to GRCh38/hg38 (HISAT2). Transcript abundance was quantified as expected counts (featureCounts, GENCODE annotation). Differentially expressed genes were identified using DESeq2 with median of ratios normalization. Significant DEGs met |log₂FC| >1 and FDR-adjusted
p
< 0.05. Results were visualized by volcano plot (ggplot2) depicting -log₁₀(FDR) versus log₂FC, with significant upregulation (red), downregulation (blue), and non-significant genes (grey).
Intracellular ROS detection using DCFH-DA fluorescence
Cellular ROS levels were assessed using 10 µM DCFH-DA (KTB1910, Abbkine). Cells (confluence < 5 × 10⁵/mL) were pre-treated with compounds in serum-free medium (37 °C, light-protected; duration compound-specific). Positive controls (50–250 µM) were applied to designated wells. After treatment/washing, cells were incubated with DCFH-DA (30 min, 37 °C, dark; min. volume: 1 mL/6-well, 100 µL/96-well) and washed. For suspension protocols, cells (1.0 × 10⁶−2.0 × 10⁷/mL) were probed post-wash.
Fluorescence quantification employed: (1) Microscopy: Adherent cells in serum-free medium/PBS; suspension cells (25–50 µL) on slides. Imaging used FITC filters (488/525 nm; background-subtracted), analyzed in ImageJ via mean intensity = IntDen/Area. (2) Flow cytometry: Trypsinized cells (0.5–1.5 × 10⁵/mL) analyzed at 488/525 nm (real-time/fixed intervals) using FlowJo v10.9.
Luciferase reporter assay
HEK293T cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 5 × 10⁴ cells/well and incubated for 24 h to achieve appropriate confluence. Transient transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 3000 according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Each well received 500 ng of the firefly luciferase reporter plasmid containing the promoter region of interest. For normalization, 50 ng of the Renilla luciferase control plasmid was co-transfected. Cells were incubated for an additional 24 h post-transfection at 37 °C and 5% CO₂. Following treatments, cells were lysed per manufacturer’s protocol (Promega Dual-Luciferase
®
Reporter Assay System), and firefly/Renilla luciferase activities were sequentially quantified using a GloMax Navigator luminometer; relative activity was calculated as firefly-to-Renilla luminescence ratio. Data from biological replicates (
n
≥ 3) were normalized to the respective control group and presented as mean ± SEM.
Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) analysis
Fixed chromatin from formaldehyde-crosslinked cells was isolated and subjected to ultrasonic fragmentation using optimized parameters targeting 200–500 bp fragments. Immunoprecipitation was performed overnight at 4 °C with anti-ZNF526 antibody (54686, ThermoFisher) or species-matched IgG control (2729, Cell Signaling), followed by Protein A/G Magnetic Beads (Thermo Fisher). DNA-protein complexes were reverse crosslinked, and purified ChIP DNA fragments were analyzed via qPCR using SYBR Green assays. Technical triplicates and input DNA normalization were applied throughout. Comprehensive procedural details follow established methodologies as referenced [
20
].
Statistical analysis
Statistical significance was assessed using Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA, followed by the Mann-Whitney U test, all conducted with SPSS software version 23.0.

We also established shZNF526 or ZNF526-KO stable cell lines(Fig.
2
G, J and Supplementary Fig.
1
B). In vitro cell growth assays showed that shZNF526 or ZNF526-KO markedly suppressed cell growth (Fig.
2

2

To elucidate the mechanism by which ZNF526 promotes cell growth, we analyzed the cell cycle distribution by flow cytometry. The results demonstrated that ZNF526 knockdown induced significant G1-phase arrest in both HCC1806 and MDA-MB-468 cell lines (Fig.
2
O, P). Conversely, ZNF526 overexpression in MDA-MB-231 cells reduced the G1-phase proportion while increasing the S-phase proportion(Fig.
2

2
R). In contrast, ZNF526 overexpression elevated the levels of both CCND1 and phospho-Rb, and reduced p21 expression (Fig.
2
S, T). In contrast to its pronounced effect on the cell cycle, neither ZNF526 knockdown nor overexpression significantly affected apoptosis in TNBC cells (Supplementary Fig. 1 C, D).
ZNF526 regulates SGOC metabolism and cellular GSH level
Previous studies have indicated that the ZNF526-V94A missense variant may regulate metabolic processes [
7
]. To fully investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying ZNF526-mediated regulation of TNBC cell growth, we performed non-targeted metabolomic analysis in MDA-MB-468 and MDA-MB-231 cells after ZNF526 over-expression. Metabolite set enrichment analysis (MSEA) of these data revealed significant enrichment in SGOC-related metabolic pathways, including glycine-serine-threonine metabolism, cysteine and methionine metabolism, and the folate-mediated one-carbon pool (Fig.
3

The SGOC pathway primarily underpins cancer cell growth through its essential roles in nucleic acid synthesis, epigenetic regulation via methylation, and antioxidant defense [

]. To determine key SGOC-derived metabolic pathway features that are essential for TNBC cell growth, we assessed the ability of key downstream metabolites to rescue the cell growth defect induced by ZNF526 knockdown. Specifically, we tested metabolites from major SGOC branches: purines and pyrimidines (nucleosides), methyl donors (S-adenosyl methionine, SAM), and antioxidants (reduced glutathione, GSH). Notably, neither SAM nor nucleosides restored the growth loss caused by ZNF526 knockdown (Fig.

The results showed that ZNF526 knockdown significantly reduced the GSH/GSSG ratio, GSH levels, and total glutathione (GSH + GSSG) content. Conversely, ZNF526 overexpression significantly increased these parameters, with a more significant increase observed in mutant ZNF526-V94A overexpressing cells (Fig.

ZNF526 enhanced cellular antioxidant capacity
Given the critical importance of GSH for cancer cells to counteract excessive Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS), we measured the cellular ROS levels after ZNF526 knockdown or overexpression. Our results demonstrated that ZNF526 knockdown markedly elevated intracellular ROS levels both in MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-468 cells. Conversely, ZNF526 overexpression effectively attenuated ROS accumulation, with cells expressing the mutant ZNF526-V94A exhibiting a more pronounced reduction in ROS levels (Fig.
4
A, and Supplementary Fig.
3

To further investigate whether ZNF526 regulates SHMT1 transcription, we conducted luciferase reporter assays using a series of luciferase reporter plasmids driven by SHMT1 promoter fragments in HEK293T cells. This cell line was chosen as a tool cell line for its well-established high transfection efficiency, which is critical for obtaining robust and reproducible results in promoter activity studies. Our results demonstrated that ZNF526 significantly enhanced SHMT1 promoter-driven luciferase activity (Fig.
5
E). Deletion mapping analysis suggested that the potential ZNF526 binding site is located within the 500 bp region downstream of the transcription start site (TSS) of SHMT1 (Fig.
5
E). Further prediction using JASPAR online database identified two tandemly arranged potential ZNF526-binding sites (designated site 1 and site 2) within this SHMT1 promoter region (Fig.
5
G). To functionally validate these sites, we engineered luciferase reporter constructs containing either the wild-type SHMT1 promoter, individual mutations in each ZNF526-binding site (mut1 or mut2) or a double mutation (mut1 + 2) (Fig.
5
G). The reporter assays revealed that individual mutations in either binding site significantly attenuated ZNF526-dependent luciferase activation, whereas combined mutation of both sites completely abolished the induction (Fig.
5
H).
To examine whether ZNF526 binds to the SHMT1 promoter, we performed chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay. The results demonstrated significant enrichment of ZNF526 at the SHMT1 promoter region compared to control IgG (Fig.
5
I).
Furthermore, luciferase reporter assays revealed that the mutant ZNF526- V94A induced significantly higher signaling compared to wild-type ZNF526 (Fig.
5
J). Consistently, ChIP-qPCR analysis demonstrated stronger binding affinity of the mutant ZNF526 to the SHMT1 promoter region (Fig.
5
K). Together, these results demonstrated ZNF526 transcriptionally activates SHMT1 expression and the ZNF526-V94A mutation has a stronger capacity.
Additionally, in order to evaluate the clinical relevance of our findings, we analyzed TCGA data and observed that there is a significant positive correlation between ZNF526 and SHMT1 mRNA levels (Fig.
5

Discussion
TNBC represents the most aggressive breast cancer subtype, marked by frequent metastasis, limited treatment options, and poor prognosis (median survival: ~18 months for metastatic cases) [
24
,
25
]. Current therapies rely predominantly on cytotoxic chemotherapy, underscoring the urgent need for targeted strategies. In this study, we identify ZNF526 as a pivotal oncogenic driver in TNBC. We demonstrate that ZNF526 potentiates tumor growth through transcriptional induction of SHMT1. The ZNF526-SHMT1 axis drives SGOC metabolic flux to promote GSH biosynthesis, enhancing antioxidant capacity and sustaining TNBC growth. These findings establish ZNF526 as an attractive therapeutic target for TNBC.
While evidence remains limited, a germline variant in ZNF526 (rs144433879) was significantly associated with Hodgkin lymphoma in genome-wide association studies, promoting its genetic heterogeneity [
6
]. Although genome-wide association studies have implicated the ZNF526 locus (particularly the rs3810151 p.Val94Ala variant) in breast cancer susceptibility [
7
], its functional role in oncogenesis remains largely unexplored. Through integrated analysis of TCGA data complemented by IHC validation, we identified TNBC-specific ZNF526 overexpression that inversely correlated with patient survival. Comprehensive functional characterization revealed ZNF526’s critical role in promoting TNBC proliferation and tumor growth. Strikingly, the Val94Ala variant displayed significantly stronger oncogenic effects than wild-type ZNF526, providing direct experimental evidence for its gain-of-function nature.
SGOC metabolic network represents a critical hub in cancer metabolic reprogramming, providing essential one-carbon units that support three fundamental processes: (1) de novo nucleotide biosynthesis, (2) SAM-dependent methylation reactions, and (3) glutathione (GSH) synthesis - all crucial for maintaining proliferative capacity and redox balance [
18
,
26
]. As a central enzyme in this pathway, SHMT1 has been increasingly recognized for its oncogenic role in various cancers, including breast cancer [
27
–
29
]. Our untargeted metabolomics analysis revealed that ZNF526 overexpression significantly disrupts SGOC metabolic homeostasis in TNBC models. Mechanistic investigations established SHMT1 as a direct transcriptional target of ZNF526, with luciferase reporter assays demonstrating promoter transactivation and ChIP-qPCR validating direct binding to the SHMT1 promoter. Notably, the Val94Ala mutation enhanced ZNF526’s functional activity, demonstrating both stronger SHMT1 promoter binding and greater transactivation capacity compared to wild-type ZNF526 - definitive proof of its gain-of-function nature. Future studies should investigate the specific structural modifications conferred by this mutation that enhance ZNF526’s transcriptional activity. Additionally, while our study has focused on SHMT1 as a key downstream target of ZNF526, we cannot rule out its potential involvement in TNBC pathogenesis through other downstream targets or pathways. Future studies should focus on a comprehensive characterization of ZNF526’s downstream mechanisms and define the full repertoire of genes it directly regulates.
ROS play a dual role in cancer biology - at physiological levels they function as important signaling molecules, while excessive accumulation leads to oxidative damage and cell death [
30
,
31
]. Notably, aggressive cancers like TNBC develop robust antioxidant defenses, particularly through the GSH system, to maintain reductive homeostasis and support uncontrolled proliferation [
32
]. Our findings reveal that ZNF526 promotes TNBC survival by upregulating SHMT1 to enhance SGOC metabolic flux, thereby increasing GSH production and elevating the GSH/GSSG ratio to reduce oxidative stress. Antioxidant treatment (NAC or GSH) rescued both proliferation and GSH/GSSG ratio in ZNF526-depleted cells. Among all key SGOC pathway outputs (nucleotide precursors, SAM, and GSH), only GSH supplementation reversed the growth inhibition caused by ZNF526 knockdown. These results unequivocally establish GSH as the key metabolic effector of the ZNF526-SHMT1 axis in TNBC. Nevertheless, we cannot rule out the possibility that ZNF526-SHMT1 axis may participate in regulating broader TNBC malignancy—such as metastasis, drug resistance, or stemness—by influencing the SAM, nucleotide pathways or other SGOC metabolic branches. This warrants further investigation in future studies.
This study elucidates a previously unrecognized oncogenic cascade in TNBC governed by the ZNF526-SHMT1-GSH axis. We demonstrate that ZNF526, particularly its cancer-associated Val94Ala variant, functions as a metabolic master regulator that drives TNBC progression through transcriptional activation of SHMT1. This leads to enhanced SGOC pathway activity and GSH production, creating a permissive redox environment for tumor growth. Importantly, our findings not only establish ZNF526 as a previously unrecognized important regulator in TNBC pathogenesis but also unveil its downstream effector SHMT1 as a druggable vulnerability. Therapeutic targeting of this axis may offer new opportunities for TNBC treatment.
Conclusion
Our study demonstrates that ZNF526 functions as a master regulator of redox homeostasis in TNBC. The gain-of-function Val94Ala variant (rs3810151) potentiates ZNF526’s oncogenic activity by augmenting its transcriptional efficiency. Mechanistically, ZNF526 transcriptionally activates SHMT1 to stimulate glutathione biosynthesis, thereby conferring oxidative stress resistance and promoting tumor growth. These findings establish ZNF526 as a critical metabolic regulator in TNBC, with therapeutic opportunities through direct ZNF526 inhibition or disruption of its downstream SHMT1/GSH axis.
