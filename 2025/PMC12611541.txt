1
Introduction
Multi'omic analysis of biological samples, encompassing genomics, transcriptomics, epigenomics, proteomics, and metabolomics, enables systems biology‐level insight into complex pathologies. Among these, metabolomic profiling offers high‐throughput global analysis of small molecule metabolites and their interactions within biological systems [
1
]. By providing a comprehensive view of the dynamic roles of metabolites in many biological processes, metabolomics can reveal perturbations that serve as early indicators of cellular dysfunction. Reflecting this potential, metabolomics has been described as the “stethoscope” of the 21st century [
2
].
Metabolites display a broad range of physicochemical properties and concentrations, making it impossible for a single instrument or analytical platform to characterize the entire metabolome. Consequently, different analytical platforms are employed to conduct either targeted metabolomics—focused on quantifying the levels of predefined metabolites—or untargeted metabolomics—discovery‐based profiling of all measurable metabolites in a sample using a specific analytical platform. Proton nuclear magnetic resonance (
1
H NMR) spectroscopy is one of the principal techniques for targeted metabolomics, valued for its high reproducibility, quantitative accuracy, and minimal sample preparation requirements [
3
] but limited by the number of metabolites that can be analyzed. Mass spectrometry (MS) is the primary platform for untargeted metabolomics [
4
], and typically coupled to separation techniques, such as liquid chromatography (LC), to reduce sample complexity and enhance compound detection [
5
]. Different MS platforms, from low‐resolution MS, such as triple quadrupole (TQ), to high‐resolution MS, such as a quadrupole time‐of‐flight (QTOF), are used for untargeted or targeted analyses, with several differences between these two approaches.
Targeted MS‐based metabolomics provides in‐depth and quantitative assessments of specific metabolites that represent distinct biological pathways or cellular physiological states; however, the coverage of detected metabolites is generally limited compared to untargeted metabolomics [
6
]. Conversely, untargeted metabolomics yields a broad range of chemical data from an analyzed sample, facilitating discovery of novel compounds, but it is limited by challenges in unknown compound identification, reduced quantitative accuracy, lower reproducibility, and reliance on sophisticated data processing workflows [
7
]. However, emerging computational technologies and access to more comprehensive metabolite libraries are aiding the identification of unknown compounds and help overcome these limitations [
8
]. Additionally, lipidomics—a growing subfield of metabolomics dedicated to the study of lipid species—has advanced into a powerful assay for elucidating the roles of lipid compounds in cellular metabolism, disease mechanisms, and biomarker discovery, through the ability to merge expanded targeted and untargeted approaches [
9
].
A typical metabolomics analysis workflow starts by selecting a sample type based on the research question, followed by sample preparation for the specific platform used (MS or NMR). Raw data acquisition is then followed by preprocessing of raw data (e.g., peak detection of metabolites) using a software for qualitative or quantitative analysis of compounds. This is followed by data processing, statistical analysis, and interpretation of identified metabolites within the biological context including pathway and molecular network analysis. Fetal congenital anomalies and genetic abnormalities complicate 3%–5% of pregnancies [
10
] and can significantly impact pregnancy and neonatal outcomes. Chromosomal abnormalities occur in about 1 in 150 live births [
11
] and congenital malformations are the leading cause of infant mortality [
12
]. Therefore individualized, precise prenatal screening and diagnosis are essential for effective counseling and clinical management. Current screening strategies for congenital anomalies or genetic disorders incorporate maternal risk factors (e.g., age and medical history), maternal serum analytes, fetal cell‐free DNA (cfDNA) analysis, and routine prenatal ultrasound imaging. Diagnostic testing, including amniocentesis and chorionic villus sampling (CVS), and targeted prenatal imaging via ultrasound and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are usually offered for pregnancies at increased risk. Despite these approaches, most fetal anomalies occur in pregnancies without identifiable risk factors and their etiology can remain undiscovered despite this screening and diagnostic strategy. In addition, fetal imaging can be limited by factors such as gestational age, fetal position, multiple pregnancy, maternal obesity, and inter‐operator variability. Some patients decline prenatal diagnostic testing because of concerns over procedure‐related risks, costs, or result turnaround times. Thus, the prenatal diagnostic field will benefit from new biomarkers that can improve screening and diagnosis for pregnancies with fetal anomalies and deepen understanding of the pathological processes underlying these anomalies.
Recent studies in children with developmental disorders have shown that metabolomic profiling can assist the interpretation of variants identified through exome or genome sequencing by adding important functional data [
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
]. Given the dynamic interactions between maternal and fetal metabolomes during pregnancy [
17
], metabolomics may hold potential as a functional assay to complement prenatal genetic testing. It may also offer insights into the mechanisms underlying different congenital anomalies or fetal genetic conditions, thereby improving counseling and prediction of neonatal outcomes. Several studies have already reported distinct metabolomic profiles associated with obstetric complications [
18
,
19
] and fetal structural and genetic abnormalities [
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
]. These metabolite signatures may illuminate pathophysiological processes associated with these conditions during fetal development that other prenatal tests cannot capture.
In this review, we examine the current state of prenatal metabolomics in the context of fetal abnormalities and genetic disorders, to explore its potential as a complementary tool in prenatal screening and diagnosis while identifying important areas for future research.
2
Methods
2.1
Search Strategy
We conducted a comprehensive literature search to identify studies reporting on the use of prenatal metabolomics in the context of congenital structural and genetic abnormalities including publications up to the end of March 2025. The search strategy encompassed four databases: MEDLINE (OVID), Embase (Elsevier), Web of Science (Clarivate), and Cochrane Library (Wiley). Using MEDLINE (OVID) (MEDLINE on OVIDSP) as the primary database, the search incorporated the following concepts: metabolomics, amniotic fluid/pregnancy/prenatal diagnosis, and congenital abnormalities. Relevant medical subjects headings (MeSH) terms were identified (see Supporting Information
S1
: Appendix), and keywords were searched using the title, abstract, and keyword fields of MEDLINE (OVID).
2.2
Study Selection
Two reviewers (SA and AMM) independently assessed all identified articles for eligibility. Additional potentially relevant articles were identified by manually reviewing the reference lists of the included publications. We retained peer‐reviewed journal articles with available full texts that adequately describe their study objectives, methods, and results.
2.3
Study Eligibility
Inclusion criteria were use of MS or
1
H NMR analytical platforms, human studies, evaluation of genetic or structural abnormalities in singleton gestations, English language, and availability of an abstract. Exclusion criteria included animal studies, in vitro cell culture studies, multiple gestations, studies without metabolomics analysis, studies addressing metabolomics without presence of genetic or structural abnormalities, and systematic reviews and meta‐analyses. Disagreements regarding inclusion or exclusion of a study were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached.
2.4
Data Extraction and Synthesis
Data extraction was performed using a standardized excel sheet, capturing study characteristics, patient population, type of genetic or structural abnormality, analytical platform, and key metabolomics findings. The primary outcomes of interest were metabolomic profiles associated with fetal genetic or structural anomalies. To improve readability and quality of language, the authors used an artificial intelligence tool for grammatical revision of this paper.

Several studies have investigated the use of metabolomics in the setting of fetal structural and genetic abnormalities. Among those, congenital heart disease (CHD) and trisomy 21 (T21) emerged as the most frequently studied conditions using prenatal metabolomics. Accordingly, we chose to present a detailed discussion of these two abnormalities in separate sections, followed by discussion of the role of metabolomic profiling in other prenatally detected genetic and fetal structural anomalies, and of its role in prenatal diagnosis of suspected single gene disorders.
3.1
Congenital Heart Disease
Congenital heart disease (CHD) is the most common congenital abnormality, with a prevalence of 8–10 per 1000 live births [
64
,
65
]. Prenatal detection of CHD improves perinatal outcome and reduces perioperative mortality, particularly in cases with severe and critical CHD [
66
]. Although genetic and chromosomal abnormalities contribute to approximately 15% of CHD [
67
], most CHD develop without identifiable risk factors, supporting the idea that a complex interplay between environmental influences and genetic susceptibility plays a significant role in the etiology of CHD [
68
]. Emerging evidence from metabolomic studies indicates a potential association between altered maternal metabolism and the development of CHD [
21
,
51
,
54
,
57
]. Most studies have examined a broad spectrum of CHD subtypes, ranging from less severe anomalies such as septal defects to more complex lesions, including tetralogy of Fallot and transposition of the great arteries. The first pilot study investigating the role of metabolomics in prenatal CHD detection used a targeted approach on first‐trimester maternal blood. This study identified alterations in acylcarnitine and sphingomyelin in pregnancies complicated by severe CHD subtypes [
22
], indicating the presence of increased maternal lipid metabolism, particularly in pathways related to fatty acid b‐oxidation. A subsequent study analyzed second‐trimester maternal serum metabolomics using gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC‐MS) and identified malonic acid, 3‐hydroxybutyric acid, methylglutaric acid, urea, androstenedione, fructose, tocopherol, leucine, and putrescine as the most discriminatory metabolites [
54
]. This study included samples from individuals with pregnancies undergoing termination due to a suspected fetal CHD. Taylor et al. employed an untargeted metabolomic approach using maternal serum and validated their findings in a subset of samples via NMR, identifying 44 metabolites associated with CHD. Common perturbations found in this study included amino acid metabolism, androgenic steroids, and elevated succinylcarnitine levels in pregnancies complicated by CHD, though changes in specific CHD subtypes were not reported [
56
]. The observations of altered amino acid metabolism and androgenic steroid levels in this study align with findings previously reported by Troisi et al. [
54
]. The most frequently implicated pathways include the urea cycle, ammonia recycling, and aspartate metabolism. More recently, Mires, et al. combined untargeted metabolomics and lipidomics on maternal serum and identified significant lipid alterations associated with fetal CHD [
60
]. Notably, these lipid changes were consistent with findings from studies analyzing maternal urine and saliva [
21
,
43
,
62
]. Xie, et al. used GC‐MS to analyze maternal urine and identified disruptions in short‐chain fatty acid and aromatic acid metabolism [
43
]. Another targeted metabolomics study on maternal urine found alterations in phospholipids, phosphatidylcholine and phosphatidylethanolamine biosynthesis, and in fatty acid metabolism [
21
]. A recent systematic review summarizing the significant maternal metabolites associated with fetal CHD concluded that the most frequent metabolic disruptions involve lipid metabolism, the methionine‐homocysteine cycle, and the
trans
‐sulfuration pathway [
69
]. Most recently, Bahado‐Singh et al. applied artificial intelligence to analyze maternal saliva metabolomics in pregnancies complicated by fetal CHD. Their analysis revealed significant alterations in arachidonic acid, alpha‐linolenic acid, and tryptophan metabolism, findings suggestive of substantial lipid metabolic dysfunction in cyanotic CHD, the most severe form of the disease [
62
]. To explore fetal‐specific metabolomic changes and minimize maternal biochemical confounders, two studies analyzed amniotic fluid (AF) metabolomics in fetal CHD‐affected pregnancies using an untargeted approach. Li et al. employed gas chromatography/time‐of‐flight mass spectrometry (GC‐TOF/MS) and identified elevated uric acid and proline levels in AF from fetal CHD‐affected pregnancies [
51
]. Another study using LC‐MS‐based metabolomic analysis of AF revealed significant changes in metabolite concentrations, particularly those associated with dimethylfuran fatty acids and serotonin derivatives [
57
]. Collectively, emerging research in metabolomics and lipidomics supports the potential for specific biomarkers to enhance prenatal CHD diagnosis, potentially outperforming conventional screening models. However, larger studies are needed to validate these findings. Additionally, the variability in metabolomic platforms, biofluid sources, and CHD subtypes examined, has contributed to inconsistent identification of specific metabolic alterations. While current evidence consistently points to maternal lipid pathway disruptions in pregnancies affected by fetal CHD, it remains an open question whether altered maternal metabolites have any direct correlation with certain fetal phenotypes. Further, studies could not reproduce a single panel of metabolites which could be used to distinguish CHD cases from controls.
3.2
Trisomy 21
Trisomy 21 (Down syndrome) is the most common chromosomal disorder, occurring in approximately 1 per 800 live births [
70
]. Prenatal detection of trisomy 21 typically relies on screening tests followed by diagnostic procedures such as chorionic villus sampling or amniocentesis to obtain a karyotype; however, these procedures carry a small risk of pregnancy loss that is not acceptable to all patients. Although the introduction of maternal cfDNA based screening tests has improved the accuracy of screening for trisomy 21, there is continuing interest in the development of new affordable non‐invasive tests that could enhance screening accuracy. Metabolomics has been investigated as a promising avenue for complementing existing screening approaches for pregnancies affected by trisomy 21. Beyond its screening or diagnostic potential, it may also provide valuable insight into the cellular and molecular consequences of the extra chromosome 21 on fetal and placental physiology and health. For example, metabolite biomarkers could help understand variability in phenotypic features of affected fetuses and contribute to the prediction of associated complications and long‐term outcomes [
48
].
Multiple studies have investigated the role of metabolomics in pregnancies complicated by trisomy 21. Chen, et al. used LC‐MS/MS for untargeted metabolomics and lipidomics analyses and found significant alterations in carbon, amino acid, glyceride, sphingolipid, and glycerophospholipid metabolism in AF of second‐trimester trisomy 21 pregnancies compared to controls [
55
]. Using the same method but for targeted metabolomics, Parfeniuk et al. identified significant differences in the levels of four AF metabolites: methylhistidine, hexanoylcarnitine, diacetylspermine and p‐cresol sulfate, which they proposed may reflect developmental differences of the nervous system and musculature [
48
]. Huang, et al., using UPLC‐MS on AF, noted significant alterations in several metabolites including coproporphyrin III, glycocholic acid, taurochenodeoxycholate, taurocholate, hydrocortisone, pregnenolone sulfate, L‐histidine, L‐arginine, L‐glutamate, and L‐glutamine. These alterations were attributed to abnormal gene expression coming from the extra chromosome 21 [
35
]. They report that the reduction in coproporphyrin III in trisomy 21 may reflect impaired erythropoiesis, while imbalances in glutamine‐glutamate concentrations have been linked to abnormal brain development and altered glutamatergic transmission in individuals with Down syndrome and in mouse models of Down syndrome [
35
,
71
,
72
,
73
]. Liu, et al. further reported lower levels of several steroid hormones and their derivatives, higher levels of glutathione catabolites coupled with reduced levels of gamma‐glutamyl amino acids, and increased levels of phospholipid catabolites, sugars, and dicarboxylic acids. These findings suggest increased oxidative stress [
47
]. Using NMR, Bahado‐Singh et al. identified alterations in several metabolites, including 3‐hydroxybutyrate, involved in brain growth and myelination, and 2‐hydroxybutyrate, involved in the defense against oxidative stress [
20
].
3.3
Metabolomics in Other Genetic and Fetal Structural Anomalies
Graca et al. conducted metabolomic profiling of pregnancies with various fetal anomalies and observed that certain metabolic alterations may be shared across different types of anomalies. While their findings do not capture the full spectrum of congenital anomalies, they point to interesting pathological processes occurring at the cellular level [
24
]. Notably, some observed metabolite changes indicated increased reliance on anaerobic energy production, as evidenced by decreased glucose and increased lactate levels. They also identified mitochondrial respiratory chain dysregulation, with elevated levels of succinate in association with specific fetal malformations. Given that succinate is a substrate for the mitochondrial respiratory chain, its accumulation may indicate reduced utilization. Moreover, a reduction in the gluconeogenic amnio acids, including alanine, glutamate, leucine, valine, tyrosine, and phenylalanine, was observed, potentially reflecting their increased use for glucose production in hypoxic tissues [
24
]. Lastly, noted decreased urea levels may indicate kidney underdevelopment or renal hypoxia, both characteristic metabolic signatures of fetal hypoxia [
24
].
Subsequent studies focusing on metabolomics for specific fetal abnormalities have confirmed the changes described by Graca et al. and identified additional metabolomic alterations. Croitor‐Sava et al. conducted metabolomic profiling of AF from pregnancies complicated by fetal congenital diaphragmatic hernia (CDH) and demonstrated that metabolomic profiles of fetuses with CDH were distinct from those of healthy controls, independent of gestational age [
32
]. Buffin‐Meyer et al. used a combination of omics approaches to identify metabolite and peptide markers in the urine of fetuses with posterior urethral valves. They concluded that multi‐omics analysis was better than metabolomics alone in predicting postnatal renal outcomes [
37
]. Zheng et al. analyzed maternal serum metabolomic profiles in pregnancies affected by fetal neural tube defects, reporting alterations linked to impaired mitochondrial respiration and reduced levels of gamma‐aminobutyric acid and glutamine [
23
]. Groenen et al. found significantly higher concentrations of succinic acid and glutamine in AF in fetuses with spina bifida compared with controls [
17
]. Increased nuchal translucency (NT) in the first trimester is a known marker for aneuploidies and structural fetal anomalies [
53
]. Murgia et al. conducted a pilot study on AF of euploid fetuses with increased NT to explore if there is an associated metabolic fingerprint. They identified nine metabolites, 2‐hydroxybutyric acid, 3‐hydroxybutyric, 1,5 Anydro‐Sorbitol, cholesterol, erythronic acid, fructose, malic acid, threitol, and threonine, that differed significantly between fetuses with increased NT and controls. These metabolites were linked to alterations in energy metabolism pathways.
3.4
Single Gene Disorders
Metabolomics in amniotic fluid of fetuses with single gene disorders has also been explored. Nolin, et al. combined metabolomic and proteomic profiling of AF from women who are Fragile X premutation carriers (55–200 CGG repeats) [
52
]. They noticed deficits in serine biosynthesis pathways impacting the Krebs cycle and proposed that maternal serine supplementation might mitigate mitochondrial dysfunction [
52
]. This study demonstrates how metabolomics can not only result in the discovery of novel diagnostic biomarkers but can also inform the development of personalized therapeutic strategies. Metabolomics of AF from pregnancies with fetal hemoglobin Bart's (Hb Bart's) disease, the most severe form of alpha thalassemia, has also been investigated. Chen et al. [
49
] found significant alterations in glutamate and glutathione metabolism, aspartate metabolism, urea metabolism and triglyceride metabolism in the Hb Bart’s group compared to controls. The authors noted that antioxidant metabolites involved in glutamate and glutathione metabolism increased in response to oxidative stress reactions [
49
].
4
Discussion
Metabolomics is an emerging precision medicine tool with the potential to identify biomarkers that provide novel insights into the pathophysiology of specific fetal structural and genetic abnormalities. Its ability to identify multiple metabolites at early gestational ages, with rapid turnaround time and acceptable cost, positions it as a promising complement to contemporary genomic technologies such as genome‐wide sequencing. Moreover, in cases where prenatal screening results are uncertain or atypical, metabolomic analysis may offer additional biomarkers that can refine diagnostic accuracy. Beyond its diagnostic potential, metabolomics also serves as a valuable modality for investigating the complex interplay between maternal, placenta and fetal biomarkers.
Studies evaluating the application of metabolomics in prenatal diagnosis, such as its role in congenital heart disease, have identified promising biomarkers that could augment existing screening modalities, specifically at early gestational ages. Similarly, metabolomic profiling may offer complementary insights into the pathophysiology of common aneuploidies, such as trisomy 21, potentially enhancing other screening modalities. As previously discussed, certain identified metabolites may reflect underlying pathological processes not readily detected using other methods. These metabolites could in the future inform prognosis and guide counseling about possible earlier clinical interventions.
However, several limitations must still be addressed in studies before metabolomics can be integrated into prenatal care. For example, current studies using
1
H NMR are constrained by the limited number of metabolites analyzed and are often hypothesis‐driven, focused on presumed biochemical alterations associated with the specific abnormalities. Importantly, metabolomic profiles for normal fetal phenotypes and development remain to be established with consideration to gestational age. Standardizing analytical methods and platforms in metabolomics research is an essential first step in overcoming these challenges. Further challenges noted in the reviewed studies include small and variable patient cohorts and heterogeneity in severity and types of anomalies studied. Differences in the severity of a given abnormality may correlate with distinct metabolomic signatures, complicating reproducibility, and cross‐study comparisons. Consequently, larger studies of well‐characterized cohorts are needed to validate findings and enable comparison of results across comparable phenotypic presentations of specific anomalies. Additionally, differences between analytic platforms, biofluid sources, and anomaly subtypes have hampered consistency in identifying specific metabolic alterations across studies, hindering the development of clinical recommendations. These challenges highlight an important opportunity for enhanced collaboration among clinicians, biotechnologists, and chemists to harmonize methodologies, expand the size of study cohorts for specific anomalies and standardize analyses. Such coordinated efforts will facilitate the integration of metabolomics into prenatal diagnostics and ultimately clinical practice.
5
Conclusion
Metabolomics represents a promising adjunct to prenatal screening and diagnosis, providing deeper insights into the cellular‐level changes associated with genetic and structural abnormalities. Despite this potential, its adoption into clinical practice remains limited. This review underscores the possible role of metabolomics as a complementary modality to current rapid advances in prenatal genetic screening and diagnosis, particularly through its ability to elucidate disease pathophysiology. Nonetheless, this review identified significant limitations in the existing literature, including heterogeneity in patient cohorts, inconsistencies among metabolomic platforms, methodological differences between targeted and untargeted approaches, small sample sizes, and limited reproducibility of findings, even within the same fetal abnormality. To strengthen the evidence supporting the clinical utility of prenatal metabolomics, it is imperative to standardize analytical platforms and refine targeted metabolite profiling to promote validation and reproducibility across studies.
