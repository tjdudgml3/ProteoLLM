Glycans are posttranslational modifications that affect myriad biological processes like cell recognition, signaling, and differentiation, predominantly through interactions with glycan-binding proteins (GBPs) at the cell surface (
1
). The galectin class of GBPs (
2
), in particular, are noted for their diverse roles in placental biology (
3
–
5
). For example, galectin–glycoprotein interactions have been implicated in angiogenesis (
6
), immune tolerance (
7
), and cell differentiation (
8
), all necessary processes for the function of the mature placenta, which must mediate the fetal supply of oxygen and nutrients while shielding the semiallogenic fetus from maternal immune attack and foreign pathogens (
9
,
10
). These complex functions are distributed among distinct cell types generated by diverging differentiation pathways of progenitor cytotrophoblasts (CTBs). One such terminal differentiation process is syncytialization, the fusion of mononuclear CTBs to create the multinucleated syncytiotrophoblast (STB) layer that composes the outermost perimeter of the placenta (
9
). The STB is directly exposed to maternal blood supply and serves as a critical exchange site for nutrients, oxygen, and hormonal signaling across its enhanced surface area (
10
,
11
). The proper formation of the STB is critical for healthy placentation, as defects are correlated to poor pregnancy outcomes, including placental insufficiency (
12
), developmental heart disease (
13
), and preeclampsia (
14
,
15
).
Galectin-3 has been shown to promote the formation of the STB via CTB fusion in cellulo, and reduced fetal and maternal expression of galectin-3 is correlated with placental insufficiency and fetal growth restriction (
16
–
18
). However, the molecular mechanism of galectin-3-mediated fusion has yet to be elucidated, and the precise glycoproteins engaging galectin-3 for this function have not been identified. While the glycan-binding preferences of galectin-3 toward β-galactoside sugars are well studied (
19
), identifying the specific glycoproteins required for binding and function are also key toward comprehensive elucidation of the mechanisms through which galectin-3 controls syncytialization. Furthermore, the elucidation of functional GBP–glycoprotein complexes facilitates the potential for precise targeting of the galectin–glycoconjugate interaction without perturbing global glycosylation or orthogonal galectin interactions. Importantly, the degree of binding and the biological outcome of GBP–glycan interactions can be influenced by the presentation of the glycan on its glycoconjugate carrier, its localization in cells or within plasma membrane microdomains, or its topological orientation relative to the extracellular space (
20
). These parameters can be dictated by the protein carrier and its three-dimensional conformation. As such, approaches that neglect the endogenous biological context of GBP ligands or focus solely on the glycome are limited. The identification of GBP interactomes has historically been stymied by insufficient tools that do not capture the transient and relatively weak interactions (K
D
~ mM) between GBPs and their ligands in live cells (
21
). Indeed, only one placental interactor of galectin-3, solute carrier family 3 member 2 (SLC3A2), has been identified by coimmunoprecipitation (
22
). While the interactions between GBPs and their ligands have been previously precluded from study in endogenous contexts due to their chemical and structural complexity, proximity labeling has emerged as a powerful tool to identify the interactions of biomolecules in live cells while preserving the native characteristics of binding (
23
).
Our lab has generated a proximity labeling platform to identify the glycan-mediated interactomes of GBPs using recombinant proteins of interest fused with the engineered enzyme APEX2 (
24
–
26
). Upon incubation of APEX2–GBP conjugates with live cells where the GBP can bind to its native counterreceptors, APEX2 catalyzes the formation of short-lived biotin phenoxyl radicals to biotinylate neighboring proteins (
27
,
28
), effectively converting the weak and dynamic GBP–glycoconjugate interactions to covalent biotin modifications, permitting isolation of tagged proteins through affinity-based streptavidin enrichment and subsequent quantitative mass spectrometry (MS)-based analysis. This method can be applied to live cells, and it can be temporally managed to capture a snapshot of the distribution of galectins and their neighboring proteins. Here, we employ this proximity labeling platform to identify the interactome of galectin-3 in the widely used BeWo cell line model of CTBs to elucidate the mechanism of galectin-3-mediated syncytialization, as well as compare it to forskolin-induced syncytialization (
29
). From the putative protein interactors identified by MS-based proteomics, we demonstrate that CD9 and integrin beta 1 (ITGB1) are functional counterreceptors of galectin-3 via CRISPR-Cas9 knockout and antibody blocking. We also identify the site and functional relevance of CD9 N-linked glycosylation and found that this modification occurs at a rare noncanonical sequon. We further demonstrate that through this interaction, galectin-3 inhibits the mobility of CD9 along the cell membrane.
Proximity Labeling Identifies Glycan-Dependent Interactors of Galectin-3.
Exogenous galectin-3 has previously been shown to promote the fusion of CTBs into multinucleated STBs using BeWo cells, a choriocarcinoma cell line used as a model for CTBs (

To evaluate whether N-linked glycans or the ability of exogenous galectin-3 to bind glycans contribute to fusion, we assessed galectin-3-mediated fusion in BeWo cells with two modes of glycan perturbation. To alter cellular N-linked glycosylation, we pretreated BeWo cells with kifunensine (10 µg/mL, 48 h), which prevents the addition of the complex β-galactoside motifs recognized by galectin-3 (

After treatment with galectin-3 (48 h), we quantified the number of nuclei in multinucleated cells relative to the total number of nuclei to derive the fusion index, which represents the extent of syncytialization. We observed a significant decrease in the fusion index for both kifunensine-treated BeWo cells and with TD139 pretreatment of galectin-3 (
Fig. 1
B
and
C

Galectin-3 Does Not Activate Canonical Syncytialization Signaling Pathways.
We sought to further explore the mechanism of galectin-3-mediated fusion, probing both biophysical and biochemical functions. Galectin-3 can multimerize through its disordered N-terminal domain (
53
) to generate a galectin lattice that can affect counterreceptor localization, clustering, and propensity to endocytose (
54
,
55
). Due to the dependence of many glycan-mediated interactions on multivalency, we posited that artificial multimers of galectin-3 would more effectively promote fusion than monomeric galectin-3. We compared the fusion indices of BeWo cells treated with WT galectin-3 or with engineered valence-restricted constructs of galectin-3 in the monomeric (G1), trimeric (G3), and hexameric states (G6) (
56
). We observed that treatment with multimeric galectin-3 (G3 and G6) increased fusion index similarly to WT galectin-3, whereas monomeric galectin-3 did not increase the fusion index (
Fig. 4
A
and
B
). These results support a model in which galectin-3 promotes placental cell fusion by oligomerizing to bind to N-glycosylated counterreceptors.

Characterization of CD9 Glycosylation at Rare Noncanonical N-X-C Glycosite.
To evaluate the role of CD9 glycosylation in its interaction with galectin-3, we characterized the site of CD9 glycosylation. While CD9 has been classified as a glycoprotein (
68
,
69
), the site of glycosylation has only been predicted, not empirically validated, and there are conflicting reports of CD9 glycosylation, where some cell types (e.g., MEC and SNU878) do not express the glycosylated form (
70
). We observed in BeWo cells that both endogenous and overexpressed CD9 appear as two discrete bands by western blotting (
SI Appendix
, Fig. S5
A
). Upon treatment of lysates with PNGase F, an amidase that cleaves glycans from N-linked glycoconjugates (
71
), we observed a collapse of the two bands to one at the lower molecular weight (
SI Appendix
, Fig. S5
A
). This observation suggests that the higher molecular weight band is N-linked glycosylated CD9 (representing approximately 10% of endogenously expressed CD9 and 40% of overexpressed CD9 by western blot densitometry), and the lower band corresponds to nonglycosylated CD9. CD9 glycosylation was also observed in the HTR-8/SVneo trophoblast cell line and human placental lysates from normal pregnancies (
SI Appendix
, Fig. S5
B
–
D
), suggesting the biological relevance of N-linked glycosylated CD9 beyond BeWo cells.
N-linked glycosylation canonically occurs at the asparagine residue of N-X-S/T sequons, in which X can be any residue except for proline (
72
). Therefore, it has been assumed that CD9 is glycosylated at either N52 or N53 of the small extracellular loop, as both residues are part of this sequon (
Fig. 5
A
). We transiently overexpressed conservative N52Q, N53Q, and N52,53Q mutants to determine which of these two sites could be modified by N-linked glycans. However, the presence of the higher molecular weight band persisted in the mutants (
Fig. 5
A
). We hypothesized that the unusual appearance of four asparagine residues in a sequence may allow for position N50 or N51 to be glycosylated to compensate for the absence of N52 or N53 glycosylation. However, even with all four consecutive asparagine residues mutated, CD9 remained glycosylated (
Fig. 5
A
and
SI Appendix
, Fig. S5
E
). While N-linked glycosylation outside the canonical N-X-S/T sequon is relatively rare, we systematically tested the remaining three extracellular asparagine residues of CD9 and found that N151Q was the only nonglycosylated mutant appearing as a single band (
Fig. 5
B
and
SI Appendix
, Fig. S5
E
). This residue is part of an unusual N-X-C sequon, empirically validated in only a handful of proteins (
73
), such as plasma protein C (
74
), von Willebrand factor (
75
), SPINT2 (
76
), and CD69 (
77
), and predicted to represent less than 3% of all N-linked glycosites across the proteome (
78
,
79
). Mutation of the cysteine residue of this N-X-C sequon to alanine also prevented CD9 glycosylation (
SI Appendix
, Fig. S5
F
), suggesting that the presence of the cysteine residue is key toward N-linked glycosylation. To confirm glycan occupancy on endogenous CD9, we immunoprecipitated CD9 from BeWo cell lysates and subjected digested peptides to deglycosylation with PNGase F treatment in O18 water. This process causes a signature
N
+3 mass modification on an N-glycosylated asparagine residue (
80
,
81
). We identified CD9 peptides mapping to position N151, with and without the
N
+3 signature mass modification, suggesting this site exists both in a glycosylated and nonglycosylated state (
SI Appendix
, Fig. S6
A
and
B
). We did not observe any
N
+3 modifications at peptides mapping to N50-53, suggesting CD9 glycosylation does not occur at these sites. These results corroborate our findings from mutational analysis that CD9 is glycosylated at N151, representing approximately 10% of total endogenous CD9 protein.

We performed a BLAST search comparing human CD9 to other species to see whether the N151 glycosylation site was conserved (
82

This site topologically places the glycan more distal to the membrane and in a more flexible portion of CD9 than the predicted small extracellular loop glycosylation site, likely increasing the accessibility of the glycan to GBP interactors. This finding enabled the generation of CD9 glycosite mutant control constructs for subsequent experiments.
Galectin-3/CD9 Functional Interaction Is Dependent on Glycosylation.
With the N-linked glycosite identified, we then assessed the glycosylation-dependent impact of galectin-3 on CD9 membrane dynamics. We measured the diffusion coefficient (

Discussion
The interactions between GBPs and their glycoconjugate counterreceptors mediate important biological processes, but these interactions can be difficult to elucidate in living cells. GBPs generally exhibit avidity for their counterreceptors, and while these interactions can be measured in vitro, the biological context of a living cell is necessary to determine whether an interaction can exhibit a functional phenotypic outcome (
86
). In this work, we demonstrate that APEX2-based proximity tagging represents an effective strategy to identify galectin-3 counterreceptors in placental trophoblasts and use network analyses to parse and prioritize glycoproteins (e.g., CD9 and ITGB1) for functional validation. Hence, binding-first approaches, like proximity tagging, nicely complement genetic screens for the identification of functional GBP counterreceptors (
87
). We also note the enhanced sensitivity of the proximity tagging protocol in detecting changes in GBP engagement relative to “binding-only” approaches, which required >10-fold higher probe concentrations for detection (
SI Appendix
, Fig. S9
A
and
C
).
The identification of galectin-3 functional counterreceptors was leveraged to reveal mechanistic insight into galectin-3-mediated fusion. Whereas both fetal and maternal serum galectin-3 are important for proper placental formation in vivo (
17
,
18
), exogenous treatment of galectin-3 is necessary to stimulate fusion in BeWo cell models. Indeed, histological staining of placental tissues, where galectin-3 expression is minimal in STBs but high in CTB, extravillous trophoblasts, and the maternal decidua (
88
), represents multiple putative sources of exogenous galectin-3. We demonstrate that galectin-3-mediated BeWo fusion is dependent on glycosylation, the galectin-3 carbohydrate recognition domain, and the oligomerization state of galectin-3. These results support a model where galectin-3 lattice formation is important for fusion. We also compared galectin-3-mediated syncytialization to forskolin-mediated syncytialization and show that, similar to galectin-1 (
65
,
66
), galectin-3 does not increase the expression of the STB marker hCGβ. Additionally, the canonical syncytialization signaling pathways are not activated by galectin-3, but we do observe an immobilizing effect on CD9 membrane protein diffusion with galectin-3 treatment to a similar degree as other work examining CD9 dynamics (
89
). Further studies will need to be performed to determine whether galectin-3 activates a noncanonical signaling pathway through its interaction network or biophysically promotes fusion, potentially generating “pseudosyncytiotrophoblasts” instead of completely differentiating the CTBs.
Our investigations led us to the finding that CD9 N-linked glycosylation occurs at a noncanonical N-X-C sequon in the large extracellular loop instead of at N-X-S/T sequons present in the small extracellular loop. We validate and characterize this glycosite via mutagenesis. There are two instances of CD9 glycosylation at position N151 present in large glycoproteomics datasets, which were not previously validated or further explored (
90
,
91
). Interestingly, the N151 position of CD9 is similar to the glycosylation site of all other human tetraspanins (except for the nonglycosylated tetraspanin CD81) (
92
,
93
), suggesting a conserved structural or functional role of this glycosite. Glycosylation at N-X-C sequons has been hypothesized to affect protein folding and disulfide bridge formation. Interestingly, the two cysteines succeeding this glycosite in CD9, C152, and C153, have been shown to participate in disulfide bond formation with C181 and C167, respectively. Thus, the effect of N151 glycosylation on CD9 protein structure and folding warrants further study. Additionally, CD9 protein–protein interactors (such as ITGB1) are believed to be mediated by the large extracellular loop (
47
,
94
,
95
). We speculate that CD9 glycosylation state could regulate these interactions, dictating the proximal protein neighborhood of CD9 and subsequent function. Finally, because the N151 glycosite is not conserved across all mammals, there could be important evolutionary implications of CD9 glycosylation in its various functional roles. Our identification of the CD9 glycosite allows for future experimentation to consider its glycosylation in other contexts, such as in immunology or cancer biology (
96
,
97
).
In summary, we present evidence that galectin-3 promotes placental cell fusion through a mechanism unique from forskolin, and it does so through interactions with glycosylated CD9 and the glycoprotein ITGB1. CD9 has been implicated in physically affecting membrane architecture, including acting as a sensor of membrane curvature, localizing to stretched membranes during cell shape remodeling (
95
,
98
). Thus, we speculate that galectin-3 may reorganize the membrane to cluster CD9, potentially inducing membrane bending at sites of high CD9 density. This work was performed in the BeWo choriocarcinoma cell line model of CTB, and other, more complex models, like spontaneously differentiating primary human trophoblasts (
99
), may also exhibit these functional relationships. By contributing to the understanding of how galectin-3 treatment affects CTB unable to differentiate without exogenous activation, we can now examine the effect of galectin-3 treatment on models with spontaneous syncytialization to determine whether galectin-3 additively contributes to primary trophoblast fusion.
Materials and Methods
Proximity Labeling and MS-Based Proteomics.
Live cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) followed by PX-Gal-3 incubation (varying concentrations, 30 min, 37 °C) in serum-free culture medium. PX-Gal-3 was preincubated with lactose (100 mM) or TD139 (15.4 µM) (15 min, RT) for competition conditions. Cells were washed with PBS three times and then incubated with biotin phenol (500 µM, 30 min, 37 °C) in serum-containing culture medium. H
2
O
2
was directly added (final 1 mM, 1 min, RT) to label cells followed by three washes with quencher solution. Cells were washed once with PBS, then scraped, and pelleted on ice for lysate-based experiments or fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for microscopy-based experiments. For MS-based proteomics, samples were lysed with a probe sonicator, and lysates were normalized before precipitation with methanol. Protein was washed with methanol, denatured (6 M urea in PBS, 0.2% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS)), reduced (200 mM tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine hydrochloride (TCEP) and 600 mM K
2
CO
3
in PBS, 30 min, 37 °C shaking), and alkylated (400 mM iodoacetamide in PBS, 30 min, RT, in dark). Samples were enriched on prewashed streptavidin-agarose beads (1.5 h, RT, rotating) and washed successively with 0.2% SDS in PBS, PBS, and 100 mM triethylammonium bicarbonate (TEAB) in water (pH 8.5). Samples were subjected to tryptic digest [2 µg of procine trypsin in 200 µL 100 mM TEAB (pH 8.5), 1 mM CaCl
2
, O/N, 37 °C, shaking], brought to 30% acetonitrile, and combined with respective TMT10plex™ Isobaric Label (Thermo Scientific, 90110) (1 h, 37 °C, occasional vortex). Reaction was quenched (6 µL 5% hydroxylamine in water, 15 min, RT) and acidified (4 µL formic acid). Samples were dried by vacuum concentration and reconstituted/combined in 5% acetonitrile, 0.5% TFA in water. Samples were desalted with Pierce™ Peptide Desalting Spin Columns per manufacturer protocol and then dried by vacuum concentration. Samples were stored at −80 °C until resuspended in 20 μL of 0.1% formic acid in water (Buffer A) and loaded onto instrument. Subsequent liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) and analysis are described in detail in
SI Appendix
.
Fusion Index Measurements.
Fixed cells were incubated with primary anti-E-cadherin (1:500, 2% BSA in phosphate-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween 20 (PBST), 4 °C, overnight), washed three times with PBST, incubated with secondary anti-rabbit-647 (1:1,000, 2% BSA in PBST, 1 h, RT), incubated with Hoechst (1:1,000, PBST, 10 min, RT), washed three times with PBST, and then imaged with the EVOS microscope system. Images were analyzed in Fiji (2.14.0) to determine total nuclei count. Briefly, a binary color system was applied to the Hoechst channel followed by a watershed to demarcate nuclei that may be touching. Particles were then counted with a size threshold of at least 200 square pixels. The number of nuclei in syncytia was then determined by counting the nuclei that lacked clear surface E-cadherin staining or cells with a clear border of E-cadherin containing more than one nucleus. Fusion index was derived by dividing number of nuclei in syncytia by the total number of nuclei and reported relative to WT BeWo cells treated with 40 µg/mL galectin-3.
Transfections, Transductions, and siRNA-Mediated Knockdown.
Transient transfections were performed with Lipofectamine 3000 (ThermoFisher) per the manufacturer’s recommendations. Viral production was performed by transfecting HEK293T cells (seeded in 10 cm
2
dish, 3 × 10
6

Medium was changed 6 h later, replenished with 5 mL of culture medium, and then harvested (60 h). For viral transduction, BeWo cells (4 × 10
5
cells) were seeded in a 6-well plate and allowed to adhere overnight in culture medium. Cells were then spinoculated with 1 mL of harvested viral media (2,000 rpm, 2 h, 37 °C) and underwent antibiotic selection (concentration previously determined in our hands by kill curve) after recovery in culture medium. For small interfering ribonucleic acid (siRNA)-mediated knockdown, BeWo cells were reverse transfected at time of seeding using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (ThermoFisher) per the manufacturer’s protocols.
RNA Extraction and RT-qPCR.
Cells were washed twice with PBS, harvested with 0.25% trypsin (10 min, 37 °C), and pelleted (500× g, 3 min). After one PBS wash, RNA was extracted using the Direct-zol RNA Miniprep kit (Zymo, R2053) per manufacturer instructions. RNA concentration was determined with the NanoDrop One
C

RT-qPCR was performed with 5 ng cDNA and 500 nM primers using the PowerUp™ SYBR™ Green Master Mix per manufacturer instructions using the QuantStudio™ 6 Pro Real-Time PCR System instrument.
GAPDH

Microscopy.
Live cell microscopy experiments were performed with a Nikon total internal reflection fluorescence and stochastic optical reconstruction microscopy (TIRF/STORM) microscope equipped with an oil immersion objective (PlanApo, 100×, NA 1.49; Leica Microsystems, Germany), a 561 nm a high power LUNV laser, and a Hamamatsu Orca Flash 4 CMOS camera. Briefly, a stack of 40,000 images from a region of interest (ROI) on the ventral plasma membrane was recorded at an acquisition speed of 6.2 ms/frame using the TIRF microscope and cMOS camera described above and saved as .ND2 file. The ROI size for cells was 100 × 100 pixels with a pixel size of 60 nm in the object plane. Raw temporal autocorrelation function (G(τ)) was computed from each 4 × 4 binned pixels (Px unit; length = 2.4 nm) of an image stack and fitted with equation 1. For confocal microscopy, two-color confocal imaging was performed using a Zeiss LSM 880 laser scanning confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) equipped with a Plan-Apochromat 63× oil-immersion objective (NA 1.4). GFP was excited with the 488-nm line of an argon laser, and emission was collected using a 500 to 550 nm band-pass filter. mCherry was excited with the 561-nm line of a diode-pumped solid-state laser, and emission was collected using a 580 to 650 nm band-pass filter. Channels were acquired sequentially to avoid bleed-through, with laser power and detector gain optimized for each fluorophore while maintaining identical pixel size (0.07 µm/pixel) and precise spatial registration between channels. For each fluorescence channel, bright spots were detected and counted on a per-cell basis using the
Find Maxima
function in Fiji (2.14.0). Two spots, one from each channel, were considered overlapping when their centroid positions were within 2 pixels (0.14 µm) of each other.
