Background
The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, caused by severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), poses a global health crisis and significant healthcare burden (Davis et al.,
2023
). By March 2023, there were over 676 million documented COVID-19 cases and more than 6 million deaths worldwide (COVID-19 Map,
2023
). Rapid development of COVID-19 vaccines has effectively controlled the COVID-19 pandemic and decreased the risk of severe disease and COVID-19-related deaths (Chi et al.
2022
). SARS-CoV-2 has rapidly evolved, and several variants have been detected, which are associated with challenges in prevention and clinical outcomes (Chen et al.,
2023
; Mendiola-Pastrana et al.,
2022
). For example, the Delta variant is linked to higher infectivity and worse clinical outcomes, whereas the Omicron variant is associated with significantly increased risk of infection (Araf et al.,
2022
; Long et al.,
2022
).
Substantial evidence has indicated that the pathophysiology of COVID-19 is linked to hematology (Jiang & Zhang,
2020
; Ong et al.,
2021
; Tobian et al.,
2022
). In the early stages of the COVID-19 pandemic, convalescent plasma was proposed as a method for the prevention and treatment of COVID-19 (Chen et al.,
2020
), which is in line with the concept of passive immunization; that is, convalescent plasma confers immediate immunity to infected patients to help them bridge the critical period until a proper immune response is generated (Al-Riyami et al.,
2021
; Hung et al.,
2011
). Data from early case series demonstrated the potential benefits of COVID-19 convalescent plasma, as evidenced by decreased viral load, rapidly increased levels of neutralizing antibodies, significantly improved clinical symptoms, and decreased mortality within 28 days (Duan et al.,
2020
; Salazar et al.,
2020
; Shen et al.,
2020
). However, the actual efficacy of convalescent plasma is difficult to assess objectively because of the high methodological heterogeneity in the selection criteria for donors and recipients, outcomes, dosage, number of transfusions, disease severity, and concentration of neutralizing antibodies (Rojas, & Anaya,
2020
).
Metabolites and proteins are important molecular components of the human plasma, and plasma metabolomic and proteomic profiles provide unique information regarding disease severity and pathological changes. Shen et al. revealed changes in characteristic proteins and metabolites in patients with severe COVID-19 and identified a molecular signature comprising 7 metabolites and 22 proteins to classify severe cases (Shen et.,
2020
). Moreover, persistent proteomic and metabolomic abnormalities have been observed in the plasma of COVID-19 survivors 6 months after discharge (Li et al.,
2022
). Therefore, proteomic and metabolomic technologies were applied in the current study to determine the molecular composition of plasma obtained from COVID-19 convalescent patients and uninfected controls.
Methods
Participant recruitment
The subjects who participated in this study included 29 hospitalized patients recovering from COVID-19 and 30 healthcare workers admitted to the Hulunbuir Chinese-Mongolian Hospital (a designated rehabilitation hospital for patients recovering from COVID-19) from December 2021 to January 2022. Patients with convalescent COVID-19 refer to those who meet the discharge criteria of the Novel Coronavirus Pneumonia Diagnosis and Treatment Protocol (Trial Eighth edition). Specifically, the following inclusion criteria were mentioned: (1) body temperature of patients returned to normal for three days or more; (2) patients had markedly improved respiratory symptoms; (3) patients exhibited a significantly improved inflammation state in their lung imaging; and (4) two consecutive negative nucleic acid tests for respiratory pathogens, with an interval of at least 24 h between the two samples. All uninfected controls had received the vaccine, and time interval between vaccination and blood collection was 197.4 ± 48.28 days. The clinical features of the patients are shown in Table
1
. Informed consent was obtained from each participant before enrollment in the study. The Ethics Committee of Hulunbuir Chinese Mongolian Hospital (No. 2022SZM-02) approved this study, and information from each subject was processed anonymously.

Sample collection
Fasting venous blood (10 mL) from each participant was collected between 6:30 AM–7 AM and left at room temperature for 30 min. Next, the blood samples were centrifuged at 13,000 r/min for 10 min, and the obtained plasma was divided into inactivated EP tubes for proteomic and metabolomic analyses or were stored at −80 °C for subsequent analyses.
Untargeted metabolomic analysis
Untargeted metabolomic profiling was conducted according to methods described in previous studies (Szczerbinski, et al.,
2022
; Wang et al.,
2020
). For metabolite extraction, plasma samples (100 µL) were mixed with pre-cooled water (100 µL) and methanol/acetonitrile (800 µL, 1:1, v/v) and subjected to ultrasound in an ice bath for 1 h. Following incubation at −20 °C for 1 h to precipitate the protein, the samples were centrifuged at 16,000 g for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was dried using high-speed vacuum enrichment centrifugation. During mass spectrometry (MS) testing, the dried sample was centrifuged at 20,000 g for 20 min at 4 °C after re-dissolving in aqueous methanol (100 µL, 1:1 v/v), and supernatant was collected for subsequent analyses. Chromatographic separation was implemented using a Waters ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm) with a column temperature of 40 °C and a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. The injection volume was 5.0 µL. The mobile phases were 0.1% formic acid water (A) and acetonitrile (B) under a gradient elution program consisting of 0–1.5 min, 0.3% B; 1.5–2 min, 0.3–0% B; 2–6 min, 0–48% B; 6–10 min, 48–10% B; 10–12 min, 100% B; 12–12.1 min, 100–0% B; 12.1–15 min, 0% B. The samples were separated by UPLC and analyzed by MS using a Q Exactive Plus mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific). In the MS/MS data acquisition process using the Q Exactive Plus mass spectrometer, data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode was employed, with full scan parameters set as follows: the resolution was 70,000 at m/z 200, the automatic gain control (AGC) target was 3e6, the maximum injection time (IT) was 100 ms, and the scan range was from m/z 70 to 1050; for dd-MS² parameters, the resolution was 17,500 at m/z 200, the AGC target was 1e5, the maximum IT was 50 ms, the isolation window was 1.0 m/z, the normalized collision energy (NCE) was set to 20, 30, and 40 eV in a stepped mode to obtain comprehensive fragmentation information, and the dynamic exclusion was set to 10 s to avoid repeated selection of the same precursor ions. MS was performed in ESI-positive/negative ion scan modes with ionization conditions as follows: spray voltage = 3.8kv (+) and 3.2kv (-); capillary temperature = 320 (±); sheath gas = 30 (±); aux gas = 5 (±); probe heater temp = 350 (±); and s-lens RF level = 50. For quality control in this experiment, we prepared quality control (QC) samples for balancing the chromatography-mass spectrometry system and measuring instrument status, and used them to evaluate system stability throughout the entire experimental process. Data were collected using MSDIAL software for peak alignment, retention time correction, and peak area extraction, followed by identification of metabolites in accordance with precise mass number matching (mass tolerance < 20 ppm) and second-order spectral matching (mass tolerance < 0.02 Da) methods by searching the HMDB and MassBank public databases and the self-built metabolite standard library of Bioprofile (Shanghai, China). For the extracted data, ion peaks with missing values > 50% within the group will not be included in subsequent statistical analysis, and peaks with RSD > 30% in quality control samples will be excluded to eliminate unstable peaks caused by experimental changes. Normalize the total peak area of positive and negative ion data separately, integrate the positive and negative ion peaks, and apply R software for pattern recognition. After preprocessing with Unit Variation Scaling (UV), the data is analyzed for subsequent analysis.
Data independent acquisition (DIA)-MS proteomic analysis
First, we performed protein enzymolysis. A total of 90 µL SDT lysis buffer was added to 10 µL plasma sample, boiled in water for 3 min, and ultrasonically crushed for 5 min. Following centrifugation at 16,000 g for 20 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was collected. Proteins in the supernatant were precipitated using pre-cooled acetone, and the precipitate was redissolved in 100 µL UA buffer (8 M Urea, 150 mM Tris-HCl pH8.0). The redissolved proteins were incubated with 1 M dithiothreitol at 37 °C for 30 min and with 50 mM iodoacetamide at room temperature for 30 min away from light. Afterwards, samples were diluted by adding 50 mM NH
4
HCO
3
(Sigma) and digested by adding 8 µg trypsin at 37 °C for 20 h. The samples were then acidified with trifluoroacetic acid to terminate enzymolysis. Subsequently, the peptide solution was desalted using a C18 cartridge for liquid chromatography-MS. Peptides were quantified using a Nanodrop One device at an optical density of A280 nm.
Peptide solution (9 µL) was mixed with 1 µL 10X iRT peptide and then LC-MS/MS was implemented using a Q Exactive Plus mass spectrometer coupled with Easy 1200 nLC (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The buffers included liquid A, 0.1% formic acid aqueous solution, and liquid B, 0.1% formic acid acetonitrile aqueous solution (acetonitrile, 85%). The column was balanced with 95% liquid (A). The samples were loaded into a Trap Column (100 μm*20 mm, 5 μm, C18, Dr. Maisch GmbH, Ammerbuch, Germany) and then gradient separation was performed on a column (75 μm*150 mm, 3 μm, C18, Dr. Maisch GmbH) at a flow rate of 300 nl/min under a linear increasing gradient consisting of 0–2 min, 5–8% B; 2–42 min, 8–23% B; 42–52 min, 23–40% B; 52–53 min, 40–100% B; and 53–60 min, 100% B. Next, DIA-MS was implemented using a Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) in positive ion scan mode with an electrospray voltage of 2.1 kV and ion scan ranging from 350 to 1200 m/z. Parameters for primary mass spectrum resolution included: 60,000 @m/z 200, AGC target = 3e6, and MaximumIT = 30 ms. The parameters for secondary ion mass spectrometry were as follows: 15,000 @m/z 200; AGC target = 1e6; Maximum IT = 25 ms; MS2 Activation Type = HCD, isolation window = 20 Th, and normalized collision energy:32.
Data processing and statistical analysis
For metabolomic profiling analysis, multivariable statistical analysis was implemented using R (version4.0.3) and R packages, including PCA, PLS-DA, and OPLS-DA. Permutation tests were used to evaluate the overfitting of all the models. Univariate analysis was used to detect the significance of metabolite changes between the two groups. The variable importance for the projection (VIP) value calculated using the OPLS-DA model was applied to determine the interpretation ability and influence intensity of the expression patterns of metabolites for classifying and discriminating the samples of each group. Then, differential metabolites were identified with both VIP ≥ 1 and
P
< 0.05. To further explore the affected pathways, KEGG enrichment analysis was performed for differential metabolites using Fisher’s exact test and false discovery rate (FDR) correction for multiple testing.
P
< 0.05 was used to present the significantly enriched pathways. DIA-MS data was processed and analyzed using Spectronaut (version 16 biognosys AD) software (Martinez-Val et al.,
2021
; Wen,
2023
) for protein identification and quantitation with a cut-off of Q value ≤ 0.01. Data analysis was performed using R software. Differential proteins were screened with parameter settings such as Student’s t-test,
P
< 0.05, and |fold change|>2.0. Protein annotation was conducted based on KEGG and Gene Ontology (GO) databases, and enrichment analyses for differential proteins were implemented with the Fisher’s exact test and FDR correction. Subcellular protein localization was analyzed based on the cellular component terms of the GO annotations. PPI networks were constructed using Cytoscape software (Kohl et al.,
2011
) based on the STRING database. Integrated proteomics and metabolomics analyses were performed using R 4.0.1 and Cytoscape version 3.8.2.
Results
Metabolomic profile changes in the plasma of patients with convalescent COVID-19
Based on an untargeted metabolomic analysis, changes in the metabolomic profiles of patients with convalescent COVID-19 and uninfected controls were explored. The base peak spectra of QC samples in both negative and positive ion detection modes were compared, and the retention time and response intensity of each chromatographic peak were highly consistent (Figure
S1
A). In total, 19,025 and 14,640 peaks were retained in positive and negative ion modes, respectively. The principal component analysis (PCA) indicated that the QC samples were tightly clustered and showed good repeatability (Figure
S1
B). These findings suggest that the variations caused by instrumental errors were small in this metabolomic analysis, and the data quality was reliable.
For both positive and negative ions, the PCA of metabolites showed that the samples in each group tended to gather internally (Fig.
1
A, B), suggesting high variability between groups and low variability within the same group. In the model established by testing the permutation test chart, R2Y and R2X represent the explanatory power of the model on the Y and X matrices, respectively, and Q2 represents the predictability of the model. The partial least squares-discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) model indicated that metabolite expression had a high interpretative and good predictive ability (R2Y = 0.995, Q2 = 0.989 in positive ion mode; R2Y = 0.995, Q2 = 0.984 in negative ion mode) for the discrimination of samples in the two groups (Fig.
1
C, D). Consistent results were obtained in orthogonal PLS-DA (OPLS-DA; Fig.
1
E, F), and permutation tests of OPLS-DA (Fig.
1
G, H) indicated that no overfitting occurred. Differential metabolites were further screened, and 415 metabolites were identified with VIP ≥ 1 and P < 0.05, including 286 metabolites in positive ion modes (Table
S1
) and 129 metabolites in negative ion modes (Table S2). For each metabolite, we reported its recognition level in Tables
S1
and S2. The differential metabolite tables include the metabolite name, metabolite ID, KEGG ID, HMDB ID, HMDB top-level classification, HMDB classification, HMDB subclass, charge mode, VIP value, P.value, -log10 (p), fold change, m/z value, and chromatographic retention time, as well as the ion peak area of each metabolite corresponding to each sample. Among the 415 significantly altered metabolites identified, 21 were clearly drug compounds (Table S3), such as Tamoxifen, Olanzapine, and Ornidazole. For other compounds with uncertain sources, such as derysphingosine, mondulone acetate, we provide metabolite names and exact masses in supplementary materials for alternative explanations. The top 30 differential metabolites with the highest importance (VIP value) are shown in Fig.

Changes in the plasma proteomic profile of patients with convalescent COVID-19
Overall, proteomic analysis identified 20,510 peptides, of which 14,933 were unique. In total, 2,828 proteins were identified, 947 of which were differentially expressed in patients with convalescent COVID-19 compared with uninfected controls (Table S4). Most differentially expressed proteins were downregulated (663, 70%) in the plasma of patients with convalescent COVID-19, and only 284 proteins (30%) were upregulated (Fig.

Discussion
In the early stages of the COVID-19 pandemic, convalescent plasma was proposed as a method for the prevention and treatment of COVID-19 and has shown potential clinical benefits in several case series (Salazar et al.,
2020
; Shen et al.,
2020a
,
2020b
). However, the therapeutic potential, safety, and underlying mechanisms of convalescent plasma remain largely unclear. Studies have proposed that multiomics datasets contribute to a deeper understanding of the mechanisms of convalescent plasma action and predict its treatment efficacy (Baros-Steyl et al.,
2022
; Gisby et al.,
2022
). In this study, we revealed large-scale changes in metabolites and proteins in convalescent plasma compared to those in uninfected controls. These metabolites and proteins were involved in multiple key pathophysiological processes, including thermogenesis, taste transduction, sphingolipid metabolism, immunoinflammatory processes, hormone synthesis, and neurodegeneration-related biological processes.
Viral infections are known to alter host cell metabolism. Although we cannot directly rule out the long-term effects of virus infection, based on existing literature, these differences may be related to the patient’s recovery process to some extent. Untargeted metabolomic profiles revealed 415 differential metabolites in convalescent plasma compared to normal controls. Among these metabolites, 28.05% were lipids and lipid-like molecules, indicating alterations in the lipid profile as demonstrated in previous studies (Sampedro-Nuñez et al.,
2021
; Song et al.,
2020
; Zhao et al.,
2022
). This could be explained by significant changes in the lipid metabolism of host cells and the overuse of cell lipid resources during viral replication (Rezaei et al.,
2022
). Significantly elevated levels of phytosphingosine, dihydrosphingosine, derysphingosine, and N-palmitoyl-D-erythrosphingosine, as well as elevated sphingolipid metabolism, were observed in convalescent plasma. Elevated levels of sphingolipids in both cells and sera were induced after SARS-CoV-2 infection, which are essential for viruses during their replication cycles (Avota et al.,
2021
; Vitner et al.,
2022
). The upregulation of differential metabolites such as sphingolipids may be related to the inflammatory response and metabolic disorders caused by the virus during infection, as well as reflecting the metabolic regulation of the body during rehabilitation. Torretta et al. suggested that dysregulated lipid metabolism could cause cell apoptosis and immunoescape by inducing lipid raft remodeling, highlighting the active involvement of sphingolipids in viral infections (Torretta et al.,
2021
). For example, membrane microdomains enriched with specific sphingolipid species can influence viral attachment and entry by segregating receptors and modulating membrane fusion and/or endocytosis (Avota et al.,
2021
; Prakash et al.,
2021
). Viral infection can cause changes in intracellular sphingolipid metabolism, and during the rehabilitation process, the body may attempt to restore normal metabolic balance, resulting in changes in sphingolipid metabolites.
In addition to metabolism, organismal system-related pathways, such as taste transduction and thermogenesis, were also altered. Taste and smell loss or impairments are common symptoms in patients with COVID-19 that might appear and persist long following the initial infection (Burges Watson et al.,
2021
); these might be attributed to disturbances caused by the virus in the oral and nasal cavities, as well as their influence on the central nervous system (Meunier et al.,
2020
). Four differential metabolites participated in taste transduction, including l-glutamate, acetylcholine, citric acid, and quinine; these had been previously demonstrated to be involved in taste transduction (Dando & Roper,
2012
; Gutierrez & Simon,
2021
; Sotoyama et al.,
2017
; Vandenbeuch, & Kinnamon,
2016
). For example, acetylcholine has been identified as a taste bud transmitter released from taste receptor cells during gustatory stimulation, enhancing taste-evoked Ca
2+
responses and afferent neurotransmitter secretion (Dando, & Roper,
2012
). Fever is a hallmark symptom of patients with COVID; during this process, thermogenesis plays an important role in this process. In this study, thermogenesis tended to be lower in patients with convalescent COVID-19 than in uninfected controls. Three differential metabolites have been implicated in thermogenesis, namely, l-carnitine, nicotine, and beta-estradiol, and these three metabolites have been reported to regulate brown adipose tissue thermogenesis (González-García et al.,
2017
; Liu et al.,
2022
). The changes in these pathways may be influenced by multiple factors, including the virus itself infection and physiological regulation during the rehabilitation process. Due to the inability of this study to directly distinguish the impact of these two factors, further research is needed to validate and deepen understanding of these results.
Dysregulated proteins between patients with convalescent COVID-19 and uninfected controls were mainly extracellular (33.5%), cytoplasmic (30.1%), and membrane proteins (17.96%), which were significantly implicated in several pathways including NET formation, complement and coagulation cascades, platelet activation, thyroid hormone synthesis, and the estrogen signaling pathway. These pathways are activated during viral infection to combat the virus, and their persistent abnormalities during the recovery process may indicate that the body’s immune system has not fully recovered. Pathological hyperactivity of the complement and coagulation systems was associated with COVID-19-related critical illness, and these two systems showed close cross-talk and were linked to a cytokine storm in patients (Ray et al.,
2023
). In severe SARS-CoV-2 infection, neutrophils are recruited and infiltrate to clear invading pathogens, and severe inflammatory reactions occur due to abundant NETosis and NET formation in neutrophils (Al-Kuraishy et al.,
2022
). Excessive NET formation has been linked to unfavorable coagulopathy, microthrombi, collateral tissue injury, and the development of acute respiratory distress syndrome (Al-Kuraishy et al.,
2022
; Borges et al.,
2020
). In addition, platelets interact with neutrophils to contribute to the microthrombi caused by NETs (Middleton et al.,
2020
). As a primary pathological event in COVID-19, hypercoagulability is associated with life-threatening thromboembolic complications caused by inflammation and platelet activation (Fard et al.,
2021
). Platelets are the main effector cells for hemostasis and pathological thrombosis, and enhanced platelet activation has been observed after exposure to plasma from patients with severe COVID-19 and COVID-19 vaccines (Hottz et al.,
2020
; Ostrowski et al.,
2021
). These findings suggest that dysregulated proteins play important roles in immunoinflammatory responses.
Further integrated metabolomic and proteomic analyses revealed several significant pathways that were influenced by both differential metabolites and proteins, including the estrogen signaling, ferroptosis, and neurodegeneration-associated pathways. Previous studies have proposed a protective role of estrogen exposure against COVID-19 because of the higher risk of developing serious complications from COVID-19 in men of all ages than in women (Costeira et al.,
2021
). Multiple mechanisms are involved in these protective effects, such as estrogen-modulated immune responses and estradiol-mediated decreases in angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 expression (Di Vincenzo et al.,
2021
). Exacerbated cognitive symptoms in critically ill patients with COVID-19 indicate the presence of neurodegenerative sequelae (Sfera et al.,
2021
). SARS-CoV-2 replication is more efficient in iron-rich senescent cells, which can cause immune dysfunction and neurodegenerative diseases by inducing ferrosenescence phenotypes in host cells (Sfera, Osorio, Maguire, Rahman, Afzaal, Cummings, Maldonado 2021). SARS-CoV-2 and its encoded key proteins can be transported to several neuronal cells or taken up by the brain endothelium, thereby facilitating the formation of pathological fibrils. For example, the SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid protein contributes to the formation of pathological heteropolymers by interacting with neurodegenerative disorder-related RNA-binding proteins, such as hnRNP1A, TDP-43, and FUS (Strong,
2023
).
This study offered several notable advantages in exploring the molecular composition and physiological changes in the plasma of convalescent COVID-19 patients. First, it employed a comprehensive analytical approach combining untargeted metabolomics and DIA-MS proteomics, which enabled a thorough and in-depth examination of plasma metabolites and proteins. This dual-omics strategy provided rich datasets that enhanced the understanding of the molecular alterations occurring in convalescent COVID-19 patients. Second, the study included a well-defined cohort of convalescent COVID-19 patients and healthy controls, ensuring the robustness and reliability of the comparative findings. Moreover, by constructing a protein-metabolite interaction network, the study not only identified differential metabolites and proteins but also elucidated their interactions and the physiological and pathological processes they were involved in, offering novel insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying convalescence from COVID-19.
Despite the significant findings, this study had several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small, particularly the number of healthy controls, which might have compromised the generality and representativeness of the study findings. In addition, the potential influence of exogenous metabolites, such as those derived from medications or environmental pollutants, was not explicitly controlled for in the study design. These exogenous factors could have introduced variability into the metabolomic profiles, potentially affecting the accuracy and interpretation of the results. Additionally, despite the comprehensive analysis that unveiled various physiological and pathological processes, the in-depth mechanisms underlying these processes and their specific roles in convalescent COVID-19 patients still warrant further exploration. Furthermore, as a cross-sectional study, it was unable to ascertain whether the alterations observed in metabolites and proteins stem from disparities resulting from viral infection, are direct outcomes of the recovery process, or arise from other elements like treatment approaches and individual variations. Future research directions include expanding sample sizes, incorporating diverse biological samples, and conducting longitudinal studies to elucidate the deeper mechanisms and long-term effects of convalescent COVID-19.
Conclusion
In summary, the current study revealed differential metabolite and protein compositions in the plasma of patients with convalescent COVID-19 compared with uninfected controls. Metabolomic profiling regulates sphingolipid metabolism, thermogenesis, and taste transduction in patients with convalescent COVID-19, while proteomic profiling reflects the regulation of immunoinflammatory responses. Estrogen signaling and neurodegeneration-associated pathways are also altered in patients with convalescent COVID-19 compared to uninfected controls. These results suggest that the phenotype of patients with convalescent COVID-19 did not return to a phenotype similar to that of uninfected controls. These findings provide novel insights into the safety and efficacy of convalescent plasma for clinical use.
