Origins of the ecdysone project
It may be difficult for scientists trained in the 21st century to imagine a world in which developmental molecular genetics was not yet an established field, a time when little was known about the structure and expression of any eukaryotic genes. As recombinant DNA technologies emerged in the 1970s, the temporal and spatial control of gene expression during development became an area of great interest. However, molecular identification of developmentally relevant genes was not trivial, especially if they encoded proteins not yet known through biochemical analyses. Critical insights were provided by the giant polytene chromosomes of dipteran insect larval tissues approaching the start of metamorphosis. Visible by light microscopy,
Drosophila melanogaster
salivary gland chromosome banding patterns constituted the first genomic map (
Bridges 1935
). Chromosome puffs represent localized clouds of nascent RNA transcripts and decondensed chromatin (
Bonner and Pardue 1977
). In the mid-1970s, observing puffs—gene expression at specific chromosomal sites, in real time—provided an exciting and powerful experimental system. A pulse of the steroid hormone ecdysone at the end of larval development triggers a specific sequence of puffing patterns in salivary glands (
Fig. 1a
and
b
; reviewed in
Russell and Ashburner 1996
). Identifying “puff genes” was expected to lead to a
molecular
understanding of a gene hierarchy through which hormone pulses orchestrate a dramatic biological transition, in this case the ∼4-day metamorphosis of a crawling larva into a walking-and-flying adult (
Baehrecke 1996
). Terminology describing ecdysteroids has undergone cycles of revision over the years, in parallel with assay improvements for the different forms (
Lafont et al. 2021
). For simplicity, we use “ecdysone” to refer to the major active insect molting hormone, 20-hydroxyecdysone (20E).

The first molecular identification of ecdysone-regulated gene sets took place under the leadership of Professor David (“Dave”) S. Hogness in the Biochemistry Department at Stanford University School of Medicine. On the occasion of the centenary of his birth, we present this study in its historical context and briefly explain how subsequent research revealed fundamentals of gene regulation—and connected those genes to organismal development. As he often told his trainees, Dave Hogness believed that the 2 most important questions in developmental biology were “
When
in time?” and “
Where
in space?” His insightful approach was to define and characterize the temporal and spatial patterns of gene expression
by cloning
developmentally regulated mRNAs. This molecular biology strategy paralleled and complemented (no pun intended) the classical genetics strategy of
isolating lethal mutants
and classifying their phenotypes to infer their functions (reviewed in
Wieschaus and Nüsslein-Volhard 2016
). See the article by
Kingsley and Talbot (2025)
in this issue for more on the Hogness lab's pioneering work, including studies of the Bithorax complex.
Dave Hogness was prescient in recognizing that experimental developmental biology was ready for molecular exploration. First, the salivary gland chromosomal puffing responses to ecdysone were reproduced with high fidelity in vitro and provided a model of steroid hormone-regulated gene expression relevant across eukaryotes. Michael Ashburner, based on his own and others’ work (including by Hans J. Becker, Wolfgang Beermann, Ulrich Clever, Hans D. Berendes, and Igor F. Zhimulev), proposed a model (
Fig. 1c
) that ecdysone, bound to a specific receptor, interacts directly with DNA to rapidly induce a set of Early puffs and to repress a set of highly active Intermolt puffs (
Ashburner et al. 1974
). From a series of elegant studies, including ecdysone dose–response and withdrawal assays, and testing the effects of chemical inhibition of hormone-receptor binding or protein synthesis (
Russell and Ashburner 1996
), Ashburner further proposed that protein products of Early puffs activate a large set of Late puffs and repress their own transcription, creating a negative-feedback loop that limits the duration of the ecdysone response. Cloning those genes would allow direct testing of the Ashburner model.
Second, international research efforts focused on ecdysone biology and metamorphosis physiology were advancing rapidly, with many areas of inquiry poised to apply molecular genetics. For example, because genes encoding many glue proteins mapped to chromosomal puff sites, their cloning would advance the study of the adhesive that secures developing flies to substrates during metamorphosis (
Beckendorf and Kafatos 1976
;
Korge 1977
;
Kress 1979
). Other areas of investigation in
Drosophila
included polytene chromosome structure (
Bonner and Pardue 1977
;
Hill and Watt 1978
), ecdysone binding sites on polytene chromosomes (
Gronemeyer and Pongs 1980
), imaginal disc evagination (
Fristrom et al. 1977
); the genetics of metamorphosis (
Kiss et al. 1976
), ecdysone responses of other tissues (
Lepesant et al. 1978
;
Maroy et al. 1978
;
Cherbas et al. 1980
), and ecdysone titers during development (
Hodgetts et al. 1977
). Parallel studies of developmental endocrinology in other insects expanded the biological context even further (
Willis 1974
;
Truman 1978
;
Riddiford 1980
).
Third, many of the emerging molecular biology methods were developed within Stanford's Biochemistry Department during a period of intense creativity and innovation (
Yi 2015
). Indeed, the Hogness lab had already achieved milestones in eukaryotic gene cloning, notably mapping cloned
Drosophila
DNA segments to polytene chromosome sites (
Wensink et al. 1974
), cloning ribosomal RNA genes (
Glover et al. 1975
), and using colony hybridization—probing a library of cloned DNAs in bacteria—to identify genes (
Grunstein and Hogness 1975
).
These 3 avenues converged at the launch of the Hogness lab's 30-year “ecdysone project.” The initial phase of the project led to the identification of gene sets in the ecdysone-regulated hierarchy, ultimately leading to revision and expansion of the Ashburner model (
Fig. 1d
and
e
). It succeeded because of technical innovation, including construction of cDNA-clone libraries enriched for developmentally regulated transcripts and screening by differential hybridization in tissues undergoing physiological changes. The method relied on hybridization to DNA from plasmid-bearing bacterial colonies affixed to membranes (
Fig. 2a to d
) in a standardized “macroarray” arrangement (
Fig. 2e to g
). It was a forerunner to microarray technology for identifying differentially expressed genes (
Fig. 2h to j
), which is now done by RNA sequencing (RNAseq). Despite their significance, these data were never published (see
Box 1
).
Box 1. The unusual publication history of the Hogness lab.
David Hogness was a beloved mentor who invested heavily in the scientific and intellectual development of his trainees (
Burtis and Arbeitman 2007
). A kind and brilliant man, he was known to be generous with ideas and reagents and to talk freely and often about the research in his lab, giving credit to the specific people involved. His graduate students and postdocs went on to excellent positions—suggesting that considerable skill and effort went into the letters of recommendation that Hogness wrote. Yet, the Wolfner et al. manuscript is hardly the only case of unpublished data or manuscript delays, as others have noted (
Burtis et al. 2003
;
Young 2020
). For example, during the same timeframe, Michael Goldberg's dissertation on sequence analysis of
Drosophila
histone genes (
Goldberg 1979
), including the first data that revealed the TATA box, also went unpublished. Hogness’ colleague Arthur Kornberg referred to David's “passionate reluctance to publish” (
Burtis et al. 2003
). The phenomenon was captured in
Gerry Rubin and Ed Lewis’ (2000)
article, on the contribution of
Drosophila
to genome research, in which they summarized significant achievements of Hogness’ group in the 1970s and early 1980s. In a single paragraph packed with scientific milestones, the cited sources were an NSF grant application awarded to Hogness in 1972 that laid out his vision for molecular developmental biology, “unpublished data” twice, “personal communication” twice, 1 brief institutional progress report, and 2 conference abstracts, along with 4 published primary research articles. Consider the challenge of managing the flow of dissertations and manuscripts on a wide range of topics from a dozen or more graduate students and postdocs producing major findings at a breathtaking pace—and without the ease of modern technology for editing and literature searches. It is easy to imagine that a senior scientist, especially one with very high standards and inclined to be a meticulous hands-on author and editor, simply could not keep up. Moreover, there were no obvious negative consequences to Hogness or his trainees, whose careers he championed. He was so open about unpublished findings in his lab and so generous with his lab's clones and protocols that progress in the field was not delayed by his slowness to publish. It was a very different time than today, when competition for postdoctoral fellowships and for extramural funding of faculty research often hinges on the strength of publication record.

Development and application of enabling technology: identification of cloned ecdysone-regulated transcripts by differential hybridization
The experimental logic detailed by Wolfner and colleagues (see
Supplementary materials
) was straightforward and transferrable to virtually any system with a developmentally regulated transcription program.
Libraries of cDNA clones
were constructed to reflect developmentally specific mRNA populations. For example, Puff Stage 1 (PS1) larval salivary gland mRNA was used to construct Intermolt cDNA libraries, and prepupal (PS11) mRNA was used to construct libraries containing Late-gene transcripts. Other libraries were constructed from salivary glands incubated in vitro with ecdysone (with puff stages verified by examining chromosome spreads of the incubated glands) or ecdysone plus cycloheximide in an effort to increase the concentration of transcripts from Early puff genes (
Supplementary File 2
, p. 50;
Supplementary File 3
,
Fig. 1c
).
Six distinct libraries, containing a total of ∼13,500 cloned cDNA segments, were constructed. For each library, salivary glands were purified by mass isolation from thousands of developmentally timed animals representing 30 to 100 g (!) of starting material (
Supplementary File 2, pp. 29–31
;
Supplementary File 3
,
Fig. 9
). The poly(A)+ mRNA fraction was used to synthesize a double-stranded cDNA population that was cloned into plasmid vectors. The resulting transformed bacterial cells were plated onto Petri dishes; colonies containing cDNA inserts were individually toothpicked into microtiter wells containing culture media and incubated to expand the cell numbers. Transferring individual cDNA clone-containing colonies into hundreds of 96-well microtiter plates was a time-and-labor-intensive, all-hands-on-deck operation, with many in the Hogness lab helping. During this phase of the project, a sign over the lab door was updated frequently to report on progress: “Over XXXX colonies picked”—calling to mind McDonald's erstwhile ad campaign, “Over 1 Million Served.”
Prior to library construction, it was essential to confirm that developmentally staged, salivary gland poly(A)+ mRNA populations were enriched in transcripts from major ecdysone-sensitive chromosome puffs. Those RNAs were used to synthesize
cDNA hybridization probes
, by labeling with
3
H-nucleotides; the labeled cDNA was hybridized in situ to salivary gland polytene chromosomes. Indeed,
3
H-cDNA populations from salivary glands of wandering larvae hybridized most strongly to major Intermolt puff sites, whereas salivary gland cDNA populations from young prepupae hybridized most strongly to Late puff sites (
Supplementary File 2
,
Table 1, pp. 42–45
). Data like these provided the “green light” to construct and screen the libraries.
The bacterial cDNA libraries were stored frozen and deployed by replica plating, using a custom-made “colony stamper” tool, onto filters for culture and differential-hybridization screening (
Fig. 2f
and
g
). Arrays of cDNA library clones were transferred from 96-well plates to nitrocellulose filters (
Fig. 2e to g
) on Petri dishes by a variation of the
Grunstein and Hogness (1975)
method. After overnight culture, the filters-on-plates were removed and processed to lyse the bacteria, denature the DNA, and secure it to the membrane. To screen the cloned cDNA libraries,
32
P-labeled cDNA probes for hybridization were generated by reverse transcription of the developmentally staged mRNA populations. Among the technical firsts within this approach were development of small-scale protocols for RNA extraction from various “tiny bits of tissue,” followed by radiolabeled cDNA probe synthesis for differential hybridization (
Wolfner 1980
).
Each replicate filter was hybridized with one of several radiolabeled stage- and tissue-specific cDNA probes. Because the amount of filter-bound DNA from each clone was likely to be in vast excess relative to its complementary-labeled cDNA in the probe, the hybridization signal was expected to reflect the relative concentration of matching probe cDNA. Thus, stronger signals reflected higher transcript concentrations. In this way, differential hybridization was an efficient way to screen cDNA clones for developmentally regulated transcripts.
In this screening scenario, one would predict that a probe made from a mature larval mRNA preparation would contain relatively higher concentrations of Intermolt mRNAs than a probe made from a young prepupal mRNA population (when Late mRNAs would be abundant). Thus, an Intermolt cDNA clone would show a stronger signal when hybridized with the larval probe vs the prepupal probe (compare
Fig. 2a
and
c
). In other words, the cDNA clone would have a developmentally specific hybridization phenotype. These predictions were confirmed, and the team identified Intermolt genes that were repressed by ecdysone and Late genes that were induced by ecdysone (
Fig. 2a to d
). The cytogenetic map location of each identified cDNA clone was determined by in situ hybridization, allowing its assignment to an Intermolt or Late puff site. Tissue specificity was explored by probing replicate filters with stage-specific cDNA probes from other tissues, notably fat body (
Supplementary File 2, p. 51
;
Supplementary File 3
,
Fig. 2
). By these criteria, most of the Intermolt and Late genes identified in the screen were provisionally classified as salivary gland specific. This fit independent findings that fat body polytene chromosomes have different puffing patterns in response to ecdysone (
Richards 1982
).
In sum, the differential screens resulted in cDNA cloning of 12 ecdysone-sensitive genes mapping to 6 chromosomal puffs, 3 Intermolt puffs (at 3C, 68C, and 90BC), and 3 Late puffs (at 4F, 10EF, and 71E) (
Supplementary File 2
,
Table 2, p. 47
). The Intermolt genes were shown (as many had predicted) to encode glue proteins, both previously known and novel. They were named
Salivary gland secretion
(
Sgs
) and numbered accordingly:
Sgs3
,
Sgs4
,
Sgs5
,
Sgs7
, and
Sgs8
(
McGinnis et al. 1980
;
Muskavitch and Hogness 1980
;
Crowley et al. 1983
;
Guild and Shore 1984
). In contrast, the Late 71E genes encode a novel family of small, secreted, basic proteins with a distinctive cysteine backbone (
Wright et al. 1996
). Cross-homology testing, Northern blotting, and DNA mapping of the cDNA clones confirmed that a single puff could contain a tightly linked, coregulated cluster of genes, 3 at Intermolt puff 68C and 5 at Late puff 71E (
Meyerowitz and Hogness 1982
;
Restifo and Guild 1986
). The 71E cluster has additional complexity, with a total of 10 Late genes plus an Intermolt glue-protein gene in the middle (
Restifo and Guild 1986
;
Wright et al. 1996
); each is now named
Eig71E
(
Ecdysone-induced gene 71E
) followed by a letter. DNA sequencing revealed that coregulated genes of each cluster are phylogenetically related by duplication (
Garfinkel et al. 1983
;
Wright et al. 1996
).
Later, transcript-accumulation patterns of many ecdysone-regulated genes were compared by Northern blotting (
Andres et al. 1993
). Both the Intermolt and Late-gene sets are coordinately regulated, with striking differences between them (
Fig. 1d
). The Intermolt genes are “shut off” abruptly at the larval–prepupal transition, while the Late-gene transcripts ramp up quickly in young prepupae. These complementary expression patterns contributed to the ease with which Intermolt and Late genes were identified by differential hybridization.
The ecdysone project's autoradiograms demonstrated how a novel macroarray design, replica plating, and colony hybridization could be used to identify genes with distinct patterns of activity (
Fig. 2a to g
). Although unpublished, the differential hybridization screen for cloning coordinately regulated sets of ecdysone-pathway genes was presented at conferences, including the first EMBO Workshop on the Molecular and Developmental Biology of Drosophila in 1978 (aka “the Crete meeting”) and invited academic seminars. Hence, the work was generally known to investigators in the fields of molecular developmental biology and insect science. The use of differential hybridization for gene identification spread quickly across all fields of biomedical science (
Fig. 2j
). Hogness’ generous distribution of the clones and protocols to other investigators enhanced ongoing avenues of research in diverse fields (
McGinnis et al. 1980
;
Garbe and Pardue 1986
;
Papazian et al. 1987
;
Vigoreaux and Tobin 1987
;
Caudy et al. 1988
).
The work of Wolfner et al. spawned new projects in the Hogness lab and launched “alumni” labs, focused on the cloned Intermolt genes (
Muskavitch and Hogness 1980
;
Meyerowitz and Hogness 1982
;
Guild 1984
) and Late genes (
Restifo and Guild 1986
), and on the hunt for the elusive Early genes. An unexpected discovery was the
Jonah
multigene family, highly expressed in the larval midgut with an Intermolt-type pattern (
Carlson and Hogness 1985
). Unanticipated molecular features of the Early-puff genes led to the realization that brute-force genomic walking, aided by occasional jumping across chromosomal breakpoints, would be required for their cloning (2B,
Chao and Guild 1986
; 74EF,
Burtis et al. 1990
; 75B,
Segraves and Hogness 1990
). One of the
Jonah
genes provided the first step of the walk to the Early gene in the 74EF puff (
Burtis et al. 1990
).
Why the Early ecdysone-induced genes were not clonable by the original approach
The very large size of the Early puffs—and the correspondingly large, strong signal seen by autoradiography for nascent RNAs (
Bonner and Pardue 1977
)—had suggested that they encoded abundant mRNAs, which would make it easy to obtain the corresponding cDNA clones. Thus, it was surprising that the differential hybridization screen had not detected any of them. Unknown at the time was the massive size of the Early genes (50 to 100 kb) and, hence, of their primary transcripts (
E74
,
Burtis et al. 1990
;
E75
,
Segraves and Hogness 1990
;
Broad Complex
,
BRC
, at 2B,
DiBello et al. 1991
). At an elongation rate of just over 1 kb/min for
E74A
(
Thummel et al. 1990
), the delay between transcription initiation and termination plus poly(A) tail addition was ∼1 to 1.5 h. In addition, splicing removes the large majority of Early ecdysone-induced newly synthesized nuclear RNAs—90% for Early-gene transcript
BRC-Z1
(
DiBello et al. 1991
). Therefore, most of the primary transcript sequences are not part of the mature, poly(A)+ mRNA. Hence, the abundance of Early-gene mRNAs in salivary glands is considerably lower than was anticipated and much lower than that of the Intermolt and Late genes.
Also unexpected was the fact, revealed subsequently by Northern blotting (
Fig. 1d
), that Early-gene transcript accumulation starts
before
the late-larval ecdysone peak (
Fig. 1b
and
d
). For Intermolt and Late genes, their salivary gland transcript levels change dramatically after ecdysone exposure in vivo and in vitro—Intermolt genes undergo rapid repression (from very high expression to off), and Late genes are transcriptionally activated (from off, rising rapidly to very high expression). However, for Early genes, their response to ecdysone changes transcript levels from modest to higher. These dynamics, in combination with the relatively low abundance of mature Early-gene mRNAs, made it effectively impossible to identify and recover Early-gene cDNA clones using first-generation differential hybridization methodology.
The reason Early genes are already “on” prior to the start of the classic Ashburner model can be explained by regulatory events that came to light in subsequent decades (
Fig. 1e
; reviewed by
Thummel 1996
). First, low-amplitude ecdysone minipeaks during the third instar (
Fig. 1b
, inset), which were not known in the 1970s, induce some Early-gene transcripts. Second, some Early-gene products induce Intermolt glue-protein genes in the mid-third instar. Therefore, the Early-gene transcripts must be present at modest levels many hours before the ecdysone peak that sparks the larval–prepupal transition (asterisk in
Fig. 1b
).
The molecular cloning of the ecdysone receptor (EcR) was achieved later by the Hogness lab based on cross-homology to other members of the nuclear receptor superfamily (
Segraves 1988
;
Koelle et al. 1991
). Based on its temporal expression profile, one could call it an Early gene (
Koelle et al. 1991
;
Fig. 1d
). EcR has both hormone-binding and DNA-binding domains, the latter recognizing ecdysone-response elements (EcREs) in genomic regulatory sequences (
Koelle et al. 1991
;
Cherbas and Cherbas 1996
). However, the functional receptor is a heterodimer of EcR with Ultraspiracle, the product of
usp
and homolog of the vertebrate retinoid X receptor (
Yao et al. 1992
;
Thomas et al. 1993
). Additional complexities, including receptor isoforms with distinct properties, effects of transcriptional coregulators, EcREs with unexpected organization, numbers, and locations, and large-scale chromatin-configuration changes, emerged from burgeoning research efforts to understand the structure, function, pharmacology, and evolution of EcRs (
Gauhar et al. 2009
;
Hill et al. 2013
;
Bernardo et al. 2014
;
Morrow and Mirth 2024
).
Downstream impact: illuminating gene regulation and salivary gland function
Research enabled by Wolfner et al.'s Intermolt- and Late-gene clones fell largely into 2 domains of molecular developmental biology. The first explored the structure and expression of eukaryotic transcription units and gene clusters. When Early-gene mutations—classical loss-of-function and transgenic gain-of-function alleles—became available, the clones made it possible to test key tenets of the Ashburner model. The second connected secreted Intermolt and Late proteins to the biology of metamorphosis. Recent data indicate they protect the developing adult from the substantial dangers of predation and infection during metamorphosis.
Gene regulation and the Ashburner model
In the mid-1980s, genomic clones of
Sgs
genes were paired with the new technique of P-element-mediated germline transformation to map the sequences responsible for their highly restricted tissue and developmental expression patterns. Several discrete sequence elements, with additive and synergistic effects, were identified in upstream regulatory regions of
Sgs3
,
Sgs4
, and
Sgs5
; some contain binding sites for transcriptional regulators, including EcR and Broad Complex (BRC) (
Kress and Swida 1990
;
Lehmann 1996
).
Perhaps the biggest test of the Ashburner model (
Fig. 1c
) was whether Early-gene proteins activate Late genes. Late-transcript abnormalities in mutants of Early proteins BRC-Z1 (a BTB-zinc finger protein) and E74A (an ETS-family member) showed that both are required for normal
Eig71E
Late-gene expression in prepupal salivary glands (
Guay and Guild 1991
;
Karim et al. 1993
;
Fletcher and Thummel 1995
). Each of these Early proteins binds to specific target sequences in
Eig71E
intergenic regulatory regions; mutagenesis of those sites prevents this binding and eliminates the activity of the regulatory elements when assayed by transgenic reporter genes in vivo (
Urness and Thummel 1995
;
Crossgrove et al. 1996
). Similar compelling evidence of direct regulation by Early-gene-encoded transcription factors was also found for Intermolt gene
Sgs4
, which requires BRC for its activation during the mid-third larval instar (
von Kalm et al. 1994
). These insights were incorporated into revisions of the Ashburner model (
Fig. 1e
).
Salivary gland secretions and the perils of metamorphosis
Tightly regulated responses to ecdysone, requiring 1 or more Early-gene transcription factors, result in sequential production of 2 sets of salivary gland proteins: a biological glue, comprising Intermolt gene-encoded Sgs proteins, and a cocktail of 10 related novel
Eig71E
Late proteins. To what ends?
Expelled from the oropharynx at pupariation, the glue spreads between the underside of the animal and the substrate and then dries within minutes, thereby immobilizing the developing fly during metamorphosis (
Farkaš 2016
). The dried glue, especially that of
Drosophila hydei
, is a remarkably effective adhesive, with strength comparable to that of mussel adhesive protein (
Monier and Courtier-Orgogozo 2022
). Several glue proteins are mucins (
Farkaš 2016
), with many oligosaccharide chains that promote viscosity and water-holding gel formation (
McShane et al. 2021
). One of these mucins is encoded by the Intermolt gene located within the 71E cluster,
Eig71Ee
(previously
71E gene VII
;
Fig. 1d
), and was known from research on immunity as gp150 (
Korayem et al. 2004
).
Field and laboratory tests demonstrated that adhesion to a substrate via endogenous glue protected pupae of several
Drosophila
species from predation (
Borne et al. 2021
). Moreover, during glue expulsion through the oropharynx, the sticky fluid picks up microorganisms, especially coliform bacteria and yeast; they become trapped as the glue hardens (
Beňová-Liszeková et al. 2019
). This mechanism could provide important protection as large-scale programmed cell death during the early pupal period generates abundant cellular debris that would be fodder for microbes (
Nicolson et al. 2015
). It is unclear how the individual Sgs proteins contribute to the mechanical and physiological properties of glue.
The
Eig71E
Late cluster is arranged as 5 pairs of genes, head-to-head and divergently oriented, with only ∼260 nucleotides between each pair (
Restifo and Guild 1986
;
Wright et al. 1996
). Only half were isolated in the original screen, probably due to an expression gradient across the cluster. The “delayed” genes were less likely to be represented within both the cDNA libraries and the Late probes. The predicted Eig71E Late proteins are small and basic and have 6 cysteine residues at highly conserved positions, which strongly predict shared tertiary structure stabilized by 3 intrachain disulfide bridges (
Wright et al. 1996
). Noting these and other similarities,
Wright et al. (1996)
proposed that the Eig71E Late proteins are related to the defensin family of antimicrobial proteins (
Eleftherianos et al. 2021
).
The
Eig71E
Late genes are transcriptionally active in the salivary glands during most of the ∼12-h prepupal period—with an abrupt cessation of transcript accumulation as dramatic as the onset (
Restifo and Guild 1986
;
Wright et al. 1996
;
Fig. 1d
). The strongest candidate for direct transcriptional repression of the
Eig71E
Late genes is the Brahma (SWI/SNF) chromatin-remodeling complex, which binds to the intergenic regions between pairs (
Zraly et al. 2006
). A complex, Early-gene-dependent cell-suicide program (
Nicolson et al. 2015
) follows the disappearance of
Eig71E
Late transcripts by 4 to 6 h. It is, therefore, remarkable that all Eig71E Late proteins were detected, in a developmental proteomics study, throughout metamorphosis, but not in adults (
Casas-Vila et al. 2017
). Where did they go?
Between the disappearance of
Eig71E
Late transcripts and death of the salivary glands is the remarkable event of pupation, which results in the developing adult being completely surrounded by a pupal cuticle, with a fluid-filled space between it and the puparium. The pupation fluid contains small, basic, cysteine-containing, newly synthesized, and highly stable salivary gland proteins (
Sarmiento and Mitchell 1982
). In a recent mass spectrometry study, all 10 Eig71E Late proteins were detected in both the lumen of the prepupal glands and later in the pupation fluid, along with well-known antimicrobial proteins (
Beňová-Liszeková et al. 2021
). The perdurance of the defensin-like Eig71E Late proteins following salivary gland death (
Casas-Vila et al. 2017
) is thus explained by their location and stability. In laboratory assays, prepupal salivary gland secretions had dose-dependent antibacterial and antifungal activities (
Beňová-Liszeková et al. 2021
). Together, the circumstantial evidence that Eig71E Late proteins have antimicrobial properties is strong enough to justify testing the hypothesis that they are part of the pupation fluid defense arsenal and asking how their mechanism of action compares with those of known insect antimicrobial peptides (
Eleftherianos et al. 2021
).
When we consider the molecular biology of insect metamorphosis, we see a striking difference between the phylogeny of Intermolt and Late genes, on the one hand, and that of Early genes on the other hand. The
Sgs
glue-protein genes are restricted to genus
Drosophila
(
Farkaš 2016
;
Da Lage et al. 2019
), and the distribution of
Eig71E
Late genes is only a bit broader (OrthoDB v12.1;
Tegenfeldt et al. 2025
). In contrast to these narrow phylogenetic distributions, the Early genes, including
EcR
, are very highly conserved across Insecta—even in those species that undergo an incomplete metamorphosis (
Truman 2019
). Early genes that do not encode transcription factors, such as Early puff
E23
(
Hock et al. 2000
) and imaginal disc Early gene
IMP-E1
(
Natzle et al. 1988
), also have orthologs throughout Insecta (OrthoDB v12.1). The phylogenetic conservation of ecdysone-induced primary-response molecules is an efficient way to ensure molting and other essential developmental transitions across all insects. However, individual taxa, under varied selection pressures, have evolved unique sets of secondary-response genes that are tailored to their anatomical, physiological, and behavioral specializations.
In closing, we can “arrive where we started” (to borrow a phrase from T.S. Eliot) to reconsider ecdysone-induced chromosomal puffing and the window it provided into the nature of gene structure and function. Intensive investigations by many groups have shed light on the molecular architecture of polytene chromosomes under different hormonal and environmental conditions (
Zhimulev et al. 2004
). Chromosome puffing is now increasingly understood in terms of covalent modifications of histones and other dynamic aspects of chromatin biochemistry (e.g.
Evdokimova et al. 2025
). The integration of molecular biology into the study of development continues to transform our efforts to understand mechanisms of gene regulation, cell genesis, differentiation and plasticity, cell–cell interaction, and morphogenesis. Dave Hogness’ vision of cloning genes regulated in time and space stands as an invaluable entry point for that journey.
