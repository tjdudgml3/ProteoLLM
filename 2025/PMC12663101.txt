Introduction
Cell cycle regulation is frequently disrupted in cancer, leading to uncontrolled cell proliferation, genomic instability, and ultimately tumor progression
1
, but also to cancer-specific vulnerabilities
2
. Targeting synthetically lethal genetic interactions offers potential therapeutic avenues to indirectly target proteins previously considered “undruggable”
3
, such as highly structurally similar cell cycle regulatory proteins
4
,
5
, while sparing normal cells and minimizing side effects
6
.
PKMYT1, a cytoplasmic membrane-associated serine-threonine protein kinase that regulates CDK1 and the G2/M checkpoint
7
, has recently emerged as a synthetically lethal target for cancers with
CCNE1
(cyclin E1) amplification and deleterious mutations in
FBXW7
and
PPP2R1A
8
,
9
.
CCNE1
amplification or overexpression increases replication stress and necessitates PKMYT1-mediated inactivating phosphorylation of CDK1, thereby preventing premature mitotic entry prior to the completion of genome duplication and subsequent catastrophic genomic instability
10
. Selective inhibitors of CDK2, the downstream effector directly activated by CCNE1, have faced challenges due to suboptimal selectivity and potential resistance
10
,
11
. PKMYT1 inhibition orthogonally disrupts the G2/M checkpoint and induces synthetic lethality in the context of
CCNE1
amplification or overexpression
12
, which may circumvent both the toxicity and resistance risks associated with CDK2-targeted therapies. Additionally, loss-of-function mutations in FBXW7, an E3 ubiquitin ligase that mediates cyclin E degradation, and in PPP2R1A, a PP2A phosphatase subunit involved in cell cycle regulation, induce dependence on CDK1 inhibition and are promising genetic backgrounds for PKMYT1 inhibition
13
–
15
.
The first-in-class PKMYT1 inhibitor RP-6306
8
and other inhibitors based on its structure feature an inner- or outer-cyclic amide as the PKMYT1 hinge-domain binder
16
. These inhibitors face dose-limiting toxicity and limited clinical benefit
9
, as well as concerns of acquired resistance mutations
17
,
18
. Furthermore, PKMYT1 has been reported to physically interact with β-catenin, non-catalytically blocking recognition by the E3-ligase SCF
β-TrCP
, thereby stabilizing β-catenin and constitutively activating Wnt signaling, which in turn enhances tumorigenesis
19
, suggesting that combined targeting of both catalytic and non-catalytic functions of PKMYT1 may improve antitumor efficacy.
Proteolysis targeting chimeras (PROTACs)
20
–
22
represent a method of protein inactivation that may avoid these pitfalls. PROTACs consist of two distinct chemical moieties: one binds the protein of interest (POI), while the other binds a cellular E3-ubiquitin ligase subunit, such as cereblon (CRBN)
23
. PROTACs induce proximity between the POI and the E3 ligase, thereby facilitating target protein degradation via the ubiquitin/proteasome system
24
. The unique and plastic POI-E3 ligase interaction interfaces and the tissue-selective nature of E3 ligases can improve selectivity over the POI ligand itself
25
–
28
. The event-driven pharmacology of PROTAC-mediated degradation eliminates the need for long residence time and saturated drug concentration.
In this work, we employ the multi-modal AI-driven Chemistry42 platform to generate a novel POI-binder chemotype with potent inhibition against PKMYT1. Following comprehensive CRBN binder screening and linker optimization, we incorporate the novel PKMYT1 inhibitor into the
D16-M1P2
PROTAC, a PKMYT1 degrader. This degrader demonstrates robust and highly specific degradation activity synergistic with
CCNE1/FBXW7/PPP2R1A
alterations.
D16-M1P2
exhibits a dual mechanism of action, encompassing both PKMYT1 degradation and inhibition, exhibiting stronger and sustained pharmacodynamic (PD) effects relative to the foundational POI inhibitor used to initiate design. Our findings provide a tool for studying PKMYT1 biology and offering a promising class of drugs to counteract PKMYT1 activity in cancer.
Results
Discovery of a novel PKMYT1 inhibitor by AI-driven generation via Chemistry42
The PKMYT1 inhibitor RP-6306 bears five H-bond donors (HBDs) located in the deep pocket of the PKMYT1 ATP-binding site, making it suboptimal as a POI ligand
29
. The soaked structure of PKMYT1 in complex with RP-6306 (PDB code: 8D6E) depicts RP-6306 with its primary amino group engaging in hydrogen bonding with the gatekeeper residue Thr187 and hinge residue Glu188, its carboxamide group forming hydrogen bonds with Cys190 and Gly191 in the hinge region, its hydroxyl of the phenol group forming a water-mediated hydrogen bond with Glu157, Phe252, and His161, and its pyridine nitrogen forming a water-mediated hydrogen bond with the side chain of Lys139 (Supplementary Fig.
1a
). To further examine the dynamics and stability of these interactions, a 200-ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulation was conducted, identifying highly persistent and stable hydrogen bonding interactions (Supplementary Fig.
1b
and
1c
). Specifically, hydrogen bonding between the primary amino group and Thr187 remains stable, with an occupancy of 88.4%, while interaction with Glu188 is sustained at 22.2%. The carboxamide group shows strong hydrogen bonding with Cys190 (80.5%) and Gly191 (97.4%), reaffirming its role as a key structural feature contributing to binding stability. MD simulations further reveal additional hydrophobic and van der Waals interactions that complement the hydrogen bonding network, such as interactions with Val124 and Phe240. These secondary interactions likely contribute to stabilizing RP-6306 within the PKMYT1 binding pocket. The consistency of these interactions across the 200 ns simulation trajectory underscores the robustness of the binding mode of RP-6306 and its critical engagement with the hinge and key active site residues of PKMYT1, providing a valuable framework for designing and identifying novel inhibitors that replicate these critical binding features.
Unlike RP-6306, the approved broad-spectrum kinase inhibitor dasatinib (PKMYT1 IC
50
63 nM) extends into the solvent-exposed region and forms a hydrogen bond with residue Gln196 (Supplementary Fig.
1d
). Despite the absence of a phenol hydroxyl group in dasatinib, a structural water molecule in the back pocket maintains stable hydrogen bonds with surrounding residues, suggesting that PKMYT1 inhibitors can extend from two conserved water molecule interactions in the back pocket of the hinge region into the solvent region (Supplementary Fig.
1e
).
We thus utilized the combined features of RP-6306 and dasatinib for molecule design. The complex structure of PKMYT1 with RP-6306 (PDB: 8D6E) served as a starting point for structural generation. The 2,4-dimethyl-3-phenol group of RP-6306 in the back pocket was anchored and kept constant. The pharmacophores of RP-6306 in the hinge region and dasatinib in the solvent area were combined into a new pharmacophore model, which was then used as the input model for AI-driven generation using the Chemistry42 platform
30
(Fig.
1a
, upper left). The strategy involved fragment replacement in the hinge region and directed fragment growing in the solvent area, using over 40 AI-driven generative models. Internal parameters such as protein-ligand interactions, 3D shape compatibility with the binding pocket, pocket volume occupation, and molecular novelty were applied as filters (Supplementary Fig.
1f
). A total of 2023 molecules were generated and subsequently subjected to clustering, re-docking, and visual inspection to evaluate their protein-ligand interactions. In addition, these re-docked molecules were broken down into fragments, with particular emphasis on analyzing the hinge region scaffolds and solvent-exposed groups. Building upon the prior analysis of protein-ligand interactions, novel fragments with favorable binding characteristics were identified. Functional groups corresponding to those potentially favorable fragments (Fig.
1a
, upper middle) were systematically incorporated into novel molecules, followed by docking simulations and visual evaluations.

Subsequently, the Golden Cubes toolkit within the Chemistry42 platform, developed to streamline the early discovery process of kinase inhibitors and predict their potential off-target effects, was utilized to evaluate the off-target probabilities of these novel molecules. Design and discovery of PROTAC D1
We proceeded with PROTAC designs targeting CRBN as the E3 ligase adapter due to its widespread expression and the proven therapeutic efficacy of clinical PROTACs that recruit CRBN
21
,
32
. To generate a virtual 3D ternary complex model of PKMYT1-PROTAC-CRBN, protein-protein docking was conducted using the “
Method 4
” within the Molecular Operating Environment (MOE) software package
33
,
34
. This process involved docking the binary complex structure of PKMYT1-binder (compound
4
) with the binary complex structure of CRBN/CRBN-binder (lenalidomide). Before the docking simulation, the output was set to include 100 conformations of the complex structure for thorough analysis. Based on the results, we selected the poses that demonstrated an appropriately close interaction between PKMYT1 and CRBN. Additionally, the 3D cryo-EM complex structure, comprising CRBN, DDB1, CUL4, and RBX1, was superimposed onto our newly modeled ternary complex, using CRBN as the reference point for alignment. The lysine on the surface of PKMYT1 was expected to extend to E2 to facilitate ubiquitination. Among the results, the pose of Lys345 outside PKMYT1 extended to E2 was selected as the suitable one (Fig.
1e
). The distance between the PKMYT1-binder and the CRBN-binder in the docking pose was 8 Å, corresponding to a linker length of approximately 8 to 10 atoms. A linker composed entirely of carbon atoms was used to generate a virtual 3D PROTAC molecule and seed iterative modification. Given that most orally bioavailable PROTACs contain semi-rigid linkers
35
, we opted not to synthesize PROTACs containing linear carbon linkers. Instead, virtual linear linkers were solely employed as a starting point for subsequent linker generation and design. Two E3-binders, lenalidomide and thalidomide, were individually selected and utilized in subsequent linker design. The E3-binder and the PKMYT1-binder were anchored, and ligand structure-based design, driven by the Chemistry42 generative AI platform, was employed to generate linker structures. After analysis of the generated linker fragments, we chose the piperidinylmethenylpiperazinyl linker therein to form compound
D1
, which was first selected for synthesis and assessment (Fig.
1f
).
Compound

All compounds induced dose-dependent reductions of both PKMYT1 and pCDK1 (T14) in HCC1569 cells (Fig.

We observed a positive correlation between PKMYT1 degradation and pCDK1 (T14) inhibition up to the maximal degradation concentration of
D16-M1P2
(Fig.

and Supplementary Fig.

. Moreover, as the drug concentration increased beyond the maximal degradation concentration, we observed a negative association, where PKMYT1 degradation decreased (“hook effect”), but inhibition of pCDK1 (T14) continued to increase (Fig.
3c
and Supplementary Fig.

). These data suggest that at high concentrations where the hook effect occurs, PKMYT1 predominantly forms a binary complex with
D16-M1P2
, resulting in strong functional kinase inhibition even in the absence of further protein degradation.
To further interrogate degradation kinetics, treatment of HCC1569 cells with 100 nM
D16-M1P2
caused rapid PKMYT1 degradation, reducing PKMYT1 levels by over 80% within 4 hours and reaching maximal degradation at 24 hours (Fig.
3d), a
rate notably faster than its natural half-life of 12.5 hours (Supplementary Fig.
3c, d
). In parallel,
D16-M1P2
time-dependently inhibited pCDK1 (T14), achieving over 50% inhibition after 4 hours of treatment at 100 nM, which was sustained for at least 24 hours. However, at lower concentrations of
D16-M1P2
, a discrepancy emerged between PKMYT1 degradation and inhibition of pCDK1 (T14). Specifically, treatment with 1 nM
D16-M1P2
for 8 or 24 hours resulted in approximately 60% reduction in PKMYT1 levels, yet this degree of degradation did not translate into significant inhibition of pCDK1 (T14). This suggests that residual PKMYT1 remaining after partial degradation is sufficient to maintain CDK1 phosphorylation, and that effective pathway suppression requires more complete PKMYT1 depletion or additional inhibitory mechanisms.
Interestingly, a short (2 hours) treatment with 100 nM
D16-M1P2
resulted in a reduction in PKMYT1 comparable to 8-24 hours of 1 nM treatment (~60%) yet produced significantly greater inhibition of pCDK1 (T14) (Fig.
3d
). This enhanced effect is likely due to direct inhibition of PKMYT1 kinase activity by
D16-M1P2
at higher concentrations, in addition to its role in promoting PKMYT1 degradation. This finding further supports the existence of dual mechanisms—target degradation and direct enzyme inhibition—contributing to pathway suppression at elevated
D16-M1P2
levels.
Collectively, these results demonstrate that partial PKMYT1 degradation alone is insufficient to fully inhibit pCDK1 (T14). Robust pathway suppression by
D16-M1P2
likely requires both substantial PKMYT1 depletion and direct kinase inhibition at higher concentrations.
D16-M1P2 demonstrates dual degradation and inhibition

with the proteasome inhibitor MG-132 or the neddylation inhibitor MLN-4924. PKMYT1 degradation was completely abolished in the presence of MG-132 or MLN-4924 (Fig.

Discussion
Our findings highlight the potential of utilizing AI-enabled platforms for both the discovery of novel kinase inhibitors and PROTAC design. Compared to reported selective PKMYT1 inhibitors, which all feature inner- or outer-cyclic amides as the hinge-domain binder, compound
4
displayed a completely distinct nitrogen heterocycle as the hinge-domain binder. The further development into the bifunctional degrader
D16-M1P2
represents a promising advance in balancing potent inhibition with extreme specificity, signifying its potential as a pure chemical probe for the scientific community to explore PKMYT1 biology. The rapid reduction of PKMYT1 levels by 90% at low nanomolar concentrations lasting beyond 24 hours post-washout underscores its potential as a chemical knockdown strategy.
D16-M1P2
functions as a dual degrader and inhibitor, with degradation being the predominant and more effective driver. This PROTAC exhibited stronger cellular potency and sustained PD effects compared to the POI-binder alone.
D16-M1P2
inhibited proliferation across cancer cell lines with favorable selectivity for
CCNE1
-amplified,
FBXW7-
, and
PPP2R1A
-defective cells, indicative of synthetic lethality between PKMYT1 inhibition and dysregulated cell cycle regulators.
D16-M1P2
exhibited antitumor efficacy in xenograft mouse models, with favorable PK properties and prolonged pCDK1 suppression. Moreover, the observed PK properties across four preclinical species, including reasonable clearance rates, favorable volume of distribution, and moderate to high oral bioavailability, further enhances its potential for clinical application.
A limitation of our study lies in the observation that, despite the clear mechanistic advantages of the degrader
D16-M1P2
, manifested as more potent and durable downstream signaling suppression at comparable or lower target occupancy, along with improved selectivity and tolerability relative to RP-6306, the overall antiproliferative potency in vitro and antitumor efficacy in vivo remain similar between these agents under the tested conditions. This apparent disconnect is likely attributable to the PROTAC “hook effect,” which constrains maximal target degradation at higher compound concentrations, thereby limiting the full therapeutic potential of
D16-M1P2
. Additionally, possible off-target activities associated with RP-6306 may contribute to its antitumor effects, effectively narrowing the observed difference in efficacy.
However, inhibition of multiple kinases, including RP-6306’s non-PKMYT1 targets such as BRAF and SRC, is known to cause complex, often dose-limiting adverse effects due to synergistic or additive disruption of overlapping signaling pathways
39
–
41
. These toxicities have long limited the clinical utility of multi-kinase inhibitors, despite robust efficacy observed in preclinical models. As such, the balance of efficacy and toxicity of current-generation PKMYT1 inhibitors provides opportunity for safer and potentially more effective target engagement strategies. Notably, our data demonstrate that
D16-M1P2
possesses an increased kinase selectivity, which may help mitigate unanticipated side effects and improve safety margins. Nevertheless, the observed hook effect remains a significant challenge for further development. These observations collectively highlight the need for continued optimization of both selectivity and degradation properties in future degrader development. Moreover, rational combination strategies may offer a promising approach to further enhance antitumor efficacy while maintaining tolerability and merit deeper investigation in future studies.
Overall, our research provides a foundational basis for the continued exploration of PKMYT1 as a drug target and highlights the merits of AI-driven approaches in discovering new chemical structures and accelerating drug discovery processes. The approaches of this study could have broader applications, potentially offering insights into a variety of other targets as well.

Crystallization and data analysis
Recombinant PKMYT1 (residues 75 to 362) protein fused to a TEV cleavable N-terminal 6XHis tag was expressed in Escherichia coli and purified as previously described
42
. The protein sample was mixed with a crystallization solution composed of 0.1 LiCl, 0.1 M HEPES pH 7.5, 20% v/v PEG 400 using the sitting drop vapor diffusion method. The crystal appeared after 2 days at 18 °C. The crystal with the proper size was harvested and fast-cooled in liquid nitrogen for data collection. The crystals producing high-quality diffraction spots were chosen for full dataset collection at the Diamond Light Source. The diffraction data were indexed and integrated with XDS and scaled by aimless. The space group is H32 and the resolution is 2.38 Å. Structure was solved by MR with Phaser. After multiple rounds of refinement with COOT and refmac5, the final coordinate has Rwork/Rfree values as 0.2402/0.2899. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation
Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation in this study was conducted using GROMACS 2023.3 software
43
to investigate the interactions between RP-6306 and PKMYT1. The crystal structure of the PKMYT1-RP-6306 complex (PDB ID: 8D6E)
44
was refined using the Structure Preparation module within MOE software. Structural refinements included the addition of missing hydrogen atoms, side chains of residues, and loops, followed by their correction and optimization. Protonation states of the atoms were determined, while the protein termini were capped to ensure stability. The entire structure was subsequently energy-minimized.
The 3D structure of RP-6306 was extracted from the refined complex. Structure optimization of RP-6306 was performed using density functional theory (DFT) at the B3LYP-D3/6-311G** level with the Gaussian 16 quantum chemistry package. Restrained electrostatic potential (RESP) charges for RP-6306 were then computed based on the optimized output file using Multiwfn software (version: 3.8(dev))
45
,
46
. The force field parameters for RP-6306 were derived using the General Amber Force Field (GAFF), and RESP charges were mapped onto the prepared 3D structure utilizing the Sobtop package (version 1.0(dev))
47
, which generated the topology file and restraint file for the small molecules required for MD simulations. The amber99SB force field was utilized to generate the parameter files for the protein PKMYT1 within GROMACS. The parameter files for RP-6306 were incorporated into the topology file of the entire model.
The entire model was then subjected to periodic boundary conditions (PBC) and solvated in a cubic water box using the TIP3P water model, ensuring a minimum distance of 10 Å between any protein atom and the edge of the box. Counterions were subsequently added to neutralize system charges and achieve a physiological salt concentration of 0.15 M. The system was first energy-minimized using the steepest descent algorithm until convergence was reached. Equilibration with atom restriction was then performed in two stages: a 1-ns MD simulation under constant volume and temperature (NVT ensemble) using the v-rescale temperature coupling method, followed by a 1-ns MD simulation under constant pressure and temperature (NPT ensemble) using the Parrinello-Rahman barostat. Finally, the fully equilibrated system without any restriction underwent a 200-ns production MD simulation with a time step of 2 fs. Trajectory coordinates were recorded every 10 ps throughout the simulation. The LINCS algorithm was employed to constrain all bonds involving hydrogen atoms, ensuring the stability of the system during simulations.
The trajectory was initially preprocessed to correct for periodic boundary effects using the
trjconv
tool in GROMACS. Subsequently, the interaction statistics between the protein and ligand were analyzed using the ProLIF package
48
, which facilitated a detailed assessment of molecular interactions.
Novel PKMYT1 inhibitor generation via Chemistry42 platform
Pharmacophores of RP-6306 and dasatinib were extracted and combined to create a new pharmacophore model. The structure of PKMYT1 with RP-6306 served as the initial starting point for AI generation via the Chemistry42 platform. The 2,4-dimethyl-3-phenol group of RP-6306 in the back pocket was anchored with each atom precisely assigned specific types. The newly combined pharmacophores in the hinge region and the solvent area were used as the input model for AI-driven generation. During this process, over 40 AI-driven generative models were utilized (Supplementary Fig.
1f
). During the generation phase, the commercially available Chemistry42 platform leverages advanced models, including deep learning for recognizing patterns within complex chemical data, reinforcement learning to optimize molecular structures based on predefined properties, and generative adversarial networks to design novel molecules
30
. To further enhance the pipeline, the platform incorporates a range of built-in filters independent of PLI scores, such as medicinal chemistry filters (MCF), similarity filters, drug-likeness evaluations, synthetic accessibility (ReRSA), and diversity filters (Supplementary Fig.
1f
). Additionally, rigorous assessment is applied to generated molecular structures, which are refined through 2D scoring modules covering drug-likeness, metabolic site prediction, and flexibility, and 3D reward modules, including pharmacophore prefilters, anchor point prefilters, and shape similarity prefilters. This comprehensive approach ensures alignment with predefined criteria, optimizing molecular properties for research and development applications.
This process led to the generation of a total of 2,023 molecules, which were clustered using the
FragFp
similarity descriptor embedded in DataWarrior software
49
. Subsequently, rigid docking simulations were carried out using MOE software, followed by visual inspection to assess their protein-ligand interactions. In parallel, these molecules were fragmented into substructure fragments for statistical analysis using the DataWarrior software. Several fragments corresponding to specific functional groups exhibiting favorable protein-ligand interactions were identified. These functional-group fragments were then combined with a fixed 2,4-dimethyl-3-phenol core, anchored from the structure of RP-6306, resulting in the design of novel compounds. Further docking simulations and visual inspections were performed to evaluate their interactions. Ultimately, one molecule (compound
1
) was selected for synthesis based on its predicted interactions and overall potential. ADMET properties of compound designs were predicted using Simulations Plus ADMET Predictor (v10).
Docking simulation study of small molecules
The compounds docking into PKMYT1 in this paper were performed using the Dock module within the Molecular Operating Environment (MOE) software (version 2022). The co-crystal structure of compound
2
in complex with PKMYT1 was utilized for the study. The preparation proceeded in two steps. First, the complex underwent a rapid preliminary refinement using MOE’s QuickPrep panel: Protonate3D was used for protonation with Asn/Gln/His side-chain flips enabled; hydrogens near the binding site were left unfixed; and energy minimization was performed with the default QuickPrep restraints. In the second step, MOE’s Structure Preparation module was applied to further refine the complex, correcting terminal residues, adjusting hydrogen counts, resolving library mapping issues, and standardizing charges. The 3D conformations of the ligands were generated using the conformational search module in MOE. Subsequently, docking simulations of the ligands with PKMYT1 were carried out using the Dock module within the software. For each ligand, 10 poses were generated as output. After thorough visual inspection, one optimal pose for each ligand was selected and proposed.
Protein-Protein docking to achieve a linker-compatible conformation of PKMYT1-CRBN interaction
Compound
4
was used as the binder of PKMYT1. The complex structure of CRBN with its binder extracted from PDB 4TZ4 was selected. The protein–protein docking was conducted using the “Method 4” within the MOE package
33
,
34
. This process involved docking the binary complex structure of PKMYT1-binder (compound
4
) with the binary complex structure of CRBN/CRBN-binder (lenalidomide). Both the binary complex structures were loaded as input, and the SMILES of two binders were added as input. Before the docking simulation, the output was set to include 100 conformations of the complex structure for thorough analysis. For each docking pose, the distance between the binders was analyzed. And the lysine site was checked by the modeling of the proteasome complex. The pose that the binders to PKMYT1 and CRBN are close enough in proximity to be connected by a linker was taken as the best protein–protein pose for PROTAC design.
Linker generation for PROTAC design via Chemistry42 platform
The distance between the binders in the selected protein–protein docked pose is the key criterion for linker selection. The proper conformation with the closest distance of 8 Å was selected. To design PROTAC, a virtual linker composed entirely of carbon atoms was used to generate a virtual 3D PROTAC molecule. The structures of the E3 binder and the PKMYT1 binder were anchored with specific atom types defined. Ligand structure-based design, driven by artificial intelligence, was employed to generate the linker. An internal fragment database was filtered and established as the privileged fragments dataset. During this process, over 40 AI models were utilized, with the molecular weight restricted to below 900. Additionally, the generation of tetracyclic and bridged bicyclic structures was allowed. The generative model and filters applied in linker generation closely mirrored those utilized in the small molecule generation.
