What is already known on this topic
Patients with anorexia nervosa experience severe and potentially life-threatening medical complications
Case reports have described both venous and arterial thromboembolism in patients with anorexia nervosa, some of which were fatal
Thromboembolism can be prevented and treated; however, use of medication should be reserved for those identified as being at risk
No study has ever established the risk of thromboembolism in anorexia nervosa
What this study adds
In this large registry-based study, we established an increased risk of thromboembolism in patients with anorexia nervosa
Patients with anorexia had an increased risk of venous thromboembolism compared to healthy controls of up to 7.1
Patients with anorexia had an increased risk of arterial thromboembolism compared to healthy controls of up to 9.5
Background
Anorexia nervosa (AN) is a complex psychiatric disorder with concomitant medical, endocrinological complications from malnutrition, starvation, and purging behavior, primarily affecting young women [
1
–
4
]. AN has the highest mortality of all mental disorders, and somatic complications including cardiovascular events are estimated to account for most of these deaths [
5
,
6
].
Thromboembolic conditions involve plaque rupture and/or formation of clots (thrombi) that obstruct blood flow, either locally (thrombosis) or after traveling to another site (embolism). In the following, the term “thromboembolism” refers to both thrombosis and embolism and includes both arterial and venous events. These conditions are divided into venous thromboembolism (VTE) and arterial thromboembolism (ATE), each with specific pathophysiological features, risk factors, treatment and health implications. Both VTE and ATE are managed with specific antithrombotic agents[
7
–
9
].
VTE typically presents as deep vein thrombosis (DVT) or pulmonary embolism (PE), with established risk factors including smoking, age, cancer, surgery, pregnancy, immobilization, and combined oral contraceptives (COC) [
7
,
8
]. Current evidence on smoking in AN is inconsistent, with some studies indicating higher prevalence and others finding no clear association [
10
,
11
]. Other risk factors, such as advanced age, are less likely to be present in patients with AN [
2
]. Case reports have described VTE in patients with AN including fatal cases [
12
,
13
].
ATE, typically related to atherosclerosis, most commonly presents as myocardial infarction (MI) or ischemic, cerebral stroke, although other manifestations can occur [
14
]. Well-established risk factors for ATE include hypertension, hyperlipidemia, smoking, diabetes, obesity, physical inactivity, and increasing age—the latter being among the strongest predictors in population-based studies [
9
,
15
]. However, patients with AN are predominantly young females with low blood pressure and often engage in compulsive physical activity, although mild hyperlipidemia is also well described [
16
]. As with VTE, multiple case reports have described ATE in patients with AN, and a recent large-scale cohort study further supports an increased risk of numerous cardiovascular conditions, including ATE, in patients with AN [
17
–
20
]. The beforementioned large-scale cohort study has contributed valuable insights into cardiovascular risk in AN, reporting elevated rates of conditions such as heart failure and arrhythmias. However, the study assessed cardiovascular outcomes in a relatively small cohort (
n
= 2081), did not address VTE, and may have lacked power to detect differences in specific arterial events such as stroke or ischemic heart disease [
20
].
Further, a few small studies of patients with AN, looking at various parts of the hemostatic system, point towards a thromboembolic prone state, though established risk factors are absent [
21
–
23
].
Despite casuistic, biological and emerging epidemiological evidence supporting an excess risk of VTE and ATE in patients with AN, no previous studies have directly quantified this risk in a controlled design focusing specifically on thromboembolic events.
Research objectives
The primary objective of this observational, registry-based study is to investigate whether the incidence of ATE or VTE is increased in patients with AN compared to an age—residence—and sex-matched group of healthy controls. A secondary objective is to explore data on mortality.
Methods
Data sources and procedures
The present study is a registry-based, national matched cohort study, based on the comprehensive and mandatory Danish health care registries. In Denmark, all contacts within the hospital health care sector are registered on an individual level using the Civil Registration System and the individual Civil Registration Number (CPR) assigned at birth [
24
]. From the Civil Registration System, information on date of birth, sex, and area of residence was extracted.
The Danish National Patient Registry (DNPR) covers hospital-based contacts and offers nationwide information on diagnoses using the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems (ICD) [
25
,
26
]. Data from the DNPR have been reported to be of high quality and with good validity on diagnoses [
27
]. From the DNPR, information on specific diagnoses was extracted.
In The National Prescription Database (NPD), data on use of prescription medication such as anticoagulant therapy are available on a prescription level using the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical Classification (ATC) codes. From the NPD, data on relevant prescription medication was extracted.
A complete list of ICD and ATC codes applied is available in Additional file 1, S1 and S2.
Population
This study is based on a nationwide cohort of all individuals with an eating disorder (ED) diagnosis in DNPR from 1994 to 2019, explained in detail elsewhere [
28
]. The patient population was defined as individuals having received a diagnosis of either AN (F50.0) or atypical AN (F50.1). Inclusion date and start of follow-up time were the first time in DNPR that the patient received a non-emergency diagnosis of AN or atypical AN. For each patient, five controls with no lifetime AN-diagnosis who were matched on date of birth, post code of residence, and sex were retrieved by Statistics Denmark. Follow-up for controls commenced at the same date as their matched case.
The patients were excluded if no controls were identified, if the AN diagnosis was given prior to 1994, or if the patient was < 8 years of age at the time of diagnosis. Patients under 8 years of age at the time of diagnosis were excluded due to the rarity of AN diagnosis in very young children and potential differences in disease presentation and risk factors. The controls were excluded if they received a diagnosis of AN, if the patient they were matched with was excluded, or if they died before the date of inclusion. Figure
1
shows the flow chart leading to the final study cohort.

We performed an evaluation of selected medical comorbidities and genetic coagulation disturbances among individuals with thromboembolic events. Comorbidities were defined as first-time diagnoses recorded either before or after the thromboembolic event. All diagnoses were identified using ICD-10 codes and a list of applied codes is available in the Supplementary Material
.
Outcomes
The primary outcome was defined as an event with a validated VTE or ATE, while death was considered a secondary outcome. Events of VTE and ATE comprising relevant diagnosis codes were constructed using generally accepted and applied diagnoses on VTE and ATE [
29
,
30
]. A complete list of applied diagnoses evaluated as outcome is provided in the Supplementary Material. Only the first diagnosis after inclusion was considered. If both thromboembolism and death happened on the same date, the thromboembolic event was considered an outcome. A diagnosis of one of the thromboembolic events was considered valid if a redeemed prescription of relevant anticoagulant or antithrombotic medication appeared in the NPD within 180 days, if the event was followed by death within 30 days, or if the event occurred during hospitalization. To account for prolonged inpatient treatment, which is common among patients with AN, we applied a 180-day window for prescription redemption, as medication administered during inpatient stays is not captured in the Danish Prescription Registry.
Registry data was available up to December 31, 2019, which marked the end of the study.
Statistical analyses
Time-to-event analyses considered the time of inclusion until the time of first VTE or ATE after inclusion, treating death as a competing risk, and censoring at the end of the study period. The risk of thromboembolic events was determined, and the two groups were compared using Fine-Gray proportional subdistribution hazards models to account for mortality as a competing event. Cluster robust standard errors were used to account for the matched design [
31
].
Time-to-death analyses considered time of inclusion until time of death, censoring at the end of the study period. The mortality rates in the two groups were compared by Cox proportional hazard regression with cluster robust standard errors.
The above analyses were adjusted for age, area of residence, and sex, through the matched design. All analyses were performed in STATA 18 (StataCorp, 2023).
Ethical considerations
Data were available in Statistics Denmark in a pseudonymized form. Approval of the study was given by the Danish Data Protection Agency (file no. 15/280490). By Danish law, ethical approval is not needed for registry-based studies. Access to the data requires application to the Danish authorities and individual approval of data handling.

The highest age at follow-up was 58 years. For controls, the mean (± SD) follow-up time was 10.8 (6.7) years, with a median of 9.6 years and an IQR of 5.2 to 15.9 years (min = 0.8, max = 26.0), with the highest age at follow-up also being 58 years.

Somatic comorbidities and coagulation disturbances
Among patients with VTE, 16.1% (18 of 112) had one of the applied comorbidities either before or after the event, compared to 9.9% (7 of 71) of controls.
For ATE, the corresponding proportions were 27.9% (19 of 68) in patients and 34.2% (12 of 35) in controls. The most frequent diagnosis was hypertension, found in 6.3% (7 of 112) of patients with VTE and 17.6% (12 of 68) of patients with ATE. For the control group, less than three individuals with an ATE had a hypertension diagnosis registered, and for VTE, the number of controls with hypertension was 11.4% (4 of 35). For the remaining comorbidities, case numbers were too low to permit detailed reporting in accordance with Statistics Denmark’s disclosure guidelines.
Diagnoses related to coagulation disturbances were found in 29.5% of VTE patients and 25.4% of controls, and in 13.2% of ATE patients and 8.6% of controls.
Discussion
In the present study, we found a remarkably increased risk of both VTE and ATE in patients compared to a control group without AN.
To our knowledge, no previous comprehensive studies specific to thromboembolism in AN have been published. Our findings suggest that thromboembolism is an underreported and insufficiently investigated complication in AN. Our findings are in alignment with the previously mentioned studies of patients with AN, looking at various parts of the hemostatic system, suggesting a thromboembolic prone state [
21
–
23
]. In a small study of only seven patients with AN, fibrinogen levels were lower and plasminogen activator inhibitor type 1 concentrations were higher than in normal weight women [
21
]. A larger, recent study using mass spectrometry-based proteomics technology found upregulation of several coagulation factors (II, V, and XI) in restrictive AN, theoretically increasing the activity potential of the coagulation cascade and thereby increasing the risk of thrombosis [
23
].
It has been found that COC increase the VTE risk with a HR of up to 6.5 [
32
]. Estrogen is generally not recommended for patients with AN, and due to the nature of the disease, patients are likely to have fertility issues and may not need COC for birth control. From a clinical perspective, one would expect the use of COC to be more prevalent in the control group thereby giving a weak underestimation bias of the HR. It was beyond the scope of this study to investigate COC use in detail.
Other risk factors for VTE in AN may be due to immobilization, dehydration, edema, and/or venous stasis, which especially can happen during admission and intensive re-nutrition. In Denmark, patients with AN are not routinely immobilized during treatment; only those severely affected by their illness may be temporarily immobile, suggesting that immobilization is unlikely to explain the overall increased risk observed in this cohort. Regarding dehydration, edema, and/or venous stasis, further clinical research is needed to elucidate these mechanisms.
Our findings demonstrate a significantly increased risk of ATE in patients with AN, consistent with a recent study, which also reported elevated risks of numerous cardiovascular complications, including ATE [
20
]. While our study is based on a large national cohort on mandatory health care registries of 12,676 patients and using validated thrombotic events to ensure high validity, and matched controls, the Tseng study examined a health-insurance registry-based population with 2081 patients and highlighted a time-dependent risk for certain conditions, such as ischemic arterial disease, emerging after 60 months.
Further, in ATE, studies on the epidemiology in young people are sparse, and there is no consensus in the literature on the definition of “young” [
33
]. A study reviewed risk factors for MI in young patients (age 44–52) [
34
]. Here, it was found that all established risk factors individually increased the risk of MI up to times 3.3 (smoking). This is in alignment with another study, reporting that young patients with MI were more likely to be active smokers and to have hyperlipidemia [
35
]. In the present study with a notably younger patient group, we found that AN increased the risk of ATE, comprising both stroke and MI, with a factor 9.7. A recent study found that in patients with AN admitted for weight restoration, 50% had significantly increased levels of cholesterol [
16
,
36
]. Increased levels of cholesterol can cause atherosclerosis and be part of the explanation for the increased risk of ATE in AN. Somatic comorbidities were infrequent overall, with largely comparable numbers between groups; further statistical analysis was not feasible due to small cell counts and considered beyond the scope of this study. While these numbers are small, a slightly higher frequency of hypertension in the patient group may reflect surveillance bias, as individuals with AN are more often in contact with the healthcare system, increasing the likelihood of diagnostic work-up and detection of conditions such as hypertension.
A previously mentioned study found increased risk of circulatory disease in AN, with the relative risk being highest during the first year after diagnosis [
37
]. The study is based on a nationwide cohort of nearly 10,000 patients with AN and reports a 60% elevated hazard for any somatic disease during the first 2 years post-diagnosis, which declined over time. While cardiovascular outcomes were included in their composite endpoints, the study did not specifically address VTE or ATE, which is the focus of the present study. A direct comparison between the two studies is therefore difficult, as the outcome definitions and analytical approaches differ substantially.
To sum up, the current clinical evidence appears multidirectional, and this study adds an epidemiological approach to the evaluation of thromboembolism in AN and reports a remarkably increased risk of both VTE and ATE. This highlights the need for larger, clinical studies on the hemostatic balance in AN to help guide clinical management and choice of treatment.
Mortality
Mortality and morbidity are well-documented to be elevated in patients with AN [
38
–
40
]. Reported mortality rates vary significantly across studies, ranging from 0.7 to 4.9 per 1000 person-years, with standardized mortality rates of 6 to 10 per 1000 person-years [
5
,
6
,
41
,
42
].
In our study, we observed a comparable mortality rate of 1.1 per 1000 person-years in patients with AN, but a notably lower mortality rate in the control group compared to other studies. This discrepancy may explain why our hazard ratio for all-cause mortality is higher than those reported in previous studies. The variations in mortality rates and hazard ratios may be influenced by differences in study design, population characteristics, whether the study specifically focuses on eating disorders, follow-up duration, and mortality rates in the control groups. Consequently, direct comparisons should be interpreted with caution due to these methodological differences.
Methodological considerations
Strengths
A major strength of this study is the large sample size of more than 12,000 patients with AN and more than 62,000 matched population controls. Another strength is the generalizability. This nationwide matched registry-based study enables the inclusion of all patients registered with contact related to AN in Denmark and therefore enables generalization of our findings within this population. Denmark’s free hospital healthcare system and the health authorities’ guidelines for specialized AN treatment ensure that most patients with AN are captured in the registers, resulting in high data representativeness and generalizability. By using the nation-wide Danish registers, we also reduced the potential selection bias which could be introduced from selective inclusion of specific hospitals [
43
]. Although it has previously been argued that the positive predictive value (PPV) of VTE diagnoses in the Danish registries is modest, ranging from 31 to 75% depending on site (emergency department and ward, respectively), by requiring use of anticoagulant therapy after an event was diagnosed, the validity becomes as high as up to a PPV of 90–99% [
44
,
45
]. The PPV in the Danish registries for ATE has also been evaluated, reporting values of up to 97% [
46
,
47
].
For AN, a recent study using medical record review by a natural deep learning language model found the validity of AN diagnosis in a Danish region to be 90% and for diagnosis of atypical AN to be 96% [
48
]. We validated our outcomes based on whether the patients received adequate therapy for their outcome, for example had a pharmacy dispensation for an oral anticoagulant or if the event was followed by death within 30 days, or if the event occurred during hospitalization. Validating outcomes in patients who remained hospitalized for prolonged periods (> 180 days) after their thromboembolism and may have had all their anticoagulants dispensed in hospital, ensured that these patients were classified as having an event, and prevented the HR from being biased downwards. To sum up, the validity of the diagnoses in the present study is considered high. Furthermore, we conducted supplementary analyses to assess the distribution of established thromboembolic risk factors that can be captured in Danish health registries, including somatic comorbidities and coagulation disturbances. As mentioned, somatic comorbidities were overall infrequent and not clearly imbalanced, and thus unlikely to account for the strong associations observed. The inclusion of these analyses enhances the robustness of our findings.
Limitations
In the Danish registers, only contacts in the hospital sector are included; there are no data from the primary general practice sector. Consequently, the present study is based solely on patients who seek treatment and have hospital contacts. This may lead to a selection bias, as milder cases of AN that are only treated in primary care may not be captured. However, it is a strength of the Danish healthcare system that there is free access to hospital treatment, which may reduce—but not eliminate—this limitation.
An important factor to consider is the absence of clinical information, such as body mass index, smoking history and severity of the ED, in the registries. Obesity is a known risk factor for thromboembolism and the prevalence of obesity in the control group is not known.
We did not have data on smoking history. While smoking is a known risk factor for thromboembolism, its strength is moderate. A meta-analysis reported a relative risk of 1.23 for VTE among current smokers and another study found an odds ratio of 2.87 for myocardial infarction [
9
,
49
]. Although we lacked data on smoking, the baseline incidence of VTE and ATE is low at younger ages and rises steeply with age. Hence, even if relative risks of smoking may be comparable or slightly higher in younger adults, the corresponding absolute risk differences remain small in our cohort with a mean inclusion age of 18.2 years [
50
]. Therefore, while we cannot rule out residual confounding, smoking is unlikely to explain the strong associations observed between anorexia nervosa and thromboembolic outcomes in this study. Additionally, the literature on smoking in AN is mixed. While earlier studies suggested increased smoking prevalence, recent evidence found no association between AN and smoking initiation or intensity [
10
,
11
].
Together, these findings suggest that smoking is unlikely to strongly confound the associations observed between AN and thromboembolic outcomes in our study.
While our study demonstrates a strong association between AN and thromboembolism, we acknowledge several potential confounders that were not accounted for in our analysis due to its registry-based methodology and therefore lack of clinical data. These include lifestyle factors such as physical activity levels, dietary habits, and stress. Future studies should aim to collect and adjust for these potential confounders to better elucidate the causal relationship between AN and thromboembolism.
Conclusions
In this observational, registry-based study, we report a considerably increased risk of both VTE and ATE among patients with AN. We believe our findings underline the importance of clinical awareness. Clinicians should be alert to symptoms of thromboembolism in AN—even in young patients without classic risk factors—and consider individualized risk assessment.
