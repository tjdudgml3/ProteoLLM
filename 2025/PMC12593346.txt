Nanoparticles (NPs) have revolutionized
targeted drug delivery, enabling tissue- and cell-specific therapy
with dosing precision that a small-molecule approach seldom achieves.
Their success stems from a uniquely modifiable design space, in which
NP core size, surface chemistry, and surface-bound ligands can be
tuned precisely to achieve tissue-selective accumulation and stimulus-responsive
payload release.
,
Once introduced into biological
fluids, however, NPs are rapidly coated by a dynamic layer of adsorbed
proteins, lipids, and other biomolecules, the protein corona (PC),
that overwrites the synthetic surface and dictates the particle’s
subsequent biological interactions.
−
The composition of PC
can further modulate cell targeting and uptake,
−
biodistribution,
,
,
immune responses,
drug
release,
,
and potential for unintended off-target
interactions. Thus, a quantitative understanding of PC composition
and formation mechanisms is essential for the rational design of NPs
that maintain their intended functionality
in vivo
and for meeting emerging regulatory expectations surrounding nanomedicine
safety and efficacy.
The consequences of PC formation are especially
critical for ligand-mediated
targeting strategies. Steric shielding by proteins can reduce the
apparent affinity of surface-grafted antibodies, peptides, or aptamers
by an order of magnitude, negating the very advantage that motivates
their inclusion.
,
On the other hand, deliberate
PC engineering, through preadsorption of dysopsonins or selective
recruitment of specific serum proteins, has been shown to enhance
organ-specific delivery.
−
These contrasting outcomes illustrate a critical
gap in our knowledge. Small changes in NP properties, such as size,
surface charge, shape, ζ-potential, or exposure conditions,
can dramatically alter the composition of the PC, yet no unified,
high-quality data set currently exists to reveal the underlying design
principles. Filling this gap will require systematic studies that
correlate precise NP parameters with PC profiles and biological outcomes,
paving the way for the truly rational design of ligand-targeted nanocarriers.
Significant efforts have been made to characterize both the impacts
of NP-PC formation on NP biological behavior, as well as PC formation
mechanisms and properties that may influence adsorption.
,
−
However, conventional methods of NP-PC characterization can be costly
and time-consuming for a typical lab. As a result, the literature
is fragmented into studies that each explore only a narrow subset
of possible physicochemical parameters.
,
−
Recently, machine learning (ML) algorithms have been applied to
predict PC signatures from published data, offering a cost-effective
approach to integrate disparate descriptions and reveal nonlinear
relationships that are unseen in traditional statistics.
,
,
,
,
However, one of the biggest challenges of
existing model development is the lack of standardized and comprehensive
data sets that can be used to reliably and accurately train a model.
To address these gaps, we conducted a systematic review of the
NP–PC literature from 2000 to 2024 and curated a relational
Protein Corona Database (PC-DB) containing 817 unique NP formulations
and quantitative PC profiles for 2497 identified proteins across four
species from 83 peer-reviewed studies. Leveraging this database,
the first meta-analysis that spans two and a half decades of PC research,
revealing statistically robust trends in protein enrichment as a function
of particle material class, core diameter, and surface charge, was
conducted. We further developed interpretable ML classifiers that
are validated across independent test sets to predict the likelihood
of the presence of key proteins: Apolipoprotein E (APOE), Apolipoprotein
B-100 (APOB-100), Complement C3 (CO3), and Clusterin (CLUS). These
proteins were selected as each has been well-characterized in existing
literature as proteins that may greatly impact NP drug delivery and
uptake into organs.
−
APOE and APOB-100 are lipoproteins known to play
a role in the intracellular transport of lipids
via
the low-density lipoprotein receptor pathways.
,
,
Functionalization of NPs with APOE and/or
APOB-100 has been shown to enhance NP targeting capability for organs
such as the brain and liver.
CO3 and
its cleavage products like C3b, have been suggested to be major opsonins
that promote complement-mediated NP internalization into monocytes,
and may improve targeting to organs like the liver and spleen.
,
,
Conversely, CLUS, also known
as apolipoprotein J, is suggested to work as a dysopsonin that may
improve the stealth properties of NPs and reduce nonspecific cell
uptake.
,
,
,
Finally, feature importance analysis was used to
map model predictions back to intuitive physicochemical variables,
generating mechanistic hypotheses that can guide subsequent
in vitro
validation. All curated data presented in this
article and the developed models are hosted on an open-access web
portal (wb.ai2tox.com), enabling researchers to query historical experiments
and obtain real-time corona predictions for user-defined NPs. Overall,
this research creates the first organized database and analysis tools
for PC, making it easier to use data to improve NP design and accelerate
the development of safer and more effective nanomedicines.

Overview of Protein Corona Database (PC-DB)
This final
database included 83 manuscripts (refer to the
Supporting Information
, SI, for a complete reference list)
from which 817 data entries of unique NP formulations, experimental
methodologies, and quantitative PC profiles were extracted. The final
database includes 2497 identified proteins across 4 different species
(human, mouse, rat, and bovine). A summary of features, such as NP
properties and experimental methodologies, included in the final database
can be found in

The database
contains 13 different classes of NP materials, including silica, metal,
lipid-based, metal oxide, polystyrene, and more. The most common NPs
were metal (28.8%,
n
= 235), silica (22.8%,
n
= 186), and lipid-based NPs (14.8%,
n

Figure

Despite the growing clinical use of lipid-based and iron oxide NPs,

literature surveys.

among more recent publications. Most studies reported PC profiles
for bare NPs (
i.e

n

Despite efforts to harmonize NP dosage across studies
(see the
Methods
section: Literature search
and data curation),
nearly half of the surveyed papers (48.8%,
n
= 399)
did not report the mass of NPs used or provided insufficient detail
to calculate it. Among entries with calculable masses, dosages ranged
from <0.001 mg to >10 mg (mean: 1.8 mg), with 45.4% using doses
of <5 mg of NPs. This wide variability in NP dosage presents a
great challenge for direct comparison of total protein adsorption,
as larger NP doses are expected to bind greater absolute protein mass
regardless of PC composition. In contrast, incubation protocols were
more uniform. Half of all studies (49.8%,
n
= 407)
incubated NP solutions for 30–60 min, most commonly at 37 °C
to model physiological conditions. A small subset used extended up
to 48 h or performed incubations on ice (4 or 0 °C), which can
alter PC formation kinetics and promote particle aggregation. Most
studies used static incubation (82.0%,
n
= 670),
while dynamic incubations, such as shaking or stirring to simulate
in vivo
flow, were reported only in a minority of cases.
Finally, centrifugation was the predominant method for isolated NP-PC
complexes (69.9%,
n
= 571), with speeds spanning
a wide range of 1500–360,000
g
; both overly
gentle and overly harsh speeds pose risks of misrepresenting PC profiles
through incomplete removal of free proteins or protein denaturation.
,
Together, these findings highlight the urgent need for standardized
reporting of the NP mass, incubation volume, and isolation details
to enable robust, cross-study comparisons of PC characteristics.

Protein Detection Rate across Nanoparticles
To better
understand which proteins are most frequently detected in the PC,
we computed the detection rate for each protein across the entire
set of 589 nanoparticle formulations in human plasma. The top 20 most
frequently detected proteins (
Table S2
)
largely consisted of complement factors, apolipoproteins, and immunoglobulin
chains alongside plasma proteins like serum albumin (detected in 77.9%
of samples) and prothrombin (76.9%). Among all proteins, CO3 emerged
as the most frequently detected protein, with a detection rate of
94.3% (
Table S2
). This was followed closely
by APOA-I (93.6%), APOE (92.3%), and Vitronectin (90.3%). Other abundant
proteins such as Immunoglobulin Heavy Constant Mu (90.1%) and Immunoglobulin
Heavy Constant γ 1 (87.4%) also showed high detection frequencies.
Interestingly, our preselected sentinel proteins APOE, APOB-100, CO3,
and CLUS were all among the top 20 most frequently detected proteins
in the data set. CO3 ranked first (94.3%), APOE ranked third (92.3%),
CLUS ranked seventh (84.9%), and APOB-100 ranked 15th (75.7%). These
findings underscore the high prevalence of these proteins across diverse
nanoparticle formulations and validate their inclusion as markers
of PC composition in subsequent analyses.
Meta-Analysis of Protein Corona Detection by Nanoparticle Material
and Size
After excluding eight NP formulations that lacked
either a diameter or a material label, we assigned the remaining 589
formulations, each measured in human plasma, to one of six size-by-material
strata (silica < 100 nm, silica ≥ 100 nm, metal/metal oxide
< 100 nm, metal/metal oxide ≥ 100 nm, lipid/polymer <
100 nm, and lipid/polymer ≥ 100 nm) and pooled detection proportions
for the four preselected PC constituents: APOE, APOB-100, CO3, and
CLUS (
Table
). APOE
and APOB-100 were detected on a vast majority of particles. For APOE,
the reference prevalence on sub-100 nm silica was 0.95 (95% CI: 0.83–0.98;
k
= 5), and all other strata ranged from 0.82 to 0.89. The
lowest estimate occurred on metal/oxide < 100 nm (0.82, 95% CI:
0.56–0.94;
p
= 0.09), a contrast that was
only suggestive and accompanied by substantial between-study heterogeneity
(
I
2
= 77%), indicating protocol differences
rather than a systematic material effect. APOB-100 followed a similar
trend, with prevalences between 0.80 and 0.87 across the inorganic
strata (all
p
≥ 0.64), and no significant
differences were observed for lipid/polymer groups. The only numerical
drop occurred on lipid/polymer ≥ 100 nm (0.59, 95% CI: 0.38–0.78;
p
= 0.15), again with elevated heterogeneity (
I
2
= 61%). CO3 was consistently detected across all strata
with pooled prevalences above 0.79 and narrow confidence intervals.
None of the contrasts were statistically significant (all
p
> 0.07), although a modest decrease was observed on
lipid/polymer
particles. CLUS exhibited the widest variability, with sub-100 nm
silica at 0.92 (95% CI: 0.64–0.99;
I
2
= 50%) and other strata ranging from 0.71 to 0.88. As with the other
proteins, no differences were statistically significant (all
p
≥ 0.18), and several strata showed moderate to
high heterogeneity (
I
2
> 40%). Although
meta-regression can, in principle, reduce heterogeneity by adjusting
for covariates such as ZP, incubation time, or surface charge, these
variables were inconsistently reported and often missing across studies.
To preserve statistical power and avoid biased estimates due to data
sparsity, we limited this analysis to universally available variables.
More complex physicochemical interactions, including nonlinear effects,
are addressed in our ML model, which integrates partially missing
data and provides a broader view of feature contributions to PC composition.

Protein Expression Analysis
Figure S3
presents the kernel density estimation plot of each property
across all normalized proteins, with distributions weighted by %-relative
protein expression (%-RPA). Most adsorbed proteins were relatively
small, with MW ranging between 0 and 100 kDa. The pI of the proteins,
which is a key factor in modulating electrostatic interactions that
may impact protein binding,
showed a
bimodal distribution, where distinct peaks were observed near 5.7
and 8.5. This distribution suggests the presence of both acidic and
basic proteins within the PC, which may reflect differential binding
affinities depending on the NP surface charge and surrounding pH conditions.
Finally, the GRAVY scores, which measure the hydrophobicity of a protein,
were found to be primarily negative (between −1.0 and 0), which
indicates that the majority of adsorbed proteins were hydrophilic.
Interestingly, ZP values were inversely proportional to the average
MW of adsorbed proteins (
Figure S4
). NPs
with near-neutral or moderately negative ZP values showed average
adsorbed protein MWs above 50 kDa, and highly negative ZPs were associated
with even larger proteins above 60 kDa. Conversely, highly positive
ZPs predominantly adsorbed smaller proteins with average molecular
weights of around 45 kDa. Other minor trends were observed across
the remaining NP and protein properties, but no significant differences
were detected, implying additional features or interactions that affect
protein binding.

prominent in Clusters 2 and 3, which were primarily composed of metal

Several BP and MF terms were particularly relevant
to cellular
internalization mechanisms (endocytosis) and suggested potential impacts
on NP cellular uptake and delivery. These terms included cell adhesion
molecule (CAM) binding, which facilitates initial contact with cell
surfaces and uptake of NPs
via

endocytosis;
complement activation, which
indicates the presence of proteins that tag NPs for internalization
(opsonization) and promote complement-mediated uptake into monocytes;
and signaling receptor binding, suggesting that
NP-PCs contains proteins capable of engaging cellular receptors that
may initiate endocytosis. Other enriched terms, such as protein-containing
complex binding, heparin binding, and sulfur-compound binding, imply
the promotion of interactions with membrane-bound proteoglycans and
receptor complexes, such as heparan sulfate proteoglycans or other
sulfated glycosaminoglycans, both of which are known to mediate NP
cellular uptake
via
clathrin- and caveolin-dependent
pathways.
,
This could also imply recruitment of proteins
like APOE that have heparin binding sites for lipoprotein remnant
clearance and may promote endocytosis
via
low-density
lipoprotein pathways.
,
Notably, Cluster 4 exhibited
the highest cumulative expression across these GO terms, especially
CAM binding, heparin binding, and sulfur-compound binding (
Figure S5
). The NPs within this cluster may,
as a result, show enhanced delivery and uptake efficiency through
the aforementioned endocytic mechanisms. Interestingly, the NP physicochemical
properties that comprised Cluster 4 were the most diverse of all identified
clusters. Cluster 4 is mostly comprised of inorganic NPs, including
metal, metal oxide, and silicaall with a wide variety of surface
modifications, sizes, and ZP values. This cluster is also the only
cluster to have carbon-based NPs (
e.g
., carbon nanotubes),
which showed a notable expression of CAM binding compared to other
GO terms (
Figure S5B
).
While not
directly involved in cellular internalization and drug
delivery of NPs, the strong presence of immune-related GO terms implies
interaction of NPs with the immune system that could potentially impact
NP stealth effects and immune clearance.
Cluster 4 in particular shows greater expression of immune categories
such as adaptive immune response, B cell-mediated immunity, immunoglobulin-related
immune response, lymphocyte-mediated immunity, and antigen binding,
compared to the other clusters. Taken together, these categories suggest
an enrichment of immunoglobulin and immunoglobulin-recruiting proteins
on the surface of NPs in this cluster. Typically, adsorbed immunoglobulins
act as opsonins and tag NPs for internalization by monocytic and granulocytic
cells, thus reducing circulation time and NP delivery efficiency.
Prior studies have shown that overexpression of immunoglobulins like
immunoglobulin G can lead to rapid phagocytosis of NPs.
This is especially a concern for clinical applications
of NPs, where diseased patients may have elevated levels of circulating
immunoglobulins
or other biomarkers.
Figure
depicts
the average cumulative expression levels of proteins associated with
cell internalization-related GO terms, grouped according to the NP
subtype, size, ZP, and surface modification charge. Across all NP
properties, signaling receptor binding was the most expressed GO term
without much deviation between groups, suggesting that proteins that
interact with membrane surface receptors are consistently adsorbed
regardless of NP designs. Certain NP properties like size followed
a general pattern with greater protein expression observed with smaller
sizes (<50 and 50–100 nm) and lower with larger sizes (100–150
and >150 nm), with the exception of complement activation (
Figure
B). This pattern
follows prior findings that smaller NP sizes tend to adsorb more protein,
likely due to increased surface area.
Other GO categories and NP properties, however, showed more nuanced
interactions. Protein-containing complex binding was highly enriched
for all NP properties, except latex and carbon NPs (
Figure
A). Carbon and latex NPs showed
disproportionately low expression for all categories except signaling
receptor binding and CAM binding, which signals a preference toward
clathrin-dependent endocytic protein binding that concurs with prior
experimental observations.
In contrast,
metal and silica NPs demonstrated high expression of all GO terms
except for complement activation and CAM binding. This binding pattern
suggests that metal (largely carboxylic acid-modified Au and Ag in
our database) NPs may be more likely to interact with caveolae-mediated
endocytic pathways than clathrin-dependent pathways.
,
CAM binding was also strongly enriched for metal oxide NPs, NPs
with extreme surface charges (<−50 and >+50 mV), and
unmodified
NPs. Moderate expression of CAM binding was observed for neutral surface
modifications, polystyrene and lipid-based NPs, large-diameter NPs
(>150 nm), and moderately charged surfaces (−50 to −10
and +10 to +50 mV). Heparin and sulfur-compound binding categories
were also highly expressed for DNA NPs, all surface modification types
except for unmodified NPs, and all and several ZP groups, including
near-neutral (−10 to +10 mV), moderately negative (−50
to −10 mV), and highly positive (>+50 mV).

Curiously, complement activation consistently showed
a more complex
pattern compared to those of the other GO categories. Contrary to
literature emphasizing cationic, lipid-based NPs as the most potent
complement activators
via
the alternative and classical
pathways,
,
complement activation was more enriched
in our data set for highly negative and moderately negative surface
charges, NP subtypes like niosomes, DNA, and polystyrene, unmodified
or neutral surface modifications, and NPs with midrange or large diameters
(50–150 nm). Moderate expression was seen with near-neutral
and positive surface charges, metal oxide and lipid NPs, and positive
surface modifications; negative surface modifications, such as carboxylic
acids, were associated with lower expression.
These results
suggest strong interactions between different NP
properties. For instance, highly negative surface charges in our database
were most often neutral or unmodified polystyrene and zeolite NPs
of small to moderately large sizes (<50 and 100–50 nm).
Highly negative polystyrene NPs have been found to significantly adsorb
proteins such as complement factors C3a or B,
resulting in alternative pathway activation in exposure studies.
Direct evidence for zeolite NPs is limited, but their high affinity
for immunoglobulins, particularly when incubated with low plasma concentrations
like those in our database,
suggests
a potential for classical pathway activation,
,
meriting further investigation. Furthermore, niosomes, largely comprised
of repeating polyoxyethylene-based units in our database, have been
previously found to result in complement activation regardless of
size or ZP,
,
possibly through the lectin pathway
or surface pattern recognition pathway.
In contrast, the lower complement activation in the positively charged
NPs may stem from the overall larger sizes (>150 nm) associated
with
this group, as well as the inclusion of metal NPs, as both NP properties
demonstrated low complement activation expression and/or low protein
adsorption overall.
However, it is important to note that while
we attempted to include
only studies that reported the majority of proteins within the PC
(total >50%-RPA), the nontargeted proteomics analysis required
to
analyze all proteins associated with NP formulations can be labor-intensive,
difficult, and costly. As a result, key proteins could be missing
from the reported profiles, resulting in skewed data and analysis.
For example, niosomes were interestingly the only NP subtype that
did not exhibit expression of any CAM binding proteins, though it
is difficult to determine if this is the result of preferential adsorption
of proteins by niosome NPs, or due to simply a lack of targeted protein
identification in the studies that included niosomes.

Additionally, we performed a feature importance analysis
to determine
which features contributed most significantly to the performance of
the highest-performing model, LightGBM (

). For all target proteins, the size of the
NP was consistently the most important feature for predicting protein
presence, followed by ZP, which was among the top three most predictive
features for all targets. The subtype of the NP also showed strong
contributions to predictive accuracy across all targets; polystyrene
and lipid-based NPs were moderately important for all targets, while
silica was highly important for APOE prediction (
Figure
A) and moderately important
for APOB-100 prediction (
Figure
B). Metal NPs were important for all but APOB-100 prediction,
which showed metal oxide as an important feature. Surface modification
charge was also a significant feature for all proteins, supporting
that surface coating significantly influences protein adsorption.
Neutral and bare NPs were consistently important, with bare NPs being
the second most important feature for CLUS prediction (

features also demonstrated high importance in all four models. Incubation
time was the second or third most important feature for APOE and APOB-100,
and agitation was highly or moderately important for all models except
APOE (
Figure

the impact of experimental methods on protein adsorption. These findings
underscore the complex relationships between NP physicochemical properties
and experimental conditions in determining PC compositions. The consistent
importance of size and surface characteristics (ZP and surface modification
charge) highlights the fundamental role of NP properties in protein
adsorption, while the influence of incubation conditions reinforces
the necessity of both standardized experimental protocols and experimental
reporting.

To complement this analysis, we have also developed

Despite the strong predictive capabilities
of the model, several
limitations remain regarding the model performance and real-world
predictive insights. First, our models largely focus on only the major
NP physicochemical properties. Other features such as PDI, NP dosage,
NP surface area, or other experimental conditions could not be included
as they were inconsistently reported across studies, though prior
research suggests that they may play major role in determining binding
site availability. For example, as discussed earlier in this article,
NP dosage was reported in fewer than half of all studies. Moreover,
among the available human data, 85.1% of samples fell within a single
dosage range (0–5 mg), offering limited variation for meaningful
pattern learning. This is similarly true for PDI, which would have
reduced the sample size to just 57 samples, all within a single PDI
range: 0.1–0.2. Inclusion of these features would therefore
reduce data set size while contributing minimal additional signal,
making it unlikely to improve model performance. In order to provide
meaningful insights into how these features impact protein binding,
additional heterogeneous and comprehensively annotated data sets are
necessary.
Furthermore, although these predictions are built
exclusively on
human data, all samples are derived from
in vitro
experiments with plasma from healthy volunteers. This distinction
is crucial, as
in vitro
systems, though controlled
and reproducible, may not fully capture the complexity of
in vivo
biological environments or reflect the protein composition
found in patients with underlying diseases or altered physiological
states. Since nanocarrier systems are designed to be administered
in such clinical contexts, caution should be exercised when extrapolating
these predictions to
in vivo
scenarios without further
validation in more representative systems.
Finally, the simplicity
of models, while useful for understanding
underlying adsorption mechanisms for individual proteins, limits their
ability to capture coadsorption dynamics, protein–protein interactions,
or variations in protein expression levels. Ultimately, our models
provide a focused, computationally inexpensive prediction: indication
of whether a given NP formulation is likely to adsorb proteins associated
with specific biological outcomes rather than capturing the full complexity
of the PC landscape. Future research should prioritize improving data
standardization (
e.g
., more consistent reporting
of experimental conditions and NP properties) and advancing ML model
architecture to capture higher-order relationships. Development of
models such as multitask deep neural networks or generative adversarial
networks capable of predicting protein coadsorption and incorporating
critical information, such as
in vivo
data sets,
could significantly improve model robustness, biological relevance,
and clinical applicability. The current versions of these models,
alongside additional functionality for users to input novel NP physicochemical
properties to retrieve predictions for targets, is available in the
cited GitHub repository.

Conclusions
To overcome the absence of a centralized
resource on NP-PC interactions,
we established PC-DB, an open-access knowledge base that integrates
817 distinct NP formulations, their experimental conditions, and quantitative
profiles of 2497 adsorbed proteins. Analysis revealed heterogeneity
in NP materials, sizes, surface chemistries, and reporting practices,
underscoring the field’s need for standardized protocols and
more rigorous data disclosure. Despite this variability, clear NP-PC
relationships emerge. Silica, polystyrene, and lipid-based NPs with
diameters below 100 nm and moderately negative to near-neutral ZPs
preferentially recruit the lipoproteins APOE and APOB-100, proteins
associated with receptor-mediated uptake pathways that can enhance
delivery efficiency to tissues such as liver and brain. Conversely,
metal and metal-oxide NPs in the 50–150 nm range, especially
those bearing strong negative charge or no surface modification, were
enriched with complement protein CO3, indicating a propensity for
immune recognition and rapid clearance. NPs presenting neutral or
PEGylated surfaces favored adsorption of CLUS, a dysopsonin linked
to prolonged circulation and “stealth” behavior. Additionally,
interpretable machine learning models (LightGBM and XGBoost, ROC-AUC
> 0.85 across targets) confirm that NP material, size, and ZP are
the major determinants of these protein-specific coronas, with incubation
time and methods contributing secondary but significant effects. These
findings illustrate how systematic data curation coupled with explainable
ML transforms scattered literature into actionable design rules: modest
adjustments to size and surface charge can shift a corona from lipoprotein-rich
(delivery-enhancing) to complement-rich (immune-activating) or clusterin-rich
(stealth) profiles. PC-DB thus provides both resources for mechanistic
studies and a practical roadmap for engineering safer, more effective
nanotherapeutics.
Methods
Literature Search and Data Curation
A systematic literature
search covering studies from January 2000 to June 2024 was performed
across PubMed, Web of Science, and JSTOR to identify experimental
papers that provided comprehensive and quantitative PC profile data.
The workflow for literature search and collection can be seen in
Figure S10
. The following keywords were used
for each search: (“nanoparticles”) AND (“corona”)
AND (“composition”) NOT (“Corona virus”)
NOT (Review­[Publication Type]). Results were restricted to the English
language and excluded preprints. This search yielded more than 377
papers to be further screened. Papers were removed from the data set
for one or more of the following criteria: (1) lack of relevance to
the search topic, (2) the full-text of the paper was not available
online, (3) the paper was not a primary experimental paper (
i.e
., a review or computational paper without novel experimental
data), (4) lack of reported NP physicochemical properties, and/or
(5) did not provide quantitative PC profile data or data is not in
an extractable form (
i.e
., heatmap or similar figure).
Ban et al.
previously performed a similar
literature search for quantified PC profiles and yielded 56 papers
that fit their inclusion criteria for their search. Our study also
screened the results from this publication and included papers that
were not identified in our literature search into our data set. While
other databases like Ban et al.
were
able to fill in missing values based on experimental calibration curves,
we only included data that could be found directly in or calculated
from information provided within the original text.
After identification
of relevant papers, information related to NP physicochemical properties
(
e.g
., NP material, hydrodynamic diameter, ζ-potential,
and polydispersity index), experimental methodology (
e.g
., incubation time, incubation temperature, and NP-PC isolation method),
and quantitative PC profiles were extracted. Each configuration of
NP properties and experimental methodology was considered as one entry
in the database. A full breakdown of these features, including supplementary
detailssuch as media additives, plasma dilution medium, and
centrifugation temperature and durationis provided in
Table S1
. Although particles larger than 1000
nm exceed the conventional nanoscale definition, they were included
based on FDA guidance,
which considers
materials up to 1000 nm and occasionally beyond as relevant to nanotechnology
when exhibiting nanoscale properties. This broader inclusion enables
a more comprehensive analysis of PC formation across functionally
relevant size ranges.
Features requiring additional calculation
included the NP dosage
and centrifugation speed. NP dosage was often omitted in the literature.
When available, units varied significantly across studies and included
mass units (
e.g
., mg, μg, mg/mL), surface area-based
units (
e.g
., mL/m
2
, m
2
/mL,
cm
2
), molar concentration, particle count, plasma:NP ratio,
or percentage by weight or volume. To calculate original dosage or
convert these units into a common basis for analysis (total NP mass
incubated, mg), we recorded, for each entry, the original dosage unit
and any auxiliary parameters provided, including solvent volume, NP
concentration, and final solution volume. For some NPs, as necessary,
if information such as NP density was not reported, we substituted
manufacturer-provided details. Despite efforts, crucial parameters
for conversion, including NP density, individual particle surface
area, plasma volume, solution volume, and final incubation volume,
were inconsistently reported, which limited our ability to harmonize
dosage data. Entries lacking sufficient details for conversion or
were inherently incompatible with mass units were flagged in the “Final
NP Incubation Mass (mg)” column as “Not Reported.”
Centrifugation speed was converted to time gravity (
×g
) if the rotor radius was reported or if the manufacturer’s
details of the instrument were provided. Finally, all time units were
converted to minutes for a direct comparison.
Other features
such as PDI, which were similarly omitted in a large
portion of the literature, could not be calculated without raw size
distribution measurements. For this feature, and similar features
where missing data could not be determined without unsupported assumptions,
corresponding entries were flagged as “Not Reported.”
For identified proteins, only proteins with valid entry IDs in
the UniProtKB database that have been reviewed and validated by UniProtKB
were included in the final database. If the protein in question was
found to have a UniProt ID that was outdated or merged with an existing
entry, the proper ID was extracted and used in the database; if an
updated ID or alternative could not be identified with a high degree
of confidence, then the protein was not included in the final database.
For each identified protein, the final database provides the associated
UniProtKB entry ID (accession number), entry name or gene name, full
protein name, and the reported or calculated quantity expressed as
%-RPA. Physicochemical properties of identified proteins, including
molecular weight, isoelectric point, and grand average of hydropathicity
(GRAVY), were either directly extracted from the UniProt Database
or calculated using extracted sequence data using the Biopython package,
Bio.SeqUtils.ProtParam.
Gene Ontology
analysis was performed using the GProfiler package
to extract associated Biological Processes, Molecular Functions,
and Cellular Compartment information for each protein. A statistical
significance threshold of 0.05 was used to identify enriched terms
and to ensure that only statistically relevant associations were considered
for further analysis.

iBAQ, log 2 Peptide Intensity, ppm, or similar. Finally, mass-
or concentration-based units for which the respective experimental
study did not provide enough information for confident conversion
were left in their original form. Entries that could not be converted
were excluded from any protein expression analyses but retained in
the final database for binary classification (
i.e
., protein present or absent) analysis and modeling.
Methods for Protein Detection Rate Analysis
To assess
the frequency of protein detection across NP formulations in our data
set, we calculated the detection rate for each protein by evaluating
the proportion of NPs in which the protein was detected. The detection
flag for each protein was assigned as binary (1 = detected, 0 = not
detected) based on the raw signal intensity, with values greater than
zero treated as “detected.” The data set included 589
unique NP formulations that were incubated in human plasma. For each
of the 1514 proteins in the data set, the detection rate was computed
as the ratio of detected instances (where the detection flag was 1)
to the total number of observations for each protein. Detection rates
were calculated for each protein individually across the entire data
set.
Random-Effects Meta-Analysis of Protein Corona Detection
To quantify the influence of nanoparticle core material and size
on the detection of common PC constituents, we conducted a stratified
random-effects meta-analysis across studies reporting protein identification
on NPs incubated in human plasma. From the curated database, we restricted
the data set to NP formulations exposed specifically to human plasma,
excluding those measured in serum, cell culture media, or other biological
fluids. We further excluded entries lacking either primary particle
diameter or material label, yielding 589 unique NP formulations with
complete metadata. Each formulation was then assigned to one of six
size-by-material strata: silica < 100 nm, silica ≥ 100 nm,
metal/oxide < 100 nm, metal/oxide ≥ 100 nm, lipid/polymer
< 100 nm, and lipid/polymer ≥ 100 nm. For each of the four
preselected proteins (APOE, APOB-100, CO3, and CLUS), we recorded
binary detection flags (present/absent) per NP formulation. For each
PC × stratum combination, we applied a random-effects meta-analysis
of proportions using the Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation
to stabilize variance. Study-level proportions were aggregated, and
95% confidence intervals were computed using restricted maximum likelihood
(REML) estimation. Between-study heterogeneity was quantified by using
the
I
2
statistic.
To compare strata,
we conducted random-effect meta-regression with the reference group
set as sub-100 nm silica. Each nonreference stratum was evaluated
for its contrast against the reference, and
p
-values
were computed for each comparison. Importantly, we limited the analysis
to material class and particle size to preserve statistical power
and maximize the study inclusion. Variables such as ζ-potential,
incubation medium, protein concentration, and surface chemistry were
excluded from this analysis due to missingness and reporting inconsistency
across studies. Instead, these features were examined separately in
our machine learning framework, which accommodates higher-dimensional
and partially missing data while allowing for nonlinear interactions.
All meta-analyses were conducted using the
Metafor
package in R (version 4.5.1), and data wrangling was performed with
tidyverse
.
Development of ML Models
We selected four key proteins,
APOE, APOB-100, CO3, and CLUS, as targets for predictive modeling
of their presence or absence in the PC formed around NPs. These proteins
were not only found to be of high frequency and diversity within the
curated database but also represent important cellular uptake mechanisms
of NPs that may be influenced by the PC composition. Four binary classification
models were used to predict the presence or absence of each protein
target: k-nearest neighbor (k-NN), LightGBM, random forest (RF), support
vector machine (SVM), and eXtreme gradient boosting machine (XGBoost).
These models were selected to represent a range of ML learning types
including ensemble learning, instance-based, and linear modeling.
A description of each ML model used in this study can be found in
Table S4
.
Data Preprocessing and Oversampling
For our prediction
data set, six NP physicochemical properties and two experimental conditions
were included as features for model training and predictions: (1)
Type, (2) Subtype, (3) Size (nm), (4) Surface Modification Charge,
(5) ζ-Potential, (6) ζ-Potential Charge, (7) Plasma Incubation
Time, and (8) whether agitation was performed during incubation. These
features were chosen as they are suggested to highly impact PC formation
in the literature,
,
and are reliably reported in
a large number of data entries in our database, allowing for consistent
feature availability across samples. The selected ML classification
models require numerical input features; for this reason, all NP physicochemical
properties and experimental condition features were transformed accordingly.

For some proteins such as APOE, the data set exhibited a
pronounced class imbalance between “Present” vs “Absent”
samples in our data set. In order to avoid or mitigate the challenges
of class imbalance in our model training, we applied the synthetic
minority oversampling technique (SMOTE) to the training data before
model fitting. SMOTE generates synthetic samples of the minority class
by interpolating between existing instances, rather than simply duplicating
them.
This technique allows for a more
balanced distribution between the positive and negative classes, thus
reducing potential bias toward the majority class during model training.

the hyperparameter space for each model. A 5-fold outer CV strategy

serving as a test set for independent model training, validation,
and evaluation. Within each outer fold, Bayesian optimization was
performed using a 3-fold inner cross-validation to fine-tune the hyperparameters.
