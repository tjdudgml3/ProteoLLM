Introduction
Stroke, as the second leading cause of death and the third leading cause of disability worldwide, has long been a significant public health challenge
[
1
]
. Ischemic stroke (IS) accounts for 60–70% of all stroke cases, imposing a heavy burden on the global economy
[
2
]
.
Programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) is a member of the B7-CD28 family. Under pathological conditions, PD-1 signaling regulates the functions of macrophages and microglia by altering their pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory phenotypes, thereby further modulating immune cell functions and influencing pathological states
[
3
]
. PD-L1 (B7-H1, CD274) is the ligand for PD-1 and is also a member of the B7 family
[
4
]
. In the CNS, PD-L1 is primarily expressed in neurons and astrocytes
[
5
,
6
]
. Most studies on PD-L1/PD-1 have focused on the field of oncology, achieving significant progress. Mounting studies indicate PD-L1/PD-1 involvement in multiple central nervous system diseases
[
7
,
8
]
, such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD)
[
6
,
9
–
11
]
, multiple sclerosis
[
12
–
15
]
, intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH)
[
16
–
19
]
, ischemic stroke (IS)
[
20
,
21
]
, spinal cord injury (SCI)
[
22
]
and traumatic brain injury
[
23
]
, etc
[
24
–
28
]
. While PD-L1/PD-1 signaling is increasingly implicated in CNS disorders, the neuronal PD-L1/microglial PD-1 crosstalk in IS remains unexplored.
RFX1 is an evolutionarily conserved transcription factor that influences processes such as the cell cycle, proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis by regulating target genes involved in these processes
[
29
]
. Studies on RFX1 in non-tumor diseases have gained traction in recent years, with emerging evidence indicating that RFX1 can regulate the activation of microglia
[
30
]
.
Although research on PD-L1/PD-1 in CNS diseases is increasing, it remains limited compared to other diseases. In IS, neurons are both the most vulnerable damaged units and the core elements with functional recovery potential. As the central cellular components of brain function, neurons are also primary targets during ischemic injury. Neuronal death determines infarct volume and clinical symptoms, closely correlating with patient outcomes and prognosis. Therefore, rescuing penumbra neurons is critical during the acute phase of IS – “time is neurons.” As resident immune cells in the CNS, microglia mount the first-line immune response, and their polarization status directly influences penumbra neuron fate. Previous studies demonstrated that astrocyte PD-L1 and microglial PD-1 interactions alleviate autoimmune CNS inflammation in acute-phase multiple sclerosis mouse models
[
31
]
. Neuron PD-L1 and peripheral immune cell PD-1 interactions were also proven related to post-intracerebral hemorrhage peripheral infectious complications in mice
[
16
]
. However, PD-L1/PD-1 interactions between neurons and microglia remain unexplored. Concurrently, although limited PD-L1/PD-1 studies exist in IS, their conclusions are inconsistent
[
32
]
. These studies employed gene-knockout mice or systemic immunosuppression using inhibitors/antibodies against both proteins to explore pathological mechanisms. Such global approaches introduce confounding factors (e.g., peripheral immune disruption) when precisely investigating neuron-microglia PD-L1/PD-1 crosstalk. Thus, specifically suppressing neuronal PD-L1 to construct localized CNS effects while preserving peripheral immune function provides a meaningful approach to accurately explore neuron-microglia PD-1 interactions during acute IS.
To investigate the crosstalk between neurons and microglia in PD-L1/PD-1 signaling, we established a middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO) mouse model and employed adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated targeted knockdown of neuronal PD-L1 to examine post-ischemic cerebral neuroinflammation and neurological deficits in the ipsilateral hemisphere. Our study demonstrates that neuronal PD-L1 modulates microglial phenotypic switching toward inflammatory states, thereby exacerbating ischemic stroke-induced neuroinflammation and neurological impairments. Concurrently, microglial PD-1 expression was found to be associated with their pro-inflammatory phenotype and regulated by the transcription factor RFX1. These findings reveal a bidirectional regulatory mechanism between neuronal PD-L1 and microglial PD-1 in ischemic neuroinflammation, providing a novel therapeutic strategy for targeted immunomodulation in stroke management. Methods
Animals
A total of 180 male C57BL/6J mice purchased from Beijing Vitalstar Biotechnology Co., Ltd., were kept in a regular 12 hour day/night cycle environment at a relative humidity and temperature with ad libitum access to food and water. All animals were raised and adaptively fed for 1 week before further experiments. The sample size selection was based on previous literature experience and the anticipated estimation of experimental effects. The ethical number is IRB2022-DWFL-074. All sample sizes were determined by first calculating minimum group sizes via power analysis, then adding 1–3 animals based on technical precision of assays and literature standards. This ensured >80% statistical power while accounting for methodological variability.

IS model and brain tissue collection
The middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO) model was induced via intraluminal filament method. Mice underwent 45-minute ischemia followed by filament withdrawal for reperfusion
[
34
]
. Exclusion criteria included cortical blood flow (CBF) >20% or <80% of pre-ischemic baseline within 10 minutes post-reperfusion. Body temperature was maintained at 37°C during recovery. For brain tissue collection, mice were anesthetized and transcardially perfused (left ventricle) with ice-cold PBS (Solarbio, P1010), followed by cervical dislocation. Craniotomy was performed by inserting scissors into the foramen magnum, splitting the calvaria along the midline, and reflecting both halves with forceps to extract the brain
[
35
]
.
Adeno-associated virus intracranial injection
To downregulate PD-L1 expression in neurons within the ischemic region, we performed stereotaxic delivery of either VA071-AAV-hsyn-MCS-PD-L1 (experimental AAV for neuronal PD-L1 knockdown) or VA071-AAV-hsyn-MCS-blank (control AAV, Shanghai IBSBIO) into the ischemic penumbra cortex of mice, with identical procedures otherwise. All procedures adhered to blinded animal experimentation protocols. Briefly, anesthetized mice underwent scalp incision and skull fixation using a stereotaxic apparatus. Three cortical sites ipsilateral to the MCAO lesion were injected (1.6 μL/site, 0.6 μL/min) with either AAV (3.5 × 10^12 vg/mL) via a Hamilton microinjector (10 μL, Germany). Post-surgery recovery was followed by an 8-week incubation period prior to MCAO induction
[
36
]
.
Neurologic assessment
Neurologic deficits were assessed at days 1 and 3 after ICH by at least 2 investigators blinded to treatment. The modified Neurologic Severity Score (mNSS), rotarod test, and foot-fault test were performed as described.
Neurologic severity score (mNSS)
The mNSS assesses neurological function on a 15-point scale, encompassing motor, sensory, reflex, and balance evaluations. A point was assigned for the inability to execute each test, with a deduction of one point for the absence of a tested reflex. The cumulative score determined the overall impairment in each mouse
[
37
]
.
Rotarod test
Mice underwent a 2-day training regimen preceding the rotarod test. Training sessions involved placing each mouse on a rotating rod apparatus (YLS-4D, Yiyan Technology Development, Jinan, China) set at speeds ranging from 4 to 40 rpm for a minimum of 200 s, three times daily. On the third day, the actual test was conducted, requiring each mouse to remain on the rod at 40 rpm for 10 min. Three tests were administered to each mouse, recording the average latency to fall from the rotating rod (rotarod latency). Mice unable to maintain grip on the rod were assigned a latency of 0 s
[
38
]
.
Foot-fault test
A foot fault was characterized by the limb dropping into the grid hole or resting with the grid at the wrist level
[
39
]
. The foot-fault test evaluated the sensorimotor function of rodents. Mice were introduced to a grid device measuring 32 cm × 20 cm × 50 cm (length × width × height) with a mesh size of 12 mm and allowed to roam freely for 5 min. The percentage of foot faults was computed using the formula: foot faults/(foot fault + non-foot fault steps) × 100. Simultaneously, the rotarod test was conducted in a quiet behavioral testing room with a temperature of 25 °C and humidity of 56%.
Y-maze alternation behavior test
The Y-maze apparatus comprised three black-painted arms (50 × 18 × 35 cm) arranged at 120° angles
[
40
]
. Mice were allowed to freely explore the maze for 8 minutes, with behavior recorded by an overhead camera. Key parameters included: a. Total arm entries: Counted when all four paws entered an arm; b. Spontaneous alternation: Sequential entry into all three distinct arms; c. Alternation rate: Calculated as (alternations/[total entries − 2]) × 100%. This test evaluates hippocampal-dependent short-term memory and cognitive flexibility, with reduced alternation rates indicating spatial working memory deficits.
Cerebral blood flow measurement
CBF was measured using Laser Speckle Contrast Imaging (LSCI) with a PeriCam PSI HR system and PIMSoft software (Perimed). The system automatically calculated the mean speckle intensity and converted it to perfusion units (PU), with a spatial resolution of 0.02 millimeters and a detection distance of 10 centimeters. During imaging, the head of anesthetized mice was fixed using a stereotaxic device. The scalp was disinfected and incised to expose the intact skull. Images were acquired before MCAO, 10 minutes after filament-induced MCAO, and 72 hours post-MCAO. Sequential imaging was performed at 5 frames per second for 2 consecutive minutes. Signal intensity was extracted from regions of interest (ROIs) drawn on the skull over the injured hemisphere and contralateral hemisphere, with each ROI fixed at 25 mm
2
. For all animals, PU values of ROIs were measured and recorded at each time point. CBF was defined as the mean PU value of the injured hemisphere ROI, while relative CBF was expressed as the percentage of the injured hemisphere mean PU value to the contralateral mean PU value. All analyses were conducted under blind conditions.
TTC staining
Following euthanasia, brains were rapidly harvested, embedded in a pre-chilled brain matrix, and flash-frozen at −20°C for 10 min. Coronal sections (2 mm thickness) were incubated in 2% triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC, BL1215B) at 37°C for 30 min (light-protected), with gentle agitation every 5 min and mid-stain inversion. TTC-stained slices were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Solarbio, P1110) for 12 h, followed by moisture removal and high-resolution imaging for infarct quantification
[
41
]
.
MRI scanning
Brain MRI was conducted on a 7 T preclinical scanner (Bruker BioSpec Avance III) equipped with a 72-mm mouse body coil and 116-mm gradient system (600 mT/m). T2-weighted imaging parameters: TR = 4500 ms, TE = 65.5 ms, FOV = 28 × 28 mm
2
, matrix = 256 × 256, slice thickness = 0.5 mm (40 contiguous slices). Infarct volumes were analyzed using Radiant DICOM Viewer, with edema correction applied via contralateral hemisphere subtraction
[
32
]
.
Western blotting
Protein lysates were quantified via BCA assay (Thermo Scientific) and separated by SDS-PAGE
[
42
]
. Following electrophoretic transfer to PVDF membranes (Millipore), blocking was performed with 5% non-fat milk. Membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies: PD-L1 (1:1000, Novus, NBP1-76769), RFX1 (1:1000, Proteintech, 26859-1-AP), β-Actin (1:2000, CST, 2118S), and GAPDH (1:2000, CST, 2118S). After TBST washes, HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:5000, ABclonal, AS014) incubation was conducted at room temperature for 1 h. Protein bands were visualized using chemiluminescence detection (Bio-Rad) with normalization to β-Actin/GAPDH.
Immunofluorescence staining
Mice were transcardially perfused with PBS and 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), followed by whole-brain removal, post-fixation in PFA, sequential cryoprotection in 15% and 30% sucrose, and OCT embedding for cryosectioning (10 μm thickness). Brain sections underwent permeabilization and blocking (1 h, room temperature) in PBS containing 5% donkey serum, 3% BSA, and 0.3% Triton X-100, then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies: Iba-1 (1:500, Novus, NB100-1028), GFAP (1:200, R&D Systems, AF3628), NeuN (1:1000, Novus, NBP1-92693), CD16/32 (1:100, BD Biosciences, 553141), CD206 (1:200, Proteintech, 81525-1-RR), PD-L1 (1:500, Novus, NBP1-76769), and RFX1 (1:50, Santa Cruz, sc-374270). After PBS washes, sections were incubated with species-matched Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500, Invitrogen: donkey anti-rabbit 488, donkey anti-mouse 546, donkey anti-goat 488) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by nuclear counterstaining with DAPI-containing Fluoroshield mounting medium (Abcam). Fluorescence images were acquired using an Olympus BX-61 microscope and analyzed via ImageJ software (NIH), with marker-specific thresholds applied for quantitative assessment of microglial/astrocytic activation and target protein co-localization
[
43

Cell transfection and culturing
GFP-labeled lentiviral vectors targeting PD-1 (GFP-shPD-1-Puromycin) and the control vector (GFP-shNC-Puromycin) were purchased from SWS Biotechnology (Tianjin) Co., Ltd
[
50
]
. BV2 cells were transfected at an MOI of 20, with the virus volume calculated using the formula: Virus volume (μL/well) = MOI × cell number/viral titer (TU/mL) × 1000. Transfection efficiency was assessed via GFP fluorescence, Western blot, and RT-PCR. A stable BV2 cell line with PD-1 knockdown (shPD-1) was established. Cells were then transfected with RFX1 overexpression plasmid (pCMV-RFX1) or control plasmid (pCMV-NC) using Lipofectamine 3000 (L3000001, Thermo Fisher), with 5 μg plasmid per transfection. qPCR samples were collected after 24 hours, and Western blot samples after 48 hours for overexpression verification. All cells were cultured in DMEM (11965-092, Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37°C, 95% air, and 5% CO2.
Oxygen and glucose deprivation/ reoxygenation (OGD/R)
OGD/R experiments were conducted to model in vitro MCAO injury, following a previously described method
[
51
]
. Briefly, BV2 cells were subjected to 1% oxygen in glucose-free DMEM (Gibco, 11966-025) at 37°C for 4 hours to induce OGD. The culture medium equilibrated to 1% hypoxia within 2 hours in the hypoxia chamber. To ensure hypoxic conditions, glucose-free DMEM was pre-equilibrated in the hypoxia workstation for 4 hours before OGD. All OGD procedures were performed in the hypoxia workstation to prevent oxygen exposure. After OGD, cells were reoxygenated for 8 hours in complete culture medium under 5% CO2 and 95% air at 37°C before harvesting. Control cells underwent identical washing and medium changes but were maintained in complete culture medium under normoxic conditions (5% CO2, 95% air, 37°C) throughout.

Statistical analysis
Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was evaluated with an F-test (for two groups). Based on these pre-tests, comparisons between two groups were conducted using the two-tailed Student’s t-test (for normally distributed data with equal variance), the two-tailed Mann-Whitney U test (for non-normally distributed data), or the two-tailed Welch’s t-test (for normally distributed data with unequal variance). For comparisons involving two independent categorical factors, two-way analysis of variance (2-way ANOVA) was employed. If significant main effects or interactions were detected by 2-way ANOVA, appropriate post-hoc tests (e.g., Sidak’s or Tukey’s test) were performed to identify specific group differences, controlling for Type I error. For comparisons involving one independent categorical factor with three or more levels, one-way ANOVA was applied. If one-way ANOVA indicated significant differences, Tukey’s HSD post-hoc test was performed. A significance level of *p* < 0.05 was used for all analyses. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9, with results expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD).
Result
Neuronal PD-L1 suppression alleviated neurological deficits after IS, improved blood flow, and reduced infarct volume.
First, we assessed PD-L1 expression in brain tissue of MCAO model mice. Compared to the sham group, PD-L1 expression peaked at 3 days post-surgery (acute phase,
P
< 0.01) and remained high at 7 days (Fig.

Mice were divided into two groups: one received cortical adenoviral injection for neuronal PD-L1 knockdown (AAV group), and the other received blank adenovirus (Blank group). Immunofluorescence confirmed successful PD-L1 knockdown in neurons (Fig.
1
D; Supplemental Digital Content, Figure S1, available at:
http://links.lww.com/JS9/E652
) without affecting astrocyte PD-L1 expression (Supplemental Digital Content, Figure S2A, available at:
http://links.lww.com/JS9/E652
). Neurological function assessments showed the AAV group had better mNSS scores (
P
< 0.01), rotarod performance (
P
< 0.05), and foot fault test results (
P

1

P
< 0.05). Laser speckle imaging revealed improved cerebral blood flow in the AAV group, with higher relative blood flow ratios (Fig. 1E and F;
P
< 0.05) and instantaneous blood flow values (Fig.

P
< 0.05) in the affected region. Small animal MRI and TTC staining confirmed smaller infarct volumes in the AAV group (Fig. 1H–J;

Discussion
In this study, we demonstrate that neuronal PD-L1 suppression attenuates IS injury by modulating the PD-1/RFX1 axis, which drives microglial polarization from a pro-inflammatory to an anti-inflammatory phenotype. Using a MCAO model, we found that targeted knockdown of neuronal PD-L1 via AAV alleviated neurological deficits, improved cerebral blood flow, and reduced infarct volume during the acute phase of IS. These neuroprotective effects were mediated by reduced PD-1 expression in microglia and a concomitant shift in their inflammatory phenotype, regulated by the transcription factor RFX1. Our findings reveal a novel mechanism of neuron-microglia crosstalk via the PD-L1/PD-1/RFX1 axis, offering new insights into IS pathophysiology and therapeutic strategies.
The neuroprotective effects of neuronal PD-L1 suppression were evident in improved neurological outcomes, enhanced cerebral blood flow, and reduced infarct volume. Mechanistically, blocking neuronal PD-L1 reduced PD-1 expression in microglia while increasing their cell numbers, accompanied by a marked reduction in pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β, TNF-α) and an elevation in anti-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-4, IL-10). This phenotypic shift was further validated by immunofluorescence co-staining, which revealed decreased expression of the pro-inflammatory marker CD16/32 and increased expression of the anti-inflammatory marker CD206 in the AAV group. Importantly, RFX1, a transcription factor exclusively expressed in microglia, emerged as a critical mediator of these effects. RFX1 downregulation following neuronal PD-L1 suppression correlated with reduced PD-1 expression and modulated microglial polarization, as confirmed by in vitro experiments showing that RFX1 overexpression reversed the anti-inflammatory phenotype in PD-1 knockdown microglia.
In this study, we observed that inhibiting neuronal PD-L1 improves cerebral blood flow (CBF) in the injured hemisphere, which we primarily attribute to altered microglial polarization
[
53
,
54
]
. Pro-inflammatory microglia release abundant pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β), damaging cerebrovascular endothelial cells and causing increased vascular permeability, vasospasm, or thrombosis
[
55
,
56
]
. In contrast, anti-inflammatory microglia alleviate endothelial inflammation via anti-inflammatory factors (e.g., IL-10, TGF-β), preserving vascular structure and vasoreactivity
[
57
–
59
]
. Moreover, anti-inflammatory microglia secrete pro-angiogenic factors like vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and TGF-β, promoting neovascularization and collateral circulation in ischemic regions to enhance local CBF
[
59
,
60
]
. As components of the neurovascular unit (NVU), microglia interact with endothelial cells, astrocytes, and pericytes. Anti-inflammatory microglia improve intercellular communication within the NVU, restoring neurovascular coupling (NVC) – the dynamic matching of neuronal activity to cerebral blood flow – thereby enhancing CBF
[
61
,
62
]
. Additionally, modulating microglial polarization during acute IS reduces neuroinflammation and edema-induced physical compression on blood vessels in the injured hemisphere, further contributing to CBF improvement.
Bioinformatics analyses of differentially expressed proteins revealed no significant enrichment in neural systems, immune response cascades, or metabolic pathways – domains frequently spotlighted in prior IS investigations. PPI network analysis underscored the relative independence and dispersal of these proteins, a pattern further mirrored in the limited number of DEPs and sparse pathway annotation. This profile notably aligns with the study’s methodological advantage: precise neuronal PD-L1 inhibition exerts minimal global proteomic perturbation in mouse brain tissue. GO enrichment analysis of the top 10 DEPs (Supplemental Digital Content, Material 1, available at:
http://links.lww.com/JS9/E652
) unveiled putative roles for GTP/GDP binding and GTPase activity in modulating energy transduction, protein conformational shifts, and signaling as “molecular switches.” These findings not only unravel potential nodes for deeper mechanistic inquiry but also illuminate unexplored mechanistic layers meriting future investigation.
The PD-L1/PD-1 axis, a well-characterized immune checkpoint in oncology, has recently garnered attention in central nervous system (CNS) diseases, including ischemic stroke (IS)
[
63
]
. However, its role in IS remains controversial and underexplored. Previous studies using systemic PD-1 or PD-L1 knockout models reported conflicting outcomes: PD-1-/- mice exhibited larger infarct volumes and exacerbated neuroinflammation due to increased peripheral immune cell infiltration
[
64
]
, while PD-L1-/- and PD-L2-/- mice showed reduced infarct sizes after MCAO
[
64
]
. These discrepancies highlight the complexity of the PD-L1/PD-1 axis, which may exert divergent effects depending on cellular context, disease stage, and compensatory mechanisms. Critically, most studies to date have focused on systemic or peripheral immune modulation, neglecting the unique interactions between CNS-resident cells, particularly neurons and microglia. Furthermore, the molecular mechanisms linking PD-L1/PD-1 signaling to microglial polarization remain unclear, leaving a significant gap in understanding how neuroimmune crosstalk shapes IS outcomes.
Recent advancements in neuroscience highlight the critical importance of intercellular crosstalk, as organisms function as integrated systems – an indisputable biological reality. Regarding PD-L1/PD-1, their expression spans multiple cell types, with co-expression occurring even in individual cells
[
3
]
. Given the sophisticated complexity of the immune system, studies employing genetically modified murine models or antibody inhibitors/agonists – whether in oncology or non-oncology contexts – inevitably perturb systemic immunity. Crucially, brain-periphery immunologic interplay introduces confounding variables when investigating neuron-microglia PD-L1/PD-1 crosstalk. Therefore, neuron-specific inhibition not only isolates peripheral immune cells to maximally preserve systemic immune surveillance function, reduces compensatory responses, and diminishes toxicity from systemic immunosuppression. More significantly, neuron-targeted inhibition leverages localized CNS effects to establish neuron-derived PD-L1 signaling as an independent variable, thereby minimizing confounding influences from other cellular PD-L1 sources and peripheral immunity. This enables precise investigation into how PD-L1 signaling within the neuronal microenvironment directly regulates adjacent microglia – the core interaction – revealing intrinsic, spatially specific immunoregulatory mechanisms within the CNS.
RFX1 is an evolutionarily conserved transcription factor known to regulate cell cycle, proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis
[
29
]
by binding to the X-box cis-acting element
[
65
]
. While its functions have been explored in various contexts, including cancer and immune regulation, its role in CNS diseases, particularly IS, remains underexplored. Recent studies have implicated RFX1 in macrophage polarization and lipid metabolism
[
66
]
, as well as in the regulation of innate immune responses
[
67
]
. For instance, RFX1 has been shown to promote the polarization of M1 macrophages and enhance the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines in vitro
[
68
]
. In the context of CNS diseases, RFX1 knockout has been reported to enhance microglial capacity to absorb soluble amyloid-beta (Aβ), thereby ameliorating Alzheimer’s disease pathology
[
30
]
.
By integrating existing evidence, it is highly probable that RFX1 may directly regulate the PD-1 promoter region. Although in vitro SPR analysis did not yield positive results, combined with molecular docking and prior literature findings, we cannot exclude the possibility that RFX1’s direct interaction with PD-1 depends on the post-IS brain microenvironment. As for why RFX1 is expressed only in microglia, this may relate to our disease model and pathogenesis timeline. Previous studies indicate RFX1’s functions in neurons primarily involve neurotransmission, learning-memory functions, and chronic neuronal damage
[
69
,
70
]
, whereas in peripheral macrophages and CNS microglia, it consistently associates with immune-inflammatory responses. Given microglia’s role as the primary CNS immune effector cells, it is reasonable that RFX1 first regulates microglia to actively respond to acute-phase neuroinflammation in IS. However, for neurons, the acute phase may not represent RFX1’s primary functional stage. Consequently, considerable work remains for our future exploratory efforts.
Our study addresses these gaps by investigating neuron-specific PD-L1 suppression and its impact on microglial PD-1/RFX1 signaling. Unlike prior approaches that rely on global PD-L1/PD-1 inhibition or knockout, we employed AAV to selectively target neuronal PD-L1, thereby avoiding confounding systemic immune effects. This precision allowed us to isolate the role of neuronal PD-L1 in regulating microglial function without disrupting peripheral immune homeostasis – a critical limitation of conventional methods. Our findings demonstrate that neuronal PD-L1 knockdown reduces PD-1 expression in microglia, shifts their polarization toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype, and attenuates IS injury, all while sparing peripheral immune cells. This neuron-specific strategy not only clarifies the localized function of the PD-L1/PD-1 axis in the CNS but also circumvents the risks of systemic immune dysregulation, offering a safer therapeutic paradigm.
A key innovation of our work is the identification of RFX1 as a novel regulator of the PD-L1/PD-1 axis in microglia. While RFX1 has been implicated in macrophage polarization
[
68
]
and Alzheimer’s disease
[
30
]
, its role in IS and interaction with PD-1 were previously unknown. Our proteomic analysis revealed RFX1 as the most significantly downregulated protein in the AAV group, and subsequent validation confirmed its exclusive expression in microglia within the injured hemisphere. This specificity distinguishes RFX1 from other differentially expressed proteins and highlights its potential as a central regulator of microglial function in IS. RFX1’s ability to regulate PD-1 expression and microglial polarization was further substantiated by in vitro experiments, where RFX1 overexpression reversed the anti-inflammatory effects of PD-1 knockdown. These findings position RFX1 as a central mediator of neuron-microglia crosstalk, bridging neuronal PD-L1 signaling to microglial inflammatory responses. To our knowledge, this is the first study to link RFX1 to the PD-L1/PD-1 axis in any disease context, underscoring its potential as a therapeutic target for modulating neuroinflammation.
The necessity of our research is further emphasized by the limitations of existing PD-L1/PD-1 studies in IS. First, prior work has predominantly focused on peripheral immune cells or broad CNS cell populations, overlooking the specific contributions of neurons – the primary victims of ischemic injury. Neurons, as the most abundant functional cells in the CNS, are uniquely positioned to modulate local immune responses, yet their role in PD-L1/PD-1 signaling has been largely ignored. Second, while microglial polarization is recognized as a critical determinant of IS outcomes
[
71
]
, the upstream regulators connecting neuronal injury to microglial phenotype switching remain elusive. Our study fills these voids by demonstrating that neuronal PD-L1 directly regulates microglial PD-1/RFX1 signaling, thereby controlling their inflammatory state. Third, the lack of cell-type-specific interventions in previous research has obscured the distinct roles of PD-L1 in different CNS cells. By selectively targeting neuronal PD-L1, we provide unequivocal evidence of its non-redundant function in neuroprotection, distinct from astrocytic or peripheral immune PD-L1. This specificity is particularly important given the complex and variable biological functions of the PD-L1/PD-1 axis, as highlighted by contrasting findings in previous studies
[
64
]
. This more precise neuronal PD-L1 suppression not only enables us to isolate the role of neuronal PD-L1 in neuroinflammation and microglial polarization, thereby providing a clearer understanding of its function in IS, but also ensures minimal impact on peripheral immune cells. This represents a critical advantage over traditional methods that relied on systemic PD-L1/PD-1 inhibition or gene knockout models, which often disrupt systemic immune homeostasis. Finally, our identification of RFX1 as a key mediator of the PD-L1/PD-1 axis represents a novel contribution to the field, offering new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying neuron-microglia crosstalk in IS.
The clinical implications of our findings are profound. Current PD-L1/PD-1-based therapies in oncology often face challenges such as immune-related adverse events and resistance, partly due to systemic immune disruption
[
72
]
. Our neuron-specific approach offers a paradigm shift by confining therapeutic effects to the CNS, minimizing off-target consequences. Moreover, the discovery of RFX1 as a PD-1 regulator opens new avenues for drug development. Small molecules targeting RFX1 could fine-tune microglial polarization without directly blocking PD-L1/PD-1, potentially overcoming limitations associated with antibody-based therapies. Certainly, it is crucial to acknowledge potential systemic immune risks, such as enhanced peripheral immune activation or autoimmune responses, which necessitate careful evaluation in future studies.
Although our study has made some innovative progress, there are still some issues worth of in-depth consideration. For instance, acute-phase injury and inflammation of IS are rapid and severe, critically determining disease outcomes, whereas chronic-phase pathological processes exhibit greater complexity. Post-acute phase, the brain enters functional repair stages with expanded cellular involvement. Hence, beyond this study, future exploration should address: whether chronic-phase astrocytes modulate microglial PD-1; potential alterations in RFX1 regulation during chronic phases; recruitment of peripheral immune cells in PD-L1 response and novel microglial crosstalk; neuronal mediation of alternative repair pathways in chronic stages; and whether precision neuron-targeted therapies reduce peripheral infectious complications. Moreover, although we used male mice to explore neuron PD-L1–microglia PD-1 crosstalk, the impact of estrogen on sex differences in IS cannot be overlooked. Previous studies indicate that estrogen levels in female rodents (including mice) exhibit significant estrous cycle-dependent fluctuations during neuroinflammatory responses
[
73
–
75
]
. These fluctuations substantially affect both microglial activation and PD-L1/PD-1 interactions across multiple cell types
[
76
–
78
]
. Estrogen’s neuroprotective effects are established in ischemic stroke and other CNS disorders
[
75
,
79
–
82
]
. Therefore, whether the microglial PD-1/RFX1 axis is influenced by estrogen – potentially altering responses to neuronal PD-L1 – remains a critical avenue for future investigation.
Despite these advances, several questions remain. First, while our study focused on the acute phase of IS, the chronic-phase dynamics of PD-L1/RFX1 signaling and potential compensatory mechanisms (e.g., astrocytic PD-L1 upregulation) require investigation. Second, the interaction between RFX1 and other PD-1-associated pathways, such as SHP-2 or PI3K/AKT, needs elucidation. Third, the translational potential of RFX1 modulation warrants validation in human-derived microglial models and post-stroke brain tissues. Fourth, the impact of sex/estrogen on microglial PD-1/RFX1 requires further experimental exploration. Finally, the roles of other differentially expressed proteins identified in our proteomic analysis, particularly those related to metabolic and immune pathways, warrant additional study to fully elucidate their contributions to the PD-L1/PD-1 axis.
Conclusion
In conclusion, our study establishes that neuronal PD-L1 suppression attenuates IS injury by reducing microglial PD-1 expression and promoting an anti-inflammatory phenotype, mediated through RFX1 downregulation. These findings underscore the importance of neuron-microglia crosstalk in IS pathology and provide a foundation for developing precision-targeted therapies. Future research should focus on optimizing long-term efficacy, addressing potential compensatory mechanisms, and translating these insights into clinically viable interventions. By bridging the gap between neuroimmunology and translational medicine, this work advances our understanding of IS and opens new avenues for therapeutic innovation.
