1
Introduction
Diabetes mellitus (DM), characterized primarily by chronic hyperglycaemia, is a complex metabolic disorder resulting from both genetic predisposition and environmental influences (Tomic et al.
2022
). Among its complications, diabetic nephropathy (DN), cardiovascular disease and cerebrovascular disease are leading causes of disability and mortality in individuals with DM (Fineberg et al.
2013
). DN is one of the most common and severe microvascular complications of diabetes, typically manifesting in the later stages of the disease (Fineberg et al.
2013
). It affects approximately 9% of patients with either type 1 or type 2 DM, making it the leading cause of end‐stage renal disease (ESRD) (Ma et al.
2025
). Once DN progresses to ESRD, patients require kidney transplantation or lifelong dialysis, both associated with poor prognosis and high healthcare costs (Fineberg et al.
2013
; Samsu
2021
). Histopathologically, DN is characterized by the extracellular accumulation of matrix proteins‐such as laminin, fibronectin and collagen‐leading to thickening of the glomerular basement membrane (GBM) and mesangial expansion (Samsu
2021
). DN progresses through five distinct pathological phases (Samsu
2021
). The first phase involves GBM thickening with preserved estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and no albuminuria (Samsu
2021
). The second phase, which generally emerges 2 years later, is marked by mild to severe mesangial expansion, still without overt clinical signs (Samsu
2021
). The third phase is defined by glomerulosclerosis and the formation of Kimmelstiel–Wilson nodules (nodular sclerosis), accompanied by microalbuminuria (30–300 mg/day), typically occurring 5–10 years after GBM changes (Samsu
2021
). In the fourth phase, advanced diabetic glomerulosclerosis is present, with albuminuria exceeding 300 mg/day (Samsu
2021
). The final phase, ESRD, is defined by a GFR below 15 mL/min/1.73 m
2
. In individuals with type 1 diabetes, alterations in kidney function typically emerge within 3–5 years following diagnosis (Sun et al.
2025
; Yu et al.
2025
). Approximately 30%–40% of these individuals eventually develop more advanced stages of kidney disease, generally occurring 10–30 years after disease onset (Sun et al.
2025
; Yu et al.
2025
). In contrast, the timeline for kidney disease progression in type 2 diabetes remains less well‐defined, primarily because the condition often goes undiagnosed for extended periods (He et al.
2025
; Hu et al.
2025
). As a result, individuals newly diagnosed with type 2 diabetes may already exhibit signs of renal impairment (He et al.
2025
; Hu et al.
2025
). Currently, proteinuria and eGFR, calculated from serum creatinine levels, are the most commonly used clinical markers to diagnose and monitor DN. However, these indicators have significant limitations. Approximately one‐third of patients experience renal function decline before the onset of albuminuria, making early detection difficult (Perkins et al.
2010
; Lee and Choi
2015
; Glassock
2010
). Moreover, albuminuria and eGFR can be affected by confounding factors such as exercise, infection, comorbidities and heart failure, and are not specific to diabetic kidney disease (Perkins et al.
2010
; Lee and Choi
2015
; Glassock
2010
). Some patients may exhibit advanced renal pathology despite normal albumin levels and eGFR (Perkins et al.
2010
; Lee and Choi
2015
; Glassock
2010
). Although renal biopsy remains the diagnostic gold standard for DN, it is invasive, expensive, time‐consuming and technically demanding (Fineberg et al.
2013
; Samsu
2021
; Zoccali
2024
). Furthermore, due to the heterogeneous nature of kidney damage in DN, biopsy specimens may not accurately represent the disease's severity or distribution, leading to potential misdiagnosis (Fineberg et al.
2013
; Samsu
2021
; Zoccali
2024
). Currently, no reliable biomarkers exist to predict which individuals with type 2 diabetes will progress to advanced kidney disease. Therefore, there is an urgent need for novel, non‐invasive biomarkers capable of detecting DN at its earliest stages‐before the onset of albuminuria or eGFR decline. Early identification would allow for timely therapeutic interventions, ultimately improving clinical outcomes and reducing the global burden of diabetic kidney disease.
Urine has emerged as an ideal diagnostic medium due to its non‐invasive collection and its ability to reflect kidney function and pathology (Vlahou and Vanholder
2025
). In recent years, it has been recognized as a cost‐effective and practical tool for monitoring renal injury, with biomarkers such as proteinuria and urinary indicators of inflammation, glomerular damage and tubular injury (Canki et al.
2024
). Extracellular vesicles (EVs)‐small, membrane‐bound particles secreted by cells‐have attracted increasing attention as potential disease biomarkers (Chen et al.
2025
; Welsh et al.
2024
). Urinary EVs (uEVs), which carry proteins, mRNAs and microRNAs, mirror the physiological and pathological states of the kidney, making them promising candidates for the early detection of diabetic nephropathy (DN) (Karimi et al.
2024
; Barr et al.
2024
; Ding et al.
2023
; Raimondo et al.
2013
; Ugarte et al.
2021
; Zubiri et al.
2014
). Proteomic analyses of uEVs have identified several proteins with potential utility as DN biomarkers (Ding et al.
2023
; Raimondo et al.
2013
; Zubiri et al.
2014
). However, current uEV isolation techniques‐including ultracentrifugation, precipitation, and filtration—are complex, costly, and not yet suitable for routine clinical application (Droste et al.
2025
).
To address the limitations of current diagnostic methods, we previously established a technique for isolating uEVs using wheat germ agglutinin (WGA)‐conjugated magnetic beads, enabling subsequent analysis by flow cytometry or real‐time polymerase chain reaction (RT‐PCR) (Yang et al.
2024
; Hu et al.
2025
; Huang et al.
2024
; Li et al.
2024
). In the present study, we combined WGA‐based uEV isolation with high‐resolution proteomic profiling and machine learning algorithms to identify uEV‐associated proteins with diagnostic potential for DN. Comprehensive proteomic analysis of uEVs from patients with various kidney diseases identified cytoskeleton‐associated protein 4 (CKAP4) as a promising candidate biomarker for DN. Quantification of CKAP4 levels in uEVs using flow cytometry across a multicenter cohort further supported its potential as a reliable diagnostic marker. Additionally, CKAP4 was found to contribute to vascular calcification in the context of DN, suggesting a potential role in disease pathogenesis.

Method and Materials
2.1
Experimental Design
To identify protein biomarkers in uEVs for the diagnosis of DN, we first conducted a comprehensive proteomic analysis of uEVs isolated from patients with various kidney diseases using WGA‐coupled magnetic beads, as described in previous studies (Yang et al.
2024
; Huang et al.
2024
). Subsequently, weighted correlation network analysis (WGCNA) (Langfelder and Horvath
2008
) and machine learning algorithms (Acharjee et al.
2020
; Chen and Guestrin
2016
; Sanz et al.
2018
) were applied to evaluate the diagnostic potential of dysregulated uEV proteins. The candidate proteins were then validated through in vitro and in vivo experiments. Finally, their concentrations in uEVs were quantified using flow cytometry across a multicenter cohort.

Between 2022 and 2024, a total of 649 random urine samples were collected in accordance with the guidelines of the International Society for Extracellular Vesicles (Welsh et al.

; Erdbrugger et al.
2021
) from five hospitals: The First Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University, Taikang Xianlin Drum Tower Hospital, The Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University, Jurong People's Hospital and The Affiliated Hospital of Jiangnan University. Detailed demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants are summarized in Tables

g
, 30 min, 4°C), followed by 0.45 µm filtration (Millipore HAWP04700), and stored at –80°C until further analysis.

To ensure accurate biomarker screening for DN, only patients with kidney diseases confirmed by renal biopsy for diagnostic and/or therapeutic purposes were included. The cohort comprised 132 DN patients, 163 patients with non‐diabetic renal diseases (NDRD), including membranous nephropathy (MN), IgA nephropathy (IgAN), focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) and minimal change disease (MCD), as well as 86 NDRD patients with coexisting diabetes (NDRD‐DM). 2.3
Cell Culture and Treatment
Foetal bovine serum (FBS) used for cell culture was processed to deplete extracellular vesicles (EVs) through a rigorous ultracentrifugation protocol (Yang et al.
2024
). The procedure included an initial centrifugation at 3000 ×
g
for 30 min, followed by filtration through 0.45 µm filters (Millipore, HAWP04700). The serum was then centrifuged at 10,000 ×
g
for 30 min and subjected to ultracentrifugation at 120,000 ×
g
for 70 min. This sequential processing was essential to ensure effective removal of residual EVs prior to use in cell culture. The human podocyte cell line (HPC) was generously provided by Dr. Moin Saleem (Children's Renal Unit, Bristol Royal Hospital for Children, University of Bristol, UK). HPCs were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% EV‐depleted FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Gibco, Gaithersburg, MD, USA) and Insulin‐Transferrin‐Selenium (ITS; Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), under a 5% CO
2
atmosphere. Cells were maintained at 33°C for proliferation and shifted to 37°C for differentiation. Mature differentiated HPCs were treated with either 30 mM high glucose (HG) or 30 mM mannitol (MA) (both from Sigma‐Aldrich), or transfected with siRNA (Genomeditech) using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen), according to the manufacturer's instructions (Yang et al.
2024
). Vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) were cultured under similar conditions in a 5% CO
2
atmosphere, as previously described (Calvier et al.
2017
). To assess the effects of HPC‐derived EVs, EVs were isolated using a sequential centrifugation protocol and added to VSMC culture medium at a concentration of 10 µg/mL, followed by incubation at 37°C.
2.4
EVs Isolation by a Sequential Centrifugation Process
Up to date the most widely used protocol for isolating EVs is sequential centrifugation. To validate the purity and efficiency of our newly developed WGA‐coupled magnetic beads for EV isolation, EVs were isolated from urine samples and or culture medium of human podocyte cell lines (HPC) using the sequential centrifugation method (Yang et al.
2024
). All centrifugation steps were performed at 4°C. Initially, samples were centrifuged at 3000 ×
g
for 30 min to remove cells and large debris, followed by a second centrifugation at 10,000 ×
g
for 30 min to eliminate remaining debris and larger EVs. Subsequently, ultracentrifugation at 120,000 ×
g
for 70 min (Beckman Ti70 rotor) was conducted to pellet the EVs. The resulting pellet was resuspended in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) and subjected to a second ultracentrifugation at 120,000 ×
g
for 70 min to remove residual soluble proteins and serum components.
2.5
Construction of WGA‐Coupled Magnetic Beads
Wheat germ agglutinin (WGA; Sigma‐Aldrich, L4895) was dissolved at a concentration of 3.0 mg/mL and conjugated to N‐hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) magnetic beads (Solarbio, M2450) following the manufacturer's protocol (Yang et al.
2024
). To evaluate the efficiency of WGA conjugation, Alexa Fluor 594‐labelled WGA (Thermo Fisher Scientific,
W11262
) was similarly coupled to NHS magnetic beads and analyzed using immunofluorescence microscopy and flow cytometry (Yang et al.
2024
).
2.6
Isolation of EVs by WGA‐Coupled Magnetic Beads and Analysis by Mass Spectrometry or Flow Cytometry
For Western blotting, nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA), and transmission electron microscopy (TEM), 1 mL of cell culture medium or urine was centrifuged at 3000 ×
g
for 10 min at 4°C and filtered through 0.45 µm membranes (Millipore, HAWP04700). Subsequently, 1 mL of Cibacron Blue‐Agarose Beads (BioVision, 7923) was added to each sample and incubated at room temperature for 30 min to remove abundant urinary proteins. The mixture was then centrifuged at 3000 ×
g
for 10 min to remove the beads. The resulting supernatant was incubated with WGA‐coupled magnetic beads at room temperature for 1 h. After incubation, the tubes were placed on a magnetic rack for 5 min to separate the bead‐bound EVs from the supernatant, which was discarded. The beads were washed twice with PBS, and EVs were eluted using a buffer containing 500 mM N‐acetyl‐D‐glucosamine (GlcNAc). The elution procedure included: (1) washing 20 µL of WGA beads three times with 400 µL PBS; (2) adding 400 µL elution buffer per 20 µL of beads and incubating on a rotator at room temperature for 15 min; (3) magnetic separation of beads and (4) collection of the eluate for downstream Western blotting, NTA and TEM analysis. For mass spectrometry (MS) analysis, 2 mL urine samples were centrifuged at 3000 ×
g
for 20 min at 4°C and filtered through 0.45 µm membranes to remove debris and apoptotic bodies. Samples were incubated with 2 mL of Cibacron Blue‐Agarose Beads at room temperature for 30 min to remove abundant urinary proteins for accurate Mass Spectrometry profiling, then centrifuged at 3000 ×
g
for 10 min to remove the beads. The supernatant was incubated with 60 µL of WGA‐coupled magnetic beads under vertical rotation for 2 h at room temperature. After magnetic separation and supernatant removal, the beads were washed twice with 1 mL PBS. EVs were eluted with a buffer containing 500 mM GlcNAc and further purified through sequential centrifugation: 3000 ×
g
for 30 min, 10,000 ×
g
for 30 min, and 120,000 ×
g
for 70 min using a Beckman Ti70 rotor. The final pellet was resuspended in PBS and subjected to a second ultracentrifugation at 120,000 ×
g
for 70 min. Purified EVs were processed for data‐independent acquisition (4D‐DIA) proteomics (Qinglian Biotech, Beijing, China), including enzymatic digestion, alkylation, desalting and purification. MS was performed using a timsTOF HT mass spectrometer (Bruker) with a Captive Spray ion source, operating in DIA mode with a scan range of m/z 300–1500 and resolution of 60,000 (at m/z 1222). TIMS parameters included an accumulation time of 50 ms, capillary voltage of 1.5 kV, and a mobility range of 0.70–1.30 cm
2
/V·s, with a total cycle time of 1.23 s. Raw MS data were analyzed using DIA‐NN software with the Homo sapiens Swiss‐Prot database and a viral species library. For flow cytometry, 1 mL of urine or cell culture medium was centrifuged at 3000 ×
g
for 10 min and filtered through a 0.45 µm membrane. The supernatant was incubated with WGA‐coupled magnetic beads for 1 h at room temperature. After magnetic separation and supernatant removal, the beads were washed twice with PBS. To block non‐specific binding, bead‐bound EVs were incubated with PBS containing 10% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min. After removal of the blocking buffer, the beads were incubated with primary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Following magnetic separation, the beads were washed twice with PBS containing 10% BSA to remove unbound antibodies. Fluorophore‐conjugated secondary antibodies were then added and incubated for another hour. Finally, the beads were washed twice with PBS and analyzed using the Attune NxT flow cytometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of CKAP4 was normalized to urinary creatinine to account for variations in urine concentration. The following antibodies were used: anti‐CD63 (Santa Cruz, sc‐5275), anti‐CKAP4 (Proteintech, 16686‐1‐AP), donkey anti‐rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor 647 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A‐31573) and goat anti‐mouse IgG Alexa Fluor 488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A‐11029). Primary antibodies were diluted 1:200, and secondary antibodies were diluted 1:500 for immunofluorescence labelling.
2.7
Analysis of EVs by Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA)
Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) was performed using the NanoSight NS300 system (Malvern, UK), equipped with a 488 nm laser and a high‐sensitivity scientific CMOS (sCMOS) camera. The sample chamber was maintained at 25°C throughout the analysis. Each measurement was conducted using the following parameters: camera level set to 12, acquisition time of 30 s, and detection threshold of 7. Particle movement was recorded in triplicate, and the resulting data were analyzed using NanoSight Software (version 2.3).
2.8
Analysis of EVs by Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)
TEM was used to directly analyze the morphology and size of EVs. EVs were fixed in a solution containing 2.5% glutaraldehyde and 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA), then postfixed with 2% osmium tetroxide. Samples were dehydrated through graded acetone and ethanol series and embedded in epoxy resin (SPI Inc., Westchester, PA, USA). Ultrathin sections (80–90 nm) were stained sequentially with 5% uranyl acetate for 15 min and 0.1% lead citrate for 5 min. Electron micrographs were acquired and analyzed using a Hitachi 7500 transmission electron microscope.
2.9
Western Blot Analysis
EVs were lysed in RIPA buffer (P0013B, Beyotime) supplemented with phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF; ST506, Beyotime) and a protease inhibitor cocktail (P1046, Beyotime). Equal amounts of protein from each sample were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate‐polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE) and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 h and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies diluted 1:1000. The primary antibodies used included anti‐CD63 (CST, 10112S), anti‐TSG101 (Proteintech, 28283‐1‐AP), anti‐CD9 (Proteintech, 20597‐1‐AP), anti‐ALIX (Abcam, ab117600), anti‐Calnexin (Proteintech, 10427‐2‐AP), anti‐GM130 (Proteintech, 11308‐1‐AP), anti‐GRP94 (Proteintech, 60012‐2‐Ig), anti‐THP (Proteintech, 11911‐1‐AP), anti‐CKAP4 (Proteintech, 16686‐1‐AP), anti‐Runx2 (Proteintech, 20700‐1‐AP), anti‐MSX2 (Proteintech, 68550‐1‐Ig), anti‐YAP (Proteintech, 13584‐1‐AP) and anti‐GAPDH (Proteintech, 60004‐1‐Ig). Following four washes with Tris‐buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween‐20 (TBST), membranes were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‐conjugated secondary antibodies: goat anti‐mouse IgG (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc‐2005) and goat anti‐rabbit IgG (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc‐2004), both diluted 1:2000. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagents (Millipore, USA).
2.10
Immunofluorescence Staining
Tissue sections or cultured cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and blocked with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) containing 10% foetal calf serum. Primary antibodies were applied and incubated overnight at 4°C. The primary antibodies used included anti‐CKAP4 (Proteintech, 16686‐1‐AP), anti‐CD63 (Santa Cruz, sc‐5275), anti‐YAP (Proteintech, 13584‐1‐AP), anti‐AQP1 (Proteintech, 66805‐1‐Ig) and anti‐synaptopodin (Santa Cruz, sc‐515842). After several washes with PBS, fluorophore‐conjugated secondary antibodies were added and incubated for 60 min at room temperature. Confocal images were acquired using a two‐photon laser scanning confocal microscope (Olympus FV3000), and fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.
2.11
EVs Uptake Experiment
HPC cells were labelled with DiI (Beyotime, C1991S) and incubated for 6 h. Following incubation, the medium was removed, and the cells were washed three times with fresh medium. The cells were then cultured in new medium for an additional 24 h, after which the conditioned medium was collected. DiI‐labelled EVs were isolated from the medium using a sequential centrifugation protocol. The labelled EVs were subsequently co‐cultured with VSMCs for 6 h, followed by nuclear staining with DAPI. Samples were then visualized using a confocal microscope (Olympus FV3000).
2.12
RT‐qPCR
Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, USA) and resuspended in diethylpyrocarbonate (DEPC)‐treated water according to the manufacturer's instructions (Yang et al.
2024
). RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a OneDrop‐2000 spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Technologies). mRNA quantification was performed by reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT‐qPCR) using the HiScript III All‐in‐One RT SuperMix Perfect for qPCR (Vazyme Biotech, R333‐01) and the Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme Biotech, Q712‐02) in 96‐well plates following the manufacturer's protocol (Yang et al.
2024
). Primer sequences used in this study are listed in Table
S2
.
2.13
Alizarin Red Staining
Calcification was assessed using Alizarin Red staining, following established protocols (Shi et al.
2022
). Cells were seeded in 12‐well plates, fixed with 70% ethanol and stained with Alizarin Red solution (Beyotime, C0138). Calcified deposits were then visualized under a microscope, and representative images were captured for analysis.
2.14
Calcium Deposition
To quantify intracellular calcium ion levels, cells were seeded in 6‐well plates. After harvesting, the culture medium was removed, and the cells were washed once with PBS. Each well was then lysed with 200 µL of sample lysis buffer. The lysates were centrifuged at 10,000–14,000 ×
g
for 3–5 min at 4°C, and the resulting supernatant was collected and kept on ice for subsequent analysis. Absorbance at 575 nm was measured using a SpectraMax 190 microplate reader (Molecular Devices), and calcium ion concentrations were calculated according to the manufacturer's instructions (Beyotime, S1063S).
2.15
Human Uromodulin (THP) Detection by ELISA
THP in the urine and uEVs was measured using human THP ELISA (Sigma‐Aldrich, RAB0751) kits according to the manufacturer's protocols.
2.16
Animal Models and Experimental Procedures
Male type 2 diabetic (db/db) and nondiabetic (db/m) mice with congenital leptin receptor deficiency were obtained from the Animal Model Research Center at Nanjing University. The first postnatal week was designated as experimental week 1. All animals were housed under specific pathogen‐free conditions with a controlled temperature of 22  ±  2°C, humidity of 60 ± 2%, and a 12‐h light/dark cycle. Mice had ad libitum access to food and water. Blood glucose levels, urine output and body weight were monitored every 4 weeks until week 20. Renal ultrastructure was examined by transmission electron microscopy to assess podocyte foot process effacement, while CKAP4 protein expression was evaluated via immunofluorescence. Urinary albumin and creatinine levels were measured from collected urine samples. An additional DN model was established using streptozotocin (STZ) in C57BL/6J male mice, following established protocols (Furman
2021
). Eight‐week‐old mice were randomly assigned to either the control (Ctrl) or STZ‐induced DN (STZ‐DN) group after a 1‐week acclimatization period, marked as experimental week 1. The STZ solution (1%) was administered intraperitoneally as a single high‐dose injection based on body weight. Ctrl mice received an equivalent volume of citrate buffer. Blood glucose levels were measured 7 days post‐injection, and mice with glucose levels ≥16.7 mmol/L were included in the STZ‐DN group. Blood glucose, urine output and body weight were recorded at 4, 8, 12, 16 and 20 weeks. Kidney tissues were analyzed by transmission electron microscopy for podocyte foot process fusion, and CKAP4 expression was determined using immunofluorescence. Urinary albumin and creatinine concentrations were also quantified. All animal procedures were approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Animal Experimental Center at China Pharmaceutical University (Approval No. 2024‐09‐146) and were conducted in accordance with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and the Declaration of Helsinki.
2.17
Single‐Cell RNA‐Seq Data Analysis
Single‐cell RNA sequencing data were reanalyzed using the Seurat package (version 4.0.3). The original Seurat object was obtained from the NIDDK Kidney Precision Medicine Project (KPMP) repository (
https://atlas.kpmp.org/repository/
) (Hansen et al.
2022
). For this study, the dataset was subset to include only samples from healthy controls (Cont) and diabetic nephropathy (DN) patients (DKD). Subsequent analyses were performed on this filtered dataset. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) plots were generated using the DimPlot function. Gene expression patterns were visualized using the DotPlot function, and specific feature distributions were displayed on UMAPs using the FeaturePlot function.
2.18
Weighted Gene Co‐Expression Network Analysis (WGCNA)
Weighted Gene Co‐expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) was performed using the R package WGCNA to identify correlations between protein co‐expression modules and disease progression (Langfelder and Horvath
2008
). Pearson correlation coefficients of protein expression levels were calculated across 58 samples, and an appropriate soft‐thresholding power (
β
) was selected to approximate a scale‐free network topology. The resulting adjacency matrix was transformed into a Topological Overlap Matrix (TOM), which was subjected to hierarchical clustering to group proteins with similar co‐expression patterns. Modules were identified using dynamic tree cutting and assigned unique colour labels. The relationship between each module and disease progression was assessed using Spearman correlation analysis between module eigengenes and a sequence of samples ordered by disease severity, with the sign of the correlation indicating the direction of association.
2.19
Selection of Biomarkers
Limma was used for differential expression analysis of proteomics. Differentially expressed proteins (|log
2
FC| > 1,
p
< 0.05, FDR < 0.05) were subjected to machine learning–based feature selection (Acharjee et al.
2020
; Chen and Guestrin
2016
; Sanz et al.
2018
). The Random Forest model ranked features based on the mean decrease in Gini index (Acharjee et al.
2020
; Chen and Guestrin
2016
; Sanz et al.
2018
). Overlapping features identified by Limma, WGCNA and the machine learning methods were analyzed using a Venn diagram.
2.20
Statistical Analysis
All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 and R software (version 4.3.1). Continuous variables were first assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± SEM and analyzed using Student's
t
‐test, one‐way or two‐way ANOVA, followed by Tukey's post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Non‐normally distributed variables were presented as median (interquartile range) and analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U or Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by Dunn's post hoc test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test as appropriate. For the proteomic datasets, differential protein expression was determined using the Limma package in R, and
p
values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method, with FDR < 0.05 considered statistically significant. To account for potential confounding factors (including age, sex and eGFR), multivariable linear regression analyses were performed when comparing CKAP4 levels across clinical groups. The adjusted models confirmed that differences in CKAP4 expression remained statistically significant after covariate correction. All statistical tests were two‐sided, and
p
< 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
3
Results
3.1
Development the Platform for EVs Capture
EVs are lipid bilayer‐enclosed structures released by nearly all cell types and are widely present in various body fluids. They carry diverse biochemical signals, including genetic material, proteins and lipids that reflect their cellular origin, making them promising sources of biomarkers for a range of diseases (Chen et al.
2025
). However, the small size and heterogeneity of EVs pose significant challenges for their efficient isolation and specific detection (Chen et al.
2025
). Notably, cells commonly express surface glycans, resulting in the enrichment of glycosylated proteins on the membranes of EVs derived from these cells. In our previous study, we developed an innovative EV isolation method using WGA‐coupled magnetic beads. This technique enabled the identification of several proteins and nucleic acids in urinary EVs that serve as potential biomarkers for chronic kidney disease (Yang et al.
2024
), breast cancer (Huang et al.
2024
) and metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease (Hu et al.
2025
). Li et al. identified five miRNAs in serum EVs isolated using WGA‐coupled magnetic beads that may serve as novel biomarkers for the early detection of hepatocellular carcinoma (Li et al.
2024
). The method leverages the affinity of lectins for glycosylated proteins on EV membranes, allowing efficient enrichment from liquid samples with high throughput and rapid processing times, thereby enhancing the diagnostic utility of EVs. Despite these advantages, clinical application revealed certain limitations. In patients with specific renal pathologies, elevated levels of large microvesicles (>500 nm) and abundant proteins such as albumin and immunoglobulins compromised the accuracy of flow cytometric analysis. These contaminants increased background fluorescence and posed a risk of instrument blockage.
To overcome these issues, we refined our isolation protocol. Urine samples were first centrifuged at 3000 ×
g
and filtered through a 0.45 µm membrane to remove microvesicles larger than 500 nm. Subsequently, Cibacron Blue‐Agarose Beads, known to bind and deplete abundant proteins such as albumin and immunoglobulins, were added to the samples. Coomassie Blue staining confirmed the effective removal of abundant proteins, while Western blot analysis demonstrated that EV content remained unaffected by the treatment (Figure
S1A,B
). After validating the integrity and functionality of the 3 µm WGA‐coupled magnetic beads via immunofluorescence microscopy and flow cytometry (Figure
S1C,D
), the beads were incubated with urine samples collected from healthy volunteers and patients with DN. The EVs captured by the WGA‐coupled magnetic beads were subsequently characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM; Figure
1A
) and Western blot analysis (Figure
1B
). Following elution, the EVs were further analyzed using TEM, NTA and Western blotting. Consistent with previous reports (Yang et al.
2024
; Hu et al.
2025
; Huang et al.
2024
), the isolated EVs displayed a characteristic bilayer membrane structure and an average diameter of approximately 100 nm (Figures
1C,D
). Moreover, they expressed canonical exosomal markers‐CD9, ALIX, TSG101 and CD63‐but lacked the endoplasmic reticulum‐associated protein Calnexin, GRP94 and Golgi apparatus‐associated protein GM130 indicating minimal contamination with non‐exosomal vesicles (Figure
1E
).

3.2
Quantitative Evaluation of EV Capture Efficiency Using WGA‐Coupled Magnetic Beads
To assess the quantitative performance of WGA‐coupled magnetic beads in capturing EVs, a series of time‐ and dose‐dependent experiments were conducted. EVs were initially isolated from 0.5, 1 and 2 mL of urine via sequential centrifugation as gold standard. Both total EV protein and ALIX expression levels increased proportionally with urine volume (Figure
S1E,F
). Next, varying volumes (0–40 µL) of WGA‐coupled magnetic beads (10⁷ beads/µL) were incubated with 1 mL of urine. The captured EVs were quantified by BCA assay and Western blotting for ALIX. A positive correlation was observed between bead volume and both total protein and ALIX expression (Figure
1F,G
), with 30 µL of beads capturing the majority of EVs from 1 mL of urine. To determine optimal incubation time, 30 µL of beads were incubated with 1 mL of urine for 0.5, 1, 1.5 and 2 h. Capture efficiency exceeded 90% after 1 h (Figure
1H,I
).
Additionally, when 30 µL of beads were incubated with increasing urine volumes (0.5, 1 and 2 mL) for 1 h, the quantity of captured EVs, as assessed by total protein and ALIX levels, increased proportionally (Figure
1J,K
). These results identified 1 mL of urine as the optimal volume for efficient EV capture with 30 µL of beads. To further validate the capture efficacy, EVs previously isolated by sequential centrifugation were serially diluted into 1 mL of EV‐depleted urine and incubated with 30 µL of WGA‐coupled beads at room temperature for 1 h. The total protein and ALIX content of the captured EVs showed a strong linear correlation with the amount of EVs added, achieving a recovery efficiency close to 100% (Figure
1L,M
). Finally, the performance of WGA‐coupled magnetic beads was evaluated using conditioned medium from HPC cells. Consistent with the results in urine, the beads effectively captured EVs from cell culture supernatants (Figure
S2
).
In conclusion, WGA‐coupled magnetic beads provide an innovative, user‐friendly and highly efficient approach for EV capture from both urine and cell culture media, enabling reliable downstream quantitative analyses.
3.3
Proteomic Analysis of uEVs Isolated Using WGA‐Coupled Magnetic Beads
To investigate the proteomic profile of uEVs as potential diagnostic biomarkers for DN, a total of 58 urine samples were collected. These included samples from 10 healthy controls (NC), 10 patients with DN, 10 patients with diabetes mellitus (DM), 19 patients with non‐diabetic renal diseases (NDRD)‐comprising 10 with renal cell carcinoma (RCC), three with focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS), three with membranous nephropathy (MN) and three with IgA nephropathy (IgAN)‐as well as nine patients with NDRD and concurrent DM (NDRD‐DM), including three each of FSGS‐DM, MN‐DM and IgAN‐DM. Detailed demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants were summarized in Table

. The protein content of uEVs from each of the 58 individuals was profiled using four‐dimensional data‐independent acquisition (4D‐DIA) proteomics, resulting in the identification of approximately 3000 proteins per sample (Table

, Table

3.5
Detect CKAP4 in the uEVs by Flow Cytometry
Quantitative analysis of CKAP4 in uEVs was conducted using WGA‐coupled magnetic beads in combination with flow cytometry as previous reports (Yang et al.
2024
; Huang et al.
2024
). To assess the platform's efficiency in capturing and detecting CKAP4‐positive uEVs, serial dilution experiments were performed using standardized CKAP4‐positive uEVs isolated from DN patient samples, suspended in 1 mL of EV‐depleted urine. The diluted uEVs were captured with WGA‐coupled magnetic beads and analyzed by both Western blotting and flow cytometry. As shown in Figure
S8A
, bead‐based capture recovered more than 95% of the EVs when compared with ultracentrifugation (used as the 100% benchmark). Flow cytometric analysis using antibodies against CD63 and CKAP4 demonstrated a dose‐dependent increase in signal intensity, correlating with the concentration of CKAP4‐positive uEVs (Figure
S8B
). To further validate the platform's applicability, varying volumes of conditioned medium were analyzed. Western blotting revealed proportional increases in ALIX and CKAP4 levels with increasing sample volume (Figure
4H
), consistent with flow cytometry results showing corresponding elevations in CD63 and CKAP4 signals (Figure
4I
). Notably, a significant increase in CKAP4 signal was detected in EVs derived from HG‐treated HPC culture medium (Figure
4G
), further confirmed by flow cytometry (Figure
4J
). Finally, the platform was applied to urine samples of varying volumes. Western blot analysis demonstrated a proportional increase in ALIX and CKAP4 protein levels (Figure
4K
), while flow cytometry confirmed corresponding increases in CD63 and CKAP4 signals (Figure
4L
). Collectively, these results demonstrate that WGA‐coupled magnetic beads combined with flow cytometry offer a robust and quantitative method for detecting CKAP4 in EVs across multiple biofluids.

3.7
CKAP4 in uEVs as a Non‐Invasive Diagnostic Biomarker for DN
Early diagnosis of DN is critical for timely intervention and improved prognosis. Proteomic and Western blot analyses revealed that CKAP4 was significantly enriched in uEVs from DN patients but undetectable in uEVs from healthy individuals and patients with non‐diabetic renal diseases (Figures
3A
and
4A
). To further investigate the diagnostic potential of CKAP4 in uEVs, we analyzed urine samples from a sex‐ and age‐matched cohort comprising 132 DN, 187 DM, 163 NDRD, 86 NDRD‐DM and 81 healthy controls. CKAP4‐positive uEVs were captured using WGA‐coupled magnetic beads and quantified by flow cytometry. As shown in Figure
S10
, both CD63 (an EV marker) and CKAP4 were detected in uEVs from DN patients, with approximately 30% of DN‐derived uEVs exhibiting CKAP4 positivity. No significant association was observed between CKAP4 levels in uEVs in either sex (
p
= 0.4090) or age (
R
2
= 0.0030;
p
= 0.5893). CKAP4 levels in uEVs were significantly elevated in DN patients compared to those with DM, NDRD, NDRD‐DM and healthy controls (Figure
7A
). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis demonstrated excellent discriminatory power of CKAP4 in uEVs for DN diagnosis. The area under the curve (AUC) for DN versus healthy controls was 0.9998, with 98.77% sensitivity and 100% specificity. For DN versus DM, the AUC was 0.9859 (95.72% sensitivity, 99.24% specificity), for DN versus NDRD, the AUC was 0.9883 (94.48% sensitivity, 100% specificity) (Figure
7B
and Table
2
). Currently, DN diagnosis relies on clinical assessment based on a prolonged history of diabetes, albuminuria and/or reduced estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), while excluding other causes of kidney injury. However, comorbid conditions such as NDRD‐DM—where primary renal diseases IgAN, MN or FSGS coexist with diabetes—can complicate diagnosis. Differentiating DN from NDRD‐DM is essential due to their differing pathogenesis, treatment strategies, progression and prognosis. Although renal biopsy remains the gold standard for distinguishing between DN and NDRD‐DM, it is invasive, expensive, labor‐intensive and technically demanding. We therefore examined CKAP4 levels in uEVs from DN and NDRD‐DM patients. CKAP4 expression was significantly higher in the DN group (Figure
7A
), with an AUC of 0.996, sensitivity of 100% and specificity of 98.48% (Figure
7B
).

DN often progresses silently and is typically not diagnosed until Stage 3, when irreversible damage has already occurred. At this stage, proteinuria is detectable, but therapeutic options are limited. Currently used clinical markers, including urinary albumin and the albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio (ACR), have poor sensitivity for detecting DN in its early stages (Stages 1 and 2), when albumin levels may still be normal. Hence, there is a pressing need to identify sensitive biomarkers for early detection and risk stratification of DN. We found that CKAP4 levels in uEVs were significantly elevated in early‐stage DN (Stages 1 and 2) compared to DM controls and were even higher in late‐stage DN (Stages 3 and 4) (Figure

Prior to biopsy, urine samples were collected for quantification of CKAP4 levels in uEVs. At baseline, all eight patients exhibited preserved renal function, with normal estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR; 90–120 mL/min/1.73 m

Therefore, CKAP4 in uEVs represents a promising tool for the early detection and monitoring of DN progression. However, larger longitudinal studies are warranted to validate these findings.
3.8
CKAP4 in EVs Derived From High Glucose‐Treated Podocytes Promotes Vascular Calcification
CKAP4 is an endoplasmic reticulum protein that localizes to the plasma membrane and can regulate the YAP signalling pathway, thereby upregulating the expression of osteogenic transcription factors MSX2 and Runx2, leading to calcium influx and vascular calcification (VC) (Shi et al.
2022
). It is hypothesized that under HG conditions, podocytes secrete EVs containing CKAP4, which are subsequently internalized by vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs), activating the YAP pathway and promoting VC.
To investigate this mechanism, EVs from HG‐treated podocytes were labelled with the fluorescent dye 1,1′‐dioctadecyl‐3,3,3′,3′‐tetramethylindotricarbocyanine perchlorate (Dil) and co‐cultured with VSMCs. Confocal imaging revealed diffuse red fluorescence around the nuclei of VSMCs, indicating effective EV internalization (Figure

). Western blot analysis confirmed that CKAP4 protein was successfully transferred into VSMCs via EVs from HG‐treated podocytes (Figure
9B
), while CKAP4 mRNA levels in recipient VSMCs remained unchanged (Figure
9C
), suggesting protein transfer rather than transcriptional upregulation.

This highlights a novel intercellular mechanism by which diabetic conditions may contribute to vascular complications.
4
Discussion
DN is one of the most common and severe complications of DM, affecting 20%–50% of patients and representing a leading cause of chronic kidney disease and end‐stage kidney disease (Fineberg et al.
2013
). DN follows a well‐defined clinical and pathological trajectory that begins with glomerular hyperfiltration and renal hypertrophy, progresses through microalbuminuria and glomerular basement membrane thickening, and culminates in overt kidney disease characterized by macroalbuminuria, hypertension, tubulointerstitial fibrosis, and glomerulosclerosis (Fineberg et al.
2013
). At advanced stages, patients experience a marked decline in eGFR and persistent proteinuria, often necessitating dialysis or kidney transplantation (Fineberg et al.
2013
). Although renal biopsy remains the diagnostic gold standard, clinicians typically rely on eGFR and urinary albumin for disease staging. However, these parameters are limited in sensitivity and specificity—particularly during the early stages of DN‐underscoring the urgent need for non‐invasive biomarkers capable of detecting renal injury before irreversible structural damage occurs.
Urine represents an ideal biological fluid for biomarker discovery because it can be collected non‐invasively and reflects kidney function directly (Vlahou and Vanholder
2025
; Canki et al.
2024
). uEVs, which encapsulate proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids shed from kidney and urinary tract cells, provide a rich source of molecular information that may not be captured by whole urine analysis (Droste et al.
2025
; Erdbrugger et al.
2021
; Merchant et al.
2017
; Vitorino et al.
2021
). Several uEV proteins, such as alpha‐1‐microglobulin/bikunin precursor, full‐length megalin and dipeptidyl peptidase‐4, have been implicated as potential indicators of DN (Barr et al.
2024
; Raimondo et al.
2013
; Zubiri et al.
2014
). However, existing EV isolation methods are time‐consuming, costly and not easily scalable for clinical use, limiting their translational potential. To address these challenges, we developed an efficient and clinically compatible workflow for uEV isolation and protein analysis using WGA‐coupled magnetic beads combined with flow cytometry. This approach enables rapid and reproducible enrichment of glycosylated EVs from small urine volumes. Tamm–Horsfall protein (THP or uromodulin) is the most abundant urinary glycoprotein and a major contaminant in uEV analyses, we pretreated samples with Cibacron Blue–Agarose beads to deplete THP before EV isolation. Quantitative analysis confirmed that less than 1% of total urinary THP remained in the EV fraction, indicating efficient removal (Figures
1B,E
and
S13A,B
). Nonetheless, low levels of THP were still detectable in isolated uEVs, consistent with previous reports (Barr et al.
2024
). Whether this residual THP represents minor contamination or selective incorporation into EVs by tubular epithelial cells remains unclear and warrants further mechanistic investigation.
Using the WGA‐coupled magnetic beads, we profiled the uEV proteome in healthy individuals and patients with various kidney diseases, revealing a consistent pattern of differentially expressed proteins associated with DN. Among these, CKAP4 emerged as a highly discriminatory marker. Quantitative proteomic analysis and validation in a multicenter cohort demonstrated that uEV‐associated CKAP4 exhibited exceptional diagnostic accuracy, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.9998 for distinguishing DN from healthy controls and 0.9859 for DN versus non‐diabetic renal disease (NDRD). These findings indicate that CKAP4 may serve as a sensitive and specific biomarker for differentiating DN from other proteinuric kidney disorders in diabetic patients.
uEVs originate from multiple cellular sources, including the kidney, bladder and immune system (Vitorino et al.
2021
). Under pathological conditions, disruption of the glomerular filtration barrier can allow EVs from the systemic circulation to enter the urine, enriching the vesicular pool with disease‐specific molecular information. Consequently, uEVs may provide insights into renal pathology sufficient to complement or even reduce the need for invasive renal biopsy. In our study, CKAP4 levels in the uEVs correlated positively with glomerulosclerosis (
R
2
= 0.73) and interstitial fibrosis/tubular atrophy (IFTA) scores (
R
2
= 0.62) in biopsy‐proven DN cases. These correlations suggest that CKAP4 in the uEVs not only reflects podocyte injury but also captures fibrotic remodelling and chronic kidney damage, thereby extending its utility beyond diagnosis to disease staging and prognostic assessment.
CKAP4 is a type II transmembrane protein that predominantly resides in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), where it functions as a structural spacer protein and participates in intracellular signalling (Shi et al.
2022
). Previous studies have reported that CKAP4 interacts with Dicer in the ER to stabilize the RNA silencing complex (Pepin et al.
2012
). However, our proteomic data revealed that Dicer was absent in uEVs (Table
S4
), suggesting that CKAP4 may be selectively incorporated into vesicles under diabetic conditions. This selective packaging likely reflects podocyte membrane stress or altered vesicular trafficking rather than direct involvement in microRNA processing. By Western blotting, we confirmed that other canonical ER proteins‐such as Calnexin and GRP94, were absent from uEVs. However, multiple ER‐associated proteins were identified in the uEVs with variations in their types and abundances across different disease conditions by proteomic analysis (Table
S7
and Figure
S13C
). These findings indicate that ER‐related proteins may be selectively sorted into EVs and secreted under specific physiological or pathological conditions (Ferreira et al.
2022
).
Our in vitro and clinical data indicate that CKAP4 upregulation is a DN‐specific event. CKAP4 expression increased in podocytes exposed to high glucose, yet remained unchanged in renal tubular epithelial cells, underscoring its podocyte specificity. In patient cohorts, CKAP4 was markedly elevated in uEVs and kidney tissues from DN patients but remained at baseline in diabetic patients without nephropathy and in those with diabetes complicated by other renal diseases such as MN, IgAN or FSGS. Together, these findings suggest that CKAP4 upregulation is not a general feature of hyperglycaemia but a specific molecular signature of DN.
Beyond its diagnostic value, CKAP4 may also contribute to disease pathogenesis through intercellular communication. Consistent with the finding of Zhao et al., integrated transcriptomic and proteomic analyses of glomeruli isolated from 50 biopsy‐proven diabetic nephropathy kidneys and 25 healthy controls revealed no significant difference in CKAP4 mRNA expression (
p
= 0.4616), whereas CKAP4 protein levels were significantly increased (fold change = 1.89,
p
< 0.001) (Zhao et al.
2023
). We also found that CKAP4 was significantly upregulated in the podocytes of DN kidneys (Figures
4B,C
and
S6
). Moreover, immunofluorescence staining also showed a gradual increase in renal CKAP4 expression of murine models with diabetic nephropathy induced by STZ or
db/db
mice (Figures
5F
and
6F
). However, these results contrast with those of Boi et al., who reported reduced CKAP4 mRNA expression in podocytes from diabetic kidney disease tissues (Boi et al.
2025
). We speculated that several factors may explain this discrepancy. First, while their study assessed mRNA levels using FISH, we examined protein abundance by immunofluorescence and proteomics. Post‐transcriptional regulation and protein stability often lead to discordance between mRNA and protein levels. Second, Boi et al. focused on CKAP4 loss‐of‐function models in zebrafish and cultured podocytes, demonstrating that CKAP4 deficiency impairs cytoskeletal organization and podocyte integrity (Boi et al.
2025
). We propose that CKAP4 might play dual roles: under physiological conditions, it maintains podocyte structure, whereas under diabetic stress, its upregulation represents a compensatory or stress‐induced response. Excess CKAP4 may then be released via EVs to mediate paracrine signalling, contributing to disease progression. Thus, the functional role of CKAP4 in DN likely depends on disease context and expression dynamics, a hypothesis that merits further in vivo validation.
Recent studies have shown that CKAP4 is upregulated in VSMCs during CKD, where it promotes vascular calcification via activation of YAP signalling and calcium influx (Shi et al.
2022
). In DN, vascular calcification develops early due to hyperglycaemia‐induced endothelial dysfunction and inflammation, leading VSMCs to adopt osteoblast‐like phenotypes. Our findings provide new mechanistic insight that high‐glucose‐stimulated podocytes secrete EVs enriched in CKAP4, which are internalized by VSMCs, activating YAP signalling and inducing the expression of osteogenic factors MSX2 and Runx2. This process enhances calcium deposition and vascular calcification. Unlike traditional mechanisms driven by mineral imbalance, this EV‐mediated pathway represents a novel mode of kidney–vascular crosstalk that may exacerbate DN progression. Targeting CKAP4‐containing EVs could therefore offer new therapeutic opportunities to mitigate vascular complications in diabetic nephropathy.
4.1
Limitations
Despite its strengths, this study has several limitations. First, the prospective cohort size was relatively modest, which may limit generalizability. Second, biopsy‐confirmed DN cases remain scarce, constraining our ability to evaluate CKAP4 expression across different pathological stages. Third, all animal experiments are conducted in male mice to minimize hormonal variability; however, sex‐related differences in EV composition and renal injury are increasingly recognized and warrant further study. Additionally, while independent discovery and validation cohorts were included, larger multicenter studies across ethnically diverse populations are necessary to confirm the robustness of CKAP4 as a biomarker. Finally, the precise cellular origin of all uEV proteins could not be fully resolved due to limited proteomic depth. Future studies integrating single‐cell transcriptomics with EV proteomics and tissue‐specific EV mapping will be valuable in addressing this question.
5
Conclusion
In summary, this study identifies CKAP4 in urinary extracellular vesicles as a novel and highly specific biomarker for the early, non‐invasive diagnosis of diabetic nephropathy. The integration of WGA‐coupled magnetic bead isolation with advanced analytical technologies‐including flow cytometry and data‐independent acquisition (DIA) proteomics‐enabled comprehensive profiling of the uEV proteome and the discovery of CKAP4 as a robust diagnostic indicator. Our findings not only validate CKAP4 as a potential diagnostic and prognostic marker but also reveal its functional role in mediating vascular calcification via EV‐mediated signalling. Collectively, this work highlights the translational potential of uEV‐based biomarkers in advancing early diagnosis, risk stratification and precision management of diabetic nephropathy.
