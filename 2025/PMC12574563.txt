Introduction
Living organisms are constantly challenged by environmental agents and endogenous stress. In eukaryotic cells, these stress stimuli can trigger a common adaptive pathway, the integrated stress response (ISR), to either restore cellular homeostasis or promote cell death depending on the severity and duration of the stress [
1
]. A key event in the ISR is the phosphorylation of eukaryotic translation initiation factor 2 alpha (eIF2α) by a family of serine/threonine eIF2α kinases. In mammals, these kinases include general control non-derepressible-2 (GCN2), protein kinase double-stranded RNA-dependent (PKR), PKR-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase (PERK), and heme-regulated inhibitor (HRI), each of which can sense distinct stress signals [
2
]. Such signals include amino acid starvation (GCN2), endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress (PERK), viral infection (PKR), and heme deficiency (HRI) [
3
,
4
]. In addition to their distinct responses, these kinases also display functional overlap or redundancy in response to certain stressors, including viral infection, nucleofection, and oxidative, heat, or ER stress [
1
,
3
,
4
]. Activation of these kinases converges on phosphorylation of eIF2α, which blocks the guanine nucleotide exchange factor eIF2B that regenerates eIF2-GTP from eIF2-GDP for re-initiation of translation, resulting in global attenuation of 5’cap-dependent protein synthesis [
1
,
5
].
Despite a reduction in global protein translation by phospho-eIF2α, the translation of mRNAs that contain short upstream open reading frames (uORFs) in their 5’ untranslated region (5’UTR) is selectively upregulated [
4
]. One such mRNA encodes activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4), a basic leucine zipper (bZIP) transcription factor. In fact, ATF4 upregulation is a hallmark of the ISR, and this protein serves as a key deciding factor in cellular fate upon ISR activation [
1
]. Mammalian ATF4 mRNA contains an uORF that overlaps the coding ORF of ATF4. Under unstressed conditions when eIF2 ternary complex levels are high, translation of the uORF in ATF4 mRNA is initiated and the coding ORF is bypassed by the elongating ribosome. Upon activation of the ISR, eIF2α is phosphorylated and eIF2 concentrations are reduced, which causes the uORF to be bypassed, allowing translation initiation from the coding ORF and the upregulation of ATF4 protein expression. As a transcription factor, ATF4 upregulates the expression of its target genes, and which genes it upregulates contribute to two contrasting outcomes of the ISR in cells: survival or apoptosis. The precise mechanisms responsible for determining which target genes are expressed remain largely unknown, although combinations of ATF4 heterodimerization partners, post-translational modifications, and histone modifications have been linked to this regulation [
3
].
Pertinent to our current research are the roles of the ISR in the host response to pathogen infection. Knowledge regarding such roles has been largely gained from studies on viral infection [
4
,
6
]. Infection with RNA viruses triggers the ISR through PERK/PKR-mediated phosphorylation of eIF2α, which, in turn, inhibits overall protein translation. This includes the translation required for viral replication, thus effectively protecting the host from the infection. However, viruses can escape or even subvert the host ISR to promote viral replication and persistence during infection [
7
,
8
]. Following exposure to bacterial pathogens, the host ISR can be activated by infection-induced cellular alterations called bacterial pathogenesis patterns (BPPs), which include bacterial growth inside the host, access to the cytosol, disruption of the cytoskeleton, and protein aggregation [
4
]. In mammals, all four eIF2α kinases have been implicated in sensing one or more BPPs, with GCN2 being the most versatile. ISR activation modulates the transcription of key genes, the translation of new proteins, and the activation of cell autonomous antimicrobial mechanisms, such as autophagy, to restore cellular homeostasis or, in the case of prolonged or severe infection, promote cell death signaling [
4
]. For example, in
Drosophila
, the GCN2-ATF4 pathway boosts innate immunity against the non-lethal bacterial pathogen
Ecc15
, whereas GCN2 inhibits immune responses to the severe pathogen
Pseudomonas entomophila
[
9
,
10
]. Overall, the ISR is regarded as an important component of cell autonomous anti-bacterial responses, but the mechanisms underlying its involvement remain unclear.
Over the past two decades, the nematode
Caenorhabditis elegans
has become an excellent model organism for studying host–pathogen interactions [
11
,
12
]. As a free-living worm found in soil and decaying organic matter,
C. elegans
feeds on bacteria and is naturally exposed to surrounding pathogenic microbes. Although the bacterial infection-induced ISR has not been studied in
C. elegans
, ISR signaling is conserved in the worm and has become a focus of recent research [
13
]. The worm genome encodes two eIF-2α kinases, GCN-2 and PEK-1 (PERK), that phosphorylate eIF-2α at S49. This serine is equivalent to S51 of mammalian eIF2α, which is phosphorylated during the ISR [
14
]. While GCN-2 mediates mitochondrial stress in
C. elegans
, PEK-1 activates the worm ISR in response to endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, resulting in the upregulation of ATF-4 (previously named ATF-5) expression and the promotion of survival [
13
,
15–17
]. Interestingly, recent aging research using
C. elegans
revealed essential roles of the ISR and ATF-4 in longevity regulation under stressed conditions [
18–20
]. These results are not surprising given the intrinsic connections between stress resistance and longevity [
21
,
22
]. Because
C. elegans
is such an advantageous model for host–pathogen interactions (i.e. fast growth, short life cycle, transparent body, ease of cultivation, infection, and genetic manipulation), it is an ideal system to investigate the regulatory mechanisms and functional significance of the bacterial infection-induced ISR.
In the current study, we explored the bacterial infection-induced ISR in
C. elegans
. We found that infection with
Salmonella enterica
induced the GCN-2/eIF-2α/ATF-4 signaling pathway, and that this pathway is involved in
C. elegans
defense against the infection. Furthermore, our RNA sequencing analysis and functional assays showed that ATF-4 suppressed the expression of ribosomal protein genes in response to
S. enterica
exposure, and that such suppression reduced worm survival against the pathogen. Because ribosomal proteins are directly involved in protein translation, our data revealed an important, novel mechanism for the reduction of global translation under stress via ATF-4 mediated inhibition of ribosomal protein expression. We also found that ATF-4 inhibited components of mitochondrial electron transport in response to
S. enterica
infection, as determined by both RNA-seq and proteomics analyses, likely functioning to dampen infection-induced production of reactive oxygen species (ROS). Moreover, loss of ATF-4 altered the expression of genes encoding collagens, a group of proteins that are tightly linked to stress resistance and longevity [
23–25
]. Overall, our findings show how ATF-4 determines cell fate in the face of bacterial infection, providing a mechanistic understanding of how the bacterial infection-induced ISR impacts host physiology.

Survival assays
Survival assays were performed following a previously published methodology [
27
]. In brief, well-fed gravid adults were placed onto fresh
E. coli
OP50 NGM plates and incubated at 25°C for 45 minutes. After this incubation, the adult worms were removed, and the laid eggs were allowed to hatch and develop at 20°C for 65 hours. Bacterial lawns were prepared by culturing bacteria in LB broth (or BHI broth in the case of
E. faecalis
) from a single colony streak for 15 ~ 16 hours at 37°C in a shaking incubator. A 30 µL drop of fresh bacterial culture was seeded onto a 3.5 cm NGM plate. Plates were incubated at 37°C for 15 ~ 16 hours and cooled to room temperature before synchronized, 65-hour-old animals were transferred to the bacterial plates. The assays were performed at either 20°C for
E. coli
OP50 or 25°C for pathogenic bacteria. Live animals were transferred to fresh plates daily until egg laying stopped. Each day, animals were assessed and deemed dead if they did not respond to touch.
Heat-killed bacteria survival assays
Synchronized animals were prepared following the previously described methods [
27
]. Liquid bacterial cultures (
E. coli
OP50 or
S. enterica
) were prepared by inoculating 100 mL of LB broth with 1 mL of fresh liquid culture that was prepared following the previously described methods. Cultures were then incubated in a shaking incubator at 37°C for 24 hours. After 24 hours, the 100 mL cultures were concentrated down to 2 mL cultures by centrifugation at 1,100 x
g
for 10 minutes to a final 50x concentration. The concentrated bacterial slurries were then split into 1 mL aliquots and heated to 95°C for 4 hours. After 4 hours, the bacterial slurries were streaked out onto fresh LB agar plates and incubated at 37°C to confirm heat killing of the bacteria. For the assay, 30 µL of heat-killed bacterial culture was placed on a fresh NGM plate and allowed to dry for 30 minutes before adding synchronized 65-hour-old worms to the plate. The assays were performed at either 20°C for
E. coli
OP50 or 25°C for
S. enterica
. Live animals were transferred daily to fresh plates with bacteria until egg laying ceased. Animals were scored daily and were considered dead if they failed to respond to touch.
Lawn occupancy assays
Synchronized animals and bacterial plates were prepared as previously described [
27
]. Synchronized 65-hour-old animals were placed at the center of a fresh bacterial lawn and allowed to move freely across the plate. The number of animals on the bacterial lawn was counted at different timepoints. Animals that had at least half their body length on the bacterial lawn were counted as occupying the lawn. Half-body length was defined using the vulva as a midpoint anatomical marker. If the vulva past the marking line during the assay, the animal was considered to be in the square the head was facing. Dead animals were excluded from the data analysis. Results were expressed as the percentage of animals on the lawn relative to the total number of animals on the plate at each timepoint.
Profiling bacteria accumulation in the nematode intestine
Synchronized animals and bacterial plates were prepared following the previously described methods [
27
]. GFP-tagged
S. enterica
were cultured in LB medium supplemented with 50 μg/mL kanamycin to maintain selective pressure for the GFP-expressing plasmid [
27
]. Synchronized 65-hour-old animals were placed onto plates seeded with GFP-tagged
S. enterica
and incubated for 24 hours at 25°C. After this incubation, animals were moved to an empty NGM plate for 15 minutes to remove any external fluorescent bacteria. They were then transferred to a new NGM plate into a droplet of M9 buffer to further wash away any external bacteria. Animals were then anesthetized using 25 mM sodium azide and placed on a 2% agarose pad. A drop of hydrocarbon oil was placed over the animals to reduce bubbles and other artifacts before being covered with a glass coverslip. The animals were visualized with a Zeiss Axio Imager M2 stereomicroscope.
Quantification of intestinal colonization
Animals and bacterial plates were prepared as described previously [
27
]. GFP-tagged
S. enterica
were grown with kanamycin (50 µg/mL). Synchronized 65-hour-old animals were infected for 1 day. Afterward, the animals were transferred to a solution of sodium azide, ampicillin, and tetracycline to remove external bacteria. They were ground in PBS with Triton X-100. Serial dilutions of the samples were plated on LB plates with kanamycin for bacterial colony counting.
Profiling pumping rates
Synchronized animals and bacterial plates were prepared as previously described [
27
]. Synchronized 65-hour-old animals were transferred to plates seeded with bacteria and incubated for 1 day at 25°C. Pumping rates were measured by observing the contraction/relaxation cycles of the terminal pharyngeal bulb under a stereoscopic microscope. For each condition, the cycles were counted three times over 30-second intervals for 10 individual animals.

Western blotting
Gravid adult animals were lysed using a 5:2 solution of sodium hydroxide and bleach, and the released eggs were synchronized for 22 hours in S-basal liquid medium at room temperature. Synchronized L1 larval animals were transferred onto NGM plates seeded with
E
.
coli
OP50 and grown at 20°C for 48 hours until the animals had reached the L4 larval stage. The animals were collected and washed with M9 buffer before being transferred to plates containing
E
.
coli
OP50 or
S. enterica
for 24 hours at 25°C. After 24 hours, the animals were collected with M9 buffer and centrifuged at 1,100 x
g
for 3 minutes to pellet the animals. The pelleted animals were then washed three times with M9 buffer containing a 1x concentration of Halt-protease inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to remove any excess bacteria. After the third wash, animals were washed in a cold hypotonic solution (15 mM HEPES KOH, pH 7.6, 10 mM KCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 0.1 mM EDTA, and 350 mM sucrose) containing a 2x concentration of Halt-protease and phosphatase inhibitor (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After centrifugation and removal of the supernatant, the animals were transferred to a Balch homogenizer and ground with 25 passes of an 18 µm clearance ball bearing to disrupt cuticle integrity. After grinding, the homogenate was transferred to a 1.5 mL tube and placed on ice for 15 minutes to allow the larger debris to settle to the bottom of the tube. The supernatant was then transferred to a new 1.5 mL tube and the protein concentration was measured using the Qubit protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Aliquots of 10 µg were prepared in 1x LDS loading buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and stored at −80°C until further use.
Western blots were performed by heating 10 µg of total protein to 70°C for 10 minutes before loading the denatured proteins onto a Nupage 4–12% Bis-Tris Gel (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After electrophoresis, the proteins were transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane. The membrane was blocked for 1 hour in TBST with 5% skim milk at room temperature with gentle rocking. After blocking, the membrane was washed three times in TBST (5 minutes/wash) at room temperature with gentle rocking. After washing, the membrane was incubated with primary antibodies diluted in 5% BSA in TBST (dilution of 1:2000) for 16 hours at 4°C with gentle rocking. The primary antibodies used were rabbit anti-phospho-eIF2α (Ser51) (Cell Signaling, Catalog No. 9721S), rabbit anti-ATF4 (Proteintech, Catalog No. 10,835–1-AP), and mouse anti-tubulin alpha antibody (AA43) developed by Walsh, C (obtained from the Developmental Studies Hybridoma Bank at the University of Iowa, Department of Biology). After the primary antibody incubation, the membrane was washed three more times with TBST. Next, the membrane was incubated with a secondary HRP-conjugated antibody (goat anti-rabbit IgG (H+L), Promega, Catalog No. W4011; goat anti-mouse IgG(H+L), Invitrogen, Catalog No. 31,430) in TBST with 5% BSA for 1 hour at room temperature with gentle rocking. After the secondary antibody incubation, the membrane was washed three more times with TBST. The membrane was finally incubated in Pierce ECL western blot substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 30 seconds before being imaged on an iBright 1500 (Thermo Fisher Scientific).
Quantification and statistical analysis
Survival curves were generated using GraphPad PRISM (version 10) software. Survival was considered significantly different from the control when
p
< 0.05. PRISM utilizes the product limit or Kaplan–Meier method to calculate survival fractions and the log-rank test, which is equivalent to the Mantel–Haenszel test, for comparing survival curves. Occupancy assays, CFU assays, GFP fluorescence, and qRT-PCR results were analyzed using two-sample
t
-tests for independent samples; with
p
-values <0.05 considered statistically significant. All experiments were performed in triplicate, unless otherwise specified. Statistical information for each figure is provided in the corresponding figure legend or Table S3.
Results
Functional loss of ATF-4 enhances
C. elegans
survival against certain bacterial pathogens
ATF4 is a key deciding factor in cellular fate upon ISR activation, either restoring cellular homeostasis or promoting cell death [
1
]. However, the precise mechanisms underlying such regulation remain largely unclear. In our investigation of ISR mechanisms in
C. elegans
, we observed that
atf-4
null animals (
atf-4
(
ok576
) strain) had a longer lifespan than wild-type (WT)
N2
animals when fed the standard laboratory worm food
Escherichia coli
strain OP50 (representative survival curves are shown in
Figure 1(A)
; the statistical analyses of all survival assays are provided in Table S3). However, when fed heat-killed
E. coli
OP50, both
atf-4
(
ok576
) and WT animals displayed comparable lifespans (
Figure 1(B)
). Although as worm food,
E. coli OP50
is generally considered nonpathogenic, it can exhibit pathogenicity in
C. elegans
under certain conditions, such as during aging or immune deficiency, and can colonize the intestine in old worms [
29
,
30
]. We hypothesized that while functional loss of ATF-4 does not impact the basic lifespan of
C. elegans
, as in the case of heat-killed OP50, it may influence worm defense against bacterial pathogenicity. To test this, we exposed WT and
atf-4
(
ok576
) animals to six paradigmatic pathogens that included three Gram-negative bacteria (
Salmonella enterica strain
SL1344,
Pseudomonas aeruginosa
strain PA14, and
Serratia marcescens
strain DB11) and three Gram-positive bacteria (
Enterococcus faecalis
strain OG1RF,
Staphylococcus aureus
strain MSSA476, and
Streptococcus pneumoniae
strain D39). Compared with WT animals,
atf-4(ok576)
mutants exhibited enhanced survival against
S. enterica
and
S. marcescens
but showed no significant differences in survival against the other pathogens tested (
Figures 1(C)
and S1). Given that different pathogens employ diverse virulence strategies, ATF-4 may contribute to defense against certain bacterial species but not others. We therefore focused our investigation on
C. elegans
defense against
S. enterica
, a clinically important pathogen responsible for infections in humans and animals, including foodborne illness [
31
]. Because
atf-4(ok576)
mutants were more resistant to live
S. enterica
, we next asked whether this effect would persist with heat-killed bacteria. Similar to the results observed with heat-killed
E. coli
OP50,
atf-4(ok576)
animals exhibited a lifespan comparable to WT animals when exposed to heat-killed
S. enterica
(
Figure 1(D)
), indicating that the
atf-4
mutation affects defense against actively pathogenic bacteria without altering baseline lifespan. To determine whether the enhanced survival of
atf-4
mutants against infection was allele specific, we employed RNAi to knock down
atf-4
expression in WT animals and subjected them to survival assays with live
S. enterica
. RNAi of the
atf-4
gene knocked down its expression by about 40% (Figure S2). Our survival assays showed that RNAi-treated animals had significantly higher survival rates than control animals (
Figure 1(E)
), confirming that the enhanced survival phenotype caused by the
atf-4
mutation is not allele specific. Collectively, these findings suggest that ATF-4 either suppresses worm survival or promotes worm death in response to certain bacterial infection.

Pathogen avoidance behavior does not contribute to the enhanced survival of
atf-4(ok576)
animals exposed to
S. enterica
The enhanced survival of
atf-4(ok576)
animals against
S. enterica
could result from an improved pathogen avoidance behavior, reduced pathogen intake or accumulation, or an altered stress response. We systemically examined these possibilities to identify the cause of the survival phenotype in
atf-4

32–34

atf-4(ok576)
animals, we examined their survival against
S. enterica
-mediated killing using full-lawn assays, where NGM agar plates were entirely covered with pathogenic bacteria to eliminate avoidance behavior. Consistent with results observed in the above-described partial-lawn assays,
atf-4(ok576)
animals exposed to
S. enterica
exhibited slower mortality compared to WT control animals (
Figure 2(A)
), suggesting that pathogen avoidance is not responsible for the survival phenotype of
atf-4
mutants.

The GCN-2/eIF-2α/ATF-4 signaling pathway is involved in
C. elegans
defense against
S. enterica
infection
Since our data showed that the enhanced survival of
atf-4(ok576)
animals following
S. enterica
exposure was not due to enhanced pathogen avoidance, decreased pathogen intake, or reduced pathogen accumulation in the intestine, and given the established role of ATF-4 in the ISR [
1
], we next investigated whether
S. enterica
infection induces the ISR in
C. elegans
. The core event in the ISR is the phosphorylation of eIF2α by one of the eIF2α kinases [
1
]. To determine whether
S. enterica
infection activates the ISR in
C. elegans
, we employed western blot analysis to probe for phospho-eIF-2α in the lysates of WT animals with or without
S. enterica
exposure. As shown in
Figure 4(A)
(full Western blot results were provided in Figure S3), some phospho-eIF-2α was evident in control animals that were fed
E. coli
OP50, reflecting an active, basal ISR in the worm. This result also indicates that
E. coli
OP50 is not totally benign without any pathogenicity to the worm, consistent with the results of our survival assays (
Figure 1(A,B)
) and previously published studies [
29
,
30
]. However, exposure to
S. enterica
significantly increased the level of phospho-eIF-2α in WT worms (
Figure 4(A)
), indicating that
S. enterica
infection further activates the ISR beyond basal-level activation in
C. elegans
. We also examined ATF-4 expression changes in response to
S. enterica
infection at the transcriptional and protein levels using qRT-PCR and Western blotting, respectively. At the transcriptional level,
atf-4
mRNA abundance remained unchanged after 24 hours of
S. enterica
exposure (Figure S4A). Under unstressed conditions, ATF-4 protein was barely detectable (Figure S4B). However, 24 hours of
S. enterica
exposure led to a robust accumulation of ATF-4 protein, indicating translational upregulation of ATF-4 (Figure S4B). These results suggest that
S. enterica
induces ATF-4 primarily at the protein level and activates the ATF-4-mediated ISR pathway in
C. elegans
.

The
C. elegans
genome encodes two eIF-2α kinases, GCN-2 and PEK-1, that phosphorylate eIF-2α during the ISR [
14
]. To determine if these kinases are involved in
S. enterica
-induced eIF-2α signaling, we crossed the established kinase-null strains
gcn-2(ok871)
and
pek-1(ok275)
with
atf-4(ok576)
animals as well as with each other to create double and triple mutants (
C. elegans
strains generated in this study are listed in Table S1, and Oligonucleotides used for genotype verification are listed in Table S2). We then assessed these strains for their survival against
S. enterica
infection. Our results showed that, similar to
atf-4(ok576)
animals, both
gcn-2(ok871)
animals and
atf-4(ok576);gcn-2(ok871)
double mutants exhibited substantially improved survival against
S. enterica
(
Figure 4(B)
), indicating that ATF-4 and GCN-2 function in the same signaling pathway to suppress worm survival or promote worm death. In contrast, loss of PEK-1 either alone (
pek-1(ok275)
animals) or in combination with
atf-4
and/or
gcn-2
(
atf-4(ok576);pek-1(ok275)
double mutants and
atf-4(ok576);gcn-2(ok871);pek-1(ok275)
triple mutants) had no impact on worm survival, indicating that PEK-1 is dispensable for
C. elegans
defense against
S. enterica
and is not part of the eIF-2α/ATF-4 signaling pathway (
Figure 4(C)
). Taken together, these data suggest that
S. enterica
infection activates the ISR in
C. elegans
via the GCN-2/eIF-2α/ATF-4 signaling pathway, and that this pathway suppresses worm survival or promote worm death in the face of bacterial infection. Additionally, we also showed that PEK-1 is not part of the eIF-2α/ATF-4 pathway.
ATF-4 regulates genes encoding ribosomal and collagen proteins in response to
S. enterica
infection
To gain molecular insights into how ATF-4 regulates
C. elegans
defense against bacterial infection, we employed RNA-seq to profile gene expression in
atf-4(ok576)
and WT animals with or without
S. enterica
infection. To this end, we collected four biological replicates of RNA from each of four groups of worms (
atf-4(ok576)
and WT animals with or without exposure to
S. enterica
for 24 hours) and then subjected these samples to RNA-seq analysis.
To examine how the lack of ATF-4 in
C. elegans
affects gene expression under nonpathogenic conditions, we compared the expression profiles of
atf-4(ok576)
animals with those of WT animals without infection. In total, 21,537 genes/transcripts were detected and quantified with a false discovery rate (FDR) of 5%. Among these, 43 genes were significantly upregulated and 40 were significantly downregulated in
atf-4(ok576)
animals relative to WT animals. Having so few genes exhibit changes in expression indicates that ATF-4 may play only minor roles in
C. elegans
under normal, unstressed conditions. Gene ontology (GO) analysis of the 43 upregulated genes identified four significantly enriched GO terms, all of which involve the immune/defense response (
Figure 5(A)
). Such enrichment resulted from the upregulation of 10 immune response genes (Table S4), suggesting that ATF-4 suppresses the basal expression of immune genes in WT animals. These results also indicate a role for ATF-4 in the control of immune homeostasis in
C. elegans
. GO analysis of the 40 downregulated genes identified seven enriched GO terms, with “structural constituent of cuticle” and “cellular oxidant detoxification” being the top two terms (
Figure 5(A)
). The enrichment of these terms occurred as a result of the downregulation of two genes encoding detoxification activities as well as the downregulation of five collagen genes (Table S5), which we and others have previously shown to function in the stress responses to pathogen infection, high temperatures, and oxidative stress [
23–25
]. Overall, our data suggest that ATF-4 plays a role in maintaining cellular homeostasis in
C. elegans
under normal, unstressed conditions.

ATF-4-regulated ribosomal proteins contribute to
C. elegans
defense against pathogen infection
An intriguing finding from our RNA-seq analysis was that 84 genes for ribosomal proteins were upregulated in
atf-4(ok576)
animals relative to WT animals exposed to
S. enterica
. Given the large number of ribosomal protein genes affected and the direct roles of ribosomal proteins in translation, we suspected that such expression changes would impact translation and could contribute to the enhanced survival phenotype of
atf-4(ok576)
animals. To test this notion, we used RNAi to individually knock down the expressions of the top regulated ribosomal genes,
rps-13
,
ubq-2
,
rpl-37
, and
rps-29
, and then examined the impact of such knockdown on worm survival against
S. enterica
exposure. As shown in
Figure 5(C)
, knockdown of
rps-13
or
ubq-2
by RNAi significantly reduced the survival of
atf-4(ok576)
animals, and to a lesser extent, such knockdown also reduced the survival of WT animals. These results suggest that these ribosomal proteins are required for worm defense against
S. enterica
infection, and that the induction of these genes in
atf-4
mutant animals contributed to their enhanced survival phenotype. However, RNAi of
rpl-37
or
rps-29
resulted in defective gonad development and egg laying, indicating their important roles in worm reproduction and development. Nonetheless, our data demonstrate that ATF-4-regulated ribosomal proteins play an important role in
C. elegans
defense against
S. enterica
infection, probably through controlling the global translation of proteins including those functioning in antimicrobial pathways and/or maintaining homeostasis.
ATF-4 regulates proteins involved in mitochondrial electron transport in response to
S. enterica
infection
To understand how ATF-4 regulates
C. elegans
defense at the protein level, we compared the proteomes of
atf-4(ok576)
and WT animals exposed to
S. enterica
using a label-free quantitative proteomics approach as we previously described [
28
]. Five biological replicates of each experimental condition were analyzed with high-resolution nano-HPLC tandem mass spectrometry. In total, 4,553 proteins were identified and quantified, of which 4,067 were identified with high confidence (1% FDR). Among these proteins, 60 and 150 were significantly upregulated and downregulated, respectively (Table S7). GO analysis of the upregulated proteins revealed two significantly enriched GO terms, namely “ATP synthesis coupled electron transport” and “respirasome” (
Figure 6(A)
). These enrichments resulted from the upregulation of three proteins, COX-4, NUO-3, and COX-7C, all of which are part of the mitochondrial respiratory chain complex (WormBase). These data suggest that ATF-4 might regulate ATP production or energy homeostasis in response to
S. enterica
infection. In support of this hypothesis,
S. enterica
has been reported to cause an increase in reactive oxygen species (ROS) in the intestine of
C. elegans
as part of its pathogenesis [
37
]. In non-photosynthetic cells, ROS is produced as a by-product of ATP production in the mitochondria and as a product of the oxidation of various biological molecules, such as NADPH [
38
,
39
]. Therefore, in WT animals, ATF-4 might suppress mitochondrial electron transport to reduce ROS production, which could lead to the demise of the worm. A similar GO analysis of the proteins that were downregulated in
atf-4
null animals did not yield any GO terms that were significantly enriched.

], our RNA-seq data revealed the significant enrichment of four GO terms related to mitochondrial electron transport (

), which is consistent with the above-described proteomics data. Indeed, the genes of two of the three proteins identified in our proteomics analysis,

and
cox-7C
, were detected and upregulated in our RNA-seq analysis, demonstrating that these genes are regulated at both the transcript and protein levels. In contrast, the other mitochondrial respiratory chain complex gene,
nuo-3
, is likely regulated only at the post-transcriptional level. To determine whether there is any connection between ATF-4-regulated mitochondrial electron transport and the enhanced survival phenotype of
atf-4(ok576)
animals against
S. enterica
, we used RNAi to individually knock down the expression of
cox-4
or
cox-7C

S. enterica
exposure. Our results showed that while knockdown of

expression did not affect the survival of
atf-4(ok576)
animals, knockdown of
cox-4
significantly reduced the survival of
atf-4

Figure 6(B,C)
), indicating that upregulation of genes involved in mitochondrial electron transport indeed contributed to the enhanced survival of
atf-4(ok576)
animals against
S. enterica
. Interestingly, individual knockdown of
cox-4
or
cox-7C
also slightly reduced the survival of WT worms (
Figure 6(B,C)
), indicating their involvement in the general defense against infection. Overall, our data suggest that ATF-4 regulates mitochondrial electron transport or ATP production and that this process is involved in worm defense against pathogen infection.
Discussion
In this study, we investigated the role of the ISR in host defense against bacterial infection using
C. elegans
, a model organism with evolutionarily conserved ISR signaling [
13
]. We found that functional loss of ATF-4 enhanced
C. elegans
survival against certain bacterial pathogens, indicating a key role for ATF-4 in the defense response. Specifically, we determined that the GCN-2/eIF-2α/ATF-4 signaling pathway of the ISR was activated by
S. enterica
infection, and that this pathway modulated
C. elegans
defense against the infection. Furthermore, our RNA-seq analysis and functional assays revealed that ATF-4 suppressed the expression of ribosomal protein genes in response to
S. enterica
infection, which reduced worm survival. Because ribosomal proteins are directly involved in protein translation, our data revealed an important, novel mechanism by which ATF-4 mediates the reduction of global translation under stress by inhibiting the expression of ribosomal proteins.
The
C. elegans-S. enterica
infection model provides a powerful system for dissecting conserved mechanisms of host defense, including the ISR.
S. enterica
can colonize the worm intestine, induce epithelial stress, and cause lethal infection, paralleling key aspects of mammalian enteric disease [
11
]. Our findings that ATF-4 regulates ribosomal protein expression and mitochondrial electron transport during infection (
Figure 5–6
) align with the known role of ATF4 in maintaining homeostasis in mammalian systems, underscoring the translational relevance of this model. Thus, the
C. elegans-S. enterica
model enables mechanistic insights into host–pathogen interactions that are often conserved across species.
C. elegans
defense against pathogen infection relies on several conserved immune pathways, including the p38/PMK-1 MAPK cascade, the DAF-2/insulin-like receptor pathway, the DBL-1/TGF-β signaling pathway, the unfolded protein responses (UPRs), and programmed cell death [
44
]. While these pathways are primarily associated with transcriptional control of defense genes [
45–47
], our current study suggests that the ISR, via GCN-2/eIF-2α/ATF-4, is involved in host defense at the levels of protein translation and metabolism. Although the precise interactions between ISR and canonical immune pathways remain to be defined, it is likely that these systems intersect to orchestrate a coordinated response to infection. For instance, ATF-4-mediated suppression of translation may serve to conserve energy and redirect translational resources toward PMK-1-induced immune effectors. Further studies will be needed to dissect how these stress and immune pathways are functionally connected.
An intriguing finding in our RNA-seq analysis was that a large number of ribosomal protein genes (82 genes) were slightly, but significantly, upregulated in
atf-4(ok576)
animals relative to WT animals upon
S. enterica
infection. The changes in expression ranged from 1.1- to 1.32-fold (
Table 1
), and the changes of the top 5 regulated ribosomal protein genes were confirmed by qPCR (Figure S5). Such small changes in expression may be necessary and appropriate for restoring cellular homeostasis, as ribosomal protein genes are known to be tightly regulated, and even small shifts in their expression can have profound effects on global translation efficiency and cellular physiology [
48
]. Our subsequent functional validation supports the biological relevance of these changes. RNAi knockdown of two top-regulated ribosomal genes (
rps-13
,
ubq-2
) significantly reduced survival during infection (
Figure 5(C)
), demonstrating that these ribosomal proteins are required for worm defense against infection. Furthermore, RNAi of other two top-regulated ribosomal genes
rpl-37
and
rps-29
led to defective gonad development and egg laying, highlighting their critical roles in worm reproduction and development. These findings suggest that organisms may possess mechanisms to tightly regulate ribosomal protein expression, even under severe stress. Subtle but functionally significant modulation of ribosomal protein genes has also been reported in mammalian models [
49
,
50
]. Together, our results support the conclusion that ATF-4 coordinates a biologically meaningful translational response through fine-tuned regulation of ribosomal genes during infection.
In addition to regulating ribosomal proteins, ATF-4 also inhibited mitochondrial electron transport in response to
S. enterica
infection, as revealed by both our RNA-seq and proteomics analyses.
S. enterica
has been shown to trigger oxidative stress in
C. elegans
as part of its pathogenesis strategy by promoting ROS accumulation in the intestinal epithelium through the redox activity of bacterial thioredoxin 1 [
37
]. In our study, we found that ATF-4 suppresses components of mitochondrial electron transport – specifically COX-4 and COX-7C – during
S. enterica
infection. Since mitochondrial respiration is a major source of ROS, this downregulation likely serves to limit excessive ROS production and protect host cells from oxidative damage. However, prolonged ATF-4 activity may impair energy metabolism, explaining the enhanced survival of
atf-4
mutants during chronic infection (our survival assays were conducted by exposing worms to
S. enterica
lawns until they died). Thus, ATF-4-mediated ISR may help maintain redox balance under infection-induced stress, acting as a regulatory mechanism to dampen collateral tissue damage, but may promote host death during prolonged infection. Based on these findings, we propose that a function of the bacterial infection-induced ISR is to restore cellular homeostasis, even at the cost of survival (in the case of prolonged infection). This explains why
atf-4(ok576)
animals with a disrupted ISR lived longer than WT animals with an intact ISR when challenged with lethal
S. enterica
infection. Such effects have also been observed in
Drosophila
in which the ISR pathway increased immune responses to the non-lethal bacterial pathogen
Ecc15
but suppressed survival against the severe pathogen
P. entomophila
[
9
,
10
]. Overall, our study links ATF-4’s role in cellular fate to the balance between cellular recovery and survival.
Our study also highlights the technical complementarity of transcriptomic and proteomic approaches. While RNA-seq detected subtle yet functionally relevant changes in ribosomal and collagen gene expression, these shifts were not captured by proteomics, likely due to lower sensitivity of mass spectrometry and insolubility of collagens in the extraction protocol [
28
,
51–53
]. By contrast, both datasets converged on the regulation of mitochondrial electron transport, supporting this pathway as a robust target of ATF-4 during infection.
The ISR is a conserved mechanism that regulates protein translation and cell fate under stress, yet its role in host defense against bacterial pathogens remains poorly defined, particularly at the whole-organism level. The mechanistic insights gained here may inform our understanding of how the ISR shapes pathogen responses and infection outcomes in higher organisms and could aid in identifying novel therapeutic targets that modulate host defense or pathogen susceptibility.
