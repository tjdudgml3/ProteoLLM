Structural Characterization of the Genetically Encoded Crosslink
To determine the effect of genetically encoded crosslinking on the structural integrity of Ub‐protein complexes, we isolated and determined the X‐ray crystal structure of the covalently stapled complex of the internal UbE mutant, Ub T9C6BrK, and the most reactive UCHL1 cysteine mutant, UCHL1 Q209C. We find that the conditions from the pilot‐scale reactivity screen can be effectively translated to a higher scale reaction without any apparent loss of efficiency of adduct formation. The crosslinked adduct of UCHL1 Q209C with Ub T9C6BrK crystallized in the P 3
2
2 1 space group and diffracted to a resolution of 2.0 Å (
Figure
3
A
). The phases were obtained by molecular replacement (MR) using the previously solved UCHL1 (PDB ID: 2ETL) and Ub structure as the search model (PDB ID: 1UBQ). Inspection of the difference Fourier map (Fo–Fc) after a round of refinement following the MR revealed distinct electron density emanating from the 9th residue of Ub and extending up to Cys 209 of UCHL1. The final refined structure depicted the electron density of the C6BrK crosslink with UCHL1‐Q209C (2Fo‐Fc map contoured at 1.0 sigma, Figure
3B
). The asymmetric unit in the crystal contains two covalent complexes, where the UAA crosslink was defined by clear, interpretable density in each subunit. The conformation of the two‐residue Ub
β
‐hairpin turn incorporating the mutation was unaltered even after replacement of the
β
‐branched threonine with the lysine‐like eUAA side chain. Here, we capture the native C‐terminus of Ub within the active site, rather than a reactivity‐driven C‐terminal electrophile UCHL1 complex. Inspection of the crystal structure does not reveal appreciable electron density after Gly76 of Ub, which corresponds to the product remnant after cleavage of the C‐terminal hexa‐histidine tag, consistent with the SDS‐PAGE analysis of the reaction mixture (Figure
2
). The electron density corresponding to the G76 carboxylate is weaker than the preceding residue in the Ub chain, indicative a lack of interaction of the carboxylate with the active site residues (except with the nearby N159), indicating a more product‐departurelike state. The superposition of the crosslinked structure with the previously solved covalent complex of UCHL1 bound to Ub VME (PDB ID: 3KW5) revealed an RMSD of 0.438 Å for backbone carbon atoms over the entire complex, indicating that covalent stapling with UAAs maintains native structural features. Binding of the suicide substrate Ub VME initiates a conformational, relay orienting the misaligned active‐site residues of UCHL1 into a productive form of the cysteine protease.
[

,
68

A comparison of the catalytic triad and phenylalanine relay between the crosslinked complex reported here and the UCHL1‐Ub VME structure reveals that eUAA‐mediated crosslinking imparts minimal changes in overall structure, including little to no local distortion, while retaining important conformational changes required for enzyme activity. This demonstrates that genetically encoded Ub probes can mediate cysteine‐directed proximity‐driven crosslinking, preserving the native conformation of the interacting proteins and conformational dynamics associated with catalytic activation.

3
Conclusion
In summary, this study introduces the next series of Ub probes designed to capture transient, multivalent Ub–protein interactions by integrating genetic code expansion with mass spectrometry‐guided structural profiling. Through site‐specific incorporation of electrophilic crosslinkers, these probes capture interactions beyond the active‐site cysteine of Dubs, suggesting their potential for uncovering functionally important sites that remain unexplored by conventional Ub C‐terminal electrophiles. Exploring other sites in Dubs is crucial as they regulate enzyme activity through allosteric mechanisms. These sites offer opportunities to develop highly specific allosteric inhibitors that avoid the challenges of targeting conserved active sites. They enable selective modulation of Dub function by exploiting unique structural and regulatory features specific to each enzyme. Specifically, in the context of DUBTACs, the discovery of such sites is crucial, as developing DUBTACs requires identifying ligands that bind Dubs at noncatalytic sites to avoid interfering with their catalytic activity.
[
76
]
Moreover, allosteric activation by Ub or UBLs is emerging as a common regulatory feature of RBR E3 ligases. This is best exemplified by Parkin, where binding of phosphorylated Ub at the interface between the RING1 and IBR domains is essential for activation. Similarly, the allosteric binding site in HOIP is required for HOIP‐mediated NF‐
κ
B activation in cells. Therefore, these sites are crucial for understanding the mechanistic regulation of E3 ligase activity. Capturing Ub interactions at these sites allows delineation of their roles in allosteric activation and conformational transitions.
[
77
]
The reversible and dynamic nature of ubiquitination underlies an intricate interplay of interactions spanning a wide range from nanomolar to high millimolar affinity. Low‐affinity, transient binding sites on E2 conjugating enzymes, E3 ligases, and Dubs have been shown to exert allosteric control of catalytic activity and processivity.
[
17
,
78
,
79
–
80
]
Much of the previous work has focused on elucidating persistent Ub binding sites such as the S1 site of Dubs, whereas structural characterization of transient sites is generally beyond the traditional workflows involving complexation followed by structure determination. To circumvent the dissociation of Ub from transient binding sites during structure determination, we envision a two‐step strategy to capture Ub through covalent tethering. This involves, first, a photoactivatable Ub UAA mutant providing a map of such binding sites, then using that information to covalently staple the complex via proximity‐driven chemoselective ligation between an Ub electrophile and an engineered cysteine of the Ub binder. While photocrosslinking enables comprehensive profiling of binding interactions, the incorporation of eUAAs at the mapped sites allows for precise, site‐specific proximity‐induced ligation. However, some of the Cys mutants of UCHL1 (R202C and T205C), as mapped by bottom‐up proteomics, did not show anticipated crosslinking formation despite their proximity to the threonine 9 position of Ub, emphasizing the need to sample several mutations within the identified photo‐crosslinked peptides.
Here, we demonstrate the feasibility of an approach where the genetically encoded Ub probes facilitate proximity‐driven crosslinking through cysteine mutants of UCHL1, validating their ability to preserve native conformation and conformational dynamics when engaged with its substrate. This study further emphasizes the significance of these probes in mapping noncanonical Ub engagement and allosteric regulation, as demonstrated by mass spectrometry‐guided profiling of UCHL5's multivalent interactions. While the structure determination of UCHL5 bound to Ub at the allosteric site remains elusive, the limited reactivity of UCHL5 observed with our probes likely stems from the fact that only two cysteine mutants were tested, in contrast to the broader panel examined for UCHL1. We anticipate that screening a larger number of cysteine mutants guided by Bpa crosslinking data will help identify a more optimal incorporation site in UCHL5, potentially leading to improved probe reactivity.
As evidenced by the discovery of cryptic and nonconventional Ub binding sites such as those found in Parkin, which is required for RBR ligase activity
[
81
]
and the Anaphase–Promoting Complex,
[
82
]
these sites play essential roles in Ub‐mediated noncovalent regulation. Capturing Ub binding at sites other than the active site through non‐natural covalent tethering is a powerful strategy to uncover such cryptic and transient modes of Ub regulation. These interactions often involve surface patches like Ile44, rather than the canonical C‐terminus. By leveraging GCE to introduce chemical handles away from Ub's C‐terminus enables direct visualization of weak interactions. This provides insights into how Ub engages its binding partners, and contributes to allosteric regulation, binding specificity, and recognition mechanisms. Capturing transient Ub binding using genetically encoded probes provides significant advantages over traditional, nonspecific crosslinkers such as glutaraldehyde and auxiliary photo‐induced cross‐linkers.
[
83
]
Although glutaraldehyde has been widely employed to stabilize large protein complexes for single‐particle cryo‐EM, it lacks site specificity and can introduce extensive heterogeneity by crosslinking multiple regions indiscriminately. In contrast, our strategy enables site‐specific covalent tethering of Ub to defined residues, thereby improving sample homogeneity and enhancing the quality of 3D reconstruction. This targeted approach allows structural characterization of transient Ub interactions in a native‐like conformation, providing structural insights into alternative Ub binding modes, allosteric sites, and mechanistic details of Ub transfer.
Expanding on this strategy, the development of genetically encoded linkage‐specific diUb probes led to a Met1‐specific ABP that selectively targets the catalytic cysteine of OTULIN. Given OTULIN's role in regulating
β
‐catenin stability, which enhances cell survival and drug resistance in triple‐negative breast cancer,
[
84
]
it presents a potential therapeutic target for which no specific inhibitors have been discovered yet. While further studies are needed to quantify the inhibitory potential and assess their effects in a cellular context, these probes have the potential to function as protein‐based inhibitors that selectively target OTULIN over other Met1‐cleaving Dubs. However, challenges related to oral bioavailability and cell permeability may arise in the context of clinical translation, particularly due to the larger molecular weight and structural complexity of diUb‐based probes compared to traditional small‐molecule inhibitors. Despite these limitations, the high production yields of these probes, combined with emerging advances in protein therapeutic delivery technologies,
[
85
]
underscore their feasibility for large‐scale production and potential for applications beyond conventional in vitro studies.

A PCR amplified ubiquitin (Ub) gene fragment flanked with the NdeI and XhoI cut sites was cloned into a modified pET‐28a vector consisting of 5′‐CATATG‐3′ defining the open reading frame by restriction digest and ligation (Bioneer and New England BioLabs) to generate the pET‐28a Ub‐His. The UCHL1 gene was PCR amplified from a pGEX‐6P‐1 UCHL1 WT plasmid and cloned into pET‐28a to support a recombinant C‐terminal hexa‐histidine tag by seamless ligation. UCHL5 WT was cloned into pGEX‐6P‐1. OTULIN and RavD were cloned into pCold HisSUMO and pGEX‐6P‐1, respectively. USP5 in pET‐28a was a gift from Cheryl Arrowsmith (Addgene plasmid # 25,299;
http://n2t.net/addgene:25,299
; RRID:Addgene_25299). Met1 linear diUb was cloned into pET‐21a as tandem gene repeats. Two different plasmids were preassembled to modularly introduce mutations in Ub unit if need be. First, pRSETA with a Ub gene flanked by a 5′ end NdeI site and a 3′ SphI site was digested and ligated into a digested pET‐21a Ub‐His plasmid bearing a 5′ end cut site NdeI that defines the open reading frame and an immediate 3′ SphI site to match the diubiquitin sequence. All mutations, including TAG mutants, were made by seamless ligation (Bioneer) using forward and reverse mutant primers. Throughout this study,
E. coli

α
was used as the cloning strain.

Purification of Recombinant Proteins
Harvested cell pellets were resuspended in pH 7.4 1X PBS with 400 mM KCl and 0.5 mg mL
–1
lysozyme. The resuspension was further homogenized by several passes under high pressure using a French press. Clarified lysate was retrieved by ultracentrifugation at 75,000 × g for 1 h. Lysate from expression pellets containing hexa‐histidine fusion proteins (UCHL1‐His WT and mutants, USP2 CD, USP5, and HisSUMO‐OTULIN) was applied to 5 mL of Ni‐NTA resin (Qiagen and ThermoFisher Scientific). The resin was washed with 40 CV of pH 7.4 1X PBS with 400 mM KCl, 10 CV of the same buffer containing 30 mM imidazole, and eluted in 1X PBS 300 mM imidazole.
Lysate expressing glutathione S‐transferase fusion proteins (RavD, UCHL5 WT, and mutants) was applied to 5 mL of glutathione‐agarose resin (ThermoFisher Scientific). The resin was washed with 40 CV of pH 7.4 1X PBS with 400 mM KCl and eluted in 250 mM Tris pH 8.0 buffer containing 500 mM KCl and 10 mM reduced glutathione. Tag cleavage was carried out by the addition of GST‐tagged Precision Protease to the elution as per the manufacturer's recommendation (GE Healthcare) during two rounds of dialysis against 5 L of dialysis buffer (pH 7.4, 1X PBS with 400 mM KCl and 1 mM dithiothreitol) overnight. Tag subtraction proceeded by applying the dialyzed sample to regenerated glutathione‐Sepharose resin twice.
The final eluted and subtracted protein was concentrated using Amicon centrifugal filter units and applied to a size exclusion chromatography column (HiLoad 16/600 Superdex 75 pg) by Akta Pure FPLC (Cytiva) using 1X PBS 1 mM DTT as the mobile phase. Fractions of interest were pooled and concentrated prior to further use.
Electrophile Crosslinking Reactions and Purification of Crosslinked Proteins
The reactions of UCHL1‐His and UCHL5 cysteine mutants with Ub‐His T9 eUAA probes were carried out in a 1:2 ratio. In reaction buffer (50 mM HEPES, pH 8.0), a total of 15 μM of the Dub was incubated with 30 μM of Ub‐His probes at room temperature. Samples were taken at 4 different time points up to 18 h and were analyzed by SDS‐PAGE through Coomassie staining.
The reaction between UCHL1‐His Q209C and Ub‐HisT9C6BrK was scaled up in the reaction buffer (50 mM HEPES, pH 8.0) at a 1:1 ratio for 4 h at room temperature. The resulting crosslinked complex was purified by ion exchange chromatography using a MonoQ 5/50 GL column (Cytiva), buffer exchanged into reaction buffer with 1 mM DTT, and concentrated to 23 mg mL
–1
as determined by the BCA assay.
Purified Met1‐cleaving Dubs (OTULIN, RavD, USP2 CD, and USP5) were incubated with Met1‐diUbG76pCaaF and Met1‐diUbG76O2beY in a 1:2 ratio. In reaction buffer (50 mM HEPES, pH 8.0), a total of 15 μM of Met1 cleaving Dubs was incubated with 30 μM of Met1‐diUbG76 probes at RT. Samples were collected at 15 min time points up to an hour and were analyzed by SDS‐PAGE through Coomassie staining.

Intact Protein Mass Spectrometry
Intact protein mass spectrometry was conducted on an Orbitrap Exploris 480 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) hyphenated to a Vanquish Neo Nano Liquid Chromatography system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operating in a heated trap‐and‐elute workflow. Protein samples were prepared by dilution in 5% acetonitrile/94.8% water/0.2% formic acid to 100 ng µL
–1
and approximately loaded onto a home‐packed trap column (150 µm × 3 cm) packed with PLRP‐S resin (Agilent, 5 μm, 1000 Å) and briefly desalted. Desalted proteins were then directed to a MAbPac Capillary Reversed Phase HPLC column (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 150 µm × 15 cm). Proteins were separated by a linear binary gradient (Buffer A: 99.9% water/0.1% formic acid, Buffer B: 80% acetonitrile/4.9% water/0.1% formic acid). A 25 min linear gradient of mobile phase B starting from 20% at 1.5 min and ending at 60% at 26.5 min was applied at a flow rate of 1.5 µL min
–1
. Eluting proteins were introduced in the mass spectrometer by nanoelectrospray ionization.
Mass spectrometry data were collected using a data‐dependent acquisition approach. Full precursor scans (MS1) were collected with a scan range of 500–2000 m/z with a resolving power of 120,000 (at 200 m/z), an automatic gain control (AGC) target of 3 × 106, and 3 microscans, and source‐induced dissociation of 15 eV. The top three most abundant precursor ions from each MS1 scan were isolated with a 3 m/z window and subjected to HCD fragmentation (35% NCE) with dynamic exclusion for 60 s. Product ion scans (MS2) were obtained with a scan range of 300–2000 m/z at 60,000 resolving power (at 200 m/z), an AGC target value of 3 × 106, 3 microscans.
Mass Spectrometry Data Analysis
Acquired MS1 and MS2 spectra were deconvoluted using the built‐in Xtract algorithm provided by Freestyle 1.2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific).
