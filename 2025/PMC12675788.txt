Background & Summary
In higher eukaryotes, gene expression is tightly regulated by epigenetic mechanisms, including the incorporation of histone variants and a wide variety of histone pos-translational modifications (hPTMs). These modifications, collectively referred to as the “histone code”, modulates chromatin structure and accessibility. For example, acetylation primarily occurs at histone tails and is associated with important cellular processes such as DNA replication and repair, and RNA transcription. Methylation, on the other hand, participates in gene regulation by either promoting or inhibiting the recruitment of histone-binding proteins. It mediates the recruitment of chromodomain-containing proteins, such as members of the heterochromatin protein 1 (HP1) family, contributing to the establishment of highly structured chromatin regions, which are typically inaccessible to the transcriptional machinery

–
4
.
Trypanosomatids of medical relevance, including
Leishmania
spp
., T. cruzi
, and
T. brucei
(also known as the TriTryps), are etiological agents of neglected tropical diseases occurring worldwide, for which efficient clinical treatments are still lacking
5
.
Leishmania
spp. are unicellular parasites of the
Trypanosomatidae
family and cause an infectious condition in mammals called leishmaniasis, which manifests in three primary clinical forms: visceral (VL), cutaneous (CL), and mucocutaneous (ML). Most cases of VL occur in Brazil, east Africa and India, with an estimated 50,000 to 90,000 new cases occurring worldwide each year. Approximately 95% of CL cases occur in the Americas, the Mediterranean basin, the Middle East and central Asia, with an estimated global incidence ranging from 600,000 to 1 million new cases annually
6
.
Leishmania
spp. parasites exhibit a complex life cycle, passing through the insect vector to the mammalian host
7
. Throughout this cycle, a complex gene expression regulation is required for the parasite to adapt to the distinct environmental conditions encountered in the different hosts.
Although gene expression in the TriTryps is primarily regulated at the post-transcriptional level
8
, studies have shown that these organisms possess chromatin structures as intricate as those of higher eukaryotes, including canonical, variant and linker histones, as well as histone post-translational modifications (hPTMs), pointing to the existence of some level of epigenetic regulation in these organisms. In this context, our group has been conducting large-scale proteomic analysis to provide a comprehensive view of the hPTM landscape on trypanosomatids. For
T. brucei
and
T. cruzi
, a vast repertoire of hPTMs has been described
9
–
24
, with various studies highlighting their roles in gene regulation, chromatin remodeling, and parasite biology. In these parasites, hPTMs add an additional layer of complexity to gene regulation, with some site-specific hPTMs being associated with regulatory elements such as transcription start sites and silencing of specific gene loci
25
. For example, acetylation and methylation mark transcriptional initiation regions in
T. cruzi
17
. Also, in
T. brucei
, di- and trimethylation of histone H3 at lysine 76 (H3K76) regulate the cell cycle
26
, methylation and acetylation act as signals for the recruitment of histone-modifying enzymes involved in the regulation of variant surface glycoproteins (VSGs)
27
,
28
and variant histones and H3 trimethylation are enriched at putative transcription initiation sites
29
,
30
. Similar mechanisms have been suggested in
Leishmania major
, where histone acetylation may influence the origin of polycistronic transcription
31

Despite this progress, however, large-scale hPTM mapping in
Leishmania
spp. has remained unexplored. Studies in this genus have primarily focused on genomic approaches, such as chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP-seq), and only a few modified histone, and two modification types (acetylation and methylation), are known
31
–
36
. To date, no study has systematically applied high-resolution mass spectrometry combined with high-throughput proteomics to map hPTMs in
Leishmania
spp., and a comprehensive large-scale profile of these modifications remains unavailable, thereby limiting our understanding of the full histone modification landscape in these parasites. To address this gap, we conducted an extensive mass spectrometry-based proteomic survey of histone modifications in
L. braziliensis
(see Methods and Technical Validation for details).

Although hPTM marks generally seem to exhibit low abundance, specific loci - predominantly involving acetylation and methylation - displayed elevated detection frequency across experiments (Supplementary Table
1
). Among the most consistently detected hPTM marks was H3K73Me3, a site equivalent to H3K76 in
T. cruzi
and
T. brucei
, which has also been reported as trimethylated in these trypanosomatids
23
,
24
,
26
. Another notable region was observed in histone H2B.V, with acetylation at positions K15, K17, and K19. These observations are consistent with the overall prevalence of acetylation and methylation in our dataset, suggesting that their prominence may be linked not only to their frequency but also to their higher relative abundance.
In terms of both number and diversity, our findings are consistent with prior reports for
T. brucei
11
and
T. cruzi
24
, as well as for higher eukaryotes
4

Leishmania
species and can be used as a basis for future functional investigations and the selection of candidate targets in the development of antiparasitic epigenetic therapies.
Methods
All experimental and computational procedures - from sample preparation to data analysis - were intentionally reproduced from the step-by-step protocols our group previously established while mapping
T. cruzi
hPTMs
23
,
24
. This deliberate continuity ensures strict comparability and reproducibility across studies. Below we summarize the workflow and highlight any deviations relative to our prior reports.
Cell culture and histone enrichment
L. braziliensis
MHOM/BR/75/M2904 promastigote were cultured to log phase in biphasic NNN (Novy, MacNeal, Nicole) and Schneider medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum
37
. Histone extraction and enrichment were performed as previously described
23
,
24
, with some modifications. Briefly, 1 × 10
9
promastigote cells were collected by centrifugation (10 minutes, 5000 × g at 4 °C). Cells were lysed by resuspending the obtained pellet in 1 ml of extraction buffer A (250 mM sucrose; 1 mM EDTA; 3 mM CaCl
2
; 10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4; 0.5% (v/v) saponin; 10 mM sodium butyrate, 1x protease inhibitor cocktail [cOmplete™, Mini, EDTA-free, Roche], and 1x phosphatase inhibitor cocktail [PhosSTOP™, Roche]), followed by centrifugation (10 minutes at 6000 × g, 4 °C). The resulting pellet was washed with 1 ml of extraction buffer B (same as buffer A, without saponin) and centrifuged again (10 minutes, 6000 × g, 4 °C). The pellet, containing the nuclei, was then resuspended in 1 ml of Buffer C (1% (v/v) Triton X-100; 150 mM NaCl; 25 mM EDTA; 10 mM Tris-HCl pH 8; 10 mM sodium butyrate, 1x protease inhibitor cocktail [cOmplete™, Mini, EDTA-free, Roche] and 1x phosphatase inhibitor cocktail [PhosSTOP™, Roche]) and then centrifuged for 20 minutes at 12000 × g, 4 °C. The pellet was washed three times in 100 mM Tris-HCl pH 8, resuspended in 1 ml of 0.4 N HCl and incubated overnight on a rotator at 4 °C. Acid-soluble proteins were recovered in the supernatant after centrifugation for 15 minutes at 10000 × g, 4 °C. The supernatant was transferred to a clean tube; acetone (8 × the initial volume) was added, and the mixture was incubated overnight at −20 °C. After centrifugation (15 minutes, 3100 × g, 4 °C) acetone was carefully removed and the pellet was washed three times with 1 ml of acetone. The protein pellet was carefully dried at 37 °C and then resuspended in 50 µl of water.
Histone derivatization and digestion
Samples were processed using two different proteomic strategies (GeLC-MS/MS and LC-MS/MS) based on protocols described in our previous work
23
,
24
. For GeLC-MS/MS, histone-enriched extracts were resolved on 15% SDS-PAGE gels and stained with Coomassie blue. Histone bands were excised, destained (25 mM NH4HCO3 in 50% ethanol, shaking in a thermomixer at 800 rpm, 25 °C), derivatized with propionylation solution (propionic anhydride in 100 mM NH4HCO3, 1:10)
38
and subjected to
in-
gel digestion
39
with trypsin (sequencing modified, Promega) at a concentration of 12.5 ng/µL for 16 h at 37 °C. For LC-MS/MS, histone-enriched extracts were directly derivatized with propionylation solution (propionic anhydride in 2-propanol, 1:3) and digested
in-
solution
40
using trypsin (sequencing modified, Promega) at a protease-to-protein ratio of 1:20, for 16 h at 37 °C. Proteins were reduced with dithiothreitol (DTT) and alkylated with iodoacetamide (IAA) prior to trypsin digestion. For both proteomic strategies, the peptide digests were desalted using C18 StageTips
41
prior to nanoLC- ESI-MS/MS analysis.
NanoLC-ESI-MS/MS
Peptide mixtures were separated by online reversed-phase (RP) nanoscale capillary liquid chromatography (nanoLC) and analyzed by electrospray tandem mass spectrometry (ESI MS/MS) as previously described
24
. Experiments were conducted at the RPT02H Mass Spectrometry Facility (Carlos Chagas Institute - Fiocruz Parana), using an EASY-nLC 1000 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled to an LTQ Orbitrap XL mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with a nanoelectrospray ion source (Phoenix S&T). Chromatographic separation of the peptides was performed using a 30-cm analytical column (75 μm inner diameter, 350 μm outer diameter) packed in-house with C18 reversed-phase resin (ReproSil-Pur C18-AQ 1.9 µm, Dr. Maisch GmbH, Ammerbuch-Entringen, Germany), kept at a constant temperature of 60 °C. Solvent A consisted of 0.1% formic acid and 5% DMSO in water; solvent B consisted of 0.1% formic acid and 5% DMSO in acetonitrile. Peptide mixtures were injected onto the column and subsequently eluted with linear gradient from 5% to 40% solvent B over 128 min at a flow rate of 250 nL/min. The mass spectrometer operated in data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode to automatically switch between MS and MS/MS (MS
2
) acquisition, applying both Collision-Induced Dissociation (CID) and Higher Energy Collisional Dissociation (HCD) to the 5 most intense peptides detected in each MS spectrum. For all samples duplicate or triplicate LC-MS/MS runs were performed. Survey full scan MS spectra (at 300–1600 m/z range) were acquired in the Orbitrap analyzer at a resolution of 60,000 (at m/z 400, after accumulation to a target value of 1,000,000 in the linear ion trap), with preview scan enabled. Singly charged precursor ions were not selected for fragmentation. Former target ions selected for MS/MS were dynamically excluded for 30 seconds. The total cycle time was approximately three seconds. Other mass spectrometric conditions were spray voltage, 2.4 kV; no sheath and auxiliary gas flow; ion transfer tube temperature, 100 °C; collision gas pressure, 1.3 mTorr; normalized collision energy using wide-band activation mode 35% for MS2. The ion selection threshold was 250 counts for MS2. An activation q = 0.25 and activation time of 30 ms was applied in MS2 acquisitions. The lock mass
42
option, using DMSO peaks
43
was enabled in all full scans to improve the mass accuracy of precursor ions.
Data analysis
Samples were analysed as described in our previous publication
24
. Peptides and hPTM sites were identified using the software Peaks Studio (version 10.6, Bioinformatics Solutions Inc)
44
–
46
. Projects were configured with “mixed” fragmentation to jointly analyze spectra acquired with different fragmentation types (e.g., CID/HCD)
47
. The sequential analysis in Peaks Studio began with
de novo
sequencing fragment spectra (Peptide
De Novo
), followed by peptide sequence match of the high quality de novo tags with Peaks DB
45
, considering the most frequent modifications. Remaining unmatched high-quality
de novo
only peptide tags were then analyzed using Peaks PTM
46
. Protein identification was performed against a database containing 9,269 sequences of
L. braziliensis
MHOMBR75M2904 (downloaded on Oct 4, 2024, from TriTrypDB,
http://www.tritrypdb.org
). For all Peaks searches the precursor mass tolerance was set to 10 ppm and the fragment ion mass tolerance was set to 0.5 Da (ion trap spectra) or 10 ppm (Orbitrap spectra). The enzyme for theoretical digestion was trypsin with specific cleavage mode, allowing for four missed cleavages. For Peaks DB searches, carbamidomethylation (C) was set as a fixed modification and the following variable PTMs were considered: monomethylation (K/R), dimethylation (K/R), trimethylation (K), acetylation (K), acetylation (protein N-term), propionylation (K), propionylation (protein N-term), methylpropionylation (K) – to account for derivatization of monomethylated lysine, phosphorylation (S/T/Y) and oxidation (M). For Peaks PTM, in addition to the modifications searched in PeaksDB, the following PTMs were included: monomethylation (protein N-term), dimethylation (protein N-term), glutarylation (K), 2-hydroxyisobutyrylation (K), hydroxylation (Y), malonylation (K), succinylation (K), ubiquitination (K), crotonylation (K), citrulination (R), methylation (Q), phosphorylation (H), acetylation (S/T), formylation (K) and deamidation (N/Q). Details for all variable hPTMs evaluated in PEAKS Studio - modification name, monoisotopic mass shift, affected residues, and termini specificity - are provided in Supplementary Table
2
. For identification of both peptides and proteins, the false discovery rate (FDR) was set to 1%. PTM sites with Ascore ≥20
48
and/or 5% minimum relative fragment ion intensity (for ion pairs showing fragmentation before and after the modified amino acid)
46
,
47
, were automatically validated. The histone-fold sequence was identified through the NCBI’s Conserved Domain Database (CDD)

,

Technical Validation
Based on previous studies by our group
23
,
24
, we implemented optimized sample preparation protocols, including propionylation of proteins before trypsin cleavage to prevent overcutting of the lysine-rich histone regions (especially in the N-terminal tails), and applied two complementary mass spectrometry-based proteomic strategies - gel-based (GeLC-MS/MS) and gel-free (LC-MS/MS) - combined with high-sensitivity, high-resolution fragmentation techniques (CID and HCD), as well as
de novo
-assisted database searching (Fig.
4
). Derivatization efficiency was assessed based on lysine coverage: of the 76 lysine residues detected across histones, 62 were found in the propionylated state (~82%). Since derivatization in this dataset was performed only at the protein level, this efficiency is considered acceptable. Nevertheless, these values probably underestimate the yield that could be obtained with an additional peptide-level step, which we intend to implement in future experiments.

We report histone sequences, except for H1, relative to their mature form (missing the initial methionine) and highlight the hPTMs identified in our work (Fig.
1
). In many eukaryotic organisms the initiator methionine is removed due to methionine aminopeptidase activity, exposing the second amino acid for further modification by enzymes, which generates greater sequence diversity and impacts different cellular functions
53
,
54
. Removal of the initial methionine is well documented in
T. brucei
55
and in
T. cruzi
23
,
24
histones. However, for
Leishmania
spp. histones the information in the literature is very scarce. In
L. braziliensis
, our data supports methionine removal for H2A and H3.V (N-terminal peptides lacking Met detected), whereas H1 was observed in both forms (with and without the initiator methionine (Supplementary Table
6
). Because N-terminal coverage was not obtained for several histones, additional analyses will be required to confirm initial methionine removal across the histone complement. N-terminal acetylation was not observed for H2A or H3.V (their N-terminal peptides were identified without acetylation). For H1, we detected an N-terminal peptide bearing acetylation in the methionine-retaining form. As for initial methionine removal, better sequence coverage is still required to establish the prevalence of protein N-terminal acetylation across all histones.
As expected, this combined strategy enhanced the diversity of the peptide population obtained, increasing both protein sequence coverage and hPTM identification compared to the individual approaches. The reliability of the resulting dataset is underpinned by multiple layers of experimental rigor: (1) high-accuracy mass spectrometry enabled peptide measurement with low mass error (Supplementary Figure
1a,b
); (2) stringent search parameters were employed, maintaining a false discovery rate (FDR) of 1% for protein identification and an Ascore ≥ 20 and/or 5% minimum relative fragment ion intensity (for ion pairs showing fragmentation before and after the modified amino acid) for confident hPTM site localization, and (3) detection across multiple experimental replicates and proteomic strategies - hPTM were typically identified by several MS/MS and consistently detected across samples and experiments (Supplementary Figure
1c,d
). Overall, the number, diversity and frequency of the histone modification types and hPTM marks identified for
L. braziliensis
are consistent with those previously reported in other trypanosomatids, providing biological coherence and supporting the reliability of our findings.
Usage Notes
Notably, propionylation of monomethyl-lysine (“monomethylpropionylation”) is isobaric with isobutyrylation (+70.04186 Da). However, because isobutyrylation was not detected in control histone samples processed without the derivatization step, the +70.04186 Da features observed here most parsimoniously reflect methyl-lysine residues that became propionylated during derivatization rather than endogenous isobutyrylation. For transparency, sites inferred from methyl-propionyl assignments are flagged with an asterisk in Fig.

T. cruzi

However, because formic acid in LC mobile phases can generate artifactual formylation, additional work is needed to establish biological origin.
