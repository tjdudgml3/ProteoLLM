Introduction
Metabolomics provides an efficient approach to describe biomarkers or characterize diseases through the detection, identification, and quantification of small-molecule metabolites in biological samples [
1
]. However, the same metabolic markers can be measured by different methods, which makes correlation of quantitative data difficult. Therefore, the development of universal approaches to interpret used to characterize natural samples such as blood serum, urine, and stool is an important task. Metabolomic analysis techniques such as HPLC-MS, Gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS), and NMR heavily rely on sample preparation, the technical specifications of the equipment employed, and the standardization procedures involving internal standards [
2
]. Both GC–MS and NMR methods have gained popularity and proven their effectiveness in identifying novel biomarkers for the early detection of various diseases [
3
]. Specifically,
1
H NMR-based metabolomics is well-suited for comprehensive, simultaneous analysis of multiple metabolites, making it ideal for metabolite fingerprinting [
4
]. GC–MS is recognized for its high sensitivity and throughput and has established itself as a valuable tool for untargeted studies of primary metabolism across various applications [
5
]. When combined with headspace extraction (Headspace GC–MS), it enables direct analysis of nearly all sample types without the need for extensive preparation, regardless of their physical state [
6
]. The main requirement is that the analytes are sufficiently volatile. Volatile organic compounds (VOCs) encompass a broad spectrum of chemicals, including fatty acids, alcohols, sulfur-containing compounds, ketones, aldehydes, phenols, indole, and its derivatives [
7
]. In this study, we concentrated on the multicomponent quantification of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), given their established association with diseases such as inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). The effectiveness of headspace GC/MS for both quantitative analysis of individual compounds and complex samples has been demonstrated previously [
8–10
]. Depending on the sample volume or physical state, different vaporization techniques can be employed. The total vaporization technique offers increased sensitivity [
11
], while the full evaporation technique is advantageous when analyzing samples with strong matrix effects [
12
].
GC–MS is commonly employed for analyzing the volatile metabolome [
13
]. While the composition of SCFAs in urine samples is well characterized, most existing data were obtained using the solid-phase microextraction (SPME) technique [
14
]. Most published studies on VOC analysis are conducted using SPME [
15–17
]. This technique employs capillaries coated internally with various phases that have differing affinities for the substances in equilibrium within the sample. During this process, discrimination of the sample occurs based on composition, as different compounds within the equilibrium vapor phase are sorbed to varying degrees [
18
]. Following desorption, the metabolite’s composition and component ratios differ from the original sample. This makes microextraction highly sensitive to sample mass, since different sample weights can alter the relative sorption of components [
19
]. Due to the inability to normalize urine samples based on hydration levels, comparing different samples by component ratios becomes problematic. The use of internal standards does not fully resolve this issue, and drying the samples to determine dry weight complicates the process further. Moreover, this approach hampers effective multi-component comparative analysis. A key challenge of the HS-GC/MS approach is the complex, non-linear relationship between the compound composition in the source phase and their relative pressures in the vapor phase, which are directly measured. To address this, a thermodynamic model has been developed to reverse-transform vapor-phase relative abundances into corresponding liquid-phase concentrations, as described by Konanov
et al
. [
20
].
The HS-20 auto-sampler for equilibrium vapor, coupled with a Shimadzu gas chromatograph–mass spectrometer, can operate in two modes. The first mode involves collecting the equilibrium vapor into a loop, which is then injected into the chromatograph. The second mode entails capturing the vapor and passing it through a cooled trap, allowing multiple cycles of vapor collection and sorption into a cryo-trap. Subsequently, the trap is rapidly heated, releasing the sorbed substances into the chromatograph. This technique enables concentration of analytes, facilitating the detection of trace amounts of compounds.
Following separation on a capillary column—selected based on the physicochemical properties of the target compounds—the analytes are analyzed by mass spectrometry in full scan mode, acquiring electron ionization mass spectra. These spectra can be compared against spectral libraries for compound identification. As a result, most substances are identified within the chromatogram [
21
]. This approach allows for detailed analysis and comparison of different urine samples, enabling multi-component comparative studies and effective differentiation between the VOC metabolomes of healthy individuals and patients.
HS-GC–MS is a powerful analytical technique widely used for the analysis of volatile compounds in biological samples, including urine. Its ability to analyze complex matrices with minimal sample preparation makes it particularly suitable for metabolomic studies and biomarker discovery. This method could be particularly valuable as a diagnostic tool for patients with IBD. IBD presents a significant global challenge in gastroenterology [
22
,
23
]. The onset of inflammatory changes profoundly affects metabolic processes throughout the body. These metabolic alterations can be detected in various biological samples, including urine, through distinctive shifts in metabolite profiles [
24
]. In this study, we conducted a detailed metabolomic analysis of urine samples from IBD patients using a modified sample preparation protocol combined with an optimized headspace GC/MS method.
Materials and methods
Subjects
Urine samples from patients (age range: 45–70 years) with IBD and healthy volunteers were recruited from the Department of Gastroenterology, Lopukhin Federal Research and Clinical Center of Physical-Chemical Medicine of Federal Medical Biological Agency, Moscow, Russian Federation. All samples were collected between September 2024 and December 2024. Stool samples were collected from adult patients upon admission to the clinic during disease exacerbation. The diagnosis of Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis was based on conventional clinical, radiological, and endoscopic features and was finally confirmed by histological examination of intestinal biopsies. The study included an adult IBD group (
n
= 10) and healthy control group comprising adults (
n
= 10). The main criteria for inclusion in the healthy control group were no history of IBD, no recent antibacterial therapy, and no special dietary restrictions. Clinical characteristics of patients with IBD, including indices of disease activity and medication, are shown in
Supplementary Table S1
.
Ethics approval and consent to participate
All experimental procedures were approved by the local ethical committee of LOPUKHIN FRCC PCM, Moscow, Russian Federation, Russia. Informed consent was obtained from all subjects.
Reagents
Chromatographic grade methanol was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, USA). Pure water was obtained from Wahaha Company (Hangzhou, China). Other reagents are potassium dihydrogen orthophosphate (CDH, catalog number: 821123) and ammonium sulfate (NeoFroxx Gmbh 1333KG001, catalog number: 62E1113f).
HS-GC–MS
A volume of 1 ml of urine sample intended for analysis was placed into a 10 ml glass vial suitable for the Shimadzu HS-20 headspace extractor, equipped with a screw cap. Then, 130 mg of a salt mixture consisting of ammonium sulfate and dipotassium hydrogen phosphate in a 4:1 ratio was added. Headspace extraction parameters were set as follows: oven temperature = 80°C, sample line temperature = 220°C, transfer line temperature = 220°C, equilibration time = 15 min, pressurization time = 2 min, load time = 0.5 min, injection time = 1 min, and needle flush time = 7 min. A cooled trap mode was employed, with the trap (TenaxTA) cooled to −20°C and desorption temperature set at 200°C. The trapping cycle was repeated three times to ensure thorough sorption. The vial was sealed and analyzed using a Shimadzu QP2010 Ultra GC/MS system coupled with a Shimadzu HS-20 headspace sampler. Separation was performed on a VF-WAX MS capillary column (30 m length, 0.25 mm inner diameter, 0.25 µm film thickness). The initial column temperature was maintained at 80°C and then ramped at 20°C/min up to 240°C with a final hold of 15 min.
Carrier gas was high-purity helium (99.999%), with splitless injection mode at a flow rate of 1 ml/min. The ion source temperature was set to 230°C, and the interface temperature to 240°C. Data acquisition was performed in full scan mode (TIC). Mass spectra were identified using the NIST 2014 spectral library. To analyze the obtained mass spectra, the NIST 2014 mass spectra library with automated mass spectral deconvolution and identification system (AMDIS version 2.72) was used.
Metabolome data processing
The HS-GC–MS data were processed as follows: the peak areas calculated by AMDIS for the selected compounds were converted into relative abundances. Volatile compound percentages were estimated by summing the percentages of confidently identified compounds for each sample in the AMDIS database. The resulting values were then recalculated as a percentage of the total number of compounds confidently identified. These conversions were necessary to avoid errors in content estimation due to unreliable matrix signals caused by noise. The GC–MS data were processed using MetaboAnalyst 5.0 software (
http://www.metaboanalyst.ca
) and GraphPad Prism 8.0.1 software. The values obtained for each patient were considered paired and consistent, confirmed by the ROUT outlier test (ROUT (
Q
= 1%)). As it was not possible to assess the normality of the distribution for all sample groups, it was assumed that the raw data did not conform to a normal distribution. The non-parametric Mann–Whitney test was used for the primary comparison between groups. After natural log transformation, normalized data were analyzed using a standard
t
-test and ANOVA. Statistical significance was determined by a two-tailed
P
< .05. Unsupervised principal component analysis (PCA) was used to reduce the number of dimensions and further explore the data. Standard procedures were applied prior to PCA. A chi-squared test was used to determine a method for imputing missing values. The missing at random (MAR) criteria were identified in the data. The BPCA method was found to be the most appropriate. Normalization and scaling procedures were then performed. Correlations between metabolites and gut microbiota composition were analyzed using the R “psych” package based on Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient.
Results
Optimization of HS-GC–MS method for urine analysis
The method of headspace (HS) extraction followed by GC–MS analysis was employed to examine urine samples from patients with IBDs. To improve analytical performance, the HS-GC–MS protocol was refined to enhance peak intensity and achieve better separation of analytes. The optimization process focused on adjusting several parameters, including the oven temperature program, urine sample volume, salt concentration, and incubation temperature and duration. Since the primary aim of an untargeted metabolomic approach is to maximize information retrieval, effective peak separation within the shortest possible timeframe is essential.
We employed a protocol that had previously proven effective for analyzing stool samples. The resulting chromatograms were significantly depleted of the anticipated volatile metabolites of the metabolome. Specifically, when sampling steam with sodium chloride or a mixture of salts, it was not possible to obtain chromatogram containing compounds suitable for analysis.
A decision was made to modify the protocol to enhance its effectiveness and reliability. The following protocol modifications have been implemented to enhance analytical performance. Optimal metabolite extraction was achieved using a mixture of ammonium sulfate and dipotassium hydrogen phosphate salts in a 4:1 ratio. Headspace extraction parameters were adjusted as follows: oven temperature was raised to 80°C; equilibration time was extended to 15 min. By increasing the sample volume and adjusting the temperature regime during sample preparation, as well as optimizing the delay time before activating the mass spectrometer, we obtained chromatogram with a moderate content of volatile components. A slight improvement was observed when using a mixture of salts. However, transitioning to a cryo-trap provided the highest quality results, yielding chromatogram with a broad range of detectable volatile compounds. A cooled trap mode was employed, with the trap (TenaxTA) maintained at −20°C and desorption temperature set at 200°C. Repeating the trapping cycle three times ensured sufficient sorption of analytes (
Fig. 1A
). Notably, a significant number of volatile metabolites from various classes were detected (
Fig. 1B
).

Among detected metabolites were the following chemical groups: cyclic ketones, long-chain fatty acids, indanone derivatives, amides, and fatty polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons. The following compounds were identified: indole, phenol and its derivatives, α-cresol, phthalate ester, lauric acid, enanthic acid, caproic acid, caprylic acid, and others.
Most of the components are naturally occurring food constituents, although some may also be metabolites resulting from the biodegradation of xenobiotics.
HS-GC–MS method for IBD urine analysis
A select group of patients with IBD was chosen to evaluate the HS-GC-MS method combined with cryo-trap extraction. In the initial phase, a comprehensive assessment of reliably detected compounds was conducted across all analyzed samples. Raw metabolomic data are available in
Supplementary Table S2
. It was observed that at least 60% of the detectable metabolites were consistently present in every sample, including SCFAs, which are commonly recognized as biomarkers for IBD in stool specimens (
Fig. 1B
).
Analysis of all detectable metabolites in the control and IBD groups, conducted using principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), revealed two distinctly separated clusters of samples (
Fig. 1C
). Analysis of the consistently detectable metabolome components further demonstrated a tendency to form two separate clusters, clearly distinguishing patients with IBD from healthy controls (
Fig. 1D
). The HS-GC-MS method has proven its validity and demonstrated high reproducibility across multiple technical repetitions, establishing it as one of the most convenient and rapid approaches for identifying volatile components in urine samples from patients with IBD.
When analyzing individual differences in the spectrum of volatile metabolites, patients with IBD recorded a significantly higher number of compounds than in the control group. The notable prevalence of compounds observed in patients with IBD is primarily linked to the presence of xenobiotic degradation products (
Fig. 2A
).

However, certain compounds are more likely to originate from the metabolism of various food components, and their elevated detection in patients with IBD—compared to the control group—may be attributed to increased intestinal permeability, which facilitates greater absorption and excretion of these metabolites. When analyzing individual stable detectable compounds in the comparison between IBD patients and the control group, a consistent increase in the concentration of most metabolites was observed (
Fig. 2B
). This trend suggests an altered metabolic profile in patients with IBD, characterized by enhanced production of metabolic degradation products and increased absorption of these components through the intestine.
The analysis of statistically significant differences between the two groups identified two of the most characteristic markers for detecting IBD through urine analysis using the HS-GC-MS method. These biomarkers are 2-heptanone and pentadecane (
Fig. 2C
).
2-Heptanone, also known as methyl
n
-amyl ketone, has an unclear role in urine, and its presence remains to be fully understood. Pentadecane, a 15-carbon alkane, is detectable in urine and has been associated with certain health conditions. Notably, elevated levels of pentadecane have been observed in the urine of pediatric tuberculosis (TBM) patients. Studies indicate that pentadecane, along with other alkanes and VOCs, shows significant alterations in TBM patients compared to healthy controls, with these changes persisting throughout treatment.
Currently, a direct link between fluctuations in pentadecane concentration and the development of IBD has not been established. However, based on previously identified patterns, it is highly probable that a biological association will be uncovered in the near future.
Discussion
Urine sample analysis presents additional challenges because the volatile compounds of interest are typically present in very low concentrations, making their detection and analysis more difficult. The spectrum of VOCs encompasses a wide and diverse range of chemical compound classes. These VOCs can be classified into various groups based on their chemical properties, including alkanes, olefins, alkynes, aromatic hydrocarbons, halogenated hydrocarbons, esters, aldehydes, ketones, and others. Therefore, given the extensive range of detectable compounds, there is potential to identify not only SCFAs as markers of IBD development but also other pertinent substances.
Solid-phase microextraction coupled with GC–MS (SPME-GC–MS) is typically preferred method for urine sample analysis due to its efficiency and sensitivity in detecting volatile compounds. The application of the SPME-GC–MS method involves the sorption of substances onto the surface of a carrier. Due to the varying physical and chemical properties of individual components and the complex nature of biological samples, this approach can potentially lead to distortions or inaccuracies in the obtained results. In this context, the use of HS-GC–MS method proved to be the most optimal choice. However, it necessitated some modifications in the sample preparation process to ensure accurate and reliable results.
The implementation of a cryo-trap (TenaxTA) combined with a triple extraction technique yielded the highest quality spectrum of detectable compounds. Among these, several were consistently identified in both patients with IBD and healthy control groups, ensuring reliable comparison and analysis. Additionally, during the experiment, the concentrations of the salts added and the sample incubation times were systematically varied to optimize conditions and ensure accurate results. As a result, not only were consistently detectable compounds identified but also certain components that contributed to the clear differentiation of the IBD group from the control group. The method was demonstrated to be both valid and highly reproducible in the tested samples, making it a promising candidate for rapid analytical approaches in the non-invasive study of urine samples for patients at risk of developing IBD.
However, it is important to note that some patients with IBD are unable to provide stool samples, which are traditionally used to analyze the volatile metabolome spectrum and assess the risk of developing the condition. Therefore, utilizing urine samples represents the most optimal approach for rapid, non-invasive testing to assess the risk of developing IBD, benefiting both patients and healthcare providers.
The observed differences need to be confirmed in a larger cohort of patients. However, at present, this method serves as a promising alternative to solid-phase techniques for the analysis of volatile compounds, offering advantages in speed and simplicity.
Conclusions
The optimized HS GC/MS method enables analysis of samples with a low abundance of volatile components, such as urine samples. This optimization can be valuable for exploring new biological markers in urine, aiding in the search for potential predictors of various pathologies, particularly considering the complexity of biological systems. In some cases—such as when collecting stool samples for research—HS-GC–MS method for urine analysis appears to be the most convenient, especially for IBD studding. The observed differences in patients with IBD warrant further validation in a larger patient cohort.
