Introduction
Low O
2
levels typically exist in normal tissues of the human body, as well as in tumors. In tumors, a hypoxic microenvironment can promote tumor progression, angiogenesis, and metastasis (
Petrova
et al.
2018
). At the cellular level, it has been demonstrated that hypoxic conditions lead to the activation of hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs), a subset of transcription factors that drive the expression of genes involved in various processes, including cell survival, proliferation, and metabolic adaptation (
Semenza 2003
,
Brahimi-Horn & Pouysségur 2009
). The best-known member of the HIF family is HIF1α, which, upon exposure to hypoxia, dimerizes with the β subunit (HIF1β) and translocates to the nucleus. The dimerized and stabilized HIF1α then acts as a transcription factor favoring, among others, metastasis, immune cell evasion, resistance to chemotherapeutic drugs, and angiogenesis in cancer cells (
Wang & Semenza 1995
,
Pouysségur
et al.
2006
).
Cell culture experiments provide crucial information but, due to practical reasons, are typically performed at atmospheric conditions. However, those atmospheric conditions represent supraphysiological oxygen concentrations for most tissues (
Alva
et al.
2022
a
) and thus do not adequately mirror the
in situ
conditions. Of note, previous studies clearly showed that O
2
-related cellular responses are rather cell-type specific and therefore must be evaluated for each (tumor) cell type (
Alva
et al.
2022
b
). While such studies have been conducted in several tumor cells, to our knowledge, granulosa cell tumors (GCTs) and/or their derived cell lines have not been thoroughly studied yet.
GCTs are a subset of ovarian neoplasms that originate from the sex-cord stromal cells of the ovary and represent the most common type of sex-cord-stromal tumors (
Khosla
et al.
2014
). The KGN cell line is derived from an invasive granulosa cell (GC) carcinoma and is widely studied as a model for adult GCTs.
Of note, due to its ability to retain many characteristics of primary human GCs, including hormone responsiveness and steroidogenic activity (
Nishi
et al.
2001
), the KGN cell line is also frequently used as a cellular surrogate model for normal, non-tumorous human GCs.
Previous studies used cobalt chloride (CoCl
2
) to chemically mimic hypoxic conditions in order to study hypoxia-associated changes in KGN cells (
Nishigaki
et al.
2020
,
Liu & Li 2023
). CoCl
2
blocks the degradation of HIF1α, resulting in stabilization of HIF1α even under atmospheric conditions (
Yuan
et al.
2003
). Intact HIF1α can then enter the nucleus and act as a transcription factor. While studies employing CoCl
2
certainly provide valuable information, CoCl
2
may have consequences that are not related to hypoxia, for example, on gene transcription and cell metabolism (
Muñoz-Sánchez & Chánez-Cárdenas 2019
). Studies in which hypoxic conditions are precisely controlled by the regulation of O
2
have not been performed in KGN cells.
We hypothesized that O
2
levels may influence the cellular phenotype and the behavior of KGN cells. Given the importance of KGN cells as a cellular model for human GCTs and normal GCs, the aim of this study was to evaluate the specific consequences of a defined hypoxic condition on KGN cells. To this end, KGN cells were exposed to 1% O
2
for 10 days. Cells kept under standard atmospheric conditions served as controls. The condition of 1% O
2
was chosen because comparable O
2
conditions were reported in previous studies with other tumor cells (e.g.
Bellot
et al.
2009
). Furthermore, it resembles the physiological
in vivo
conditions in large human ovarian follicles (
Redding
et al.
2007
,
Lim
et al.
2021
), a situation we have previously studied in cultured primary human GCs (
Höfner
et al.
2023
).
We performed a set of experiments, including a proteomic analysis, evaluation of cell parameters and cell migration, and HIF1α localization studies. We also studied the angiogenic factor VEGFA (vascular endothelial growth factor A), which is regarded as one of the factors often regulated by HIF1α (
Yang
et al.
2024
). The results, which reveal the strong impact of hypoxia in KGN cells, provide a resource for future studies.
Materials and methods
Culture and collection of KGN cells
The KGN cell line, i.e. adult-type GCT-derived cells, was obtained from RIKEN BioResource Center with permission from T Yanase (
Nishi
et al.
2001
). KGN cells were handled and cultured as described (
Schmid
et al.
2021
) in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium/Nutrient Mixture F-12 (DMEM/F-12, Gibco, UK) complemented with 10% fetal calf serum (Capricorn Scientific GmbH, Germany) and 0.1 μg/mL Normocin (InvivoGen Europe, France) under standard conditions of 37°C with 5% CO
2
and 95% humidity.
For the experiments, KGN cells with passage numbers between 28 and 35 were used. While it is known that KGN cells change their phenotype and proliferation behavior after prolonged passaging (
Imai
et al.
2008
), we found stable cell doubling times in the mentioned passage range in pilot studies.
All experiments were repeated several times, as indicated in the results and figures. For a schematic outline of the experimental workflow, see Supplementary Fig. S1 (see section on
Supplementary materials
given at the end of the article). In brief, cells were, in general, seeded in equal cell numbers (1.5–2 × 10
6
per T75 flask) into two cell culture flasks (Nunc™ EasYFlask™ 75 cm
2
Nunclon Delta, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). If higher cell numbers (2 × 10
6
) were seeded, the two flasks were directly placed in different O
2
conditions (atmospheric O
2
, 1% O
2
). In case of lower cell numbers of 1.5 × 10
6
per T75 flask, both flasks were previously incubated for 24 h in standard atmospheric oxygen conditions before the 10-days experiment was started.
To evaluate the influence of prolonged exposure to hypoxic conditions in KGNs, one cell culture flask was exposed for a total of 10 days to 1% O
2
, while the other was incubated in atmospheric O
2
conditions, i.e. close to 21% O
2
. Two incubators were used (Galaxy 170R and Galaxy 170S CO
2
Incubator, New Brunswick, Eppendorf AG, Germany), which allowed exact control of the O
2
concentration. The cells were passaged on day 3 and 6 or 7, depending on cell growth or experiments. On day 6 or 7, the KGN cells were then re-seeded in 60 mm tissue culture dishes (Sarstedt, Numbrecht, Germany), and additional RNA and protein samples were collected (see below) and kept frozen at −80°C until further use. Cell counting between those steps was conducted using the CASY Cell Counter (Schärfe Systems, Germany; see below).
On day 10, the KGN cell morphology was documented (Leica DM IL LED microscope; Leica DFC3000G camera and Leica Application Suite X; version 3.7.0.20979; all Leica Microsystems GmbH, Germany). Next, cells were washed once with PBS, trypsinized (0.05% Trypsin–EDTA; Gibco), resuspended in 1 mL PBS, and counted (CASY Cell Counter, see below).
Cell counting and volume measurements
The automated CASY Cell Counter was used to perform staining-free viability measurements, distinguishing dead from living cells, and thus allowing determination of cell numbers (on day 3, 6 or 7, 10) and the mean volume (on day 10) of the cells, as described before (
Kaseder
et al.
2022
).
Scratch-wound healing assay: cell migration
Cell migration was examined by analyzing scratch assay experiments. KGN cells were seeded in three 60 mm tissue culture dishes for each condition, with cell numbers of 5–6 × 10
5
per dish. Cells were incubated at atmospheric and hypoxic O
2
levels for a total of 10 days (passaging on day 3 and 7) before the scratch was made. On day 7, 4 × 10
5
KGN cells were seeded into 30 mm tissue culture dishes (Sarstedt). On day 10, the scratch was made using a 10 μL pipette tip (10 μL graduated TipOne® Filter Tip, Natural (sterile), Starlab GmbH, Germany), and the tissue culture dishes were placed back in the corresponding O
2
condition incubators. The cells were photographed after 0, 6, 24, and 48 h using the aforementioned microscope and microscopy camera. The experiment was repeated three times, with three replicates each.
To evaluate migration behavior, confluence analysis was performed on each image using ImageJ software (
Schindelin
et al.
2012
). Migration velocity analysis was performed using one time-lapse recording obtained from one of the replicates with the JuLI™ Br Live Cell Movie Analyzer (NanoEnTek, Korea). In each recording, the migration of 16 randomly selected cells was tracked using the ImageJ Manual Tracking plugin (developed by Fabrice Cordelières, Institut Curie, available from:
https://imagej.net/ij/plugins/track/track.html
). The mean migration velocity was then calculated with the ImageJ Chemotaxis Tool plugin (freely available on the homepage of ibidi GmbH:
https://ibidi.com/manual-image-analysis/171-chemotaxis-and-migration-tool.html
). To create the Supplement Video, the video tools VSDC Video Editor version 9.2 and Microsoft Clipchamp version 3.1.11820.0 were used.
RNA isolation, RNA integrity control, qPCR
For RNA isolation, cells were centrifuged at 16,903
g
at room temperature for 2.5 min, the supernatant was discarded, 350 μL of RLT buffer (RNeasy Plus Micro Kit; Qiagen, Germany) was added, and the samples were stored at −80°C until use. Samples were thawed and processed as described previously (
Höfner
et al.
2023
). RNA concentration was determined with the NanoDropTM 2000/2000c spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and cellular RNA integrity was examined using the Agilent RNA 6000 Pico Assay Kit and 2100 Bioanalyzer Instrument (Agilent Technologies, Germany). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed as described previously (
Höfner
et al.
2023
). In brief, 1,000 ng RNA was subjected to reverse transcription using SuperScript™ II (Invitrogen, USA). Quantitative PCRs were carried out using LUNA® universal qPCR master mix (New England Biolabs GmbH, Germany) on a LightCycler 96VR System (Roche Diagnostics, Germany) with 4 ng cDNA per reaction. The housekeeping gene tyrosine 3-monooxygenase/tryptophan 5-monooxygenase activation protein zeta (
YWHAZ
, primer sequence in 5′-3′: for CCG CTG GTG ATG ACA AGA AA, rev ACA CAG AGA AGT TAA GGG CCA; amplicon size 132 bp; RefSeq:
NM_145690.3
) was used as an internal control to normalize results for vascular endothelial growth factor A (
VEGFA
, primer sequence in 5′-3′: for GCA ATG ACG AGG GCC TGG AGT GTG; rev ACC GGG ATT TCT TGC GCT TTC GTT; amplicon size 219 bp; RefSeq:
NM_001025366.3
,
NM_003376.6
,
NM_001204385.2
). Primers were designed using the primer3 online tool (
http://primer3.wi.mit.edu
). PCR conditions consisted of 40 cycles (steps of denaturation (95°C; 10 s), annealing/elongation (60°C; 30 s), melting (95°C; 10 s, 65°C; 60 s, and 97°C; 1 s), and cooling (37°C, 30 s)). Omission of the reverse transcriptase step (-RT) or water (H
2
O) instead of cDNA in the reaction served as negative controls. The identities of the PCR products were verified upon sequencing. The primer efficiency for
VEGFA
was tested with a standard curve and external standards based on the specific
VEGFA
amplicon of 219 bp. After serial dilutions of resulting DNA standards, the final sensitivity levels between 1 pg and 1 ag of the specific
VEGFA
transcript per sample were achieved. The efficiency (E), defined by the equation
E = 10^(−1/slope) – 1
× 100% (slope was −3.4107), reached 96.4%.
Protein isolation and protein concentration measurement
For protein isolation, cells were washed with PBS two more times and centrifuged at 18,440
g
at room temperature for 2.5 min. The supernatant was discarded, 150 μL of RIPA buffer (radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer, Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added to the samples and vortexed. Samples were stored at −80°C until use. They were thawed and sonicated, kept on ice for 30 min, and centrifuged at 21,170
g
and 4°C for 15 min. The supernatant was used for further analysis. To determine the protein concentration, Lowry assay (DC Protein Assay, Bio-Rad Laboratories, USA; FLUOstar OPTIMA microplate reader, BMG LabTech, Germany) was used.
LC-MS/MS analysis
A proteomic analysis by liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) was performed using two different KGN sample pairs (passage numbers 34 and 35; three technical replicates for each group). Pairs of KGN cells were cultured for 10 days under atmospheric and hypoxic (1% O
2
) conditions. After a washing step with PBS, cells were trypsinized and counted (CASY). The cells were washed five times with PBS, all liquid was removed, and the pellet was stored at −80°C until use. Samples were processed as previously described (
Höfner
et al.
2023
). For the analysis, FDR was set to 0.1, s0 = 0.1 and permutations were 250. The proteins were considered significant based on the FDR calculated by the software Perseus (
Tyanova
et al.
2016
). Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed with the resulting proteome data using the annotation version GO database 10.5281/zenodo.6799722 Released 2022-07-01, the analysis type: PANTHER overrepresentation test, test type Fisher’s exact, and correction according to FDR (all terms are shown for FDR <0.05), and the annotation data set: GO biological process complete (
Ashburner
et al.
2000
,
Thomas
et al.
2022
,
Gene Ontology Consortium
et al.
2023
). In the results, only terms with a
P
-value better than 0.05 were displayed. To compare the KGN cells’ proteome data set with the GCs’ proteome data, the previously published data set of human GCs exposed to 1% O
2
for 4 days was used (see: ProteomeXchange Consortium, dataset identifier PXD042934).
Immunocytochemistry
For immunocytochemical (ICC) staining, KGN cells (passage number 35) were seeded in equal cell numbers of 5 × 10
5
into two 60 mm plastic tissue culture dishes. One dish was exposed to atmospheric O
2
concentrations, the other to 1% O
2
, for 10 days. Cells were split on day 3 and 7 of cultivation. In preparation for the immunocytochemical staining and its evaluation, on day 7, KGN cells were then seeded onto 12 mm glass coverslips (Cat. No. 0117520, Paul Marienfeld GmbH & Co.KG, Germany; 15,000 cells/coverslip). To evaluate effects of a shorter exposure time to hypoxia, additional KGN cells were exposed to 1% O
2
for 2 h.
Next, cells were fixed with 4% formaldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) in PBS for 15 min at room temperature, followed by three washing steps with 1x PBS and incubation in blocking buffer (5% goat serum (Sigma-Aldrich) diluted in 0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich)) for 30 min at room temperature. An anti-HIF1α antibody (mouse IgG1 monoclonal; 1:200 diluted in 5% serum/PBS, Cat.: ab16066, Abcam, UK) was applied for 1 h at room temperature followed by three washing steps with PBS. For visualization, cells were incubated with a goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (Alexa Fluor™ 647, diluted 1:1,000; Cat. No.: A21236, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 1 h at room temperature. After three further washing steps, nuclei were stained with DAPI (1 mg/mL, 1:2,000 diluted in H
2
O; Sigma-Aldrich) for 5 min at room temperature. Samples were embedded in Dako mounting medium (Agilent Technologies, Germany) on a glass slide. Controls consisted of normal goat serum instead of the first antibody or mouse IgG from serum (diluted 1:200; Cat. No.: I-5381, Sigma-Aldrich) as an isotype control. Confocal microscopical examination was performed at the core facility bioimaging of the Biomedical Center with an inverted Leica SP8 microscope. Images were acquired with a 40×/1.30 objective and using ImageJ2 (version: 2.14.0/1.54f). The staining experiments were repeated with a total of four batches of cells. Random images were taken from all experiments. The ratio of nuclear versus cytoplasmic localization of HIF1α in cells exposed to normoxia and hypoxia was then evaluated by two investigators.
Statistical analysis
Microsoft Excel (version 16.69.1, Microsoft, USA) was used to analyze cell numbers, RNA and protein concentrations, qPCR results, cell confluence in the scratch-wound healing assay, and the cellular HIF1α staining. Cell number on day 10, RNA concentration, and protein concentration values were normalized to the cell number seeded on day 6 or 7 of cultivation. Statistical analysis was conducted with GraphPad Prism 9 software (version 9.4.1, GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA). For the statistical analysis of qPCR data, the ΔCq values (ΔCq = Cq(target gene)/Cq(housekeeping gene)) under hypoxic and atmospheric conditions were used. To assess normal distribution, the Shapiro–Wilk normality test was employed (setting
α
= 0.05). When Gaussian distribution could be assumed, a paired two-tailed Student’s
t
-test was used to analyze the cell number, mean volume, RNA concentration, protein concentration, qPCR results, percentages of nuclear localization of HIF1α, and the cell confluence after 0 and 24 h in the scratch-wound healing assay. For non-parametric data of the cell confluence after 48 h in the scratch-wound healing assay and RNA integrity, the Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed rank test was utilized. To analyze the migration velocity data, the Mann–Whitney test was used. For all statistics,
α
was set to 0.05,
P
-values are displayed as *
P
< 0.05, **
P
< 0.01. For visualization, data are depicted as median with 25 and 75% percentile ±10 and 90% percentile.
Results
Hypoxia does not visibly change the morphology of KGN cells, but affects cell number, cell volume, and RNA levels
After 10 days of hypoxic conditions (1% O
2
), no discernible changes in shape and clustering of the KGN cells were noted by microscopic inspection (
Fig. 1A
). However, the mean cell volume slightly, yet significantly, increased (
Fig. 1B
,
n
= 19,
P
= 0.0038). Cell counting revealed a small, yet significantly, decreased cell number in hypoxia (
Fig. 1C
,
n
= 19,
P
= 0.0017). While RNA concentrations significantly decreased in hypoxia (
Fig. 1D
,
n
= 6,
P
= 0.0238), the high and unchanged RNA integrity number (RIN; Bioanalyzer measurements;
Fig. 1E
,
n
= 7,
P
= 0.5000) revealed that hypoxic conditions did not affect RNA quality. One outlier was found and excluded from the comparative RNA concentration analysis in
Fig. 1D
. The protein concentrations did not significantly differ between the two groups (
Fig. 1F
,
n
= 5,
P
= 0.1353).

Hypoxia affects the abundance of 132 cellular proteins in KGN cells
The proteomic analysis of two samples of KGN cells (with three replicates each,
n
= 6) exposed to 1% O
2
for 10 days showed that hypoxia significantly changed the abundance of 132 proteins (57 proteins up-, and 75 proteins down-regulated; FDR = 0.1;
Fig. 2A
).

Comparison of proteomic effects of hypoxia in KGN cells and human GCs
KGN cells often are used as a surrogate model for human IVF-derived GCs. The cellular proteome data of KGN cells exposed to 1% O
2
for 10 days and the recently published proteome analysis of GCs exposed to hypoxic O
2
conditions of 1% for 4 days (
Höfner
et al.
2023
) (see ProteomeXchange Consortium, dataset identifier PXD042934) were therefore compared. A total of 29 proteins were found to be significantly up-regulated in both cell types, while 43 proteins were significantly down-regulated in KGN cells and GCs upon exposure to prolonged hypoxia (Supplementary Fig. 2A; Supplementary Tables S1 and S2). Of note, 60 proteins were significantly up- or down-regulated under hypoxia solely in KGN cells (28 proteins up-, 32 proteins down-regulated) and not in human GCs, indicating that KGN cell-type-specific responses do exist. Pathways associated with cellular response to hypoxia, gluconeogenesis, canonical glycolysis, fructose-1,6-bisphosphate metabolic process, and peptidyl-proline hydroxylation to 4-hydroxy-L-proline were up-regulated in both cell types. The pathway of mitochondrial 5′-end processing was significantly down-regulated in both KGN cells and human GCs exposed to hypoxia (Supplementary Fig. 2B).

Up-regulation of HIF1α nuclear expression in response to hypoxia
Immunocytochemistry was performed to evaluate HIF1α expression in KGN cells (
n
= 4; example shown in
Fig. 4A
). After cultivation of KGNs under atmospheric conditions for 10 days, a faint, diffuse cytoplasmic expression of HIF1α was detected. However, in some cells, staining was also present in the nuclei. Nuclear staining indicates that HIF1α is translocated to the nucleus and implies its involvement in cellular reactions. Upon acute (2 h), as well as chronic hypoxia for 10 days, a predominant nuclear staining pattern prevailed. The visual impression was confirmed upon an evaluation of nuclear versus cytoplasmic HIF1α location. In cells grown in atmospheric conditions, a nuclear localization was found in 42.8% ± 9% of cells (means ± standard deviation; individual results: 32.2, 40.9, 44.0, and 54.0% in the four experiments). When exposed to 1% O
2
for 10 days, nuclear HIF1α location increased to 96.2% ± 2.0% (94.2, 94.6, 97.4, and 98.5%). This was almost identical to the values found when cells were exposed to acute hypoxia for 2 h, namely 93.5 ± 4.8% (86.5, 94.5, 95.4, and 97.6%). The values for both chronic (
P
= 0.0008) or acute hypoxia (
P
= 0.0042) were statistically significantly different from the ones in atmospheric conditions (paired
t
-test).

Discussion
The degree of hypoxia in tissues and tumors mainly depends on the presence of blood vessels and cellular O
2
consumption. For GCTs, only limited information concerning these parameters is available. It may, however, be assumed that in an early phase, the tumors lack blood vessels (pre-vasculature phase) (
Li
et al.
2021
), implying massive hypoxia. In later phases, a large variation in the density of blood vessels in GCTs was reported in two studies. Some areas of the tumor were described as completely avascular, and others displayed only a few vessels (
Jurić
et al.
2001
,
Färkkilä
et al.
2011
). Therefore, the presence of hypoxic regions in parts of solid GCTs can be assumed, as well.
By using CoCl
2,
previous studies in KGN cells (see Introduction;
Nishigaki
et al.
2020
,
Liu & Li 2023
) have touched on the topic of hypoxia in this widely used cellular model for human GCTs and non-tumorous GCs. Yet the impact of a defined and pathophysiologically relevant hypoxic condition on KGN cells was unknown. The principal objective of this study was thus to examine the cell-specific consequences and thereby to provide a resource for better understanding of this widely used
in-vitro
model.
While the actual O
2
conditions in
in-situ
GCTs remain unknown and may vary depending on the region within the tumor, we chose 1% O
2
for our study with KGN cells because comparable or even lower O
2
concentrations were reported in other tumor types (e.g.,
Bellot
et al.
2009
). Furthermore, 1% O
2
is relevant in the physiological context of the ovary, namely in the large human ovarian follicle. Depending on the size of the follicle, the existing data indicate O
2
levels ranging from 1 to 5% in the preovulatory follicle (
Redding
et al.
2007
). In a previous study, we mimicked the conditions in the human preovulatory follicle using human IVF-derived primary GCs and found that 1% O
2
significantly impacted the phenotype and function of primary GCs (
Höfner
et al.
2023
).
We observed that chronic hypoxia did not visibly affect the general morphology of KGN cells. The reasons for the slight increase in cell volume under hypoxic conditions remain to be studied. Likewise, a slightly diminished cell number under hypoxic conditions was found, with impaired cell proliferation and/or cell death in KGN cells being the likely reason. This view is further supported by the results of the scratch-wound healing assays. KGN cells exposed to hypoxia had a reduced capacity to close the gap, but the individual cell velocity was not significantly altered. It seems possible that the overall reduced proliferation but unchanged cell migration velocity observed in hypoxia may be associated with the clinical characteristics of adult-type GCTs, which are known to grow slowly (
Andersson
et al.
2022
).
The mRNA and protein levels may be regarded as an estimate for cellular activity. We found a significant reduction in RNA concentrations but unchanged protein levels. This is in line with results of decreased mRNA and total RNA levels under hypoxia in other cell types, such as endothelial cells, and may be explained by changed transcription rates, RNA degradation rates, and reduced mRNA stability (
Tiana
et al.
2020
).
These points remain to be evaluated in KGN cells. However, an overall decrease in cellular transcriptional/translational activity under hypoxia can be excluded, as revealed by the analysis of the proteome data. The analysis showed that 75 proteins were down-regulated, while 57 proteins were significantly more abundant in hypoxia. Thus, the response to hypoxia elicited distinct adaptations with down-regulation of some and up-regulation of other gene products.
A GO enrichment analysis offered insights into the pathways affected by hypoxia. The down-regulated pathways included mitochondrial translation, mitochondrial tRNA modification (mitochondrial tRNA 3′ and 5′ end processing and mitochondrial tRNA methylation), and glutamine catabolism. As seen in other cells, hypoxia affected and reduced mitochondrial biogenesis in KGN cells, likely mediated by HIF1α (
Roy
et al.
2020
). Such changes in KGN cells were therefore not unexpected.
The downregulation of glutamine catabolism, as identified by GO analysis, was unexpected. Previous publications described an increase in reductive carboxylation of glutamine in hypoxia in order to generate cytosolic citrate for fatty acid synthesis (
Semenza 2013
). Upon closer inspection, we found glutaminase (GLS) among the less abundant proteins in hypoxia, a result which may explain the GO term result. Glutaminase is an enzyme that catalyzes the hydrolysis of glutamine to glutamate and ammonia. It plays an important role in generating energy for metabolism. Nonetheless, we also identified cytoplasmic aconitate hydratase (ACO1) among the top 20 more abundant proteins (see
Table 1
). ACO1 catalyzes the process of fatty acid synthesis, leading to the assumption that the hypoxia-mediated metabolic adaptation, characterized by a shift from oxidative glucose metabolism to reductive glutamine metabolism, is occurring in KGN cells.
The up-regulated pathways included the cellular response to hypoxia, glycolysis, and gluconeogenesis, results that again were not unexpected. Among the increased proteins were PGK1 (phosphoglycerate kinase 1) and LDHA (L-lactate dehydrogenase A chain). With PGK1 being one of the enzymes involved in glycolysis and LDHA catalyzing the conversion of pyruvate to lactate with the linked oxidation of NADH to NAD in anaerobic glycolysis, these results thus also support a metabolic shift towards anaerobic pathways. This metabolic adaptation is a common response to hypoxic stress and enables cells to maintain ATP production despite reduced oxygen availability (
Kierans & Taylor 2021
). It is also known that cancer cell metabolism, in general, depends on this shift to glycolysis (
Roy
et al.
2020
).
The more abundantly expressed proteins included AK4 (adenylate kinase 4), BNIP3 (BCL2/adenovirus E1B 19 kDa protein-interacting protein 3), BNIP3L (BCL2/adenovirus E1B 19 kDa protein-interacting protein 3-like), and NDRG1 (N-Myc downstream regulated 1). It has been suggested that AK4 plays a role in the progression of various cancer types, among others in serous ovarian cancer (
Huang
et al.
2020
). AK4 was also reported to be a transcriptional target of HIF1α (
Jan
et al.
2019
), which is up-regulated upon exposure to chronic hypoxia, for example, in pulmonary arterial smooth muscle cells (
Wujak
et al.
2021
). This increase of AK4 in KGN cells under hypoxic conditions may thus be of future interest regarding GCT progression and eventually treatment options.
N-myc downstream-regulated gene 1 (NDRG1) is known to be involved in angiogenesis, cell proliferation and differentiation, apoptosis, as well as tumor progression and metastasis, and is up-regulated in response to hypoxia (
Nishigaki
et al.
2022
). NDRG1 was also reported to play a role in the luteinization of human GCs, regulated by HIF1α (
Nishigaki
et al.
2025
). Higher levels of NDRG1 and BNIP3 upon exposure to 1% O
2
are in accordance with results of studies conducted in KGN cells when CoCl
2
was used to mimic hypoxia (
Nishigaki
et al.
2022
,
Liu & Li 2023
). In these studies, BNIP3 increased autophagy via the mTOR/ULK1 pathway (
Liu & Li 2023
). Hypoxia-induced autophagy in KGN cells via BNIP3 and BNIP3L may be part of a survival mechanism in GCTs. It may promote tumor progression, as proposed by Bellot (
Bellot
et al.
2009
), and if so, points to the importance of the hypoxic microenvironment for the survival of the ovarian neoplasm.
Surprisingly, the pathway associated with peptidyl-proline hydroxylation to 4-hydroxy-L-proline was up-regulated in KGN cells in hypoxic conditions. The up-regulation of the catalyzing step in HIF1α degradation may imply that chronic hypoxia in KGN cells might involve a decrease in HIF1α stability, consequently reducing its nuclear localization and thereby limiting its transcriptional activity.
When we examined HIF1α using an established, validated antibody, we observed a prevailing nuclear localization under both acute (2 h) and prolonged hypoxia (10 d), which was significantly increased when compared to controls in atmospheric conditions. In view of the proteomic findings, this result indicates that HIF1α activation still dominates even after 10 d, despite the upregulation of peptidyl-proline hydroxylation to 4-hydroxy-L-proline.
Interestingly, we also found nuclear localization of HIF1α in an unexpected high percentage of KGN cells when they were exposed to atmospheric conditions. The reason remains to be fully examined, but a peri-cellular hypoxic environment generated by metabolically active cells in their vicinity, as was recently reported (
Tan
et al.
2024
), may be a likely explanation and is in line with the proliferating, active, and O
2
-consuming nature of KGN cells.
Many prior publications reported that VEGFA levels increase in hypoxic conditions (
Ramakrishnan
et al.
2014
,
Yang
et al.
2024
). This factor has an important pro-angiogenic function in the ovary, especially during the formation of the corpus luteum (
Nishimura & Okuda 2020
,
Guzmán
et al.
2023
). However, in our analysis of the cellular proteome of KGN cells, VEGFA was not detectable, implying low abundance.
VEGFA
was detectable in KGN cells by qPCR, but the transcript levels were not significantly changed upon exposure to hypoxia for 10 days. If transferable to the situation
in-situ
, low levels of pro-angiogenic factors, including VEGFA, may be related to the described low density of blood vessels in GCTs. Additional studies addressing the expression levels of this and other angiogenesis factors in GCTs
in-situ
will be required.
In a plethora of publications listed in PubMed, KGN cells were used as a surrogate model for primary human GCs. In a previous publication (
Höfner
et al.
2023
), we have studied the cellular adaptation of IVF-derived, cultured human GCs to 1% O
2
. These primary cells undergo differentiation to luteal cells and then, within a few days, initiate cell death (
Bagnjuk & Mayerhofer 2019
). Therefore, cellular consequences in GCs were evaluated after 4 d rather than after 10 d, as in the present study. Nonetheless, the cells are related, and therefore we compared the proteomic data. The results revealed both shared and unique proteomic responses. While 29 proteins exhibited a similarly increased abundance, 43 proteins demonstrated a similar decrease in abundance in both cell types. An analysis of shared up-regulated pathways included the cellular response to hypoxia and glycolysis, emphasizing the metabolic shifts in response to a hypoxic environment present in both cell types.
Of note, the comparison also revealed that a considerable number of proteins were uniquely regulated in KGN cells. Clearly, this may be related to the different exposure times to hypoxia (4 versus 10 days), which is an obvious limitation of such a comparison. However, the uniquely regulated proteins in KGN cells may represent specific adaptations associated with their tumorous character. Bearing this distinct possibility in mind, KGN cells may not be an ideal surrogate model for primary GCs, a fact that should be given further consideration in future studies.
A limitation of our study is based on the fact that we only examined consequences of 1% O
2
. While this level is comparable to the O
2
levels present in other tumors (
Bellot
et al.
2009
,
Godet
et al.
2022
) and in the human large preovulatory follicle (
Fischer
et al.
1992
,
Redding
et al.
2007
,
Lim
et al.
2021
), the range of O
2
levels in GCTs
in-situ
remains unfortunately unknown.
This topic is of importance, as recent research has highlighted the effects of the hypoxic tumor microenvironment on cancer therapy (
Klemba
et al.
2020
,
Roy
et al.
2020
,
Retamal
et al.
2025
). Furthermore, a reduced response of ovarian epithelial cancer cells to chemo- and immunotherapy treatment due to hypoxia was reported (
Klemba
et al.
2020
,
Roy
et al.
2020
). Even though surgical removal remains the primary treatment for adult-type GCTs, chemotherapy is part of the current treatment options in cases when surgical intervention is not feasible (
Brink
et al.
2022
). Therefore, further investigations addressing blood vessels and the impact of hypoxia in GCTs are necessary to facilitate the development of novel and potentially more effective treatment modalities.
In conclusion, our study reveals that prolonged hypoxic conditions induce significant phenotypic and proteomic changes in KGN cells. If transferable to the
in-situ
situation, the results demonstrate how hypoxic conditions in the tumor environment can influence the phenotype and the behavior of GCT cells and thereby may contribute to the heterogeneity of GCTs.
