Introduction
Brain arteriovenous malformations (bAVMs) represent abnormal vascular lesions characterized by direct connections between arteries and veins, bypassing the normal intervening capillary network [
1
,
2
]. These abnormal, fragile vessels predispose patients to intracranial hemorrhage, seizures, and long-term neurological deficits, with onset often occurring in younger individuals [
3
,
4
]. Despite advances in surgical and endovascular techniques, current treatments remain anatomically focused and carry significant procedural risks [
5
,
6
]. A key clinical challenge is the lack of reliable tools to predict or prevent hemorrhage. To date, no validated molecular markers or actionable signaling pathways have been identified to guide risk stratification or inform precision treatment strategies in bAVM management [
7
–
9
].
The recent studies have implicated endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndoMT) in the pathogenesis of bAVMs [
10
–
12
]. EndoMT involves the loss of endothelial traits such as tight junctions and apicobasal polarity, while acquiring mesenchymal features that enhance motility, ECM deposition, and fibrosis [
13
–
15
]. This phenotypic shift is characterized by reduced expression of endothelial markers such as VE-cadherin (CDH5) and CD31, and increased expression of mesenchymal proteins including vimentin, α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA, ACTA2), and fibronectin [
16
,
17
]. Although EndoMT has been linked to other vascular malformations and cerebral cavernous malformations, its upstream regulatory mechanisms in bAVMs remain poorly defined [
18
,
19
]. This knowledge gap limits a deeper understanding of bAVMs progression and the molecular drivers of hemorrhagic risk.
Sphingosine-1-phosphate receptor 1 (S1PR1) is a G protein-coupled receptor that plays a central role in maintaining endothelial homeostasis. It is critical for vascular barrier integrity, suppression of inflammation, and stabilization of the cytoskeleton [
20
–
22
]. S1PR1 signaling ensures proper localization of VE-cadherin and supports tight junction assembly in endothelial cells. Dysregulation of this pathway has been linked to vascular leakage, abnormal angiogenesis, and pro-inflammatory endothelial activation across various pathological conditions [
23
–
25
]. However, its expression pattern and functional role in bAVM pathophysiology remain largely unexplored.
In parallel, CCN1 (also known as CYR61), a matricellular protein involved in cell–matrix communication, has been recognized as a potent effector of EndoMT, angiogenesis, and ECM remodeling [
26
–
28
]. Although aberrant CCN1 expression is implicated in fibrotic, inflammatory, and neoplastic vascular remodeling, its upstream regulation and role in bAVMs remain undefined [
29
–
31
].
In this study, we sought to determine whether S1PR1 downregulation contributes to bAVM lesion instability by facilitating endothelial plasticity and matrix reorganization. Through integrative analysis combining human tissue profiling, in vitro endothelial models, and transcriptomic–proteomic interrogation, we demonstrate that S1PR1 deficiency triggers an EndoMT-like transition in endothelial cells and identify CCN1 as a key downstream effector. Moreover, in vivo validation using zebrafish models confirmed that perturbation of S1PR1 or CCN1 induces cranial hemorrhage and vascular instability, further supporting its pathological relevance in cerebrovascular malformations. These findings delineate a novel S1PR1–CCN1 axis that links vascular barrier disruption to migratory mesenchymal activation, offering new mechanistic insight and therapeutic targets for stabilizing cerebrovascular malformations.
Materials and methods
Cell culture and gene knockdown and overexpression
Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were cultured in endothelial cell medium (#1001, ScienCell) and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. All experiments were conducted using up to the sixth passage (P6) cells.
For gene knockdown, two independent short hairpin RNA constructs targeting S1PR1 (sh-S1PR1#1 and sh-S1PR1#2) were cloned into the pLKO.1 lentiviral vector (Tsingke). Lentiviral particles were generated in HEK293T cells and used to transduce HUVECs in the presence of polybrene (8 μg/mL). Stable knockdown lines were selected with puromycin (2 μg/mL) for 5–7 days. CCN1 knockdown was performed using a validated shRNA sequence (sh-CCN1) targeting human CCN1. A non-targeting scrambled shRNA (sh-NC) was used as control. For S1PR1 overexpression, the full-length human S1PR1 coding sequence was subcloned into the pLVX-IRES-Neo lentiviral vector (Tsingke). After viral transduction, HUVECs were selected with G418 (300 μg/mL, Gibco) for 7–10 days. An empty vector (Vector) served as control. Gene silencing and overexpression efficiency were confirmed by qRT-PCR and Western blotting, using β-actin as an internal control.
RNA extraction and quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR)
Total RNA was extracted from cultured human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) using the RNA-Quick Purification Kit (ESUNBIO, Shanghai, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer. For cDNA synthesis, 1 μg of total RNA was reverse transcribed using the HiScript II Reverse Transcriptase (Vazyme, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, China) on a QuantStudio real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems). GAPDH served as the internal control. All gene-specific primers used in this study were designed using Primer-BLAST and synthesized by Tsingke Biotech (Beijing, China). Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2
−ΔΔCt
method.
Western blot
Total protein was extracted from cultured HUVECs using the Elabscience Total Protein Extraction Kit (Elabscience, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, China). Equal amounts of protein (30–50 μg) were separated on 10% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). After blocking in 5% BSA, membranes were incubated overnight with primary antibodies against S1PR1 (ab233386, abcam), CCN1 (ab230947, abcam), EndoMT markers (N-cadherin (#82,968-1-RR, Proteintech), α-SMA (#14-9760-95, ThermoFisher), and VE-cadherin (83766-2-RR, Proteintech), followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (ab205718, abcam) and ECL detection (Bio-Rad, USA). Molecular weight markers were included in all Western blot experiments and indicated on both sides of each blot (in kDa), using Prestained Protein Marker II (10–200 kDa; Servicebio, China, Cat# G2058-250UL) and 180 kDa Prestained Protein Marker (Vazyme, China, Cat# MP102). Lentiviral knockdown and overexpression experiments were conducted using two independent systems. Specifically, sh-NC was used for knockdown experiments, while an empty vector (vector control) was used for overexpression experiments. Molecular weight markers were included in all Western blot experiments and are indicated on the right side of each blot (in kDa). Uncropped versions of the blots are provided in Figs. S1–S4. All blots were derived from independent biological replicates and processed in parallel within each experimental group.
Wound healing assay
To assess lateral migratory capacity of endothelial cells, HUVECs were seeded at a density of 5 × 10
5
cells/well in 6-well plates and cultured under standard conditions until a confluent monolayer was formed. A linear scratch was generated at the center of each well using a sterile 200-μL pipette tip. Detached cells were gently removed with PBS, and the culture medium was replaced with serum-free EGM-2 to minimize proliferation-driven closure. Bright-field images were captured at 0 and 24 h using an inverted microscope (Nikon ECLIPSE C1, Japan). For each well, five non-overlapping fields of view along the wound area were imaged. The wound area was quantified using ImageJ software (NIH), and the migration rate was calculated as: Migration (%) = [(initial wound area − remaining wound area at 24 h)/initial wound area] × 100. All quantifications were performed using consistent threshold settings in ImageJ across experiments, and results were verified by two independent observers in a blinded manner. All experiments were performed in triplicate (
n
= 3) with at least three biological replicates to ensure reproducibility.
Transwell migration assay
Vertical migration was assessed using 24-well Transwell chambers with 8-μm pore polycarbonate inserts (Labselect, China). A total of 2 × 10
5
HUVECs suspended in serum-free medium were added to the upper chamber, while the lower chamber was filled with a complete medium containing 10% FBS to serve as a chemoattractant. After 24 or 48 h of incubation at 37 °C, non-migrated cells on the upper surface were removed with a cotton swab. Migrated cells on the underside were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 20 min, and rinsed with water. Images were acquired using an inverted microscope (Nikon ECLIPSE C1). For each insert, five random non-overlapping high-power fields (HPFs) were selected and cell counts were performed manually using ImageJ, with thresholding for automatic detection. All quantifications were performed using consistent threshold settings in ImageJ across experiments, and results were verified by two independent observers in a blinded manner. All experiments were performed in triplicate (
n
= 3) with at least three biological replicates to ensure reproducibility.
RNA sequencing in S1PR1 knockdown endothelial cells
To investigate transcriptomic alterations following S1PR1 depletion, total RNA was isolated from HUVECs transduced with either control shRNA or S1PR1-targeting shRNAs using RNA-easy Isolation Kit (Vazyme Biotech, R002-01). RNA was extracted 72 h post-transduction to allow for effective S1PR1 knockdown. RNA quality and concentration were assessed using Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100, and only samples with RIN > 7.0 were used for library construction. Poly (A) + RNA was enriched and fragmented, followed by cDNA synthesis and adaptor ligation using a standard stranded mRNA-seq library protocol. High-throughput sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, producing 150-bp paired-end reads. Quality control and adapter trimming were conducted using fastp, and clean reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using HISAT2. Gene expression levels were quantified as FPKM values with StringTie, and differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2, with cutoffs of fold change > 1.2 and adjusted
P
< 0.05. Gene expression levels were quantified using feature Counts for downstream differential expression analysis with DESeq2 (based on raw counts). FPKM values, calculated via StringTie, were used solely for visualization. Pathway enrichment was conducted using KEGG and Reactome databases.
Label-free quantitative proteomic profiling following S1PR1 knockdown
To complement transcriptomic analysis and define proteomic consequences of S1PR1 silencing, whole-cell protein lysates were extracted from HUVECs using Elabscience Total Protein Extraction Kit (Elabscience, China). Protein concentration was determined by BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, China), and sample integrity was confirmed via SDS-PAGE. Proteins (30 μg per sample) were reduced, alkylated, and digested with trypsin overnight using a filter-aided sample preparation (FASP) protocol. Peptides were desalted using C18 Stage Tips prior to mass spectrometry. Resulting peptides were analyzed by LC–MS/MS on a Q Exactive HF-X Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher) coupled to a nanoLC system (EASY-nLC 1200). Peptides were separated on a C18 analytical column (75 μm × 25 cm, 2 μm particle size) using a 90-min linear gradient from 5 to 35% acetonitrile in 0.1% formic acid, at a flow rate of 300 nL/min. The data were acquired in data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode at high resolution (full scan at 60,000, MS/MS at 15,000). Raw data were processed with MaxQuant (version 2.1) against the UniProt human database, and differentially expressed proteins were defined by fold change > 1.2 and
P
< 0.05.
Public dataset validation of S1PR1 and CCN1
To validate the endothelial-specific expression patterns and disease relevance of S1PR1 and CCN1, we systematically analyzed publicly available transcriptomic datasets derived from both normal and pathological human brain vasculature. Single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets were retrieved from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA,
https://www.proteinatlas.org/
) and integrated to assess cell type-resolved expression profiles.
To verify vascular specificity across systems, additional cross-organ scRNA-seq datasets were examined, confirming consistent enrichment of both genes in endothelial compartments from multiple tissues. Previously published scRNA-seq data from normal and bAVM human brain vasculature were re-analyzed to assess the distribution of S1PR1 and CCN1 across endothelial subpopulations [
32
]. All computational findings were cross-referenced with in-house experimental data, including immunohistochemistry (IHC) and dual immunofluorescence (IF) staining for CD31/S1PR1 and CD31/CCN1, to confirm the consistency of endothelial localization patterns at the protein level.
Bioinformatic and functional enrichment analyses
For transcriptomic and proteomic datasets, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and proteins (DEPs) were identified based on predefined thresholds of |fold change|> 1.2 and adjusted
P
-value < 0.05. All statistical analyses and visualizations were performed using R software (version 4.2.2), including volcano plots, hierarchical clustering, and Circos plots for genomic distribution.
Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were conducted using the DAVID database (
https://david.ncifcrf.gov/
). For complementary pathway interrogation, Reactome pathway enrichment analysis was carried out using the ReactomePA package (version 1.44.0, Bioconductor). Protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks were constructed based on the STRING database (
https://string-db.org/
), with a minimum interaction confidence score of 0.4. Network visualization and hub protein identification (based on degree centrality) were performed using Cytoscape software (version 3.9.1).
Human bAVM sample collection
Surgical specimens of human bAVMs were collected from patients undergoing neurosurgical resection at Huashan Hospital, Fudan University. All tissue acquisition protocols were approved by the Ethics Institutional Review Board at Huashan Hospital, Fudan University (HS2022-1087), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Preoperative imaging, including digital subtraction angiography (DSA) and noncontrast computed tomography (CT), was performed to evaluate lesion characteristics. Based on the radiological evidence of hemorrhage, bAVM samples were categorized into ruptured and unruptured groups.
Histological and immunostaining procedures
Paraffin-embedded human bAVM tissues specimens were processed for histological and immunohistochemical evaluation. Sections were cut at a thickness of 4 μm and mounted onto glass slides.
IHC staining
To assess the protein expression and spatial distribution of S1PR1 and CCN1, IHC staining was performed on human bAVM sections. Following deparaffinization and rehydration, antigen retrieval was conducted by heating slides in sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 15 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched using 3% hydrogen peroxide. Sections were blocked in 5% normal serum and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against S1PR1 or CCN1 (Proteintech, China). The next day, sections were treated with HRP-conjugated goat antirabbit IgG (Abcam, ab205718, China) for 2 h at room temperature. Detection was performed using a DAB substrate kit (Proteintech), followed by hematoxylin counterstaining. Slides were scanned with a digital whole-slide scanner (3DHISTECH, Hungary). The percentage area of S1PR1 was calculated as: % Area of S1PR1 = (GFP-positive area)/(total stained tissue area) × 100%.
Dual IF staining
To examine endothelial co-localization of S1PR1 or CCN1 with CD31, dual IF staining was conducted. After antigen retrieval and permeabilization with 0.3% Triton X-100, tissue sections were blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h and incubated overnight at 4 °C with rabbit anti-S1PR1 or anti-CCN1 (Proteintech) in combination with mouse anti-CD31 (Abcam, ab9498). After washing, Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (green; Proteintech) and Cy3-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (red; Invitrogen, USA) were applied for 2 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Beyotime, China). Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Nikon ECLIPSE C1), and colocalization analysis was performed using ImageJ (NIH, USA). Colocalization between S1PR1 or CCN1 and CD31 was analyzed using ImageJ software (NIH, USA) with the Colocalization Finder plugin, and quantified by calculating Manders’ coefficient (M2), which reflects the proportion of S1PR1 or CCN1 signal overlapping with endothelial CD31 signal.
Zebrafish maintenance and genetic manipulation
Wild-type and transgenic zebrafish (Danio rerio) were maintained under standard conditions at 28 °C using an automated aquatic housing system (ESEN, Beijing, China) and cultured in 10% Hank’s solution [
33
]. All transgenic lines were bred on the nacre background to ensure pigment-free transparency for optimal imaging. The
Tg (kdrl:EGFP)
and
Tg (gata1a:DsRed)
lines were utilized to visualize vascular structures and circulating red blood cells, respectively. All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines.
CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockdown of
s1pr1
To generate
s1pr1
crispants, two guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting the gene (sequences: ACAAGTTGACCCCAACGCAA and TCTCTGAAGAACCATTGCGT) were designed using the online CRISPR tool (
http://CRISPR.mit.edu
) and synthesized by Genscript (USA). At the one-cell stage, embryos were microinjected with 600 pg of Cas9 protein (Invitrogen, USA) and 125 pg of each sgRNA. Negative control embryos were injected with Cas9 protein alone. Genomic DNA from injected embryos was extracted, and editing efficiency was assessed via PCR amplification of the target loci (primer sequences: 5′-CGTGTGCTGCTTCATCATCC-3′ and 5′-CCAGACGGTACTGATGAGCA-3′) followed by Sanger sequencing.
Endothelial cell-specific overexpression of
ccn1
To drive targeted overexpression of
ccn1
in endothelial cells, its full-length cDNA was cloned into the Tol2-kdrl-tetoff-H2B-BFP-P2A vector (Azenta, USA), enabling kdrl promoter–mediated expression. Plasmids were verified by sequencing before use. One-cell stage Tg (fli1a:DsRedEx) embryos were co-injected with 20 pg of the construct and 20 pg of Tol2 transposase mRNA. As negative controls, embryos received equal amounts of Tol2 mRNA and empty backbone vector. All injected larvae were raised under identical conditions, and transient expression (F0) was used for downstream imaging and phenotype evaluation.
In vivo imaging and phenotypic assessment
Live embryos or larvae at appropriate developmental stages were embedded in 1% low-melt agarose (Lonza, Switzerland) supplemented with 0.2% tricaine (MS-222) in glass-bottom dishes (35 mm, MatTek, USA) for imaging. Confocal z-stack images were acquired at room temperature using an Olympus FV3000 microscope (Olympus, Japan), with z-intervals of 3–5 µm and a resolution of either 512 × 512 or 1024 × 1024 pixels. All acquisition settings were kept consistent within experimental groups. Fluorescent signal intensities and vascular phenotypes, including cranial hemorrhage and structural abnormalities, were quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software. For overexpression and crispant experiments, all image analyses were conducted in a blinded manner.
Statistical analysis
Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 8.4.2). All quantitative experiments were conducted with at least three independent biological replicates, and exact sample sizes (
n
) for each experiment are indicated in the corresponding figure legends. For pairwise comparisons between two groups, two-tailed unpaired Student’s
t
-tests were employed. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Bonferroni or Tukey post hoc tests was applied to assess significance across groups. All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). A p value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
Results
S1PR1 is enriched in brain endothelial cells and downregulated in ruptured bAVM tissues
To investigate the baseline expression profile of S1PR1 in the human brain, we first analyzed large-scale transcriptomic datasets from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) and Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) projects. Both datasets showed that S1PR1 is broadly expressed across multiple brain regions, with particularly high levels in the cortex, thalamus, hippocampus, and basal ganglia (Fig.
1
A, B). Importantly, cell-type-specific expression data from the HPA brain single-cell RNA-seq dataset revealed that S1PR1 expression is most prominently enriched in brain endothelial cells, in comparison to neurons, glial cells, immune populations, and other parenchymal cell types (Fig.
1
C). Spatial transcriptomics (Stereo-seq) of the human cerebral cortex from HPA datasets further supported this enrichment, showing significantly elevated S1PR1 expression in vascular-associated cells compared to astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, microglia, and neurons (Fig. S5A). Moreover, a cross-tissue analysis of core cell types revealed that S1PR1 is consistently expressed at high levels in endothelial cells across diverse organ systems, underscoring its conserved vascular specificity (Fig. S5B, C). Collectively, these findings support the hypothesis that S1PR1 plays a critical and possibly conserved role in maintaining endothelial function and vascular integrity. To explore whether S1PR1 expression is altered under pathological conditions, we performed immunohistochemical (IHC) staining of human bAVM tissues. The expression of S1PR1 in ruptured bAVM tissues was significantly decreased compared with unruptured bAVM tissues (Fig.
1
D). These results were further validated by dual immunofluorescence staining with co-labeling of CD31 (an endothelial marker) and S1PR1 (Fig.
1
E). In unruptured bAVM tissues, S1PR1 exhibited extensive colocalization with CD31. However, in ruptured bAVMs, this colocalization was visibly reduced. Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis revealed that S1PR1 is expressed across multiple endothelial subpopulations, including arterial (Art1–3), venous, capillary, and venule clusters in both normal adult brain and bAVM tissues (Fig. S5D, E). Together, these findings indicate that S1PR1 is selectively enriched in brain endothelial cells under physiological conditions, and significantly downregulated in the endothelium of ruptured bAVMs, suggesting S1PR1 silencing as a potential contributor to endothelial dysfunction and lesion instability in bAVM pathogenesis.

S1PR1 regulates EndoMT and migratory capacity in endothelial cells
To determine the functional role of S1PR1 in regulating endothelial function and stability, we performed both loss-of-function and gain-of-function experiments in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs). Lentivirus-mediated knockdown and overexpression of S1PR1 were established in HUVECs using two shRNA constructs (sh-S1PR1#1/2) and a full-length S1PR1 expression vector, respectively. Knockdown and overexpression efficiency were validated by qRT-PCR and Western blotting (Fig.
2
A–D). S1PR1 knockdown induced a shift toward a mesenchymal phenotype, as evidenced by the upregulation of canonical mesenchymal markers (N-cadherin and α-SMA) and concomitant downregulation of endothelial markers (VE-cadherin), at both mRNA and protein levels (Fig.
2
E, F). Functionally, S1PR1 knockdown significantly increased endothelial cell motility. In transwell assays, where S1PR1 knockdown significantly increased the number of migrating cells at both 24 h and 48 h time points; the overexpression group showed reduced migration (Fig.
2
G, H). These findings were further supported by scratch wound healing assays, S1PR1-silenced cells demonstrated accelerated wound closure at 24 h as compared to controls, while overexpression suppressed migration (Fig.
2
I, J). To assess these effects in vivo, we generated
s1pr1
-knockdown zebrafish crispants using the CRISPR/Cas9 system (Fig.
2
K). Although these embryos exhibited normal gross morphology during early development, a marked increase in cranial hemorrhages was observed at 2 days post-fertilization (2 dpf) (Fig.
2
L). This phenotype was further confirmed by extravasation of red blood cells and localized blood pooling in the cranial region, as visualized by transgenic reporters and confocal imaging (Fig.
2
M). These vascular abnormalities in
s1pr1
-deficient embryos reinforce the critical role of S1PR1 in maintaining vascular integrity and endothelial barrier function in vivo. Collectively, our in vitro and in vivo results demonstrate that S1PR1 is essential for maintaining endothelial phenotype and vascular stability.

Transcriptomic profiling reveals ECM remodeling following S1PR1 knockdown
To explore the global transcriptional consequences of S1PR1 knockdown, we performed RNA sequencing in HUVECs following shRNA-mediated knockdown. Violin plots of log-transformed fragments per kilobase million (FPKM) values demonstrated comparable overall transcript distributions across all biological replicates, confirming the high quality and consistency of the sequencing data between control (nc) and S1PR1 knockdown (sh) groups (Fig.
3
A). Scatter plot analysis (log₂FPKM) revealed widespread transcriptomic alterations in S1PR1 knockdown cells as compared to negative controls (Fig.
3
B). Volcano plot visualization revealed a total of 948 DEGs following S1PR1 knockdown in HUVECs, including 343 upregulated and 605 downregulated DEGs (Fig.
3
C). Hierarchical clustering of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) confirmed clear separation between groups, indicating distinct gene expression profiles (Fig.
3
D). To gain insights into the genomic distribution of transcriptional changes, a Circos plot was generated to map the chromosomal distribution of DEGs across chromosomal loci. Altered genes were broadly distributed across the genome, with certain chromosomal regions showing more pronounced signal density (Fig.
3
E). To investigate the biological implications of S1PR1 knockdown, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis. Notably, upregulated genes were enriched in terms associated with ECM organization, and basement membrane remodeling (Fig.
3
F). In contrast, downregulated DEGs were significantly enriched in endothelial-specific structural components, such as cell–cell junctions, actin cytoskeleton, and plasma membrane complexes (Fig.
3
G). These findings suggest that S1PR1 loss leads to transcriptional reprogramming that favors matrix remodeling and junctional destabilization, key features of EndoMT and endothelial dysfunction.

S1PR1 knockdown triggers dual programs of ECM remodeling and epigenetic repression in endothelial cells
To further delineate the molecular programs affected by S1PR1 knockdown, we performed KEGG and Reactome pathway enrichment analyses using DEGs derived from the S1PR1 knockdown transcriptome. KEGG analysis of upregulated genes revealed significant enrichment in pathways associated with cell–cell interaction and structural integrity, including tight junctions and Hippo signaling, along with diverse metabolic circuits such as amino sugar metabolism and vitamin B6 biosynthesis (Fig.
4
A). These findings suggest that S1PR1 knockdown disrupts endothelial barrier architecture and triggers compensatory metabolic adjustments. Conversely, downregulated genes were strongly associated with inflammatory and cytokine signaling cascades, including the JAK-STAT pathway, TNF signaling, and complement/coagulation cascades. Additional suppression of genes involved in cytoskeletal remodeling further supports the idea that S1PR1 knockdown weakens cellular structural dynamics (Fig.
4
B). Reactome analysis also demonstrated that upregulated genes were notably enriched in negative regulation of PI3K/AKT signaling, Wnt signaling, and adherens junction interactions (Fig.
4
C), reinforcing the transcriptomic evidence of ECM and junctional reorganization. In contrast, downregulated genes mapped to Signaling by Nuclear Receptors, Condensation of Prophase Chromosomes, and Formation of the beta—catenin: TCF transactivating complex (Fig.
4
D), implying that S1PR1 knockdown suppresses key regulators of endothelial transcriptional stability. Collectively, these pathway analyses suggest that S1PR1 silencing in endothelial cells initiates a dual program characterized by enhanced matrix remodeling and impaired transcriptional regulation, which may synergistically prime cells toward EndoMT and vascular fragility.

Integrated transcriptomic and proteomic profiling reveals ECM remodeling and cytoskeletal dysregulation following S1PR1 knockdown
To investigate the proteomic consequences of S1PR1 knockdown and their relevance to endothelial phenotypic plasticity, we performed label-free quantitative proteomics in S1PR1-knockdown HUVECs. Boxplot analysis of log
2
-transformed total protein abundance confirmed comparable overall distributions between sh-S1PR1 and control groups, indicating technical robustness and minimal batch effects (Fig.
5
A). Hierarchical clustering of differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) revealed clear separation between the two groups, with 213 proteins significantly upregulated and 171 downregulated upon S1PR1 knockdown (Fig.
5
B, C). Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network of DEPs identified several high-connectivity hub proteins, including AURKB, CDK2, RPA2, CHEK1, and replication-related factors. These hub nodes are primarily associated with DNA replication, cell cycle progression, and checkpoint regulation, suggesting that S1PR1 knockdown perturbs proliferative and genomic stability pathways (Fig.
5
D). These features are indicative of replicative stress, a hallmark of phenotypic plasticity that may predispose endothelial cells toward mesenchymal conversion. GO annotation of DEPs revealed significant enrichment in pathways related to cell cycle progression, protein transport, and organelle membrane complexes, suggesting dysregulation of cellular homeostasis and intracellular transport processes following S1PR1 knockdown (Fig.
5
E). KEGG pathway enrichment of upregulated proteins revealed pathways related to cell cycle progression, DNA repair, RNA processing, and insulin signaling (Fig.
5
F), indicating enhanced biosynthetic and proliferative responses following S1PR1 knockdown. In contrast, downregulated proteins were predominantly enriched in pathways associated with spliceosome function, oxidative phosphorylation, and multiple neurodegeneration-related processes, reflecting impaired RNA processing, mitochondrial dysfunction, and metabolic stress (Fig.
5
G). These changes may indirectly contribute to loss of endothelial homeostasis and structural stability. Integrated transcriptomic and proteomic analyses revealed a convergent shift toward extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, cytoskeletal disorganization, and junctional destabilization upon S1PR1 knockdown. Enrichment of pathways such as basement membrane organization, and extracellular region highlighted a pro-fibrotic and migratory phenotype. Among the dysregulated genes, CCN1 (CYR61) emerged as a representative ECM-associated gene that was consistently upregulated at both the RNA and protein levels. Given its established roles in promoting endothelial permeability, vascular inflammation, and cytoskeletal remodeling, CCN1 may act downstream of S1PR1 to mediate endothelial plasticity.

CCN1 is upregulated upon S1PR1 deficiency and correlates with EndoMT signatures in human bAVMs
As revealed by proteomic profiling, the expression of CCN1 was significantly upregulated upon S1PR1 knockdown (Fig.
6
A). qRT-PCR and Western blot confirmed that CCN1 was markedly upregulated upon S1PR1 knockdown at both transcript and protein levels (Fig.
6
B, C). These findings suggest that S1PR1 negatively regulates CCN1 expression, forming a functional S1PR1–CCN1 axis in endothelial cells. To assess the relevance of CCN1 in human disease, we evaluated CCN1 protein expression in human bAVM tissues. Quantification of immunohistochemistry showed significantly higher CCN1 levels in ruptured bAVM tissues compared with unruptured bAVM tissues (Fig.
6
D, E). Dual immunofluorescence staining with co-labeling for CD31 and CCN1 demonstrated substantial colocalization of CCN1 with CD31, indicating that endothelial cells are a major source of CCN1 expression in bAVM tissues. Moreover, this endothelial-specific upregulation of CCN1 was more prominent in ruptured bAVM tissues (Fig.
6
F), suggesting that altered endothelial CCN1 expression may contribute to the progression and instability of bAVMs. To further characterize the cell-type specificity of CCN1 expression, we re-analyzed publicly available single-cell RNA sequencing data from human brain endothelium [
32
], comparing normal and bAVM tissues (Fig.
6
G, H). To functionally assess the role of ccn1 overexpression in vivo, we generated zebrafish with endothelial-specific overexpression of ccn1 via Tol2-mediated transgenesis (Fig.
6
I). The efficiency of overexpression was confirmed by fluorescence imaging and qRT-PCR (Fig.
6
J). Although embryos displayed no gross developmental defects, ccn1 overexpression significantly increased cranial hemorrhage incidence at 2 dpf (Fig.
6
K, L), accompanied by extravasation of red blood cells in the cranial region. In addition, vascular imaging at 5 dpf revealed dilated intersomitic vessels and increased cerebrovascular density in ccn1-overexpressing larvae compared with controls (Fig.
6
M, N), consistent with a hyperangiogenic and unstable vascular phenotype. Together, these data indicate that CCN1 is a downstream effector of S1PR1 deficiency, enriched in endothelial cells of ruptured bAVMs and capable of inducing vascular defects in vivo. These findings position CCN1 as a potential driver of vascular instability and a mechanistic link between endothelial dysregulation and bAVM pathology.

CCN1 knockdown functionally rescues EndoMT and hyper-migration induced by S1PR1 suppression
To determine whether CCN1 mediates the pro-EndoMT phenotype induced by S1PR1 knockdown, we performed rescue experiments using shRNA-mediated knockdown of CCN1 in S1PR1-silenced HUVECs. Quantitative RT-PCR confirmed efficient knockdown of S1PR1, accompanied by a significant upregulation of CCN1. Co-transfection with sh-CCN1 effectively reduced CCN1 expression without altering S1PR1 transcript levels, indicating specific and efficient silencing of the target gene. These transcriptional changes were further validated at the protein level by Western blot analysis (Fig.
7
A–C). Functionally, CCN1 knockdown significantly attenuated the EndoMT phenotype induced by silencing S1PR1. The upregulation of mesenchymal markers (N-cadherin, α-SMA) and downregulation of the endothelial marker VE-cadherin in the S1PR1 knockdown group were markedly reversed upon CCN1 co-silencing, (Fig.
7
D–F). Consistent with these molecular changes, CCN1 knockdown also significantly impaired the heightened migratory capacity induced by S1PR1 knockdown. In transwell assays, the enhanced migration induced by S1PR1 knockdown was significantly suppressed by CCN1 inhibition at both 24 h and 48 h (Fig.
7
G). Similarly, wound healing assays demonstrated accelerated closure in sh-S1PR1 cells, which was substantially delayed following CCN1 knockdown (Fig.
7
H). Collectively, these findings position CCN1 as a key downstream effector linking S1PR1 knockdown to EndoMT initiation and enhanced endothelial motility. Targeting the S1PR1–CCN1 axis may thus offer a rational therapeutic strategy for restoring endothelial stability and preventing vascular remodeling in bAVM.

Discussion
bAVMs are structurally unstable vascular anomalies prone to rupture, often resulting in devastating intracranial hemorrhage in young patients. Although the mechanisms underlying vessel instability remain incompletely defined, increasing evidence implicates endothelial plasticity and EndoMT in vascular remodeling and fragility [
34
,
35
]. In this study, we uncover a novel regulatory axis in which S1PR1 silencing drives EndoMT via upregulation of the matricellular protein CCN1, establishing a mechanistic link between endothelial dysfunction and ECM remodeling in bAVM pathogenesis.
Through analysis of single-cell and bulk transcriptomic datasets from the Human Protein Atlas and publicly available vascular atlases, we confirmed that S1PR1 is preferentially expressed in human brain endothelial cells, consistent with its well-established role in maintaining vascular barrier integrity and limiting pathological angiogenesis [
20
,
22
,
36
]. Notably, our IHC analyses of resected human bAVM tissues revealed a significant reduction of endothelial S1PR1 expression in ruptured lesions, implicating its loss as a potential molecular contributor to hemorrhagic transformation. These findings align with murine studies showing that endothelial S1PR1 deletion leads to aberrant angiogenesis, increased permeability, and intracranial hemorrhage [
37
,
38
]. Mechanistically, S1PR1 knockdown in HUVECs induced a pronounced EndoMT-like phenotype, marked by increased expression of mesenchymal markers such as N-cadherin and α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), and reduced expression of the endothelial marker VE-cadherin. These molecular alterations were accompanied by enhanced cell motility in both scratch wound and Transwell migration assays, indicating a functional shift toward a mesenchymal state. In contrast, S1PR1 overexpression restored endothelial identity and suppressed migration, underscoring its critical role in maintaining endothelial phenotype and stability.
Importantly, these phenotypic changes were not isolated to surface marker shifts, but reflected global transcriptomic and proteomic reprogramming, as revealed by integrated omics analyses. Transcriptomes for S1PR1 silencing HUVECs revealed strong upregulation of pathways involved in basement membrane disassembly and ECM–receptor interaction, hallmarks of structural remodeling and junctional weakening. In parallel, proteomic profiling confirmed widespread alterations in cytoskeletal regulators, junctional proteins, and DNA replication factors, indicating that replicative stress and junctional instability may act in concert to destabilize the endothelium, thereby promoting mesenchymal conversion and cellular motility. These results corroborate prior observations that junctional disruption and cytoskeletal instability are permissive to EndoMT induction in vascular malformations [
39
–
41
]. Among the dysregulated factors, CCN1 emerged as a prominent transcriptionally and translationally upregulated effector following S1PR1 knockdown. CCN1 is a matricellular ECM-associated protein known to promote angiogenesis, inflammation, and EndoMT in various pathological conditions, including pulmonary hypertension and atherosclerosis [
42
,
43
]. In our study, silencing CCN1 in S1PR1 silencing HUVECs significantly reversed mesenchymal marker expression and restored endothelial phenotype, while also reducing migration, thereby functionally validating CCN1 as a downstream effector of S1PR1. These findings are consistent with a previous study reporting that CCN1 activates TGF-β/Smad signaling to promote EndoMT and vascular remodeling [
42
]. Our data extend this by positioning CCN1 as a central mediator that connects S1PR1 silencing to ECM-driven mesenchymal transition in human bAVMs. Notably, we also validated in situ co-expression of CCN1 with the endothelial marker CD31 in bAVM tissue via dual IF staining, further reinforcing its endothelial origin and pathological relevance. To further explore CCN1 expression patterns, we analyzed endothelial subclusters using single-cell transcriptomic data. CCN1 showed an arterial-biased expression in both normal and bAVM endothelial populations. However, these clusters represent transcriptional phenotypes rather than anatomical locations. Since our study focuses on endothelial-level dysregulation, we interpret CCN1 upregulation as a marker of endothelial activation rather than a bAVM-specific arterial event. Nonetheless, this subcluster-level insight provides a valuable foundation for future studies investigating spatial heterogeneity and endothelial remodeling in bAVMs. Moreover, CCN1 expression was significantly elevated in ruptured bAVM lesions, suggesting it may not only mediate phenotypic change but also serve as a biomarker of lesion instability and rupture risk.
From a translational perspective, our findings provide a strong rationale for therapeutically targeting the S1PR1–CCN1 signaling axis in bAVMs. S1PR1 agonists such as fingolimod have been shown to enhance endothelial barrier function and reduce vascular leak in preclinical models [
44
,
45
], while anti-CCN1 strategies are under investigation for fibrotic and neovascular diseases [
46
,
47
]. Given that both proteins are cell-surface or secreted, they offer pharmacologically tractable intervention points. Moreover, elevated CCN1 in ruptured lesions highlights its potential utility as a biomarker for endothelial activation and hemorrhagic risk stratification in bAVM patients.
Conclusion
Our findings define the S1PR1–CCN1 signaling axis as a key molecular pathway driving endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition, extracellular matrix remodeling, and enhanced endothelial migration in the context of brain arteriovenous malformations (bAVMs). Loss of S1PR1 compromises vascular stability and promotes a pro-fibrotic, migratory endothelial phenotype through CCN1 induction. As a secreted matricellular protein consistently upregulated at both transcriptomic and proteomic levels, CCN1 represents a tractable downstream effector linking S1PR1 dysfunction to lesion progression. Targeting the S1PR1–CCN1 axis offers a promising strategy for restoring endothelial homeostasis and reducing hemorrhagic risk in bAVM patients.
