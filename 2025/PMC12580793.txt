Introduction
Nuclear pore complexes (NPC) are large macromolecular structures that perforate the nuclear envelope, composed of multiple copies of about 30 different proteins known as nucleoporins (NUP). NPCs have traditionally been studied as the primary gateways regulating selective nucleo-cytoplasmic transport in eukaryotic cells (
1
,
2
). In addition, transport-independent roles have been assigned to NUPs, including genomic integrity maintenance and organization, as well as in regulating gene expression (
3
–
7
).
As NUPs regulate key mechanisms involved in cancer pathogenesis, it is unsurprising that emerging evidence points to their dysfunction as a source of malignancy (
8
). Alterations in NUPs implicated in cancer include changes in expression levels, chromosomal translocations resulting in onco-fusion proteins, and single point mutations (
9
,
10
). In the context of NUP expression changes, we previously uncovered that
POM121
upregulation at the NPC enhances nuclear import of key oncogenic transcription factors (TF) driving prostate cancer aggressiveness (
11
). Beyond transport-related functions, a discrete set of other on-pore NPC structural components have been shown to be required for pore assembly in proliferative cancer cells (
12
) or to regulate tumor cell growth and migration through mechanotransduction and chromatin modulation (
13
,
14
). Remarkably, in the case of leukemias, NUP98 oncogenic fusions have been shown to be dynamic and localize inside the nucleus (
15
–
19
). However, the role of nucleoplasm-localized off-pore NUPs in solid tumors has not been investigated.
In this study, we examined the role of nucleoplasm-residing NUPs in prostate cancer, a solid tumor exhibiting dramatic NUP expression changes during disease progression (
11
), and identified soluble POM121 (sPOM121) as a top upregulated off-pore nucleoplasm-localized NUP with clinical relevance in driving tumor aggressiveness. sPOM121 is an isoform expressed from an alternative transcription start site (TSS) in the
POM121
gene, which encodes for a protein that lacks the transmembrane domain responsible of nuclear pore anchoring with putative gene expression roles in human cells (
20
). Through comprehensive epigenomic and proteomic interactome analyses, we discovered that sPOM121 significantly enriches at gene promoters together with the chromatin remodeler and transcriptional activator SMARCA5 (
21
,
22
). In addition, we found that the phenylalanine−glycine (FG)-rich C-terminal region of sPOM121 is key in regulating a distinct gene network by ensuring SMARCA5 localization at active transcriptional nuclear condensates. Integrated transcriptome and chromatin accessibility analysis identified β-catenin (
CTNNB1
) as a pivotal gene activated by sPOM121 in advanced metastatic prostate cancer. Functional studies in both experimental and patient-derived models revealed that the sPOM121/β-catenin axis drives disease progression and triggers immune evasion. These data suggest that the link between NUPs and cancer in solid tumors extends beyond the physical boundaries of the NPC, with nucleoplasm-residing NUPs like sPOM121 propelling cancer cell oncogenic signaling and tumor progression from deep within the nucleus. Intervening at these critical and less-explored regulatory functions may offer novel strategies to disrupt previously inaccessible transcriptional networks, paving the way for targeting off-pore nucleoplasm-localized NUPs as a new frontier in cancer therapy.
Results
sPOM121 Promotes Prostate Cancer Aggressiveness
To identify off-pore nucleoplasmic NUPs that contribute to the pathogenesis of prostate cancer, we mined The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) transcriptome dataset at the isoform level to determine the expression of NUPs exclusively localized in the nucleoplasm, like sPOM121 (20), and NUPs with both NPC and nucleoplasmic localization (
5
,
15
–
19
,
23
–
27
). This analysis identified sPOM121 as the most significantly upregulated nucleoplasm-localized NUP in prostate cancer compared with normal prostate tissue (
Fig. 1A
). Remarkably, using complementary orthogonal methods, we found that sPOM121 mRNA and protein expression increased during the natural history of prostate cancer, with the highest levels detected in advanced metastatic tumor samples. To specifically detect and quantify sPOM121 mRNA, we performed RNA-ISH–RNAscope in tissues from a cohort of patients with primary and metastatic prostate cancer using a probe that binds to its unique noncoding exons (two and three) located in its distinct 5′ untranslated region (UTR; Supplementary Fig. S1A). RNA-ISH signal analysis revealed that sPOM121 mRNA expression significantly increased from normal to primary prostate cancer, with levels being highest in metastatic samples (
Fig. 1B
). As a positive control, we evaluated NPC-associated POM121 (TM-POM121) in the same samples using a probe against its unique transmembrane (TM) domain–coding exon. The specificity of the probes against each
POM121
transcript isoform was confirmed with an siRNA against sPOM121 5′UTR in multiple prostate cancer cells, which specifically decreased sPOM121 mRNA and protein expression (Supplementary Fig. S1B–S1E), nucleoplasmic detection (Supplementary Fig. S1D), and RNA-ISH signals (Supplementary Fig. S1E), without affecting TM-POM121.

IHC (
Fig. 1C
) and transmission electron microscopy analysis (
Fig. 1D
) of patient tumor tissues using an antibody that detects both POM121 isoforms revealed off-pore nucleoplasmic localization of sPOM121. Quantification of nucleoplasmic sPOM121 confirmed at the protein level that sPOM121 expression is highest in metastatic prostate cancer samples. Notably, sPOM121 expression was also higher in metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer cells when compared with normal prostate and primary prostate cancer cell models (Supplementary Fig. S1F and S1G). Altogether, these results identify for the first time that the nucleoplasm-residing NUP, sPOM121, is expressed in prostate cancer and that its expression increases as the disease progresses to advanced metastatic stages.
To determine the
in vivo
consequences of sPOM121 expression on prostate cancer aggressiveness, we evaluated the effects of sPOM121 depletion using a short hairpin RNA (shRNA) that specifically targets its 5′UTR and proficiently depletes sPOM121 mRNA (Supplementary Fig. S1H) and protein (Supplementary Fig. S1I), without affecting TM-POM121 levels. To investigate how sPOM121 expression affects metastatic disease, mice intracardiac injection of prostate cancer cells was performed. Intracardiac injection of sPOM121-depleted, luciferase-tagged prostate cancer cells (DU145 and 22Rv1) into mice induced a significant decrease in tumor burden (
Fig. 1E
) and increased mouse survival (Supplementary Fig. S1J). Inversely, intracardiac injection of sPOM121-overexpressing prostate cancer cells into mice increased tumor burden and reduced survival when compared with mice intracardially injected with empty vector (EV) control cells (Supplementary Fig. S1K and S1L). Notably, the effects of sPOM121 in modulating the aggressiveness of prostate cancer were further corroborated using patient-derived preclinical models. Indeed, sPOM121 knockdown in three organoid models generated from patient-derived xenografts (PDX;
Fig. 1F
) decreased the number and size of organoids (Supplementary Fig. S1M and S1N). Furthermore, intracardiac injection of sPOM121-depleted organoid prostate cancer cells into mice resulted in increased mouse survival when compared with controls (
Fig. 1G
). Overall, these data show that sPOM121 may play a pivotal role in metastatic prostate cancer and provide the rationale to investigate the mechanisms by which it contributes to disease aggressiveness.
Chromatin-Associated sPOM121 Is Enriched at Gene Promoters
Next, we aimed to define the molecular functions of sPOM121 in advanced prostate cancer. Because of the nucleoplasmic localization of sPOM121, we initially interrogated the association of sPOM121 with chromatin. To this end, we performed subcellular fractionation experiments using prostate cancer cell models (DU145 and 22Rv1). This analysis revealed that sPOM121 was preferentially enriched in the chromatin fraction and was undetectable upon sPOM121 knockdown, whereas full-length TM-POM121 was enriched in nuclear fractions (
Fig. 2A
; Supplementary Fig. S2A and S2B).

To further interrogate the potential role of chromatin-associated sPOM121, we assessed its genome-wide localization by chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) and identified a 46% overlap with promoters and 5′UTR genomic regions (
Fig. 2B
) near TSS (Supplementary Fig. S2C). Interestingly, motif analysis revealed that sPOM121 peaks were highly enriched for RNA polymerase II (Pol II)–associated co-factor motifs, such as the TATA-box binding protein–associated factor 1 (TAF1) (
Fig. 2C
; ref.
28

Fig. 2D
; Supplementary Fig. S2D). Most notably, supporting the clinical relevance of this observation, the interaction between sPOM121 and RNA Pol II phospho S5 observed by PLA significantly increased in human metastatic prostate tumor samples when compared with localized primary prostate cancer tissues (
Fig. 2E
).
Moreover, gene ontology analysis of sPOM121 peaks identified categories enriched in transcription-associated processes, cell–cell adhesion, and cell division (
Fig. 2F
). Five genes within the top gene ontology categories were initially selected for further validation based on their strong sPOM121 ChIP-seq peaks near their TSS, including
CTNNB1
,
CXXC1
,
PREB
,
CRK
, and
ENSA
(
Fig. 2G
), which were corroborated by ChIP-qPCR comparing control and sPOM121-depleted prostate cancer cells (Supplementary Fig. S2E). These data suggest that sPOM121 localizes at active transcriptional sites near gene promoters within the nucleoplasm of prostate cancer cells.
sPOM121 Localizes at Gene Promoters through SMARCA5 Interaction
To uncover the sPOM121 chromatin interactome, we performed rapid IP for mass spectrometry of endogenous proteins (RIME) assays comparing control and sPOM121-depleted cells. More than 250 proteins were differentially enriched when comparing controls and sPOM121 knockdown samples, from which 168 (67.2%) proteins were decreased after sPOM121 depletion in three prostate cancer cell models (
Fig. 3A
; Supplementary Table S1). Remarkably, sPOM121 RIME data showed enrichment of biological pathways implicated in chromatin binding, regulation of mRNA, and transcription, suggesting the involvement of this soluble NUP in these processes (
Fig. 3B
; Supplementary Fig. S3A). The top sPOM121-interacting proteins were ranked considering peptide enrichment (max razor + unique peptides), greatest fold change decrease after sPOM121 knockdown (sPOM121/Cnt KD), and absence in IgG controls in all three cell models (
Fig. 3C
). Of note, data mining of proteomic datasets identified that the top interacting proteins with sPOM121 found in our RIME study overlapped with proteins previously shown to form a complex with RNA Pol II, including SMARCA5, SLTM, DDX54, or RBM25 (
29
).

To validate our RIME data and determine the strongest interactors of sPOM121 at chromatin, we performed IP assays using either total protein or nuclear fractions (
Fig. 3D
; Supplementary Fig. S3B). We identified SMARCA5 as the main sPOM121-interacting protein in chromatin fractions in three independent prostate cancer cell line models. This result was confirmed by reverse IPs of SMARCA5 using same prostate cancer cell extracts and nuclear fractions (Supplementary Fig. S3C and S3D). Subsequently, PLA corroborated a close association between sPOM121 and SMARCA5 in the nucleus of prostate cancer cells (
Fig. 3E
). These findings were confirmed in prostate cancer tissue samples by PLA, in which sPOM121–SMARCA5 interaction was highest in metastatic tumors when compared with primary prostate cancer samples (
Fig. 3F
). As SMARCA5 was found in complex with RNA Pol II (see
Fig. 3C
) and was the only protein identified by our RIME analysis to exclusively interact with sPOM121 at chromatin (see
Fig. 3D
; Supplementary Fig. S3D), we aimed to further investigate the nature and functional consequences of their interaction.
SMARCA5 (
SNF2H
) is a chromatin remodeler of the ISWI family specialized in promoting transcriptional activation at promoter regions (
30
). Thus, we interrogated whether SMARCA5 could bind to the top genes identified in our sPOM121 cistrome analysis. To this end, we performed SMARCA5 ChIP-seq and found a strong overlap with sPOM121 ChIP-seq (
Fig. 3G
), including peaks in top sPOM121-regulated genes (
Fig. 3H
). Indeed, ChIP-qPCR comparing control and SMARCA5-depleted conditions confirmed SMARCA5 enrichment at top genes regulated by sPOM121 (Supplementary Fig. S3E). Importantly, upon the knockdown of SMARCA5, sPOM121 occupancy at gene promoters decreased (
Fig. 3I
). Altogether, these results demonstrate that SMARCA5 is key in facilitating sPOM121 localization at gene promoter sites.
The sPOM121 C-Terminus Is Key for SMARCA5 Interaction and Nucleoplasmic Condensates Localization
We next sought to investigate the sPOM121 protein region involved in the interaction with SMARCA5 and its potential functional relevance in gene regulation. To this end, we generated sPOM121-GFP fusion mutants bearing deletion of distinct protein regions (
Fig. 4A
), which were stably expressed at similar levels in prostate cancer cells (Supplementary Fig. S4A), and tested their ability to interact with SMARCA5 via IP assays. We found that the CT fragment of sPOM121 was necessary and sufficient for its interaction with SMARCA5. The ΔC and M sPOM121 mutants showed no interaction with SMARCA5, whereas the expression of the CT sPOM121 alone interacted with SMARCA5 (
Fig. 4B
; Supplementary Fig. S4B). These results were corroborated by reverse SMARCA5 IPs (
Fig. 4C
; Supplementary Fig. S4C).

We then interrogated the ability of sPOM121 CT deletion to bind to the promoter of genes co-regulated with SMARCA5. ChIP-qPCR in prostate cancer cells expressing sPOM121-GFP deletion mutants after endogenous sPOM121 knockdown (Supplementary Fig. S4D) showed a significant decrease in the enrichment of sPOM121ΔC to top co-regulated genes (
CTNNB1, CXXC1
, and
ENSA
) when compared with full-length sPOM121 (sPOM121-FL;
Fig. 4D
; Supplementary Fig. S4E). Remarkably, the expression of the sPOM121 CT fragment rescued this effect (
Fig. 4D
; Supplementary Fig. S4E). Importantly, sPOM121ΔC mutant expression resulted in a decrease in total and nascent mRNA of sPOM121 and SMARCA5 co-regulated genes when compared with sPOM121-FL (Supplementary Fig. S4F and S4G).
Moreover, detailed fluorescence microscopy analysis revealed that the CT deletion reduced sPOM121 at nucleoplasmic foci, whereas the expression of the CT fragment alone increased foci number compared with sPOM121-FL and sPOM121ΔC (
Fig. 4E
). Interestingly, the CT of sPOM121 is enriched in phenylalanine–glycine (FG) repeats (
31
–
33
), which are intrinsically disorder regions (IDR) reported to be important to form oncogenic condensates that act as transcriptional regulatory hubs (
15
–
17
,
34
,
35
). These results encouraged us to assess whether sPOM121 nucleoplasmic foci localize at condensates. Supporting sPOM121 localization at condensates, we observed that sPOM121 precipitated efficiently upon precipitation of IDR-containing proteins with biotinylated isoxazole (b-isox;
Fig. 4F
). Of note, the FG-containing CT fragment of sPOM121 was predominantly precipitated with b-isox (Supplementary Fig. S4H). Moreover, sPOM121 foci disappeared in a time-dependent manner after the treatment of prostate cancer cells with 1,6-hexanediol (HD), a compound used to dissolve condensates (
Fig. 4G
). In addition, fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) assays showed a dynamic diffusion of sPOM121 at nuclear foci with an average half time of 2.6 ± 1.2 seconds and a mobile fraction of 0.7 ± 0.10 (
Fig. 4H
).
To further delineate the functional role of sPOM121 localization at nuclear condensates, we mutated the phenylalanine residues in its FG repeats to serines (FS). We found that although the FS mutation led to a decrease in nucleoplasmic condensates of both sPOM121-FL and CT fragments (
Fig. 4I
) and impaired sPOM121 and SMARCA5 binding to co-regulated target genes (
Fig. 4J
), the interaction between sPOM121 and SMARCA5 remained intact (
Fig. 4K
; Supplementary Fig. S4I). Of note, these results were corroborated when dissolving condensates with HD and performing co-IP, PLA, and ChIP-qPCR assays (Supplementary Fig. S4J–S4L), suggesting that condensates facilitate sPOM121–SMARCA5 targeting to gene promoters. To further investigate this observation, we generated a chimera in which the FG-rich CT fragment in sPOM121 was substituted by the IDR of FUS, a protein known to form nuclear condensates (
36
–
39
). This domain swapping re-targeted sPOM121 to nuclear condensates (
Fig. 4L
) but impaired interaction with SMARCA5 (
Fig. 4M
; Supplementary Fig. S4M). In addition, immunofluorescence (IF)–DNA-FISH analysis showed that sPOM121 foci spatially co-localized with the
CTNNB1
promoter whereas the foci of FUS-IDR chimera did not (Supplementary Fig. S4N). Most importantly, we observed that both FS-mutant and sPOM121-FUS chimera significantly reduced expression levels of sPOM121 target genes (
Fig. 4N
).
Altogether, these results suggest that sPOM121 regulates gene expression by both interacting with key transcriptional co-regulators and targeting genes at nuclear condensates.

Remarkably, these studies revealed a strong overlap between the RNA-seq of sPOM121 knockdown and the ATAC-seq from both sPOM121 and SMARCA5 knockdown cells (
Fig. 5A
).

sPOM121 Boosts Prostate Cancer Aggressiveness through β-catenin Upregulation
Next, we sought to identify the main sPOM121 downstream effector pathways contributing to the aggressiveness of metastatic prostate cancer. We focused on the WNT/β-catenin pathway, as
CTNNB1
(β-catenin) gene expression strongly decreases after sPOM121 depletion (Supplementary Fig. S6A) and is known to have a role in tumor progression across a variety of tumor types (
41
–
45
), including prostate cancer (
46
–
50
). Confirming the role of sPOM121 in regulating β-catenin levels, we observed that either sPOM121 or SMARCA5 depletion decreased β-catenin protein expression (Supplementary Fig. S6B). sPOM121 Promotes Immune Evasion in Prostate Cancer
Based on the potential effect of sPOM121 in transcriptionally regulating immune biological processes (see

) and that the WNT/β-catenin pathway has been previously reported to enable immune evasion in multiple tumor types (
60

To determine whether the sPOM121 inhibits tumor immune surveillance, we used mouse prostate cancer cell lines (TRAMP-C2 and MyC-CaP) and immune-competent mice (C57BL/6 and FVB mice) to analyze antitumor immune responses. Remarkably, sPOM121 is not expressed in rodents as this isoform was a late evolutionary acquisition selected in hominids (
20

Next, we expressed sPOM121 in TRAMP-C2 and MyC-CaP cells and observed that sPOM121 expression increased both mRNA (Supplementary Fig. S7B) and protein levels of β-catenin (Supplementary Fig. S7C). To functionally study the impact of sPOM121 on immune evasion, we injected TRAMP-C2 and MyC-CaP cells expressing sPOM121 or EV subcutaneously into C57BL/6 and FVB/N male mice, respectively. Once tumors formed, Poly(I:C) was intratumorally injected to trigger an immune response. Poly(I:C) administration decreased the growth of control (EV) tumors, whereas the growth-inhibiting effect of Poly(I:C) disappeared in sPOM121-expressing tumors (

Notably, we observed that the lack of T cells in NOD/SCID mice decreased the differences in tumor growth between control and sPOM121 (Supplementary Fig. S7G) and β-catenin–overexpressing tumors (Supplementary Fig. S7H).

Discussion
The role of nucleoplasm-residing NUPs in solid tumors remains largely unknown. In this study, we demonstrate that sPOM121 plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis of prostate cancer, a frequent and deadly solid tumor in males (
69
,
70
), by enhancing the expression of oncogenic signaling pathways through its interaction with SMARCA5 at active transcriptional onco-condensates.
First, using complementary methods, including mining transcriptomic datasets of patients with prostate cancer, tissue RNA-ISH–RNAscope, and transcription electron microscopy analysis, sPOM121 was identified as the main off-pore nucleoplasm-residing NUP consistently upregulated in metastatic prostate cancer compared with normal prostate and primary prostate cancer. These clinical findings were further validated functionally
in vivo
, in which intracardiac injection of sPOM121-depleted and sPOM121-overexpressing prostate cancer cells significantly affected mouse survival in opposing directions.
Notably, sPOM121 loss- and gain-of-function studies, combining transcriptomic with epigenomic analyses, identified its enrichment at promoter sites of genes driving the aggressiveness of prostate cancer. Unbiased proteomic analysis mapping the sPOM121 chromatin interactome identified multiple proteins associated with active transcription. Detailed molecular studies revealed the chromatin remodeler enzyme SMARCA5, involved in activating transcription at gene promoters (
21
,
22
), as a main interacting protein and binding partner at sPOM121-regulated genes. Indeed, we demonstrate that the C-terminal fragment of sPOM121 is key in mediating its interaction with SMARCA5 and regulating sPOM121-dependent gene expression. Moreover, as the C-terminal fragment of sPOM121 contains FG repeats involved in liquid droplet formation and localization at nuclear condensates (
71
,
72
), we demonstrate that this protein region also facilitates sPOM121–SMARCA5 localization at active transcriptional condensates potentiating target gene activation. We hypothesize that this IDR of sPOM121 is required for both spatial organization into nuclear condensates, as well as a scaffold for the recruitment of transcriptional activators, as substitution for another IDR from FUS protein diminished both the interaction with SMARCA5 and expression of sPOM121 target genes. In this scenario, it is plausible that sPOM121 aids in establishing multicomplex chromatin-bound onco-condensates by recruiting additional co-factors besides SMARCA5, which remain to be studied to precisely determine how sPOM121 modulates gene expression.
Furthermore, from a conceptual standpoint, our results support the hypothesis that the hominoid-specific
POM121
locus evolution resulting in the alternative start site for nuclear sPOM121 and transmembrane TM-POM121 expression (
20
) can be co-opted by solid tumors, like prostate cancer, to promote disease progression via a dual-axis regulatory mechanism, whereas TM-POM121 enhances nuclear import of key TFs (
11
) and off-pore sPOM121 mediates transcription at nuclear onco-condensates. These findings reveal that isoform-specific functions of POM121 are not two incidental but rather two convergent evolutionary adaptations employed by prostate cancer cells to ensure tumor evolution.
Another key finding of this study is that
CTNNB1
, encoding β-catenin, is a principal transcriptional target of sPOM121. The WNT/β-catenin signaling pathway confers multifaceted pro-oncogenic properties in multiple tumor types, and in the context of prostate cancer, its activation is associated with disease progression (
46
–
50
). Although prior studies have predominantly attributed
CTNNB1
upregulation in the context of post-transcriptional regulation via miRNAs or long noncoding RNAs (
73
,
74
), here, we define a transcriptional control mechanism whereby sPOM121 together with SMARCA5 directly binds the
CTNNB1
promoter to modulate its expression and promote β-catenin–induced prostate cancer stemness, invasiveness, and resistance to therapy.
Beyond this, we also observed that sPOM121 drives immune evasion and confers poor response to immune checkpoint therapy, which is particularly relevant from a clinical standpoint because prostate tumors are among the least responsive cancer to immune checkpoint blockade due to their immunologically “cold” microenvironment. These results are in line with previous studies demonstrating in other tumor types how increased β-catenin expression induced by
CTNNB1
mutations, which increase β-catenin protein stability by preventing its degradation, is associated with immune evasion (
60
,
64
,
75
,
76
). In sum, here, we reveal a prostate cancer cell–intrinsic mechanism by which the sPOM121/β-catenin axis promotes immune evasion and confers poor response to immune checkpoint therapy.
From a therapeutic standpoint, we show that sPOM121-induced biological effects can be partially counteracted by targeting the WNT/β-catenin signaling pathway. Pharmacologic inhibition of WNT/β-catenin activity led to reduced tumor aggressiveness and resulted in increased intra-tumoral CD8
+
T-cell infiltration in sPOM121-expressing prostate cancer preclinical models. Remarkably, WNT/β-catenin inhibition increased the efficacy of anti–PD-1 therapy in sPOM121-expressing syngeneic mouse models, suggesting a novel therapeutic strategy to increase the limited responses to immune checkpoint blockade therapy observed in prostate cancer. These results provide proof of concept that disrupting sPOM121-dependent signaling may lead to novel therapeutic strategies and strongly support the rationale for developing targeted therapeutic strategies against NUPs, with sPOM121 emerging as a compelling candidate.
Finally, it is worth noting that, although the sPOM121/β-catenin axis is a major signaling pathway enhancing tumor progression, additional sPOM121-regulated molecules and mechanisms may contribute to this phenomenon. For instance, our ChIP-seq and transcriptomic data point toward a potential role of sPOM121 in regulating molecules involved in genomic integrity, such as ENSA, which have been recently shown to be involved in chromosomal instability tolerance in prostate cancer (
77
,
78
).
In all, the findings presented here position sPOM121 as a key orchestrator of a transcriptional hub at nuclear condensates that amplifies oncogenic programs, such as β-catenin, to drive aggressiveness in prostate cancer. Future work will be essential to define the full repertoire of sPOM121 downstream effectors, as well as to continue investigating the therapeutic potential of interfering with off-pore nucleoplasm-localized NUPs and their significant contribution to cancer pathogenesis.

RNA-ISH in Prostate Cancer Cells and Human Tissues
The RNAscope 2.5 HD Duplex assay was performed by Advanced Cell Diagnostics following their proprietary high quality-controlled method for ISH to visualize single RNA molecules per cell in FFPE tissues. This methodology allowed the detection of sPOM121 mRNA at a single-cell resolution with appropriate spatial context. Duplex probes were designed and produced by Advanced Cell Diagnostics. The BaseScope probes were custom designed to target unique sequences in the 5′UTR exon 2 to 3 junction of sPOM121 transcript (red) and in the TM coding exon of TM-POM121 (green/teal). Standard RNA Pol II subunit A (
POLR2A

BaseScope was performed following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, freshly cut FFPE tissues or cell line pellets were de-paraffinized, permeabilized, and treated with peroxidase hydrogen blocker before boiling at 98°C to 100°C in target retrieval for 15 minutes. Paired double-Z oligonucleotide probes were hybridized for 2 hours at 40°C, followed by a series of signal amplification and washing steps. All the incubation steps at 40°C were performed in a HybEZ Hybridization System. Hybridization signals were detected by chromogenic reaction using the FAST Red and Green dyes. Specificity of the probes was evaluated in prostate cancer cell lines embedded in FFPE after being treated with control or sPOM121 siRNA. Digital slide scanning (40X/image) of RNA-ISH assay in human prostate cell lines and tumor tissue slides was performed in the Aperio CS2 Digital Image Scanning system. Digital images of each sample were visually assessed and the average number of probe signals per cell was quantified for the entire tissue core. The scores ranged between 0 and 4 (“0”: negative or an average of less than 1 signal dot per 20 cells; “1”: 1–3 dots per cell; “2”: 4–10 dots per cell with very few dot clusters; “3”: >10 dots per cell with less than 10% positive cells having dot clusters; and “4”: >10 dots per cell with more than 10% positive cells having dot clusters). An average of five distinct regions was scored for each tissue sample.
IHC
IHC analyses were conducted on prostate cancer FFPE tissue sections from human samples, PDXs, and syngeneic mouse tumors. Tissue sections (5 μm) were deparaffinized and processed following standard peroxidase-based IHC procedures using antibodies against POM121 (GeneTex, RRID: AB_10732546), β-catenin (BD Biosciences, RRID: AB_397555), CD8a mouse and human (Cell Signaling Technology, RRID: AB_2756376 and RRID: AB_2800052), and granzyme B mouse and human (Cell Signaling Technology, RRID: AB_2857976 and Abcam, RRID: AB_2114427). Quantification of positive cells was determined by counting the number of tumor cells in 10 contiguous high-power fields in three different areas of each section and referred to the total number of counted cells. Samples were classified as sPOM121 “low” (<10% prostate cancer cells with nucleoplasmic sPOM121) or “high” (≥10% of prostate cancer cells with nucleoplasmic sPOM121).

ChIP was performed as described previously (
77
). In brief, cells were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde solution for 10 minutes at room temperature and quenched by 0.125 mol/L glycine. Chromatin was obtained by lysing cells in serial nuclear extraction buffers LB1 (50 mmol/L HEPES-KOH pH 7.5, 140 mmol/L NaCl, 1 mmol/L EDTA, 10% glycerol, 0.5% NP-40/IGEPAL CA-630, and 0.25% Triton X-100), LB2 (10 mmol/L Tris-HCL pH 8, 200 mmol/L NaCl, 1 mmol/L EDTA, and 0.5 mmol/L EGTA), and LB3 (10 mmol/L Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 100 mmol/L NaCl, 1 mmol/L EDTA, 0.5 mmol/L EGTA, 0.1% Na-deoxycholate and 0.5% N-lauroylsarcosine sodium salt) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Sonicated chromatin was used for IPs with the following antibodies (5 μg) pre-bound to magnetic beads (Dynabeads, Invitrogen): POM121 (GeneTex, RRID: AB_10732546), SMARCA5 (Abcam, RRID: AB_2191856), GFP (Cell Signaling Technology, RRID: AB_1196615), or normal rabbit IgG (Abcam, RRID:AB_2687657) and incubated overnight at 4°C with rotation. RIME
RIME was used to identify sPOM121 protein interactors at chromatin as described before (
83
) comparing control and sPOM121 depletion in three prostate cancer cell lines (22Rv1, DU145, and VCaP). Briefly, 48 hours after knockdowns (control or sPOM121 siRNA), 3 × 10
7
cells were washed with PBS, cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde, and quenched with glycine solution for 5 minutes. Cells were collected, washed in ice-cold PBS, and pelleted before flash freeze. Chromatin was obtained by lysing cell pellets in serial nuclear extraction buffers LB1, LB2, and LB3 following similar steps as in ChIP methodology and sonicated on a Bioruptor Pico sonicator (Diagenode) for 30 cycles of 30 seconds to shear DNA to an average length of 200bp. Triton X-100 was added to the fragmented chromatin to a final 1% before IP with magnetic beads prebound to POM121 antibody (GeneTex, RRID: AB_10732546) or normal rabbit IgG (Cell Signaling Technology, RRID: AB_1031062) overnight at 4°C with rotation. Magnetic beads were washed 10 times with RIPA buffer and twice with 100 mmol/L ammonium bicarbonate, all supernatant was removed, and beads were frozen at –80°C and submitted to the Proteomics and Metabolomics Core Facility at The Wistar Institute for on-bead trypsin digestion, LC/MS-MS, and preliminary analysis.
Peptide mixtures were loaded directly onto the analytic column and analyzed by Q Exactive Plus Mass Spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Reversed-phase analytic separation was performed using a C
18
column. Solvent A was 0.1% formic acid/water and solvent B was 0.1% formic acid/80% acetonitrile. Fractions were run on a 70-minute gradient with solvent B: 5% to 28% B over 60 minutes, 28% to 40% B over 5 minutes, 40% to 90% B over 1 minute, and constant 90% B for 4 minutes. Column was re-equilibrated at initial conditions for 10 minutes. Potential carryover between samples was minimized by injecting solvent A and using a 30-minute gradient (5%–90% B) with the same solvents between experimental runs (blank run). Tandem mass spectra were extracted by Proteome Discoverer version 2.3 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and UniProt human database (January 10, 2018) using MaxQuant 1.6.3.3 (Matrix Science). Peptide identifications were accepted if FDR was less than 1% and at least a single razor and unique peptide. The abundance of a protein in a sample was determined by log
2
transformation of the intensity values, and fold changes were also calculated based on intensity values. Enriched sPOM121 binding proteins at chromatin were identified by comparing siRNA control and sPOM121 and confirming absence in the IgG control. To improve the confidence of the identified proteins, only proteins with at least two razor and unique peptides in any of the siRNA control and siRNA sPOM121 samples were considered as positive.

Quantification and Statistical Analysis
Statistical analysis and graphical representation were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (GraphPad, RRID: SCR_002798) and SPSS version 19.0 (RRID: SCR_00286). Data are expressed as mean ± SD from at least three independent experiments. Sample size and number of independent experiments (replicates) used are specified in each figure or figure legends. For
in vivo
animal work, group size and power calculation were based on our previous experience and publications. Statistical analyses were performed using the Student’s
t

P
