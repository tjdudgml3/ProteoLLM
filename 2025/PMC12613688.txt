Background
The tick
Rhipicephalus (Boophilus) microplus
is associated with significant economic losses for beef cattle production because it can cause skin lesions, anemia, weight loss, health problems, including tick-borne diseases such as anaplasmosis and babesiosis, and even mortality in animals [
1
]. The serious economic losses resulting from tick infestations mainly occur in tropical climate regions where these parasites develop more easily [
2
].
Tick control strategies involve costs with acaricides and intensive labor. Synthetic chemical ixodicides are the most commonly used tool for tick control; however, the indiscriminate use of these products has led to the selection of drug-resistant ticks, thus reducing effective measures to combat tick infestation [
2
,
3
]. Another problem caused by the indiscriminate use of chemical acaricides is the presence of chemical residues in animal products for human consumption as well as in the environment, harming the health of consumers and compromising natural resources [
4
]. Thus, the use of chemical acaricides is not a sustainable strategy because of economic, consumer, and environmental concerns and the development of effective control strategies of tick infestations is necessary. Although vaccines targeting
R. microplus
hold promise for integrated control strategies, their implementation remains challenged by key limitations, including genetic variability of tick populations, incomplete and short-lived protection, and high associated costs [
5
,
6
]. Within this context, genetic selection for improved resistance of cattle to ticks remains the most sustainable alternative to reduce tick infestation in the next generations, which may be combined with other widely used strategies [
7
,
8
].
The evaluation of tick resistance in breeding programs is a possible alternative for the selection of resistant animals. However, this approach requires the direct collection of animal phenotypes based on tick counts, which is an extremely laborious process and not feasible for large-scale breeding programs. Knowledge of the genomic architecture of cattle resistance to ticks will contribute to identifying biomarkers for this economically important trait. Such biomarkers could be used to quickly identify animals that are resistant to ticks. Several genomic [
9
–
12
] and transcriptomic studies [
7
,
13
]– [
14
] aimed at detecting biomarkers for tick resistance have emerged in recent years, which showed that tick resistance in cattle is controlled by many genes. However, ncRNAs participate at every stage of the tick-host interaction, subverting host responses to ticks [
15
] and contributing to the complexity of this trait. Therefore, additional approaches are necessary to validate genomic and transcriptomic studies, which would contribute to elucidating the mechanisms underlying the resistance of cattle to ticks, specifically at the proteomic level.
Proteomic studies investigating tick resistance in cattle are scarce [
8
,
16
,
17
], despite the great economic impact of this trait on livestock production. Proteomics is a powerful tool that is widely used for large-scale investigation of protein content, functions, interactions, and connections among proteins in a given cell or tissue [
18
], allowing us to understand the fluctuations in protein abundance levels under different physiological conditions. Blood plasma is an ideal biological fluid to investigate potential biomarkers for tick resistance by proteome analysis since it can be obtained by a relatively simple semi-invasive procedure and changes in the blood plasma proteome due to tick infestation occur earlier than in other tissues [
8
]. Furthermore, the blood plasma proteome carries information of the overall health status of the animal at the systemic level since it interacts will all tissues of the organism through circulating factors [
17
].
It is well established in the literature that zebu cattle (
Bos taurus indicus
) are more resistant to tick infestations than taurine cattle (
Bos taurus taurus
) [
19
–
21
]. However, tropically adapted taurine cattle breeds may show greater resistance due to their rusticity and become more productive in their own environments than exotic breeds [
22
]. The Caracu beef cattle breed originated from uncontrolled crossings between Alentejana, Arouquesa, Barrosã, Minhota, and Mirandesa cattle breeds brought from the Iberian Peninsula by Portuguese settlers in the 16th century. The breed has undergone an intense process of natural selection as a result of high environmental temperatures, food scarcity, and the presence of parasites [
23
], which resulted in a rustic breed.
Caracu is currently considered a genetic reservoir of
B. taurus
and corresponds to the largest herd among Brazilian naturally adapted cattle breeds owing to its rusticity, adaptability, resistance to tick, and good productive and reproductive performances [
24
–
29
]. Nowadays, Caracu cattle are used in crossbreeding programs, particularly with zebu breeds, producing animals with high heterosis and favorable meat quality traits. A study evaluating tick counts in cattle following three successive artificial infestations reported that Nellore (8.20 ± 5.74 ticks), Caracu (107.40 ± 84.56 ticks), and Simmental (293.00 ± 189.71 ticks) exhibited distinct resistance profiles, with Caracu showing an intermediate level of resistance [
22
]. Another study assessing tick counts after artificial infestation reported that Caracu (125.7 ± 65.5 ticks) exhibited higher resistance than Senepol (273.2 ± 193.5 ticks), highlighting the superior tick resistance of Caracu among taurine breeds [
29
].
Since studies on tick resistance in tropically adapted taurine cattle breeds are limited, proteomic analysis will substantially increase our understanding of how adapted breeds respond to tick infestation and which proteins contribute to the development of tick resistance, thus allowing us to develop strategies that reduce tick infestation. Therefore, the aim of this study was to identify biological processes, pathways, and key proteins related to the responses of a tropically adapted taurine cattle breed to
R. microplus
tick infestation based on changes in the blood plasma proteomes of tick-resistant and -susceptible animals.
Methods
Experimental groups
Resistance to
R. microplus
was assessed based on tick counts in 162 naturally tick-infested Caracu cows over a period of 18 months, with an interval of 60 days between each tick count, totaling eight evaluations, as described by [
27
]. All cows, from the same herd, were raised in a rotational grazing system using
Brachiaria sp.
forage and supplemented with mineral salt on an experimental farm located in Sertãozinho, São Paulo, Brazil (21° 17’ S and 48° 12’ W). Briefly, each cow was restrained in a cattle crush for direct counting of adult female ticks through observation and feeling by rubbing the palm of a hand on the animal’s body.
The mean tick count of all eight evaluations was used to rank the animals. Blood samples for proteomic analysis were obtained from a subset of 16 extreme animals, including the eight most resistant (lowest tick counts, RES group) and the eight most susceptible (highest tick counts, SUS group) at the end of the 18 months period.
Blood samples were collected from the jugular vein of each animal into 6-mL BD Vacutainer™ K
2
EDTA tubes. The samples were centrifuged at 3,000×
g
for 15 min at 18 °C and 500 µL of plasma was immediately stored at −80 °C until the time of liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis.
Proteomic analysis
Proteomic analysis was carried out as previously described by [
26
], with minor modifications. The albumin present in plasma samples was removed using Cibacron Blue agarose beads (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) packed in Pierce™ screw-cap spin columns (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For each sample, 10 µL of plasma was diluted in 590 µl of Tris-binding buffer (50 mM, pH = 8.0) and applied to the column, followed by incubation for 30 min on an orbital shaker. The eluent was collected and reapplied to the column to increase albumin retention. The collected samples represent albumin-depleted plasma. The samples were dried in a speed vac, resuspended in 20 µL of Tris-Cl (pH = 8.0) containing 8 M urea, and used in proteomic analysis.
For the confirmation of albumin depletion, the electrophoretic profile of individual samples was evaluated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) on 12% Precise™ Protein Gels (Thermo Scientific, USA). The samples with ~ 20 µg protein were resuspended in XT Sample Buffer (Bio-Rad, USA) containing 20 µg dithiothreitol to break disulfide bonds and stabilize proteins, followed by incubation for 5 min at 100 °C. The samples were then alkylated by incubation with 100 µg iodoacetamide for 20 min at room temperature. Electrophoresis was performed in a Mini-PROTEAN II Electrophoresis System (Bio-Rad, USA) at 40 V/gel for 35 min, followed by 70 V for 2 h. The gel was stained with GelCode™ Blue Stain Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).
The Bradford method [
30
] was applied for protein quantification using Protein Assay Dye Reagent Concentrate (Bio-Rad, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A standard curve was constructed with different dilutions of bovine serum albumin (Sigma, USA). The samples were evaluated in triplicates of 20 µL each and the total protein mass in each sample was estimated by measuring absorbance at 595 nm in a SpectraMax Plus 384 spectrophotometer (Molecular Devices, USA).
A 50-µg aliquot of each albumin-depleted sample was prepared for mass spectrometry, which consisted of protein reduction and alkylation, enzymatic digestion with trypsin, and desalting. Briefly, protein disulfide bonds were reduced by the addition of 50 µg dithiothreitol and incubation for 1 h at 37 °C. Alkylation of the proteins was performed by the addition of 250 µg iodoacetamide and incubation for 1 h at room temperature. The sample volume was diluted 5 times with 0.05 M Tris-HCl (pH = 8.8), followed by overnight incubation with 0.5 µg trypsin at 37 °C. Lastly, the samples were desalted using a C18 resin (Supelco, USA) equilibrated with a 2% acetonitrile solution containing 0.1% formic acid and the material of interest was eluted with 50% acetonitrile.
The samples were dried in a SpeedVac (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) in low protein absorption tubes (Eppendorf, USA) and 1 µg of each sample was injected into an Orbitrap Eclipse latest generation mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) coupled to a nanoflow chromatography system (Dionex Ultimate 3000 RLSCnano System, Thermo Fisher Scientific, DE). The peptides were separated on a NanoEase MZ Peptide BEH C18 Column (130Å, 1.7 μm, 75 μm x 250 mm; Waters, USA) at a flow rate of 300 nL/min over an acetonitrile gradient of 4–50% acetonitrile for 90 min. MS1 spectra were acquired in the range of M/Z 375–1500 (120,000 resolution, automatic gain control (AGC) target of 1E6 ions, with a maximum injection time of 100 ms). The most abundant ions were submitted to MS/MS at a resolution of 15,000, collision energy of 30%, isolation window of 1.2 m/z, and AGC target of 1E5.
Data analysis
The Trans-Proteomic Pipeline (TPP) was used for the processing, validation, and quantification of the proteomic data obtained by LC-MS/MS. Raw data were converted to mzXML using the ProteoWizard suite [
31
] and processed using the Comet v.2018 algorithm [
32
] against the UniProt bovine reference genome (Proteome ID: UP000009136, accessed Jan 2025), consisting of 59,260 protein sequences. Peptide Prophet [
33
] and Protein Prophet [
34
] algorithms were used for data validation and scoring. Peptides with FDR ≤ 5% were retained for protein identification to enhance proteome coverage, addressing the inherent limitations in sensitivity associated with plasma proteomics due to the exceptionally high dynamic range of protein concentrations. The intensities for each peptide were extracted for quantification with the Xpress algorithm [
35
], with a precursor mass tolerance of 20 ppm. The peptides and their intensities were grouped to generate the protein intensity using a script in R language [
36
]. Proteins with signal intensity ratios ≥ 2 (log-fold change ≥ 1) and ≤ 0.5 (log-fold change ≤ −1) (
P <
0.05) were considered to be differentially abundant.
Functional enrichment analysis was performed using the DAVID v.2023q4 tool [
37
] for all differentially abundant proteins identified between the SUS and RES groups and proteins detected exclusively in one of the groups to identify significant (
P <
0.05) gene ontology (GO) terms (GO biological processes and GO molecular functions) and pathways (KEGG and REACTOME).
Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis of differentially abundant proteins identified between the SUS and RES groups and proteins detected exclusively in one of the groups was performed with STRING v.12.0 [
38
] using the following parameters: full STRING network type (edges indicate both functional and physical interactions), default confidence score (0.4, medium confidence level), four active interaction sources (databases, experiments, text mining, and co-expression), and confidence as the meaning of edges. All disconnected nodes in the PPI network were removed.
Results
Herd-level tick count data have been reported previously [
27
], with an average of 13 ± 12.3 ticks. The mean tick count in the RES group (3.05 ± 1.61) was significantly lower than that in the SUS group (35.91 ± 10.32) across all eight evaluations. During blood collection in spring, the difference between groups increased substantially, with 0.5 ± 0.92 and 69.75 ± 21.99 ticks observed in the RES and SUS groups, respectively.
In our previous study performed with the same herd [
27
], we observed that tick infestation was significantly influenced by the age of the animal, where cows exhibited higher infestation levels than heifers (
P
< 0.0001). Based on this consideration, only cows were included in the proteomic analysis, and no significant differences in age were observed between the SUS (57.00 ± 6.04 months of age) and RES (58.62 ± 5.70 months of age) groups.

In addition, 152 differentially abundant proteins were detected, of which the abundance of 51 and 101 proteins was significantly increased in plasma samples of the SUS and RES groups, respectively (Additional file S2). Functional enrichment analysis
The changes in blood plasma proteomes cause by tick infestation occur early compared to other samples like skin [
8
], which is an advantage when investigating biomarkers for tick resistance. This study also provided valuable insights into biological processes, metabolic pathways, and potential biomarkers that contribute to tick resistance in an adapted cattle breed, with the results showing a clear difference in blood plasma protein content between the RES and SUS groups.
The biological processes, molecular functions, pathways, and potential biomarkers detected herein, which may contribute to the resistance of cattle to ticks, as well as their relationship with physiological processes known to drive tick resistance in cattle, are discussed below.
Hemostasis
Hemostasis (R-BTA-109582) is a physiological mechanism that regulates, prevents, and stops bleeding after blood vessel injury. This process involves a complex interaction between vascular and extravascular receptors [
71
]. Hemostatic factors interact directly and indirectly with both the complement system and the coagulation system to promote homeostasis and a protective response to tissue damage and inflammation [
72
]. After vascular injury caused by penetration of the tick hypostome into the host’s skin, the hemostasis system is activated quickly and in a controlled manner, starting with vasoconstriction, the formation of blood clots, and the action of anticoagulant factors, events that culminate in the occlusion of vascular injuries and maintenance of blood vessel integrity [
73
].
The complement system is involved in both innate and adaptive immune responses, playing a central role in host homeostasis, inflammatory responses, and protection against pathogens [
74
]. This system comprises a cascade of different proteins, receptors, and regulators expressed in the membrane that act at different locations, such as plasma, tissues, and cells [
74
]. The essential role of the complement system in both adaptive and innate immunity suggests its potential contribution to protecting the host against tick infestation [
16
]. In addition, the complement system interferes with both cell activation and cell migration to the site of tick attachment, which results in the rejection of ticks [
8
] or failure of female engorgement [
75
], contributing effectively to the expression of tick resistance. Studies have shown that tick infestation alters the number of different complement system proteins in serum of tick-susceptible and -resistant cattle [
8
,
16
]. The enrichment of biological processes such as complement activation (GO_BP:0006956) and complement activation, classical pathway (GO_BP:0006958) observed in the present study supports the critical role of the complement system in host resistance against tick infestation.
The coagulation system works together with the complement system. The former participates in the body’s first line of defense, protecting the host’s blood circulation through the formation of blood clots, storage and release of immune mediators, and induction of the inflammatory response [
76
]. Blood clotting is an extremely important host defense mechanism against tick infestation, impairing the blood supply to ticks [
8
]. A greater abundance of proteins related to the coagulation cascade are found in serum of tick-resistant animals when compared to susceptible animals [
7
,
8
,
16
]. A transcriptome study of cattle skin also demonstrated differentially expressed genes related to the coagulation cascade between susceptible and resistant animals [
75
]. Studies have shown higher expression of genes involved in blood clotting and wound healing in tick-resistant cattle compared to susceptible animals [
8
,
75
,
77
], reducing tick infestation. The enrichment of blood coagulation–related metabolic pathways identified in the present study – including platelet activation, signaling and aggregation (R-BTA-76002), platelet degranulation (R-BTA-114608), and complement and coagulation cascades (bta04610) - corroborates previous research that molecular components of the coagulation cascade actively mediate host defense mechanisms against tick infestation.

Albumin (

Hemoglobin subunit beta (

Vesicular transport
Endocytosis (bta04144) is a key pathway for several functions in the organism, including signal transduction, regulation of cell surface receptors, cell morphogenesis, and homeostasis [
89

This process is fundamental for tissue homeostasis and the innate immune response, in addition to providing a link to the adaptive immune response [

Cell proliferation and migration
As reviewed by [
95
], the proliferation and migration of cells to an inflammatory site are a crucial aspect of host immunity. During tick feeding, immune cells and key molecules recognize skin damage and secrete proteins that enhance the recruitment of immune effectors to the bite site to promote inflammation, hemostasis, tissue repair, and healing. However, tick salivary molecules secreted at the bite site influence the host’s immunological and hemostatic responses by modulating the proliferation and migration of leukocytes, keratinocytes, and endothelial cells [
96
]. The host’s wound healing is thus delayed, and hemostasis and immune responses (innate and adaptive) are disturbed, leading to the successful attachment and development of tick larvae.
In a transcriptome study of skin biopsies from Braford animals resistant and susceptible to tick infestation [
42
], cellular activation and migration were anti-tick responses observed for all immune cell types and mostly represented by growth factors, chemokines, cytokines, and other biological molecules in tick-resistant hosts. In the present study, the enrichment of biological processes such as somatic stem cell population maintenance (GO_BP:0035019), epithelial cell proliferation (GO_BP:0050673), positive regulation of fibroblast migration (GO_BP:0010763), and regulation of cell migration (GO_BP:0030334) may also contribute to resistance to tick infestation by modulating cell proliferation and migration, thereby facilitating hemostasis and tissue repair.
Hippo signaling pathway (R-BTA-2028269) is a regulatory network that orchestrates cell proliferation, differentiation and survival which, in turn, regulate homeostasis, growth, and the repair and remodeling of tissues [
97
]. Hippo signaling also contributes to angiogenesis, vasculogenesis, and lymphangiogenesis [
98
]. In addition, Hippo signaling modulates the immune response and inflammation, protecting the h.ost tissue against injury and contributing to the tissue repair process [
99
].

Fibroblast growth factor 5 (

Integrins are heterodimeric integral transmembrane proteins composed of non-covalently associated alpha and beta subunits. A given chain may combine with multiple partners, which results in different integrins that participate in cell surface adhesion and signaling [
106
]. Integrins play key roles in cell proliferation, cell migration, apoptosis, hematopoiesis, blood coagulation, tissue repair, and angiogenesis, as well as in inflammation and infectious processes [
106
,

Integrin alpha-1 and integrin alpha-4 were highly abundant in the SUS group, while integrin beta-3 was abundant in the RES group. Integrin alpha-1 is involved in cell-cell adhesion and may play a role in inflammation and fibrosis [
108

ITGA1
as a putative gene influencing resistance to tick infestation in Braford cattle [
42

Integrin beta-3, in turn, is involved in different processes, including regulation of T-cell migration, cellular response to platelet-derived growth factor stimulus, regulation of cell-substrate adhesion, and regulation of fibroblast migration. Integrin beta-3 associates with the alpha IIb chain in platelets, playing a critical role in hemostasis through the modulation of platelet functions such as adhesion and aggregation [

Calcium plays a dynamic role by providing signals that control a range of essential cellular processes. Its role in host defense mechanisms [

115

Actin
Actin is one of the most abundant and versatile proteins in mammalian cells. Several fundamental cellular processes, such as mitosis, endocytosis, and motility, require the assembly, maintenance, and disassembly of actin filament networks [

A proteomic study of plasma-derived small extracellular vesicles in cattle with high and low tick resistance also detected several differentially abundant proteins related to actin filament binding. Although myosins are actin-based motor proteins that are mainly involved in the generation of mechanical energy in eukaryotic cells, they have the capacity to enhance thrombin generation and anticoagulant activity, contributing to hemostasis [

Lipids and lipoproteins
Lipids are fundamental components, and their main functions include the supply of energy to the body. Lipoproteins are essential for the transport of lipids throughout the body; in addition, they play a crucial role in the defense against pathogenic microorganisms, facilitating removal from areas of invasion and infection [

Although the factors underlying this relationship remain unknown, it is important to highlight that blood cholesterol levels can interfere with the ability of animals to respond to external stimuli, directly influencing the immune response and metabolic homeostasis in response to environmental challenges such as tick infestation [

Apolipoprotein A-I (

Scavenger receptors
Scavenger receptors are membrane-associated pattern recognition receptors that are mainly involved in host-pathogen interactions, inflammation signaling, innate and adaptive immunity, and homeostasis [
138
]. Scavenger receptors trigger immune responses against a repertoire of ligands through diverse mechanisms like endocytosis, phagocytosis, and adhesion [

Hydrogen peroxide (H

Tyrosine
Tyrosine is a non-essential amino acid. At higher concentrations in the host, tyrosine can selectively damage and kill hematophagous arthropods, including ticks [

Ten proteins were found to be related to tyrosine metabolism (bta00350) or protein tyrosine phosphatase activity (GO_MF:0004725) (Additional file S4), where members of the protein tyrosine phosphatase family are highlighted, in addition to the previously discussed tensin-2 protein. Receptor-type tyrosine-protein phosphatase F (

All protein tyrosine phosphatases detected herein were more abundant in the SUS group. This finding may be due to the role of these proteins in the modulation of protein tyrosine phosphorylation, one of the key regulatory mechanisms in which activation signals are transmitted to platelets in order to respond to vascular injury [

10 Insulin-like growth factor
The insulin-like growth factor (IGF) system is a complex system of ligands, receptors, and binding proteins (IGFBPs) that play a vital role in all aspects of physiology [
148
]. IGFBPs bind IGFs in the circulation, controlling the function of the latter by prolonging their half-life and modulating tissue access [
148
]. IGFs have been shown to participate in immune responses and in tissue repair [
149
]. In studies on wound healing, the activity of IGF-I was enhanced by IGFBPs [
148
]. A role of IGFs in tick infestation of cattle was attributed to their selective basophil chemoattractant properties [
62
].
Ten proteins related to the regulation of IGF transport and uptake by IGFBPs pathway (R-BTA-381426) were identified (Additional file S4), where fibrillin-1 is highlighted, in addition to the proteins encoded by the previously discussed

Conclusion
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study that described differences in blood plasma proteomes between tick-resistant and tick-susceptible animals of a tropically adapted taurine cattle breed. The present results provide important insights into the molecular mechanisms involved in responses to
R. microplus
infestation, contributing to the identification of potential biomarkers underlying tick resistance and susceptibility in a tropically adapted taurine breed.
