Introduction
Heart failure (HF) represents a major health burden in developed countries, with current therapeutic approaches largely failing to target the underlying pathophysiology of the disease [
1
]. Existing treatments, including pharmacotherapy in earlier stages and left ventricular assist device (LVAD) implantation and heart transplantation in more advanced stages, are non-specific in terms of the underlying pathology and are often insufficient in addressing the complex molecular mechanisms driving heart failure [
2–7
]. Heart disease is usually triggered by stimuli such as hypertension, valve disease, or myocardial infarction, which induce changes in gene expression that promote structural and functional alterations in the heart [
4
,
5
,
8
]. These changes compromise the heart’s ability to pump blood efficiently, triggering compensatory remodeling. Initially, this adaptive response manifests as cardiac hypertrophy, which temporarily enhances pump function and decreases wall tension. Although considered to be initially compensatory and reversible, persistent pathological stress leads to progressive myocardial growth and fibrosis, culminating in heart failure [
9–12
]. Even in the instances of recovery following heart failure, the heart rarely regains its pre-disease state and instead enters a pathological phase characterized by impaired function that is neither failing nor completely healthy [
13
].
These disease-associated changes leading to heart failure are frequently governed by global changes in gene transcription that are preceded by epigenetic alterations to chromatin. Gene expression can be activated or silenced, depending on the chromatin environment, which is influenced by chromatin accessibility by transcription factors and other transcriptional machinery complexes [
14
,
15
]. One epigenetic mechanism that contributes to the chromatin landscape and gene expression is the post-translational modification (PTMs) of histone proteins [
16–18
]. Histones are subjected to many PTMs, including methylation on arginine (R) or lysine (K) residues. Regarding lysine methylation, this residue can be reversibly methylated and can accept up to three methyl groups (mono-, di-, or tri-methylation, abbreviated as me1, me2, and me3, respectively) [
19
].
Analysis of global changes in histone PTMs can provide insights into how the transcriptome changes in response to stress or disease. Currently, only three studies have examined the global abundance of specific histone methylation sites in the context of heart disease, namely: histone H3 lysine 4 tri-methylation (H3K4me3), H3 Lysine 9 tri-methylation (H3K9me3), and H3 lysine 36 tri-methylation (H3K36me3) in human heart failure patients and rodent models of heart failure [
20–23
]. These studies have shown that there are global increases in H3K4me3 abundance and decreases in H3K9me3 in cardiac tissue from mice or humans in heart failure [
20
,
22
]. In addition, studies in both rats and humans used ChIP-Seq-based profiling to show that the genomic regions containing H3K4me3, H3K9me3, or H3K36me3 change substantially in cardiac tissue from heart failure patients compared to healthy donor controls [
22
,
23
], indicating a significant shift in which genes are being actively transcribed. These results suggest that the modulation of histone methylation is important in the development and progression of heart disease. However, studies examining global changes at other sites of methylation on histones have not been carried out.
In general, histone H4K20 methylation has been implicated in gene silencing, heterochromatin formation, mitosis, and genomic stability [
24
,
25
] (
Figure 1A
). More specifically, mono- and di-methylation of H4K20 are marks found on active genes implicated in cell cycle regulation and DNA repair [
26
], while tri-methylation is most associated with heterochromatin formation and gene repression [
24
]. Historically, histone H4K20 methylation has been studied primarily in human HeLa cells, fruit flies, and mouse embryonic fibroblast cells [
25
,
27–32
]. In these non-cardiac cells, the relative abundance of each methylation state was quantified and shown that di-methylation is the most abundant at up to ~80% of histone H4K20 residues, mono-methylation accounts for ~10%, and tri-methylation is the least abundant mark and accounts for ~5%, with the remaining 5% being unmodified [
25
,
26
,
33–37
] (
Figure 1B
). The addition of each of these methyl groups is carried out by separate enzymes called lysine methyltransferases (MTs), and these methyl marks are selectively removed by lysine demethylases (DMs) [
15
,
38
]. Interestingly, in contrast to the 16 methyltransferases identified that regulate histone H3K4 [
15
,
39
,
40
], there are relatively few enzymes known to regulate H4K20 methylation [
38
]. Specifically, there are six histone methyltransferases and six demethylases that have been shown to regulate H4K20 (
Figure 1A
), with no single known methyltransferase or demethylase that adds/removes all three (mono-, di, and tri-methylation) marks, indicating the necessity of cooperative addition and subtraction of the histone H4K20 methylation marks [
21
]. This may also suggest that other enzymes that regulate this PTM have not been identified to date.

We recently utilized cell and animal models of cardiac dysfunction to examine histone H4K20 tri-methylation abundance. Our recently published data show that histone H4K20me3 is increased in isolated cardiomyocytes during acute hypertrophic stress but is decreased in cardiac tissue after long-term exposure to isoproterenol (ISO, a β-adrenergic receptor agonist known to induce cardiac hypertrophy) or ischemic injury in mice (
Figure 1C
) [
21
].
Histone MTs and DMs regulating H4K20 methylation have been challenging to detect, with low expression or no detection in large data sets such as the Human Proteome Atlas [
38
]. Consequently, only a few datasets have reported the detection of these enzymes in the heart, with even less known about their role in cardiac disease [
38
]. To address this gap, we investigated the expression of all methyltransferases and demethylases that regulate methylation on histone H4K20, including six MTs and six DMs, in various models of cardiac disease by utilizing isolated cells as well as cardiac tissue from mice and human patients. In addition, we performed the first profiling of H4K20me2 in the human heart.
Materials and methods
The experiments detailed in this manuscript adhere to ARRIVE guidelines.
Cell culture
H9c2 cardiac myoblast cells acquired from the American Type Culture Collection or ATCC (Manassas, VA) were cultured in growth medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) with 1% penicillin/streptomycin, changing media as needed (every 2–3 days) until they were 70% confluent, then split. Cell passage numbers were kept under 10. The H9c2 cells were differentiated, following established protocols [
41–43
]. Specifically, 48 hours after plating, the media was switched to 1% FBS with 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and changed daily [
43
]. Differentiated cells were treated with sterile phenylephrine (Sigma Aldrich, PE; 100 μM for 24 or 48 hours) and harvested for western blotting and qRT-PCR analysis.
Quantitative real time PCR analysis
Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, as previously published [
44
,
45
]. Total RNA was transcribed using SuperScript III First‐Strand Synthesis system for qRT‐PCR (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol to produce cDNA. The cDNA transcripts were amplified on the Bio-Rad CFXConnect Real-Time System with SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad). Expression levels were normalized against loading control by subtracting the mean cycle number for each experimental group from the mean cycle number for the loading control from the same group. Fold change was calculated using the ΔΔCt method (delta delta Ct). The average was calculated with error bars generated from the standard error of the mean. Significance was determined with the unpaired Student’s
t
-test (

,

,
4D
,
5B

5C-G

Figures 2G-J
,
3G-H
,
4H-K
), with significant values indicated with
p
< 0.05. Each qRT-PCR assay was performed in technical replicates from at least three biological replicates. While statistical significance was used as a primary criterion for reporting differential expression, observed changes were interpreted within the context of technical reproducibility and consistency across biological replicates. The enzymes evaluated in this study have never been investigated in the rodent or human heart. Accordingly, considerable time and effort were dedicated to the development and cross-validation of these primers, and the sequences of those validated are listed in supplementary table S1.

Electrophoresis and western blotting
Western blotting analysis was carried out as previously published [
21
,
45
]. Briefly, cells were harvested in lysis buffer containing 0.15% Nonidet P-40, 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 15 mM NaCl and protease inhibitor cocktail. Proteins were separated by standard SDS-PAGE using Laemmli buffer, then transferred to nitrocellulose membrane and stained with Ponceau S to confirm uniform loading and protein transfer. Membranes were then blocked with 10% powdered milk reconstituted in 1xTris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween detergent (TBST), and protein signals were detected by enzyme-linked chemiluminescence (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Images were taken on a Bio-Rad Chemidoc MP and analysed with Image Lab software. The average intensity was quantified with error bars generated from the standard error of the mean. Significance was determined with the Student’s
t
-test (
Figures 2E,F
,
3E,F
,
4F-G
,
5E-G
), with significant values indicated with
p
< 0.05. Antibodies used in this study are as follows: Histone H4 (Abcam, ab10158); histone H4-trimethylated-K20 (Abcam, ab9053); histone H4-dimethylated-K20 (Abcam, ab9052) β-TUBILIN (Abcam, ab6046). Antibody against histone H4 from the previous polyclonal antibody lot (ab10158) (GR3281096-1) indicated a decrease in histone H4 abundance in isoproterenol and LAD ligation samples in our previous publication [
21
], where it was not indicated with mass spectrometry quantitation. For the data presented in this study, we used another lot of the same antibody (GR3304787-1) on the same samples, and the results indicated no change in histone H4 abundance. While we acknowledge the variability on histone H4 signal in the blots (
Figures 3D, 4E, 5D
), our conclusion that overall histone H4 abundance remains unchanged between diseased and control conditions is supported by statistical analysis, which revealed no significant differences across experimental groups. The variability in histone H4 signal may reflect biological or technical factors, such as tissue heterogeneity, circadian influences, or mixed cell populations. As previously discussed [
21
], dynamic changes in total histone protein levels are rarely reported and typically observed in non-cardiac systems [
35
]. Importantly, discrepancies may stem from impaired antibody binding caused by nearby histone post-translational modifications (PTMs), a known challenge in histone biology [
46
].
Mouse models of heart disease
All protocols involving mice conform to the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the University of Utah (Protocol # 21–10004 approved on 6 October 2021). In this study, we used both male and female FVB mice. All efforts were made to minimize pain and distress during performed procedures, anesthesia, and isolation of the heart.
Isoproterenol infusion via miniosmotic pumps
The 10–15 week old mice were randomly assigned to two groups: ISO (Isoproterenol group,
n
= 4) or Sham (control group,
n
= 4). Mini-osmotic pumps filled with isoproterenol or saline control were surgically implanted into mice, which administered 30 mg/kg/day for 6 weeks with the Alzet model 2006, releasing 0.15 μL per hour [
21
]. Mice were monitored weekly by echocardiography. The hearts were harvested at 6 weeks, ventricles separated, and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C until they were used for analysis.
Permanent occlusion of the lad surgeries
FVB mice, 16–19 weeks old, were randomly assigned to two groups and subjected to permanent occlusion (PO) of the left anterior descending coronary artery (LAD,
n
= 5) or sham surgery (Sham,
n
= 5), as reported previously, with minor modifications [
21
,
47
]. Briefly, mice were anesthetized, and the chest was opened through the left thoracotomy. After visualization of the LAD under the microscope, a suture was placed around the LAD coronary artery and permanently occluded. The apex of the left ventricle was observed for evidence of myocardial blanching, indicating an interruption in coronary flow. The Sham control group underwent the same surgical procedure, except that the LAD was not ligated. Cardiac function was monitored weekly by echocardiography. Mice were euthanized 48 h or 3 weeks post-operatively, the heart was harvested, and tissue samples from ventricles were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C until they were used for downstream analysis.
Echocardiography
Echocardiography (ECHO) was performed on a Vevo 2100 (Visual Sonics) to determine cardiac parameters in live mice as described previously [
20
,
48
], including left ventricular function (ejection fraction [EF]).
Time points selection across cardiac disease models
Time points were selected to capture key phases of disease progression specific to each cardiac model. In the ISO model, a 6-week time point was chosen to reflect chronic cardiac remodeling resulting from sustained adrenergic stimulation. In the PO model, 48 hours and 3 weeks post-occlusion were used to represent acute myocardial injury and sub-chronic remodeling, respectively. For in vitro experiments using differentiated H9c2 cardiomyoblasts treated with phenylephrine, 24- and 48-hour time points were selected to assess early and sustained hypertrophic responses. These time points were not intended to be directly equivalent but were chosen to align with biologically relevant stages in each model, enabling the study of molecular and epigenetic dynamics across acute and chronic cardiac stress conditions.

Human cardiac tissue samples for western blotting
For protein analysis, human cardiac samples were lysed in 1X RIPA buffer (Cell Signaling Technology) comprising 2X protease and phosphatase inhibitor (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Samples were incubated on ice for 15 min, then 5 μL of 100 mmol phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) was added to the lysate. Samples were rotated for 30 mins at 4°C, followed by 10 mins of centrifugation at 4°C at 15,000 × rcf. Then, the supernatant was removed and placed into a new tube. Next, the Pierce BCA Protein Assay kit (Thermo Scientific) was used for protein quantification. Then, an equal volume of 2X Laemmli buffer with 10% DTT was added to the supernatant and boiled for 10 mins at 98°C. An equal amount of total protein was used for both patients and controls to ensure that the samples were comparable.
Statistical analysis
Unless specifically noted, data were collected from technical triplicates of at least three biological replicates of three independent experiments and presented as the means with standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical significance was evaluated using the unpaired Student’s
t
-test or one-way ANOVA for pairwise comparisons. The statistical analysis of log2 values of methyltransferases and demethylases for transcript analysis of human heart samples was performed using a two-way ANOVA with a post hoc Tukey’s test for multiple comparisons. An asterisk * that indicates
p
< 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The data were analysed using Microsoft Office Excel or GraphPad Prism.
Results
Acute hypertrophy in isolated cardiomyocytes has no effect on H4K20me2 abundance but decreases regulatory enzymes
In our previous work, we reported increased levels of histone H4K20me3 during acute hypertrophy in isolated cardiomyocytes [
21
]. Therefore, next, we wanted to evaluate levels of histone H4K20me2, as well as all enzymes that regulate methylation at this site, in the same model. To do this, we cultured H9c2 rat ventricular myoblasts, differentiated them to mimic cardiomyocyte phenotype, which we confirmed by detecting increased expression of cardiac troponin I,
Tnni3
(
Figure 2B
). Next, we subjected differentiated H9c2 cells to a hypertrophic stimulus (phenylephrine, PE) [
50
]. Adrenergic stimulation with PE induces early molecular changes within the first 24 h, including upregulation of fetal genes, such as
Nppa
, a well-established early marker of cardiac hypertrophy [
51
]. Although we measured
Nppa
expression at 48 h, its transcriptional induction begins within the initial hours of PE exposure. These early molecular changes precede the phenotypic features of hypertrophy, such as increased cell size, which emerge with sustained stimulation (
Figure 2A
). After 48 h of PE treatment, we observed a ~ 2.5 fold increase in
Nppa
expression, consistent with activation of the hypertrophic gene program (
Figure 2B
). Quantification of histone H4K20me2 abundance by western blotting revealed no significant differences (
Figure 2C,D
). We quantified transcript levels of each of the methyltransferases and demethylases at both the 24-hour and 48-hour time points after adrenergic stimulation and found that at 24 hours, only
Kdm7b
expression was increased (
Figure 2E,F
). However, at 48-hours, almost all enzymes were downregulated, including methyltransferases:
Kmt5a, Mmset
,
Kmt5b
, and
Kmt5c
, and demethylases:
Kdm7b, Kdm9, Kdm1a
,
Rad23a
,
Rad23b
, and
Kdm7c
. The only two showing no change in expression in this system are
Smyd3
and
Smyd5
. This highlights changes in these enzymes, which may drive our previously reported changes in histone H4K20me3. Of these differentially regulated methyltransferases and demethylases,
Kmt5b
,
Kmt5c
,
Rad23a
,
Rad23b
, and
Kdm7c
all regulate trimethylation, which we previously demonstrated is upregulated in the cellular model of acute hypertrophy [
21
]. The differences between 24- and 48-hours may also be inciteful into the function and temporal regulation by these methyltransferases and demethylases.
ISO-induced hypertrophy in mice has no effect on histone H4K20me2 but moderately alters methyltransferase and demethylase expression
Cardiac dysfunction, characterized by pathological remodeling, is accompanied by molecular changes within the heart, including changes at the transcriptional level and histone PTMs [
51
]. To investigate whether histone H4K20me2 and enzymes regulating methylation at this site are altered in the diseased heart, we analysed cardiac tissue from mice treated with isoproterenol (ISO), a β-adrenergic receptor agonist known to induce cardiac hypertrophy (
Figure 3A
). In this model, mice were implanted with a mini osmotic pump, delivering ISO continuously for six weeks. Analysis of heart weight to body weight ratios (HW/BW) and
Nppa
expression (5.5-fold increase) confirmed cardiac hypertrophy and chronic cardiac dysfunction in these mice (
Figure 3B,C
). Immunoblotting analysis showed no changes in histone H4K20me2, as compared to the control, normalized to either histone H4 or β-TUBULIN (
Figure 3D-F
). Although a slight decrease in histone H4 abundance is visually apparent in the ISO group, the quantification did not show a statistically significant difference compared to controls. This is consistent with our previous findings, where mass spectrometry analysis also revealed no significant change in global H4 levels under similar conditions [
21
]. Further, we quantified the methyltransferases and demethylases in cardiac tissue from these mice and observed fewer changes in enzyme abundance compared to acute hypertrophy in isolated H9c2 cells, with only
Kmt5b
,
Smyd5
, and
Kdm9
being decreased (
Figure 3G,H
).
Mice with ischemic heart disease display altered methyltransferase and demethylase expression
To further investigate levels of histone H4K20me2 and enzymes that regulate this mark in cardiac dysfunction, we tested another mouse model of heart failure by inducing ischemic injury via ligation of the left anterior descending (LAD) artery (
Figure 4A
). Mice were analysed at both an early time point after ischemic injury (48 h) and after pathological remodeling (3 weeks post-injury). Cardiac function measured by echocardiography confirmed the functional decline in response to injury (
Figure 4B
). The analysis of heart weight to body weight ratios did not show significant differences due to the combined effects of hypertrophic growth and cell death occurring at the same time but in different regions of the heart (
Figure 4C
). Increased expression of
Nppa
also confirmed cardiac dysfunction in these mice, as compared to Sham controls (
Figure 4D
).
Our immunoblotting analysis of histone H4K20me2 in cardiac tissue at 3 weeks post-injury, revealed no significant changes as compared to histone H4 or GAPDH, nor was there a reduction in total histone H4 levels (
Figure 4E-G
). In terms of methyltransferase and demethylase expression, we detected reduced expression of
Kdm7b
and
Rad23a
at the 48 h time point, with no changes in other enzymes (
Figure 4H,I
). At 3 weeks post-injury, additional changes were observed: methyltransferases
Kmt5b
,
Kmt5c
, and
Smyd5
were decreased, and the demethylase
Kdm7c
was downregulated. These data show unique temporal regulation of these alterations over time. The decreased expression of enzymes regulating histone H4K20me3 mark (
Rad23a
,
Kmt5c
, and
Smyd5)
is consistent with the previously reported decrease of this mark in a murine model of ischemic injury [
21
].
Human patients with ischemic cardiomyopathy display altered histone methyltransferase and demethylase expression
To assess the relevance of histone H4K20 modifications to human health, we analysed heart tissue samples from patients with ischemic cardiomyopathy (ICM) and compared them to donor controls (
Figure 5A
). The evaluation of cardiac dysfunction was confirmed by decreased left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and increased left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVEDD) as measured by echocardiography (
Figure 5B,C
). Clinical characteristics are detailed in supplementary tables S2-S4. Immunoblotting analysis suggested a trend toward reducing both di- and tri-methylation of histone H4K20 in ICM patients, compared to healthy donors, but it did not reach statistical significance (
Figure 5D-G
). Although Ponceau S staining shows uniform protein transfer and confirms that the samples were not compromised during processing. High variability in human samples that often reflects underlying biological differences, such as comorbidities or genetic background, and variability in sample collection, suggests that a larger sample size would be beneficial for future analysis of these modifications.
For the transcriptomic analysis of enzymes regulating histone H4K20 methylation marks, we analysed samples from patients undergoing LVAD implantation as a bridge to heart transplant (
Figure 6A
). Patients were classified as responders or non-responders based on the improvement in echocardiography data after LVAD implantation (
Figure 6B,C
) [
49
]. The RNA-Seq analysis of these heart samples revealed a significant increase in gene expression of
MMSET
,
KMT5C
, and
SMYD5
methyltransferases (
Figure 6D
). Interestingly, the demethylases
RAD23A
decreased in expression while
KDM7C
increased in expression (
Figure 6E
). These findings indicate which methyltransferases and demethylases are altered in heart failure and may play a role in cardiac stress.
Histone methyltransferase and demethylase expression
To facilitate the comparison of these data across model systems, we compiled all the data into a single table (
Table 1
) and then added any published information from the literature (bottom row) regarding the enzymes known to methylate histone H4K20. We then sought to identify patterns in which enzymes were most consistently regulated across these models of cardiac dysfunction. We prioritized genes differentially regulated in both human and mouse models of heart disease. This comparison showed that
SMYD3, KDM1A, and RAD23B

Discussion
This study provides the first analysis of the global abundance of histone H4K20 methylation and the enzymes regulating this site in various models of heart disease (
Figure 7
). While extensive research has focused on multiple epigenetic modifications on histone H3 during heart disease, histone H4 modifications have remained relatively understudied [
21
,
54
,
81
]. In a previous study, we utilized both cell and animal models of cardiac dysfunction to examine histone H4K20 tri-methylation abundance during disease. Our results show that this mark is increased in isolated cardiomyocytes during acute hypertrophic stress but is decreased in the heart after long-term exposure to isoproterenol or ischemic injury in mice [
21
]. We expanded that study in our work here to determine the global abundance of histone H4K20 di-methylation and the expression of proteins that regulate this mark in the heart. Despite H4K20me2 being the predominant form of methylation in non-cardiac cells, its global levels remained unchanged across cell and animal models examined. Cardiomyocytes are terminally differentiated and thus exit the cell cycle, which is generally regulated by mono- and di-methylation of H4K20. While this may be the main reason for the lack of differences in global expression, it is also possible that it may be differentially regulated at specific genomic loci, resulting in no change across global levels. It is also possible that H4K20me2 may play a less prominent role in the heart under stressed conditions. However, its role in heart physiology under normal conditions or during heart development is unknown. Although, the histone H4K20me2 has been consistently reported as the most abundant methylation state in various systems [
35–37
], the biological significance of this high abundance remains unclear. Nonetheless, our study contributes to this ongoing discussion by extending these observations to the heart. We acknowledge that without locus-specific data (e.g., ChIP-qPCR or ChIP-Seq), any speculation about gene-specific regulation remains preliminary. However, our rationale is grounded in the broader understanding that histone modifications rarely act in isolation. Chromatin regulation often involves multivalent interactions and combinatorial post-translational modifications (PTMs), which can influence transcriptional outcomes even in the absence of global changes. This concept is well-supported in the literature [
82–84
]. Therefore, while our current data do not demonstrate locus-specific changes, we believe that the stable and abundant presence of histone H4K20me2 in the heart may reflect a foundational chromatin state, potentially involved in maintaining cardiac identity or genome stability. Future studies employing ChIP-Seq and integrative epigenomic approaches will be essential to uncover the functional implications of this modification in cardiac health and disease.
Furthermore, we conducted the first quantitative assessment of the global abundance of histone H4K20 di- and tri-methylation in human heart failure patients. Interestingly, both marks show a trend toward downregulation; however neither reached statistical significance. The disease models based on cultured cells or inbred mice show higher consistency and lower heterogeneity between samples. This is not the case with human samples, where cardiac tissue is collected from different individuals that vary significantly from one another (including race, sex, age, and comorbidities). This may lead to larger variability when analysing data from these samples, especially in smaller cohorts of patients.
The methyltransferases and demethylases that are necessary to regulate the methylation of histone H4K20 have been minimally studied in the heart, with only two primary research articles looking at one methyltransferase and one demethylase [
53
,
54
]. Our profiling of H4K20-modifying enzymes highlighted several key players in regulating this mark in the heart.
KMT5C
and
SMYD5
, two methyltransferases involved in histone H4K20me3, were consistently downregulated across cardiac disease models. The reduction in MT’s abundance is likely responsible for the observed decrease in H4K20me3, potentially contributing to the dysregulation of gene expression and cellular function in cardiac disease. Interestingly, we also detected reduced levels of
RAD23A
and
KDM7C
, both demethylases that demethylate the same histone H4K20 site. These findings suggest a coordinated regulation of histone H4K20 methylation by both MTs and DMs, which alludes to the dynamic transcriptional landscape of the diseased heart.
It is important to note that phenylephrine (PE) and isoproterenol (ISO) activate different adrenergic receptors (α₁ and β-adrenergic receptors, respectively) and thereby engage distinct downstream signaling pathways. PE primarily activates the PKC/MAPK signaling cascade [
85–89
], whereas ISO acts through the cAMP/PKA pathway [
89–91
]. These differences may lead to divergent effects on nuclear signaling, transcription factor activity, and potentially histone modifications. While direct evidence linking these pathways to specific changes in H4K20 methylation is currently lacking in the cardiac context, prior studies in other systems have shown that β-adrenergic signaling can influence histone acetylation and methylation patterns [
92
]. Therefore, the differences in gene expression observed between the PE- and ISO-treated models may, in part, reflect receptor-specific effects on chromatin regulation. Further studies are needed to dissect how adrenergic signaling specificity shapes the cardiac epigenetic landscape.
The discrepancies between human and rodent models in terms of gene expression changes further highlight the complexity of histone modification regulation in the heart. In contrast to the human heart failure samples, where both increases and decreases in MT and DM expression were observed, rodent models consistently showed decreases in these enzymes. This may suggest that there is dynamic temporal regulation of histone H4K20 methylation and that additional time points may need to be evaluated to fully understand this process. Notably, while we observed a decrease in the expression of the enzymes involved in histone H4K20 methylation, it is important to consider that these proteins are not solely governed by abundance via transcriptional regulation. Protein stability, post-translational modifications, and enzymatic activity are likely to play critical roles in modulating the activity of these enzymes. Thus, changes in histone H4K20 methylation may not be entirely attributable to transcriptional changes in the methyltransferases and demethylases themselves but also to alterations in their functional status or interactions with other cellular signaling pathways. Further studies are therefore required to elucidate the precise mechanisms by which these enzymes regulate histone H4K20 methylation and how these modifications influence cardiac function in health and disease. One promising avenue of research involves chromatin immunoprecipitation coupled with next-gen sequencing (ChIP-Seq) to identify the specific genomic loci regulated by histone H4K20 methylation. Previous studies have suggested that histone H4K20 trimethylation is enriched at repetitive elements such as LINEs and LTRs, but its distribution across the genome in the context of cardiac disease remains unexplored. ChIP-Seq of histone H4K20 methylation marks, combined with profiling of the corresponding methyltransferases and demethylases, will provide valuable insights into the epigenetic regulation of gene expression in the heart.
In conclusion, this study represents the first investigation of histone H4K20 methylation and its regulatory enzymes in the context of cardiac disease. Our findings suggest that changes in the expression of key methyltransferases (
KMT5C
and
SMYD5
) and demethylases (
RAD23A
and
KDM7C
) contribute to the observed alterations in histone H4K20 methylation during heart disease. These enzymes may play an important role in maintaining cardiac function by modulating the epigenetic landscape of the heart under stress. However, we acknowledge that our study provides correlative evidence, and mechanistic experiments are needed to confirm the direct role of these enzymes in regulating histone H4K20 methylation in the heart, to better understand the functional consequences of these modifications, and to assess their potential as therapeutic targets in cardiac disease.
