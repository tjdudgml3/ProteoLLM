Introduction
Wheat is a vital food crop that plays a critical role in ensuring food security, which is fundamental for social stability and sustainable economic development. However, infection by the fungus
Fusarium graminearum
, the causal agent of Fusarium head blight (FHB), severely reduces grain yield, which leads to significant economic losses [
1
]. Furthermore, during the infection phase of Fusarium head blight, mycotoxins, such as deoxynivalenol (DON), nivalenol (NIV), T-2 toxin, fumonisin (FUM), and zearalenone (ZEN), are produced [
2
]. These mycotoxins contaminate grains and pose serious health risks to both humans and animals. Exposure to these toxins can result in a range of adverse effects, including intestinal irritation, feed refusal in livestock, vomiting, skin dermatitis, immunosuppression, anorexia, and growth retardation [
3
,
4
].
Chemical control remains the primary strategy to manage FHB, with fungicides, such as carbendazim, propiconazole, and tebuconazole, widely employed [
5
]. However, heavy reliance on chemical methods presents several challenges, including the improper use of pesticides, emergence of pathogen resistance, and concerns over pesticide residues in food and the environment. In recent years, an increasing awareness of human and animal health, along with growing environmental concerns, has spurred interest in sustainable alternatives, particularly strategies of biological control [
6
]. Among these, microbial biocontrol agents have shown significant potential in suppressing
Fusarium
pathogens. Notably,
Bacillus subtilis
[
7
],
Pseudomonas
[
8
],
Streptomyces
[
9
], and
Trichoderma
[
10
] have emerged as promising candidates for the eco-friendly management of Fusarium head blight in wheat.
B. subtilis
produces cyclic lipopeptides through its metabolic activity, which effectively suppress the growth and proliferation of
F. graminearum
. This inhibition contributes to a reduction in the incidence of wheat scab and a decrease in the accumulation of mycotoxins, such as DON and NIV [
11
,
12
]. Notably,
B. subtilis
strain BS-Z15 synthesizes mycosubtilin (Myco), a lipopeptide compound from the iturin family, which exhibits potent antifungal activity against a broad spectrum of plant pathogenic fungi [
13
,
14
]. Although previous studies have demonstrated that the Myco produced by
B. subtilis
can inhibit
F. graminearum
[
7
], its antifungal efficacy against
Fusarium culmorum
remains unexplored. Furthermore, no comparative analysis has been conducted to evaluate the antifungal performance of purified Myco versus its crude extract.
Initial studies demonstrated that Myco, a secondary metabolite produced by
B. subtilis
BS-Z15, exhibits antagonistic activity against
Verticillium dahliae
, the causal agent of cotton (
Gossypium hirsutum
) Verticillium wilt. Myco not only inhibits the pathogenic effects of
V. dahliae
on cotton plants but also significantly reduces the incidence of disease [
15
]. Furthermore, Myco has been shown to enhance plant systemic resistance [
16
]. Subsequent research revealed that strain BS-Z15 can also suppress the growth of
F. culmorum
, the fungal pathogen that causes Fusarium head blight on wheat. Notably, we observed an intriguing phenomenon: the antimicrobial crude extract (AP), which contains equivalent concentrations of Myco, demonstrated superior antifungal efficacy compared to purified Myco alone. This finding was consistently validated in preliminary pot experiments.
While Myco is established as the primary antifungal compound in the secondary metabolites of
B. subtilis
BS-Z15, and the AP represents an initial purification product containing Myco, our results unexpectedly showed that the AP inhibited
F. culmorum
more strongly than purified Myco. This observation raises a critical scientific question: What accounts for the enhanced antimicrobial activity of AP? This study aims to preliminarily elucidate the differential mechanisms of action between AP and Myco against
F. culmorum
, thereby addressing this important gap in research.
Materials and methods
Plant materials and treatment
The experimental material consisted of the ‘213’ wheat cultivar, which was obtained from Changnong Seed Company (Changji, Xinjiang, China). Healthy wheat seeds were surface-sterilized with 1% sodium hypochlorite (NaClO), thoroughly rinsed 5–7 times with distilled water, and then soaked in sterile water for 2 h. The sterilized seeds were planted in a growth substrate composed of perlite and turf soil (1:3, v/v). The seedlings were cultivated under controlled environmental conditions at 22 °C with a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod (16L:8D).
Determination of the myco content in the BS-Z15 secondary metabolite AP
B. subtilis
BS-Z15 was isolated and purified by our research group. We collected two products as previously described by Lin Rongrong in our laboratory [
17
]. The first product was the crude antifungal extract (AP) obtained from acid precipitation during the initial extraction phase, and the second was the purified antifungal compound mycosubtilin (Myco).
The compounds were prepared by dissolving 0.01 g of lyophilized AP and 0.006 g of Myco in 600 µL and 400 µL of DMSO, respectively. The solutions were then filtered through 0.22 µm oil-phase sterile membranes. The content of Myco in the AP was quantified by comparing the chromatographic peak area of Myco with known standard concentrations utilizing high-pressure liquid chromatography (HPLC)
.
The chromatographic conditions included mobile phase A of water and mobile phase B of 100% acetonitrile. The UV detection wavelength was set at 210 nm, with an injection volume of 40 μL. The mobile phase elution program is detailed in Online Appendix Table
S1
.
Effects of the BS-Z15 secondary metabolites on the growth of F. culmorum
Effects of the BS-Z15 secondary metabolites on the mycelial growth of F. culmorum
The strain of
F. culmorum
was provided by Dr. Zhao Heping from Beijing Normal University (Beijing, China). The fungus was activated and cultured at 28 °C for 4 days, after which it was inoculated onto carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) medium [
7
] and cultured at 28 °C with shaking at 180 rpm for an additional 3 to 5 days. The spore concentration was adjusted to 1 × 10
5
CFU/mL using a hemocytometer. Concentrations of Myco solutions at 5 µg/mL, 10 µg/mL, and 15 µg/mL were added to 1 mL of potato dextrose agar (PDA) medium, along with AP solutions containing equivalent Myco concentrations of 29.4 µg/mL, 58.8 µg/mL, and 88.2 µg/mL. This mixture was then poured into a 12-well cell culture plate containing a filter paper disc, and 10 µL of the 1 × 10
5
CFU/mL
F. culmorum
spore suspension was added to the treatment wells. Sterile water was included in the medium as a control. The plates were incubated at 28 °C for 4 days, and the diameter of the fungal colonies was then measured and photographed to calculate the inhibition rate.

Effects of the BS-Z15 secondary metabolites on the spore growth of F. culmorum
A spore suspension of
F. culmorum
was inoculated into 5 mL of CMC liquid medium, with the final spore concentration adjusted to 1 × 10
5
CFU/mL. The antifungal agents Myco and AP were added to the solution at final concentrations of M5, M10, and M15 (for Myco) and A5, A10, and A15 (for AP), respectively. The cultures were incubated at 28℃ with shaking at 180 rpm. Every 24 h, 200 µL of the fungal suspension was transferred to a 96-well plate, and the OD
600
was measured. This process was repeated for 4 consecutive days. All the treatment groups were performed in triplicate (n = 3).
Effects of the BS-Z15 secondary metabolites on the morphology of F. culmorum
A 1 mL aliquot of an
F. culmorum
spore suspension (1 × 10
5
CFU/mL) was centrifuged at 4℃ for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The pellets were then treated with either M15 or A15 solutions in Eppendorf tubes and incubated for 0 h, 6 h, and 24 h. Following incubation, the samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 min at 4℃ to remove the Myco solution, followed by three washes with PBS. A volume of 1 mL of 2.5% glutaraldehyde was added to each group to fix it and stored at 4℃ for 24 h. After fixation, the samples were washed three times with PBS and then dehydrated using a graded ethanol series (30%, 60%, 70%, 80%, 90% and 95%, each for 15 min), followed by three additional dehydration steps in 100% ethanol (15 min each). The samples were then immersed in
tert
-butanol for 30 min and lyophilized for 3 h to prepare for observation using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Prior to SEM imaging, a small amount of sample was carefully transferred onto conductive adhesive using a toothpick, and loose particles were removed with a rubber bulb. The samples were sputter-coated with gold because they were non-conductive. Finally, the samples were observed and photographed under an SEM.
Control efficacy of the BS-Z15 secondary metabolites against fusarium head blight in wheat seedlings
Vermiculite and sand were mixed in a ratio of 1:3 and sterilized at 121 °C for 60 min. The mixture was then placed into pots measuring 10 cm × 9 cm, with 10 plump wheat seeds sown in each pot. The pots were maintained at a temperature of 24 °C under a photoperiod of 16 h light/8 h dark (16L:8D) for 15 days. Once the wheat reached the two-leaf stage, they were categorized into the following groups: healthy (CK1), diseased (CK2), carbendazim (CK3), and groups M5, M10, M15, A5, A10, and A15. Each group consisted of 10 plants per pot, with three pots allocated per group. All the groups, except for the healthy one, were inoculated with a spore suspension of
F. culmorum
at a concentration of 1 × 10
5
CFU/mL (10 mL per pot). The corresponding treatments were applied via spraying (10 mL per pot) 1 day post-inoculation. The disease severity was recorded at intervals of 7 days over a period of 21 days. The control efficacy of BS-Z15 secondary metabolites against wheat scab was assessed based on the disease index, utilizing the grading standards for wheat scab. 18
]:

Control efficacy of the BS-Z15 secondary metabolites on detached wheat spike

F. culmorum
(approximately 1 × 10
5
CFU/mL). The inoculated spikes were maintained at 28 °C with 80% relative humidity for 24 h. Subsequently, each spike was sprayed with 1 mL of one of the following treatments: carbendazim (positive control), M5, M10, and M15 (Myco treatments), or A5, A10, and A15 (AP treatments). The treated spikes were placed in trays, covered with aluminum foil to maintain humidity, and incubated for an additional 5 days. The experimental design included three biological replicates per treatment group, with each replicate consisting of three spikes. The disease progression was monitored and recorded, and the control efficacy was calculated based on disease severity. The disease severity was graded according to the following standards:

Enhancement of resistance in diseased wheat by the BS-Z15 secondary metabolites
The impact of BS-Z15 secondary metabolites on the wheat growth was assessed by measuring various physiological indices, including the plant height, root length, and both the aboveground and belowground dry and fresh weights of the diseased wheat after 21 days of treatment with the BS-Z15 secondary metabolites. The chlorophyll content in the leaves of wheat susceptible to disease treated with the BS-Z15 secondary metabolites was evaluated using 0.02 g of fresh, clean wheat leaves that were weighed and cut into filaments approximately 5 mm long and 1 mm wide. These filaments were placed into three test tubes, which were wrapped in black plastic bags, and 10 mL of anhydrous ethanol was added dropwise. The extracts were then allowed to steep in the dark at room temperature for approximately 3 days, with the possibility of reducing the immersion time by shaking the tubes until the wheat leaves became colorless or white. The extract was subsequently reconstituted to a final volume of 10 mL. The chlorophyll content was determined using the following formulae. Anhydrous ethanol was as the blank control. Each replicate was measured three times at different wavelengths, and the average value was recorded.

Treatment of spore samples for the transcriptome analysis
A volume of 10 mL PDB liquid medium was inoculated with a spore solution of
F. culmorum
to a final concentration of 1 × 10
5
CFU/mL. Myco and AP were added separately at a concentration of 1% (v/v), which resulted in final concentrations of M15 and A15, respectively. The cultures were incubated at 28 °C with shaking at 180 rpm, and samples were collected at 6 h and 24 h. The samples were centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 10 min; the supernatant was discarded, and the pellets were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C. The samples were sent to Shanghai Majorbio Medical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) to sequence the transcriptome.
Bioinformatics analysis and validation of the differentially expressed genes
Following quantification of gene/transcript read counts using featureCounts, the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were analyzed with DESeq2R (http: //bioconductor.org/packages/stats/b-ioc /DESeq2). Genes with |log2(fold change)|≥ 1 and adjusted
P
-value (
P
adjust) < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed. The P-values were calculated and adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction method, with
P
-value (
P
adjust) < 0.05 considered statistically significant. The sequenced genes and transcripts were compared and annotated singly with six public databases (NR [
19
], Swiss-Prot [
20
], Clusters of Orthologous Genes (COG) [
21
], Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) [
22
], Gene Ontology (GO) [
23
], and Pfam [
24
], and the annotated information of all the genes in each database was analyzed statistically after the details were obtained.
Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) was employed to validate the transcriptome data. Three DEGs were selected, and β-tubulin was used as the reference gene. The primer sequences for qRT-PCR are provided in Onilne Appendix Table S3, and the reaction system and qRT-PCR process were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions of a Tiangen Biotech Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) kit.
Full spectrum LC–MS analysis of the BS-Z15 secondary metabolites
The secondary metabolites of BS-Z15 were extracted from the acid precipitate (AP) as described by Lin Rongrong [
17
]. In this study, a precise quantity of the BS-Z15 secondary metabolites was measured and extracted using an extraction solution under low-temperature conditions. After centrifugation, the supernatant containing the metabolites was collected for a liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC–MS) analysis. A 100 µL aliquot of the sample was accurately transferred to a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube, dried under nitrogen, and then reconstituted with 100 µL of a reconstitution solution (acetonitrile: water = 1:1). The sample was vortexed for 30 s, subjected to low-temperature ultrasonic extraction for 5 min (5℃, 40 kHz), and then centrifuged for 10 min (13,000 g, 4℃). The supernatant was transferred to an injection vial with an insert for analysis. The chromatographic conditions were as follows: the column used was an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 (100 mm × 2.1 mm i.d., 1.8 µm; Waters, Milford, MA, USA); mobile phase A consisted of 95% water and 5% acetonitrile (containing 0.1% formic acid), while mobile phase B comprised 47.5% acetonitrile, 47.5% isopropanol, and 5% water (also containing 0.1% formic acid). The injection volume was 2 µL, and the column temperature was maintained at 40℃. The gradient elution program for the mobile phase is detailed in Online Appendix Table S4.
Statistical analysis
GraphPad Prism 9 (Boston, MA, USA) was employed to conduct a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) on the data. When
P
< 0.05, the least significant difference (LSD) test was applied to assess the significance of differences between the treatments. Different lowercase letters were used to indicate significant differences among the groups. The graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 9 and Adobe Illustrator 2019 (Adobe, San Diego, CA, USA).
Results and analysis
Determination of the Myco content in the AP of BS-Z15 secondary metabolites
The production yield of the AP was approximately 0.15 g per L of beef extract-peptone medium under standard cultivation conditions. We compared the chromatographic peak areas of AP and Myco samples quantitatively using HPLC. The results indicated that approximately 17 µg of Myco is present in every 100 µg of AP (Online Appendix Fig. S1).
Inhibitory effect of the BS-Z15 secondary metabolites on F. culmorum
Treatment with the BS-Z15 secondary metabolites demonstrated dose-dependent antifungal activity against
F. culmorum
and significantly inhibiting both the mycelial growth (
P
< 0.05) and conidiation, which ultimately reducing the fungal biomass by average to 77.77% at the A15 within 96 h of treatment. Notably, the crude AP exhibited superior inhibition efficacy compared to the purified Myco at equivalent concentrations, which suggested that there were synergistic effects from the additional bioactive components in AP (Fig.
1
).

Efficacy of the secondary metabolites from B. subtilis BS-Z15 at controlling wheat scab

Effects of the BS-Z15 secondary metabolites on infected wheat
After 21 days of treatment with
B. subtilis
BS-Z15 secondary metabolites, significant improvements were observed in the plant height, root length, biomass, chlorophyll content, and soluble sugar content compared to the infected control group. These results suggest that BS-Z15 secondary metabolites may effectively mitigate the damage induced by pathogens by suppressing the growth and reproduction of the fungi, thereby reducing the structural damage to plant cells and depletion of nutrients. This protective effect subsequently alleviates the inhibitory impact of pathogen infection on plant growth and development (Fig.

Furthermore, the contents of chlorophyll and soluble sugar in all the groups treated with the BS-Z15 metabolites did not exhibit concentration-dependent increases. Interestingly, higher concentrations of metabolites resulted in slightly reduced physiological indices compared to the treatments with lower concentrations.
Treatment with the
B. subtilis
BS-Z15 secondary metabolites significantly upregulated the activities of key antioxidant enzymes—superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), and catalase (CAT)—in the wheat plants infected with Fusarium. Concurrently, we observed marked reductions in the accumulation of malondialdehyde (MDA) and oxygen free radical (OFR), which indicated the alleviation of oxidative stress. Notably, the induction of these defense enzymes showed no concentration-dependent relationship across the BS-Z15 metabolite treatment groups. In particular, application of the antimicrobial preparation AP15 demonstrated superior efficacy, and it substantially enhanced the ability of wheat to resist disease while effectively suppressing the accumulation of phytotoxic compounds. These findings suggest that the BS-Z15 metabolites, particularly in their crude AP formulation, can prime the antioxidant defense system of wheat against Fusarium infection. (Fig.

Discussion
The cyclic lipopeptide Myco, biosynthesized by
B. subtilis
BS-Z15, represents a homolog of the iturin family within the surfactin class. It demonstrates multifunctional bioactive properties, including pronounced antifungal activity, antioxidant capacity, and immunomodulatory effects [
13
,
26
]. Previous studies have documented the inhibitory efficacy of Myco against various phytopathogenic fungi, notably
V. dahliae
[
17
] and
Aspergillus flavus
[
27
]. Notably, our current findings reveal that the AP, when compared to purified Myco at equivalent concentrations, significantly enhanced the antifungal performance and superior disease control efficacy against wheat Fusarium head blight. This demonstrated both prophylactic and therapeutic potential through in vitro and in vivo evaluations.
A transcriptome analysis was conducted to investigate the inhibitory mechanisms of AP and Myco against
F. culmorum
. Although both compounds showed similar inhibitory effects on
F. culmorum
, their modes of action differed significantly. A comparative analysis of the DEG annotations revealed that the AP triggered more stress response genes in
F. culmorum
than Myco, which suggested a stronger toxic effect. The KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the DEGs demonstrated that the Myco treatment primarily affected the carbohydrate metabolism and amino acid metabolism pathways in
F. culmorum
, along with other key metabolic pathways essential for cell growth. In contrast, the AP treatment showed an opposite pattern of metabolic disruption. Furthermore, the proteasome, peroxisome, and DNA replication pathways – all critical for the growth, development, and virulence of plant pathogenic fungi – were significantly impacted by these treatments.
The 26S proteasome is a highly conserved ATP-dependent protease complex in eukaryotic cells that mediates the degradation of damaged, misfolded, or obsolete proteins and generates peptides that serve critical roles in cellular signaling, antigen presentation, and amino acid recycling. Furthermore, this proteolytic system regulates virtually all essential cellular pathways, including cell cycle control, DNA replication and repair, immune signaling, and neurotransmitter transduction [
28
]. Notably, inhibition of the proteasome has been demonstrated to attenuate virulence in
Candida albicans
[
29
]. Our findings reveal that treatment with the BS-Z15 secondary metabolites significantly downregulates multiple genes within the proteasomal pathway of
F. culmorum
, which leads to the suppression of proteasomal activity and consequent reduction in fungal virulence. SEM observations demonstrated that both the Myco and AP treatments induced severe morphological alterations in
F. culmorum
, including the shrinkage of hyphae, deformation of conidia, and perforation of cells. Mechanistically, the impaired proteasomal activity prevented the timely clearance of misfolded proteins, which resulted in their accumulation intracellularly [
30
,
31
]. This subsequently induced the deformation of cell walls and altered membrane permeability. These changes critically disrupted cellular transport, intercellular communication, and morphological maintenance in
F. culmorum
[
32
], which was remarkably consistent with the membrane integrity defects observed in the fungal hyphae treated with AsR 416 [
33
].
DNA replication is a fundamental process of life and essential for all organisms as it ensures the transmission of genetic information from parent to offspring. Following treatment with the BS-Z15 secondary metabolites, key genes involved in the DNA replication process of
F. culmorum
were downregulated. This downregulation inhibited DNA replication, thereby preventing normal cell division and impeding the growth and development of
F. culmorum
. Previous studies have also demonstrated that the BS-Z15 secondary metabolites inhibit the growth and sporulation of
F. culmorum
.
In the development of fungi, peroxisomes play a crucial role in meiosis, as well as in the maturation and germination of sexual spores [
34
]. Following treatment with the BS-Z15 secondary metabolites, multiple genes within the peroxisome pathway of
F. culmorum
were downregulated. This inhibited morphogenesis and growth of its cell wall. The downregulation of peroxisome function in
F. culmorum
not only affected the germination and growth of its spores but also weakened its infectivity towards plants. Peroxisomes are essential for the virulence of fungi by providing energy, acetyl-CoA, and succinate for the biosynthesis of carbohydrates, formation of appressoria, and generation of turgor pressure [
25
]. Plant pathogenic fungi require efficient peroxisomal fatty acid catabolism to maintain the integrity of their infection structures [
35
]. In pathogens that lack appressoria, the virulence of all
pex
mutants is diminished, which results in milder disease symptoms in wheat, corn, short fescue (
Festuca brachyphylla
), and tomato (
Solanum lycopersicum
) [
36
–
39
]. Pot experiments indicated that spraying AP and Myco reduced the disease index of infected wheat, probably because AP and Myco inhibited the normal fatty acid β-oxidation metabolism in the peroxisomes of
F. culmorum
. This resulted in the abnormal development of infection structures and a reduction in its infectivity towards plants.
An LC–MS analysis showed that the AP contains antifungal substances, including 2-hydroxycinnamic acid [
40
], cinnamaldehyde [
41
,
42
], acrylamide [
43
], piperidine [
44
], myriocin [
45
], palmitic acid [
46
], phenyllactic acid [
47
], and quinic acid [
48
]. Myriocin has been shown to inhibit the growth of
F. graminearum
and significantly inhibit both mycelial growth and spore germination, with a certain concentration dependence [
45
]. SEM revealed that treatment with a specific concentration of myriocin induced abnormal morphology in the
F. graminearum
mycelia, which was characterized by irregular shrinkage, depression, and local perforations on the surface. This indicates that myriocin affects the structure and function of the mycelia by altering the permeability or integrity of the cell membrane, thereby inhibiting the normal growth of
F. graminearum
[
45
]. This finding is consistent with the conclusion that both the AP and Myco treatments induce morphological changes in
F. culmorum
. Additionally, the AP contains substances that influence plant growth, such as L-histidine,
trans
-4-coumaric acid, N-lactyl-phenylalanine, indole lactic acid, 4-methoxycinnamic acid, and L-phenylalanine.
The superior effect of AP at controlling wheat scab compared to Myco can be attributed to two primary factors. First, the combined use of multiple substances in the AP enhances the plant’s systemic resistance, thereby increasing its ability to withstand external stress. Secondly, the combined use of multiple substances in the AP disrupts the cell wall of
F. culmorum
and interferes with its metabolic pathways, inhibiting its growth and development, which, in turn, reduces the pathogen’s infectivity.
The results indicated that the control effect of the BS-Z15 secondary metabolites on wheat scab initially increased and then decreased. This phenomenon may be explained by the fact that with increasing drug concentration, while higher concentrations may more effectively inhibit pathogens, they may also adversely affect plant growth and not necessarily enhance the plant’s disease resistance. A previous study by Zhang Hui showed that low concentrations of Myco promote plant growth, whereas high concentrations inhibit it [
16
]. This is consistent with the findings of this study. Future field trials should be conducted to determine the appropriate concentrations to control wheat scab. Further research is needed to identify the specific substance or combination of substances that interacts with Myco to enhance the antifungal effect of AP. While the previous production of AP relied primarily on beef extract-peptone medium, our research group has achieved a 4.2-fold increase in yield through the systematic optimization of the BS-Z15 fermentation parameters. This breakthrough not only enhances production efficiency but also facilitates cost-effective scaling – a critical step toward transitioning AP from laboratory research to industrial applications.
Despite demonstrating considerable potential for plant disease control, the practical application of microbial metabolites faces persistent challenges related to stability in the environment and the efficiency of delivery. Key limiting factors include the degradation, structural alteration, or inactivation of bioactive components under environmental stressors, such as extreme temperatures, high humidity, UV radiation, and variable soil pH, which all compromise their antifungal efficacy. Further reductions in field performance may occur owing to the rapid dissipation caused by rainwater leaching or wind drift. Nevertheless, these obstacles may be addressed through the development of targeted formulations and optimized application strategies. With such advancements, microbial metabolites are poised to become an indispensable component of future integrated plant disease management frameworks and thus, offer sustainable alternatives to conventional chemical controls.
Conclusion
This study primarily examined the secondary metabolites of
B. subtilis
BS-Z15, which revealed that both the antifungal active substance Myco isolated from these metabolites and the crude antifungal substance AP, which contains an equivalent amount of Myco, exhibit control effects on wheat scab. Notably, the AP is more effective against the pathogen
F. culmorum
and its disease wheat scab than Myco alone. In addition to Myco, the AP encompasses antifungal compounds and substances that influence plant growth, which suggests that Myco may synergistically interact with these components to enhance the control of wheat scab. The findings of this study indicate that AP is a viable option to manage wheat scab, thereby reducing the costs of application and offering theoretical guidance for the further development of BS-Z15 as a biocontrol agent.
