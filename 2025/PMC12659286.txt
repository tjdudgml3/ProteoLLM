Introduction
Metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) has become the most common chronic liver disease among children and adolescents worldwide [
1
,
2
], affecting approximately 13% of the general pediatric population and up to 47% of those with obesity [
3
]. This burden is rising rapidly in developing regions such as East and Southeast Asia, where rapid urbanization and lifestyle transitions have accelerated the dual epidemics of childhood obesity and metabolic dysfunction [
4
–
6
]. In China, the prevalence of childhood overweight and obesity has surged nearly fivefold over the past three decades [
7
], and MASLD has emerged as a silent but significant contributor to long-term liver-related and cardiometabolic morbidity [
8
].
A recent global consensus [
9
] emphasized the urgency of population-specific MASLD risk identification models [
10
]. Yet MASLD in children is often undiagnosed due to its subclinical onset, absence of early symptoms, and lack of approved pharmacologic therapy. Without timely intervention, pediatric MASLD may progress over time to advanced fibrosis, cirrhosis, and even hepatocellular carcinoma, making early identification and risk stratification a clinical and public health priority [
1
,
2
]. Current diagnostic tools remain inadequate. Liver biopsy is invasive, magnetic resonance imaging proton density fat fraction (MRI-PDFF) is costly and often inaccessible [
11
], and vibration-controlled transient elastography (VCTE), though safer, remains operator-dependent. Consequently, there is a pressing need for reliable, non-invasive, and scalable biomarkers to support early detection and risk assessment, especially in high-risk groups. Most existing non-invasive tests rely on anthropometric (e.g., waist-to-height ratio [WHtR]) or metabolic indices (e.g., triglyceride-glucose index [TyG]) [
12
–
16
]. Notably, many of these indices are designed primarily as surrogates of insulin resistance or adipose dysfunction rather than liver fat per se, for example, the metabolic score for insulin resistance (METS-IR) [
17
], the single-point insulin sensitivity estimator (SPISE) [
17
], and the visceral adiposity index (VAI) [
15
]. Such indices may have reduced predictive power in children with overweight or obesity where such measures are uniformly high [
18
,
19
]. Although genetic variants such as
PNPLA3 I148M
and
TM6SF2 E167K
have been associated with MASLD and show moderate per-allele effects, their combined impact may be substantial, yet their utility in pediatric risk stratification remains uncertain [
20
,
21
].
High-throughput proteomics offers promising opportunities for improving MASLD diagnosis [
22
]. As MASLD reflects a systemic immune-metabolic disorder, the circulating proteome may offer early biological insights into disease pathophysiology [
23
]. In adult populations, proteomic biomarkers have demonstrated utility for identifying hepatic steatosis, fibrosis, and related outcomes [
21
,
24
], but pediatric applications remain an unmet need, and comprehensive proteomic-based risk models have yet to be broadly developed and validated in children.
In this study, we integrated proteomic, anthropometric, metabolic, and genetic data to (1) identify plasma proteins associated with pediatric MASLD; (2) develop a proteomic risk score (ProScore) for identifying MASLD in children with overweight or obesity; and (3) evaluate the discriminative performance of ProScore relative to conventional anthropometric/metabolic indices and genetic risk scores (GRS), including stratified analyses across genetic backgrounds.
Methods
Study design and participants
This cross-sectional study used baseline data from the SCIENT trial (ClinicalTrials.gov:
NCT05482165
), a cluster-randomized trial conducted across six primary schools in Ningbo, China (September 2022 to June 2023). Inclusion criteria were Grade 3 children aged 8–10 years with overweight or obesity defined by Chinese national standards [
25
], whereas exclusion criteria included a history of heart disease, hypertension, diabetes, asthma, viral hepatitis, or nephritis; obesity secondary to endocrine disorders or medication; abnormal physical development or deformity; inability to participate in school sports; and recent (within 3 months) weight loss induced by vomiting or drug use. The study design and sampling methodology have been described previously [
25
,
26
]. Ethical approval was granted by the Ningbo First Hospital Ethics Committee (No. 2021-R168), and written informed consent was obtained from all children and their guardians. For inflammatory proteomic profiling, a subset of 161 children was selected, and plasma proteins were measured with the Olink Explore 96 Inflammation panel (Olink Proteomics, Uppsala, Sweden), using blood samples collected at baseline (Fig.
1
).

Study design and analysis workflow
Anthropometric measurements and laboratory test
Trained personnel from local community health centers conducted anthropometric assessments, following standardized protocols. Waist circumference (WC) was measured at the midpoint between the lower rib margin and iliac crest at end-expiration. Height and weight were measured with participants barefoot and minimally clothed. WC, height, and weight were recorded to the nearest 0.1 cm using a mechanical stadiometer, MyoTape, and a body composition analyzer (InBody770, Biospace, CA, USA) [
27
], respectively. Fasting venous blood samples were collected after an overnight fast. Students were instructed not to eat or drink after 10:00 pm the night before, and venipuncture was performed at school between 7:30 and 8:30 am. Only children in good health participated, and those who felt unwell on the day were excluded from the blood collection. Alanine aminotransferase (ALT), fasting plasma glucose (FPG), fasting insulin (FINS), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and triglycerides (TG) were then measured on an automated platform (Beckman Coulter AU5800, Brea, USA) [
13
]. Systolic and diastolic blood pressure (SBP and DBP) were measured to the nearest 1 mmHg using an electronic sphygmomanometer (Omron HBP-1300). Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m
2
). We selected eleven conventional indices that have been proposed in prior literature as markers of adiposity, insulin resistance, or metabolic dysfunction relevant to MASLD in pediatric populations. These included waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) [
13
], METS-IR [
17
], SPISE [
17
],pediatric NAFLD fibrosis index (PNFI) [
28
,
29
], VAI [
13
,
15
], lipid accumulation product (LAP) [
13
],triglyceride-glucose index (TyG) [
13
], TyG-ALT [
30
], TyG-WC [
13
], TyG-WHtR [
13
], and TyG-BMI [
13
]. The formulas for these indices are provided in Supplementary Table
1
.
Definition of pediatric MASLD
Steatotic liver disease was assessed using the FibroScan Handy device (Echosens, Paris, France), which employs VCTE to quantify hepatic fat and stiffness via the controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) and liver stiffness measurement (LSM), respectively. The median and interquartile range (IQR) from 10 valid measurements were recorded, and results with IQR/median > 30% were excluded. Hepatic steatosis was defined as CAP ≥ 248 dB/m, and fibrosis as LSM ≥ 7.0 kPa [
31
]. MASLD was diagnosed based on the presence of hepatic steatosis plus at least one cardiometabolic risk factor [
9
]. As all participants were overweight or obese, they fulfilled at least one required risk factor and therefore met the criteria for MASLD diagnosis [
13
].
Plasma proteomic assessment
Plasma proteomic profiling was performed using the Olink Explore 96 Inflammation panel, which quantifies 92 inflammation-related protein biomarkers through a high-throughput, antibody-based proximity extension assay (PEA). In this method, each target protein is recognized by a pair of antibodies conjugated to unique oligonucleotides. Upon dual binding to the protein, the oligonucleotides are brought into proximity, allowing hybridization and subsequent extension by DNA polymerase to generate a unique DNA sequence. These DNA barcodes are then quantified using next-generation sequencing, enabling highly specific and sensitive detection [
32
]. To ensure data robustness, proteins with detection rates below 75% across samples were excluded, resulting in 82 proteins retained for downstream analysis. Protein abundance levels were expressed in normalized protein expression (NPX) units, which are log2-scaled and internally controlled to correct for technical variation.
GRS construction
A total of 10 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were selected based on a previously published genome-wide association meta-analysis identifying 17 loci associated with MASLD [
33
]. SNPs were included if they had a minor allele frequency (MAF) > 0.05 in the target population and no evidence of interfering homologous sequences in primer design. For loci with design limitations, proxy variants in strong linkage disequilibrium were used (e.g., rs738409 for rs738408). Genotyping was performed using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight (MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometry, with primers designed using the MassARRAY Assay Designer4.0 (Agena Bioscience, San Diego, CA) and data analyzed using MassARRAY TYPER4.0 (Agena Bioscience, San Diego, CA) [
34
,
35
]. One SNP (rs429358) significantly deviated from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (
P
< 0.05) in the control group and was excluded from further analyses. Thus, nine SNPs were ultimately included in the GRS model: rs1260326, rs17817449, rs2642438, rs4918722, rs58542926, rs626283, rs7029757, rs738409, and rs7661964 (Supplementary Table
2
). Each SNP was coded additively as 0, 1, or 2 according to the number of MASLD risk alleles. For example, for rs738409, with G allele designated as the risk allele, genotypes were coded as CC = 0, CG = 1, and GG = 2. The GRS for each individual was calculated as the sum of risk alleles across the nine loci, with higher values indicating greater inherited risk of MASLD [
36
].
Statistics
The study design and data analysis workflow are presented in Fig.
1
. Baseline characteristics were compared between MASLD and non-MASLD groups. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR) depending on normality, and compared using Student’s
t
-test or non-parametric tests, respectively. Categorical variables were expressed as counts (percentages) and compared using the chi-square test.
To identify circulating proteins associated with MASLD, we first performed univariable logistic regression models (Model 1), with MASLD status as the dependent variable and protein levels as predictors. Proteins with
p
< 0.05 were considered statistically significant and retained as candidate predictors for model building, to preserve sensitivity at the feature-screening stage with a moderate sample size. Additionally, false discovery rate (FDR)–adjusted p values (q) were reported to provide multiplicity-aware robustness. Multivariable logistic regression (Model 2) was then conducted to adjust for potential confounders, including age, sex, BMI, and school (as a categorical covariate). Proteins that remained significant in both unadjusted and adjusted models were considered robust candidates and subjected to functional enrichment analysis and predictive modeling.
Pathway enrichment analyses were performed using Gene Ontology (GO: Biological Process, Cellular Component, Molecular Function;
https://geneontology.org/
), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG;
https://www.kegg.jp/
), and Reactome databases (
https://reactome.org/
). Only terms with Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted
p
(pBH) < 0.05 were considered significant.
To evaluate the diagnostic performance of candidate proteins, we implemented a two-step machine learning framework comprising (1) model-specific variable-importance ranking and (2) sequential forward feature selection. A panel of six machine learning classifiers was implemented: logistic regression (LR), random forest (RF), decision tree (DT), support vector machine (SVM), XGBoost, and LightGBM. All models used protein levels as input features and MASLD status as the binary outcome.
To ensure comparability and limit overfitting, we used harmonized, conservative settings. LR: no regularization (penalty = none; solver = lbfgs; max_iter = 1000). RF: n_estimators = 100, max_depth = 3. DT: max_depth = 3. SVM: linear kernel with C = 1.0 and probability = True (coefficients interpreted only for linear SVM; no nonlinear kernels used). XGBoost: max_depth = 3, n_estimators = 100, eval_metric = 'logloss'. LightGBM: max_depth = 3, num_leaves = 15, n_estimators = 100. All models used the same random seed to ensure reproducibility. Variable importance was assessed for each model individually: for LR and SVM via absolute coefficient values, and for tree-based models via built-in importance scores (i.e., information gain). Features were then ranked according to their importance scores in descending order for each model. A sequential forward selection procedure was employed whereby features were added iteratively to the model in ranked order. At each step, a fivefold stratified cross-validation was performed to estimate the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). The process continued until the inclusion of additional features no longer improved the mean cross-validated AUC, with performance plateau defined quantitatively as ΔAUC < 0.01 across two consecutive additions (i.e., the mean cross-validated AUC gain was < 0.01 in two successive steps).
The optimal feature combination, defined as the smallest subset of proteins that achieved the highest cross-validated AUC, was selected to construct the ProScore. The corresponding machine learning model incorporating these proteins was then applied to the full dataset to generate predicted probabilities, which served as the ProScore for subsequent comparative analyses. To further validate the robustness of the model, the cohort was randomly divided into a training set comprising two-thirds of the population and a test set comprising the remaining one-third. Model performance was then evaluated in the test set. In addition, a school-based validation was performed: data were reallocated based on the distribution of schools and the number of participants within each school (approximately a 7:3 ratio), with four schools (n = 110) assigned to the training set and the remaining two schools (n = 51) designated as the test set, to evaluate internal generalizability across schools. We further compared the discriminative performance of ProScore with that of twelve indices, including WHtR, METS-IR, SPISE, PNFI, VAI, LAP, TyG, TyG-ALT, TyG-WC, TyG-WHtR, TyG-BMI, and the GRS. Differences in cross-validated AUC were assessed using DeLong’s test. To evaluate potential complementarity, ProScore was combined with each conventional index in composite models, and improvements in AUC were tested using the same method. Bootstrap resampling (2,000 iterations) was also applied to these comprisons, ProScore vs each index and ProScore + index vs ProScore alone, to confirm the robustness of p-values. Additionally, sex-stratified AUCs of ProScore were evaluated and compared using the DeLong test.
Due to the non-normal distribution of GRS, Spearman’s rank correlation was used to assess the association between ProScore and GRS. The cohort was then stratified into low and high GRS groups based on the median value. Diagnostic performance within each GRS strata was evaluated using ROC curves, and AUCs were compared using DeLong’s test.
All statistical analyses and visualizations were conducted in R (v4.3.1; packages:
pROC

). Two-sided
p
< 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
Results
Baseline characteristics of the study population
Among the 161 children included, 42 (26.1%) were diagnosed with MASLD. All 42 fulfilled the steatosis criterion (CAP ≥ 248 dB/m); 1/42 (2.4%) additionally met the fibrosis threshold (LSM = 7.4 kPa). No participant exhibited isolated fibrosis (LSM ≥ 7.0 kPa with CAP < 248 dB/m). As shown in Table
1
, there were no significant differences in age (median 8.40 vs. 8.50 years) or sex distribution (girls: 36.1% vs. 35.7%) between the two groups. Children with MASLD had higher median BMI, BMI Z-score, WC, FINS, ALT and TG compared to their non-MASLD counterparts (all
P
< 0.001), while FPG showed a borderline difference (
P
= 0.063). HDL-C levels did not differ significantly (
P
= 0.226). In terms of derived indices, children with MASLD had significantly higher values for WHtR, METS-IR, TyG, TyG-ALT, TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, TyG-WHtR, PNFI, VAI, and LAP (all
P
< 0.001) and significantly lower SPISE (
P
< 0.001). GRS was significantly higher among MASLD participants (median 9.00 vs. 8.00,
P
= 0.004).

Among the 82 inflammatory proteins analyzed, 20 (
P
< 0.05 for all) were significantly associated with MASLD in the unadjusted model (Model 1), of which 9 (q < 0.05 for all) remained statistically significant after FDR correction (Fig.

Optimal model and protein panel for MASLD diagnosis

ProScore outperforms conventional indices, with no added value from their combination
The ProScore, comprising six selected proteins, achieved an AUC of 0.836 for distinguishing MASLD from non-MASLD individuals. This discriminative performance was higher than that of all twelve conventional indices when evaluated individually (Fig.
3
A). Among these, the TyG-BMI index yielded the highest AUC (0.783), while the GRS performed the least effectively (AUC = 0.648). DeLong’s test confirmed that ProScore significantly outperformed each of the individual metrics (
P
< 0.05 for all comparisons). We then evaluated whether adding each conventional index to the ProScore could further improve classification performance. As shown in Fig.
3

P
> 0.05 for all). These results were further supported by bootstrap resampling (2000 iterations), which yielded consistent results across most of the indices (Supplementary Table
4
).

Discussion
Main findings
In this study, we identified 15 inflammatory proteins significantly associated with pediatric MASLD, many of which are involved in cytokine activity and cytokine–receptor interactions. From these, we developed and validated a six-protein biomarker panel (FGF-21, CDCP1, CD244, OPG, Flt3L, MCP-1), termed the ProScore, for identifying MASLD in children with overweight or obesity. ProScore showed higher diagnostic performance than conventional anthropometric and metabolic indices, as well as a GRS, with a cross-validation AUC of 0.84. Its performance remained comparable in a school-based validation split (AUC = 0.83) and the addition of conventional non-invasive markers did not meaningfully improve discrimination. Stratified analyses showed that the ProScore achieved higher diagnostic accuracy in children with lower genetic risk.
In context with current literature
Recent targeted proteomics in adolescents with obesity have identified partial serum protein signatures associated with hepatic steatosis [
38
]. A proteomic study in an Italian pediatric cohort reported numerous altered inflammation- and stress-response proteins associated with MASLD [
39
]. Building on this work, our study utilized a high-throughput panel measuring 92 inflammation-related proteins in a well-characterized cohort of children with overweight or obesity. Notably, the Italian cohort profiled metabolism/cardiometabolic-focused Olink Target 96 panels, whereas our work used an inflammation-focused panel. Thus, the assayed protein sets share only a small fraction of targets, which plausibly explains the limited concordance in single-protein markers despite consistent pathway-level biology (cytokine/chemokine signaling and stress responses) across studies [
39
]. We identified 15 proteins independently associated with MASLD and developed a six-protein classifier (ProScore) that demonstrated good diagnostic accuracy (cross-validated AUC = 0.84). This performance was higher than that of conventional anthropometric and metabolic indices, as well as a GRS. Although such indices may perform reasonably well in the general population, their diagnostic accuracy tends to decline with increasing body weight [
13
]. For example, while the TyG index can achieve an AUC of up to 0.785 in general pediatric cohorts [
40
], its performance drops below 0.70 in children with overweight or obesity [
18
]. Our findings align with recent adult multi-omics work showing proteomic scores (AUC 0.83–0.85) outperforming genetic risk scores (AUC 0.59–0.66 [
21
]. The limited additive value from anthropometric, metabolic, or genetic indices suggests that circulating proteins may capture complementary biological information relevant to MASLD detection. The proteins comprising the ProScore are all inflammatory proteins, reflecting systemic immune-metabolic processes directly implicated in MASLD pathogenesis. Although cost remains a limitation, ongoing advances in assay scalability and affordability may improve the translational feasibility of proteomic approaches in the future.
A six-protein inflammatory signature (FGF-21, CDCP1, CD244, OPG, Flt3L, and MCP-1; ProScore) was identified that distinguishes MASLD from non-MASLD, which may collectively reflect immune–metabolic perturbations directly implicated in MASLD pathogenesis. FGF-21, a hepatokine, is elevated in response to hepatic lipid accumulation and has been proposed as a biomarker of hepatic stress and metabolic dysregulation [
41
]. A longitudinal analysis also revealed associations of FGF-21 with obesity, metabolic syndrome, and MASLD in children [
42
]. By contrast, pediatric evidence for CDCP1 and CD244 remains limited, and inferences derive largely from adult liver disease and immunology, for example links between soluble CDCP1 and risk stratification for steatosis/steatohepatitis and the role of CD244 in immune regulation and tissue remodeling, supporting biological plausibility but warranting pediatric-specific validation [
24
,
43
,
44
]. From a biological standpoint, MCP-1 recruits monocytes, promotes low-grade inflammation, and is linked to insulin resistance and fatty-liver phenotypes. In pediatric MASLD, higher MCP-1 has also been associated with greater fibrosis severity [
45
,
46
]. The inclusion of OPG is biologically credible given its function as a decoy receptor in the RANK/RANKL axis and reported links to vascular/inflammatory remodeling relevant to metabolic liver disease [
47
]. Flt3L, a hematopoietic cytokine essential for dendritic-cell development, aligns with antigen-presentation and immune-activation pathways implicated in metabolic inflammation and MASLD [
48
–
50
]. Taken together, these proteins map onto complementary pathophysiology, including hepatocellular stress (FGF-21), immune-cell trafficking (MCP-1) and immune remodeling (CDCP1/CD244/Flt3L), with OPG reflecting cytokine–osteokine crosstalk linked to metabolic liver disease. This integrative profile may improve diagnostic characterization by reflecting both liver-intrinsic and systemic inflammatory signals. The enrichment of these proteins in canonical cytokine and chemokine pathways, as shown by our functional analyses, is consistent with hepatic transcriptomic and histologic findings, supporting their translational relevance as circulating biomarkers for early MASLD detection.
Interestingly, we found that ProScore discriminated greater discrimination for MASLD in children with lower genetic risk than in those with higher genetic risk. A likely explanation is that, when inherited risk is low, the phenotypic expression of MASLD is more likely driven by modifiable metabolic exposures that amplify systemic inflammation, signals our inflammation-focused proteome captures sensitively [
51
]. By contrast, high-GRS carriers of variants such as PNPLA3 I148M and TM6SF2 E167K tend to accumulate hepatic fat via hepatocellular mechanisms (impaired lipid-droplet remodeling or reduced very low density lipoproteins lipidation and secretion) that may be partly decoupled from systemic insulin resistance, lipids, and inflammation [
52
,
53
]. GCKR P446L additionally shifts hepatic glucose–lipid fluxes [
54
]. These patterns suggest that genetics (trait) and proteomics (current disease state) may offer complementary information into pediatric MASLD.
Limitations
Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, external validation in larger pediatric cohorts with broader age ranges is necessary to confirm the generalizability of our findings. To mitigate this limitation, we applied rigorous fivefold cross-validation and internal replication strategies, both of which produced consistent results, further supporting the robustness of our findings. In addition, as participants were recruited from six distinct school-based centers, we conducted an external validation by training the model on data from four schools and testing it on the remaining two. This school-based split further supports the reproducibility and potential generalizability of our findings. Second, the proteomic panel used focuses on inflammation-related proteins; while appropriate given the pathophysiology of MASLD, other pathways (e.g., oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, lipid metabolism) may harbor additional relevant biomarkers. Moreover, independent validation of the six-protein ProScore using conventional quantitative assays (e.g., enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays, high-performance liquid chromatography-based methods, or targeted mass spectrometry) is warranted to establish cross-platform concordance and absolute quantification. Third, pubertal status was not assessed and may confound proteomic measurements, though we adjusted for sex and found similar performance across sexes. Future studies should incorporate Tanner staging and/or hormonal profiling. Lastly, we relied on CAP and LSM measurements from transient elastography for MASLD diagnosis, which, although non-invasive and widely accepted, may not fully capture histological nuances such as lobular inflammation or ballooning.
Implications for clinical or public health practice
Our findings are relevant to healthcare policymakers, public health practitioners, and clinicians working to mitigate the escalating health crisis posed by pediatric MASLD [
2
]. The ProScore demonstrated better performance than conventional indices, including the TyG index, WHtR, and METS-IR, all of which are commonly used proxies for hepatic steatosis or insulin resistance [
13
,
55
]. Importantly, the added value of ProScore was evident not only in overall performance but also in its robustness across strata of genetic risk. The ProScore may provide non-invasive, high-precision MASLD screening in high-risk pediatric groups without fasting, imaging, or operator-dependent methods. As proteomic technology becomes more accessible, population-level screening warrants evaluation. Future work should include longitudinal validation to assess trajectories and treatment responsiveness, and external replication in independent pediatric and adult cohorts.
Conclusion
In this study, fifteen proteins were significantly associated with MASLD, and a six-protein inflammatory signature (ProScore) was identified that distinguishes MASLD from non-MASLD in children with overweight or obesity. This ProScore performed better than conventional metabolic and genetic indices and may serve as a promising, non-invasive, biologically-informed tool pending further validation.
