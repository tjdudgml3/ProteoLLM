Study Highlights
WHAT IS THE CURRENT KNOWLEDGE ON THE TOPIC?
The current practice utilizes Caco‐2 and Madin‐Darby Canine Kidney (MDCK) cells for predicting oral absorption. However, these models fail to physiologically replicate the human intestinal epithelial barrier because they lack metabolic competency and do not include other essential cell types, such as mucus‐producing goblet cells. Due to these limitations, accurately predicting oral absorption, gut extraction, CYP3A4 induction, and complex DDIs using these models can be challenging.
WHAT QUESTION DID THIS STUDY ADDRESS?
We comprehensively evaluated the EpiIntestinal™ model by measuring the expression levels of key drug‐metabolizing enzymes and transporters using RNA‐Seq analysis and proteomics. Next, we compared this model with Caco‐2 in predicting oral absorption by using a set of 18 compounds with known human fraction absorbed. We also examined the prediction of clinical PK for digoxin and dabigatran etexilate (P‐gp substrates) through PBPK modeling that incorporated permeability data from the EpiIntestinal™. Furthermore, the model was assessed for CYP3A induction and associated DDI risk using rifampicin (inducer) and midazolam (CYP3A substrate) in PBPK modeling. Finally, the EpiIntestinal™ was investigated to predict P‐gp and BCRP‐mediated DDIs using two clinical case studies of vepdegestrant.

The EpiIntestinal™ mimics the human intestinal barrier and has the potential to accurately predict the fraction absorbed (

HOW MIGHT THIS CHANGE CLINICAL PHARMACOLOGY OR TRANSLATIONAL SCIENCE?
While Caco‐2 and MDCK cells are valuable for high‐throughput drug permeability screening, EpiIntestinal™ serves as a translational model in later studies, refining drug absorption and DDI risk assessments in drug development.
Oral administration is the most preferred route of administration for medications; however, it comes with its challenges, as drugs must navigate the complex environment of the gastrointestinal (GI) tract. MATERIALS AND METHODS
Material
Caco‐2 cells were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and EpiIntestinal™ microtissues were obtained from MatTek (Ashland, MA). Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), nonessential amino acids (NEAA), glutamine, penicillin, streptomycin, and Hank’s balanced salt solution (HBSS) were procured from Thermo Fisher (Waltham, MA). 24‐well Corning Transwell inserts were purchased from Sigma‐Aldrich (St. Louis, MO). All the compounds used in this study were procured from either Sigma‐Aldrich (St. Louis, MO) or MedChemExpress LLC (Monmouth Junction, NJ).
Liquid chromatography mass spectrometry
Bioanalysis of compounds was conducted using an API 6500 or 7500 Sciex triple quadrupole mass spectrometer linked to an Exion or Shimadzu liquid chromatographer. Samples were diluted 2–3‐fold with internal standard (IS) solution (clozapine or alprazolam in 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile). Quantitation of test compounds was conducted using standard curves prepared by comparing peak area ratios of analyte over IS to known concentrations of test compound. Compounds were separated using C18 reverse phase Waters columns (Acquity CSH, Coretecs, Acquity Premier BEH or Xbridge). The mobile phases were 0.1% formic acid in water or 0.1% formic acid in 10 mM ammonium formate (A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (B). The gradient elution was 0–0.2 min, 5% (B), 0.2–0.8 min, 5–95% (B), 0.8–1 min, 95–5% (B), 1–1.5 min, 5% B. The flow rate was 0.8 or 1 mL/min. Electron spray ionization in positive mode was used to detect analytes. Data were processed using Analyst version 1.7.2 or OS version 3.1.6.44. MRM transitions for analytes are shown in

.

They were used in experiments within 1 week of recovery.
Gene expression analysis of

RNA‐Seq analysis was performed to quantify gene expression of DMETs in Caco‐2 and EpiIntestinal™ microtissues at Novogene Corporation Inc (Sacramento, CA). Both Caco‐2 cells (on day 21) and EpiIntestinal™ microtissues were lysed in RLT buffer. Cell lysates were shipped in dry ice to Novogene Corporation Inc. for human mRNA sequencing and quantification. To measure

Briefly, Total RNA was extracted from the cell lysate using the TRIzol method. Messenger RNA was purified using poly‐T oligo‐attached magnetic beads and used for sequencing analysis, as detailed in the supplemental file. Gene expression levels were estimated by the abundance of transcripts mapped to the genome or exon. The number of reads mapped to each gene was counted, with read counts being proportional to gene expression level, gene length, and sequencing depth. The FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript sequence per Million base pairs sequenced) for each gene was then calculated based on gene length and read counts. The FPKM data of major DMET genes in EpiIntestinal™ was then compared to that in Caco‐2 cells using a ratio (FPKM

Measurement of bidirectional transport of compounds
The apparent permeability (apical‐to‐basolateral, A>B) of 18 compounds with a human absorption range of 1–95% (
Table
S5
) was assessed in Caco‐2 cells and EpiIntestinal™ microtissues using HBSS buffer at pH 6.5 (apical) and pH 7.4 (basolateral). Caco‐2 cells or EpiIntestinal™ microtissues were incubated with HBSS buffer containing 5 μM of a test compound and 100 μM of Lucifer Yellow (zero permeability marker) on the donor side, with blank HBSS buffer on the receiver side. After 2 hours, samples from both sides were collected and analyzed by LC–MS/MS for compound quantification. Paracellular transport of Lucifer Yellow on the receiver side was determined by measuring fluorescence at excitation and emission wavelengths of 423 and 530 nm, respectively. Monolayers were considered intact in Caco‐2 when Lucifer Yellow leakage was < 1% and in EpiIntestinal™ microtissues when leakage was < 4% (data not shown).
Measurement of metabolic stability of selected compounds
The metabolic stability of eight compounds—CYP3A4 substrates (midazolam, atorvastatin, lovastatin, simvastatin, budesonide, buspirone), CES1 and CES2 substrate (dabigatran etexilate), and UGT substrate (raloxifene)—was assessed using EpiIntestinal™ microtissues under the same conditions as the permeability experiments. Compounds were incubated at 10 μM in HBSS buffer (pH 6.5) on the apical side (0.25 mL), with HBSS buffer (pH 7.4) on the basolateral side (0.5 mL), for periods of 15, 30, 60, 120, and 240 minutes. After incubation, 50 μL samples were collected from both sides, mixed with 50 μL acetonitrile (with an internal standard), and cell lysates were prepared using 400 μL acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid. Samples were centrifuged, 300 μL supernatant was dried, reconstituted with 100 μL HBSS buffer, and mixed with 100 μL acetonitrile (with an internal standard). LC–MS/MS was used to analyze the remaining compound and the major metabolite/s on the donor side, their transfer to the receiver side, and cellular accumulation at each time point, with a Thermo Fisher Q Exactive.
Induction of CYP3A4 activity in EpiIntestinal™ microtissues
To assess the potential for known PXR agonists to induce CYP3A4, EpiIntestinal™ microtissues were treated with increasing concentrations of prototypical inducers, carbamazepine (0.003–100 μM), phenytoin, or rifampicin (0.3–100 μM) for 72 hours, with media changes every 24 hours. Vehicle control samples contained the same concentration of DMSO as the test compounds and were kept below 0.5%. Enzyme activity for CYP3A4 was determined by adding 15 μM midazolam to the apical (0.2 mL) or basolateral (0.5 mL) compartments and monitoring for 1’‐hydroxymidazolam (1’‐OHmidazolam) formation following a 15 min incubation. The concentration of midazolam was selected to approximate reaction velocity (
V
max
) and the timepoint was within the linearity of the metabolite formation considering changes in activity due to induction. 1’OH‐midazolam formation was quantified using a Sciex 7500 with m/z of 342 ➔ 203 for metabolite and 345.1 ➔ 206.1 for d5‐1’OH‐midazolam as internal standard. Rates of formation were calculated from observed concentrations and divided by the vehicle control activity to determine the fold induction.

DISCUSSION
This study evaluated EpiIntestinal™ microtissues for measuring
F
a
and
F
g
to predict oral absorption and assess CYP3A4 induction and transporter‐mediated DDI risks. We compared the expression profiles of clinically relevant DMETs in this model to those in Caco‐2. RNA‐seq data revealed significantly higher expression of cytochromes P450, including
CYP2B6
,
CYP3A4/5
,
CYP2D6
, and
CYP2J2
, in EpiIntestinal™, indicating high metabolic competency. Although targeted proteomics confirmed CYP3A4/5 expression, levels were 4‐ to 32‐fold lower than in the human ileum (
Table
S4
). Our objective was to understand the clinical relevance of DMET protein levels in EpiIntestinal™ by comparing its proteomics data with published human ileum data; however, it is important to note that direct comparisons are challenging due to variability in preparation and analytical sensitivity across study methods.
28
Furthermore, the reported CYP3A4 abundance in the human ileum is derived from the mucosa (an enterocyte‐enriched fraction), making direct comparisons challenging. In contrast, the reported CYP3A4 abundance in the human jejunum is obtained from cell lysate, similar to the present study. As a result, the CYP3A4 abundance in EpiIntestinal™ appears to be similar to that in the human jejunum (
Table
S4
). Nonetheless, metabolism data confirmed the formation of relevant metabolites for CYP3A4/5 substrates like midazolam, budesonide, buspirone, and statins, further validating the model’s metabolic competency (
Figure
S2
).
CES2 is the primary carboxylesterase in the human small intestine, but both CES1 and CES2 isoforms are present in EpiIntestinal™ microtissues. Cui
et al
. examined the metabolism of dabigatran etexilate, a double prodrug hydrolyzed by CES2 in the small intestine and by CES1 and CES2 in the liver. Their study reported the formation of only the active drug (BIBR953) via CES1 and CES2, with no intermediates
26
(
Figure
S4
). In contrast, we observed BIBR1087 as the major metabolite (CES1‐mediated), with minimal amounts of BIBR0951 and BIBR953. This discrepancy may be due to different CES1 and CES2 activities under varying experimental conditions; Cui
et al
. used culture media (pH 7.4) for up to 24 hours, whereas we used HBSS buffer (pH 6.5) for up to 4 hours. Further research is needed to understand the impact of these conditions on CES1 and CES2 activities. Taken together, it is crucial to interpret findings with an understanding of the model’s limitations for compounds metabolized by intestinal CES2.
UGT2B17 > UGT1A1 > UGT2B15 showed a physiologically relevant expression pattern in EpiIntestinal™ microtissues (
Table
S4
), while UGT1A8 and UGT1A10, which are predominantly expressed in the human small intestine, were not detected by RNA‐seq analysis. However, the formation of a major glucuronide metabolite of raloxifene—primarily metabolized by UGT1A8 and UGT1A10 in the gut
29
—demonstrated the functional activity of these UGTs in the microtissues. Further investigation to confirm the expression of UGT1A8 and UGT1A10 at the protein level would improve the reliability of this model for understanding drug metabolism involving these UGTs.
Previously, EpiIntestinal™ microtissues have been shown to perform better compared to Caco‐2 in terms of IVIVc for oral absorption
25
; however, the current study shows only moderate improvement (
Figure
2
). This inconsistency could be due to different compounds and assay conditions across studies. Our focus was on low‐to‐moderately permeable compounds, a challenge for Caco‐2 models.
2
We tested 18 compounds in both models under consistent conditions (apical pH 6.5, basolateral pH 7.4), with only five compounds matching those in Ayehunie S
et al
.’s study. No specific Caco‐2 assay details were provided in their study. In this study, both models had similar false positives, but Caco‐2 had more false negatives (
Table
S5a,b
). The EpiIntestinal™ microtissues identified enalaprilat and cimetidine as false positives, with overpredictions of 26% and 177%, respectively. While our current IVIVc analysis employed a human %
F
a
of 60 for cimetidine, reported values typically range from 60 to 70.
30
Consequently, the predicted %
F
a
of 75 seems to align closely with the observed data. Enalaprilat is recognized as a substrate of the organic anion transporting polypeptide 2B1 (OATP2B1),
31
which plays a significant role in drug absorption in the intestine.
32
,
33
Consistent with this, we observed a higher A>B Papp (1.7 × 10
−6
cm/second) than B>A Papp (0.59 × 10
−6
cm/second) for enalaprilat in the EpiIntestinal™ model, suggesting active transport via OATP2B1. Further studies are needed to confirm OATP2B1’s role using a selective inhibitor. Notably, OATP2B1 was the most abundant transporter protein in our analysis (
Table
S4
), suggesting its robust expression may have contributed to enalaprilat’s overprediction. In the Caco‐2 model, aztreonam and reserpine were indicated as false positives. Aztreonam, a monobactam antibiotic, is poorly absorbed,
34
and its overprediction suggests that Caco‐2 may not adequately represent oral absorption for poorly permeable compounds. Reserpine undergoes substantial metabolism in the gut by esterases
35
; however, Caco‐2 cells lack metabolic competence, leading to an overestimation of its oral absorption (predicted
F
a
of 71 vs. observed
F
a
of 50). In contrast, the metabolically competent EpiIntestinal™ microtissues provided a more accurate prediction of reserpine absorption (predicted
F
a
of 62).
Both models moderately underpredicted the oral absorption of digoxin (
Table
S5a,b
). To further evaluate their translatability for predicting the clinical PK of digoxin and dabigatran etexilate (P‐gp substrates), we used PBPK modeling with Papp data from each model. The EpiIntestinal™ model’s predicted
C
max
closely matched clinical
C
max
for both drugs, accurately capturing absorption, while Caco‐2 significantly underpredicted
C
max
. These results highlight the high clinical relevance of EpiIntestinal™ microtissues, especially for drugs affected by P‐gp and BCRP.
EpiIntestinal™ was also assessed to predict the
F
g
of drugs. By integrating CL
int
and permeability data into PBPK modeling, the model predicted the
F
g
of 5 (out of 6) CYP3A4/5 substrate drugs reasonably well (within twofold except buspirone) (
Figure
S3
). This demonstrates the clinically relevant metabolic activity of CYP3A4/5 in EpiIntestinal™ microtissues. Further research involving a wider array of compounds that are substrates for other CYPs, UGTs, and sulfotransferases (SULTs) will be essential in highlighting the utility of EpiIntestinal™ in predicting
F
g
.
Induction of intestinal CYP3A4 and P‐gp plays a crucial role in DDIs. Activation of PXR, especially by strong inducers like rifampicin, increases these protein levels,
36
thereby affecting the PK of substrate drugs by reducing absorption and increasing clearance, which can lower their plasma concentrations and therapeutic efficacy.
36
,
37
Quantitatively assessing the induction potential of CYP3A4 and P‐gp is therefore crucial for predicting their PK impact and DDI risk. While primary human hepatocytes are used to measure hepatic CYP3A4 induction,
38
reliable
in vitro
tools for assessing intestinal CYP3A4 and P‐gp induction are lacking. Our data showed that EpiIntestinal™ effectively expressed PXR and demonstrated
CYP3A4
and
ABCB1
(P‐gp) induction by rifampicin, consistent with previous studies. Previous research by Brueck
et al
. and Burk
et al
. reported threefold induction of
ABCB1
and 4.8‐fold induction of
CYP3A4
mRNA in the human duodenum following rifampicin treatment (600 mg for 8 days), respectively.
39
,
40
To assess the net effect of CYP3A4 induction in the liver and intestine, we evaluated rifampicin’s induction parameters using TruVivo and EpiIntestinal™ microtissues for hepatic and intestinal
CYP3A4
, respectively. By incorporating induction parameters from both models, PBPK modeling provided a more accurate prediction of rifampicin’s DDI with midazolam, a sensitive CYP3A4 substrate, compared to using only TruVivo data (
Figure
5
). Overall, EpiIntestinal™ microtissues show promise as an
in vitro
platform for assessing intestinal CYP3A4 and P‐gp induction potential in predicting DDI risk.
This study also highlighted the potential of EpiIntestinal™ microtissues in predicting P‐gp and BCRP‐mediated DDIs through two clinical case studies. Vepdegestrant has been identified as an
in vitro
inhibitor of both P‐gp (IC
50
of 0.9 μM) and BCRP (IC
50
of 4.9 μM) (internal unpublished data) and was subsequently indicated as a precipitant of these transporters in the clinic. In the clinical DDI study, vepdegestrant produced approximately a twofold increase in the oral exposure of dabigatran etexilate, a P‐gp substrate, while showing no significant impact on rosuvastatin, a BCRP substrate. EpiIntestinal™ microtissues accurately captured both the positive DDI for P‐gp and the negative DDI for BCRP, unlike Caco‐2, at clinically relevant enterocyte concentrations of vepdegestrant (
Figure
6
). These findings underscore the utility of EpiIntestinal™ microtissues in identifying intestinal P‐gp and BCRP‐mediated DDIs in humans. While current
in vitro
models often yield high rates of false positives and negatives for these interactions, the EpiIntestinal™ microtissues offer a promising alternative for validating potential DDI risks associated with intestinal P‐gp/BCRP. DDIs can be complex when multiple enzymes and transporters, such as CYP3A4 and P‐gp, are involved in gut metabolism. Many oral drugs are dual substrates for CYP3A4 and P‐gp, complicating DDI assessments, especially when precipitants affect both through various mechanisms like direct inhibition, time‐dependent inhibition, and induction. In such complex scenarios, EpiIntestinal™ microtissues provide a comprehensive platform for evaluating DDI risks. Further studies are necessary to enhance confidence in this model’s ability to predict complex DDIs.
Developing effective
in vitro
systems to predict human intestinal absorption is essential due to the complexity of intestinal physiology. While Caco‐2 cells are commonly used for drug permeability testing, their lack of DMEs limits their effectiveness for drugs undergoing extensive metabolism. Emerging gut CIVMs with human intestinal organoids in 2D or 3D cultures show promise, but more data are needed to establish their
in vivo
relevance. No single CIVM replicates all metabolic functions, so it’s crucial to align their capabilities with specific ADME research purposes, like drug screening, dose predictions, and DDIs. While Caco‐2 cells are useful for high‐throughput screening of drug permeability, EpiIntestinal™ is suitable for subsequent studies on selected leads to refine drug absorption and DDI risk evaluations. These findings highlight the potential of integrating advanced
in vitro
models into the drug development pipeline to better understand complex interactions and support safer, more effective therapeutic regimens.
