1
Introduction
Honey bees play an indispensable
role in ecosystems and agriculture.
Through their pollination activities, they contribute toward global
food production and ecological diversity.
In recent years, honey bee populations have been exposed to multiple
environmental stressors, including parasites such as
Varroa
mites, pathogens, and climate change, leading
to a decline in colony health and a significant increase in winter
colony losses.
−
This decline in honey bee populations not only affects
beekeepers’ livelihoods but also poses a potential threat to
agricultural production. Therefore, novel monitoring technologies
are urgently needed to assess bee health and develop effective mitigation
strategies that enhance the colony resilience to environmental changes.
Proteomics has emerged as a powerful tool in honey bee research,
offering direct insights into the physiological state of colonies
by analyzing protein expression levels, post-translational modifications,
and interaction networks. As direct executors of cellular functions,
proteins provide a real-time and dynamic representation of cellular
processes, making proteomics a valuable approach for understanding
honey bee biology. Proteomics also offers a more relevant measure
of functional expression of the genome than transcriptomics, given
the often weak correlation between mRNA and protein abundance.
An important consideration when an experimental
approach is chosen to answer a scientific question is whether the
method chosen can be the best approach for answering that question.
For example, should we use proteomics to answer this question or could
it be answered faster and better using genome sequencing? In the context
of honey bee biology, in particular, proteomics has provided solutions
for questions that ostensibly might best be answered by using genomics.
Proteomics has enabled the identification of key functional molecules
and signaling pathway alterations, facilitating the discovery of critical
proteins directly involved in the adaptation of honey bees to environmental
changes, including disease resistance. The use of proteomics has facilitated
a selective breeding program for unique behaviors of bees that enable
disease resistance.
As a nonmodel organism
with limited genomic resources, honey bees exemplify how proteomics
can provide critical biological insights. Normally, genetic approaches
(e.g., genome-wide association studies, classical Mendelian breeding,
etc.) can be used for selective breeding, but these have so far failed
in honey bees, likely because of the extremely fast recombination
rate in bees.
However, proteomics
alone is insufficient to fully characterize
the biological complexity of honey bees or any other biological system.
With advancements in omics technologies, multiomics integration is
emerging as a comprehensive approach for honey bee research (
Figure
). Metabolomics,
which analyzes small-molecule metabolites (<1500 Da), offers a
functional readout of cellular processes shaped by genetics, environment,
and microbiome interactions, helping to uncover metabolic responses
in honey bees under different physiological states and environmental
stresses, such as fluctuations in amino acid, sugar, and organic acid
levels. Lipidomics, which focuses on the composition and metabolism
of lipids, is crucial for understanding energy storage, cellular signaling,
and stress resistance in honey bees. Lipid profiles can shift rapidly
in response to external stressors, revealing adaptive changes in energy
mobilization and signal transduction pathways.

2
Bee Omics
2.1
Proteomics
Proteomics has been extensively
utilized in honey bee research, providing insights into their physiology,
−
immune responses to parasites and infections,
−
adaptation to environmental stressors,
−
and the composition of honey bee products.
−
Early studies utilized gel-based and affinity-based proteomics to
study targeted proteins in honey bees and how they affect the bee’s
health and physiological states.
,
More recently,
mass spectrometry-based proteomics enabling the entire proteome to
be studied at once has gained more attention, particularly liquid
chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS). This approach
enables both data-dependent acquisition (DDA) and data-independent
acquisition (DIA) in conjunction with label-free or chemical labeling
strategies to reveal changes in honey bee proteome due to factors
such as parasite infection, environmental changes, and genetic mutations.
Isotopic labeling, such as Tandem Mass Tags (TMT) and Isobaric Tags
for Relative and Absolute Quantification (iTRAQ), has been widely
used for honey bee proteomics research. For example, TMT isotope-labeled
LC–MS/MS has been used to analyze proteomic changes in honey
bees under heat stress conditions through 3,799 proteins identified.
However, these labeling techniques increase
sample complexity and experiment costs and have higher requirements
for quantification software. To address these issues, label-free shotgun
LC–MS/MS methods have been developed.
Many used this approach to study the health of honey bee colonies
through proteomic variations.
−
Proteomics is more than just measuring the
differential protein expression. One key area proteomics can address
is how proteins interact with one another.
,
Measuring protein interaction networks enables the identification
of protein complexes and novel functional associations, offering a
network-level perspective on bee signaling pathways and behavioral
mechanisms. For instance, Skinnider et al. (2023) employed cofractionation
mass spectrometry in conjunction with machine learning to construct
the first honey bee interactome, revealing numerous protein complexes.
Advanced statistical methods, such as
principal component analysis
(PCA) and t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE), play
a crucial role in uncovering complex relationships within high-dimensional
mass spectrometry data from honey bee studies.
These approaches help to identify key protein expression
patterns associated with developmental stages, immune responses, and
environmental stressors. Complementing these methods, enrichment analysis
techniques like Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) and functional
analysis allow researchers to link differentially expressed proteins
to specific biological pathways relevant to honey bee health, such
as metabolic regulation and immune defense.
Together, these strategies provide deeper insights into the molecular
mechanisms underlying honey bee physiology, behavior, and responses
to environmental challenges.
Proteomics is transforming honey
bee research by enabling a systems-level
understanding of the proteins that drive key biological processes.
The integration of advanced mass spectrometry techniques with robust
statistical and computational tools allows researchers to move beyond
simple protein identification to mapping dynamic protein interactions,
regulatory networks, and functional pathways. These insights are critical
for unraveling the molecular mechanisms behind honey bee development,
immunity, and responses to environmental stressors. As proteomic approaches
continue to evolve, they will play an increasingly vital role in addressing
pressing challenges in honey bee health, from pathogen resistance
to the impacts of climate change and habitat loss.
2.2
Metabolomics: Real-Time Snapshots of Physiological
States
While proteomics provides insights into protein-level
changes, metabolomics offers a complementary perspective by capturing
the dynamic state of small-molecule metabolites. Metabolomics has
become a crucial tool for understanding the complex processes that
influence honey bee physiology and their responses to stress at the
level of small molecules present in a system. Advanced analytical
techniques, including proton nuclear magnetic resonance (
1
H NMR) and various mass spectrometry methods (i.e., GC–MS,
LC–MS), allow for detailed profiling of small-molecule metabolites.
−
These metabolites range from primary energy sources, such as glucose
and trehalose, to secondary signaling compounds, such as pheromones.
While
1
H nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (NMR)
provides a quick and nondestructive way to analyze abundant metabolites,
mass spectrometry techniques offer greater sensitivity for detecting
lower-abundance substances, including pheromones.
Additionally, computational tools such as XCMS
(for peak alignment and feature extraction)
and MetaboAnalyst
(for multivariate statistical
analysis and pathway mapping) have standardized metabolomic workflows,
improving reproducibility across different studies.
Metabolomics
enables precise mapping of metabolic perturbations in honey bee tissues,
hemolymph, and gut systems, offering a functional lens to decode physiological
adaptations to environmental and pathogenic stressors. By measuring
shifts in small-molecule profiles, this approach bridges molecular
dynamics with the health outcomes of the organism.
−
Recent studies have shown that changes in metabolite levels under
nutritional or stress conditions can provide important biological
insights. For instance, Wang et al. (2022) employed
1
H
NMR metabolomics to demonstrate that sublethal glyphosate exposure
suppresses branched-chain amino acids (leucine, valine, and isoleucine)
and lysine in worker bees.
These amino
acids are critical for protein synthesis, immune function, and neurotransmitter
production, suggesting glyphosate disrupts nitrogen metabolism and
synaptic signaling.
While acute exposure
did not alter foraging behavior, chronic depletion likely exacerbates
colony vulnerability by impairing brood care. Similarly, in larvae
infected with
Ascosphaera apis
, Li
et al. (2020) identified a metabolomic crisis marked by a 2-fold upregulation
of 5-oxo-ETE,
a pro-inflammatory eicosanoid
linked to oxidative stress. Concurrent declines in glutathione precursors
(e.g., cysteine, glycine) and antioxidant enzymes indicate pathogen-driven
redox imbalance.
This dysregulation,
heightened inflammation, and compromised detoxification may explain
larval mortality, positioning 5-oxo-ETE as a candidate biomarker for
early chalkbrood detection.
A comparative study of newly emerged
vs laying queens revealed
metabolic remodeling: carnitine and fatty acid levels surge during
egg production, while amino acids decline.
This shift appears to reflect enhanced mitochondrial β-oxidation
to fuel oogenesis and compensatory antioxidant synthesis (e.g., elevated
NAD
+
), underscoring the metabolic cost of reproductive
output. Such findings challenge the notion of queen longevity as purely
genetic, emphasizing instead the role of metabolic plasticity in balancing
fecundity and senescence.
Overall, metabolomics has become a
cornerstone in decoding the
interplay between environmental stressors and honey bee health, capturing
real-time metabolic shifts that complement genomic and proteomic data.
Machine learning-enhanced metabolomics now enables predictive diagnostics
for colony collapse and precision beekeeping strategies. To advance
the field, standardized protocols and bee-specific metabolite databases
are urgently needed. Future integration with spatial omics and field-deployable
sensors will transform metabolomics from a research tool into a frontline
solution for safeguarding pollinators against anthropogenic threats.
2.3
Lipid Dynamics in
A. mellifera
While proteomics and metabolomics provide valuable insights
into protein function and metabolic state, lipidomics offers a complementary
view by focusing on the roles of lipids in honey bee biology. Lipids
are essential regulators of energy storage, signaling pathways, and
stress responses. Lipidomics has emerged as a transformative tool
for unraveling the multifaceted roles of lipids in honey bee physiology,
spanning energy regulation, stress adaptation, and neurobiological
resilience.
−
Advances in LC–MS-based lipidomics now enable simultaneous
profiling of pheromone components, metabolites, and lipids, uncovering
metabolic rewiring linked to physiological states such as age, mating
status, and environmental stress.
Central
to these processes is the fat body, a multifunctional organ that orchestrates
lipid storage, mobilization, and signaling, making it pivotal for
colony health. Coupled with high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS),
lipidomics precisely measures hundreds of lipid species, revealing
how these molecules underpin survival strategies in ecologically critical
contexts, from nutrient scarcity to pathogen challenges. For instance,
many queen pheromones are lipid-derived compounds, including short-chain
hydroxy fatty acids (e.g., 9-HDA) and fatty acid methyl esters (e.g.,
methyl oleate), which are central to the queen retinue pheromone (QRP)
blend. Structural nuances, such as hydroxylation, double-bond positioning,
and isomerism, govern pheromone specificity and bioactivity, enabling
precise worker behavioral responses. Furthermore, lipid mediators
like prostaglandins and thromboxanes, critical for oogenesis and immunity,
have been identified as key players in laying queens.
Viral infection in honey bee queens reduces methyl
oleate, a key lipid-pheromone linked to worker attraction. This methyl
oleate decline stems from ovary atrophy, not direct infection, suggesting
workers detect reproductive decline to initiate supersedure (the process
where workers make the collective decision to replace their queen).
While ovary restriction mimics methyl oleate
loss, it spares triacylglycerols and immune responses, highlighting
unidirectional energy trade-offs favoring reproduction over immunity
during infection. Apolipophorin-III (ApoLP-III), a lipid transporter,
diminishes with ovary restriction, implicating its dual roles in nutrient
allocation and immune regulation. Methodologically, advanced lipidomics
outperforms traditional metabolomics in resolving critical isomers
such as methyl oleate, correcting prior misinterpretations. Future
work must clarify if methyl oleate depletion directly triggers supersedure
and define ApoLP-III’s mechanistic role. These insights underscore
lipidomics as pivotal for unraveling metabolic drivers of colony resilience.
Under resource-poor conditions, honey
bees exhibit remarkable metabolic
plasticity, as demonstrated by Castaños et al. (2023),
who used untargeted lipidomics to reveal profound
lipid remodeling in worker bees subjected to starvation or sugar-only
diets. Triglycerides (TAGs) and diglycerides (DAGs) surged, serving
as emergency energy reservoirs, while free fatty acids (FFAs) declined
sharply, indicating rapid β-oxidation to sustain basal metabolism.
Additionally, fatty acid desaturase (FADS) genes were upregulated,
enhancing lipid unsaturation to maintain membrane fluidity and metabolic
flexibility.
Additionally, lipidomics
provides a new perspective for understanding the honey bee’s
nervous system and behavior. Morfin et al. (2022) were the first to
map the lipidome of the honey bee brain and investigate the effects
of neonicotinoid pesticides.
The results
showed that the lipid composition of a healthy honey bee brain differs
from that of other organisms, exhibiting an abnormally high proportion
of alkyl-ether-linked phospholipids and a low content of plasmalogens.
This unique lipid profile may make the honey bee brain more susceptible
to environmental neurotoxins.
Limitations in Lipidomics and
Metabolomics: While lipidomics and
metabolomics further contribute to critical insights into honey bee
physiology, significant challenges persist in these fields. A major
limitation is the vast unknowns in lipid and metabolite annotation,
particularly for nonmodel organisms like
A. mellifera
; over 80% of detected ions in biological samples remain unannotated,
hindering functional interpretation. Structural isomerismcritical
for pheromone specificity (e.g., methyl oleate vs coeluting isomers)
and short-chain hydroxy fatty acidoften escapes detection
in traditional workflows, leading to the mischaracterization of key
signaling molecules. Critically, the identification of small molecules
predominantly depends on MS/MS spectral matching, with the unequivocal
validation of compounds requiring authentic chemical standardsthe
recognized “gold standard” for confident annotation.
Nevertheless, the limited scope of existing spectral libraries coupled
with the lack of well-characterized standards for numerous lipids
and metabolites represents a fundamental bottleneck that constrains
discovery and biological interpretation. Addressing these limitations
is crucial for advancing the field and gaining a deeper understanding
of the chemical ecology and physiology of
Apis mellifera
.

Contemporary Technologies for Bee Omics
Modern omics technologies, including mass spectrometry, have enhanced
our ability to study honey bee biology at the molecular, spatial,
and cellular levels. While traditional approaches reveal interesting
biological changes, they often lack resolution; spatial and single-cell
omics now fill this gap. These new technologies capture cellular heterogeneity
and tissue-specific adaptations, offering a refined view of stress
responses. This section outlines cutting-edge omics tools used in
bee research and sets the stage for integrative multiomics analysis.
3.1
Spatial Omics
Spatial omics technologies
enable researchers to map the molecular organization within honey
bee tissues. In the multicellular systems, cells do not function in
isolation but are strongly influenced by others in the microenvironment.
Significant advancements in spatial analysis,
including Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption/Ionization (MALDI)
and mass spectrometry imaging (MSI),
have enhanced our spatial understanding of the
honey bee tissues.
−
For example, MALDI MSI was used to analyze honey bees infected with
Nosema
microsporidia, revealing distinct spatial
distributions of peptides and proteins in different tissues such as
the gut, brain, pharyngeal gland, and hemolymph.
Other experiments include the identification of hymenopteran
peptides from the brain and retrocerebral complex of adult worker
honey bees,
whereas Pratavieira et al.
demonstrated a clear correlation between honey bee aggressive behaviors
and the presence of neuropeptides using MALDI imaging.
By preserving the spatial context of proteins,
metabolites, and lipids, these methods enhance our understanding of
tissue-specific responses to environmental and pathological challenges.
3.2
Single-Cell Omics
Despite advancements
in spatial technologies, their low resolution limits the ability to
capture fine-scale molecular interactions, potentially missing crucial
molecular information. Single-cell analysis has gained significant
attention in recent decades due to its ability to investigate molecular
information at the cellular level, which is often lost in traditional
bulk analysis. In honey bees, single-cell genomics and transcriptomics
have identified subpopulation heterogeneity, elucidating specific
cell types associated with queen bee brain development,
gene expression signatures of worker bees,
pathogen-associated cells responsible for infections,
and honey bee gut symbionts.
These studies have provided important insights into understanding
the cellular dynamics and development of honey bees.
With the
increasing attention to proteins, there have been significant advancements
in single-cell proteomics, shortly followed by single-cell lipidomics
and metabolomics,
,
offering deeper insights into
honey bee cellular mechanisms. Specifically, recent technological
innovations have introduced highly sensitive mass spectrometers and
automated sample preparation workflows.
−
Moreover, advanced sample
handling technologies, such as cellenONE and Tecan Uno, enable precise
single-cell manipulation.
,
Microfluidic devices,
such as Nanodroplet Processing in One pot for Trace Samples (nanoPOT)
and Active Matrix Digital Microfluidics (AM-DMF),
as well as miniaturized sample preparation systems
such as Nanoliter-Scale Oil-Air-Droplet Chip
have further improved protein detection. The recent introduction
of state-of-the-art mass spectrometers, such as Orbitrap Astral Mass
Spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), has significantly enhanced
the capability to detect more than 5,000 protein groups from individual
cells.
This advanced instrument offers
superior sensitivity and resolution, enabling the detection of low-abundance
proteins in complex samples. Its high dynamic range and precision
in quantification allow for accurate profiling of both abundant and
rare proteins, while its enhanced peptide fragmentation capabilities
improve protein identification. These features are particularly valuable
for single-cell proteomics, where capturing cellular heterogeneity
is crucial.
Following the success of single-cell proteomics,
single-cell lipidomics
has emerged, capturing hundreds of lipid features within single cells.
,
Efforts to integrate multiomics single-cell data provide unprecedented
insights into subpopulation heterogeneity and complete molecular profiling.
Applying these technologies to honey bees holds
immense potential for understanding cellular heterogeneity across
developmental stages and castes, investigating responses to environmental
stressors, and unraveling molecular mechanisms underlying pathogen
resistance.
3.3
Single-Cell Spatial Omics
While single-cell
omics provide comprehensive molecular information, they lack spatial
context within tissues. High-resolution spatially resolved technologies,
such as barcoding in tissue for spatial omics sequencing (DBiT-seq),
have significantly advanced our ability to map the distribution of
proteins, metabolites, and lipids within tissues.
Additionally, many have introduced innovative spatial omics
integration analysis tools, such as SIMO
or CAST,
to achieve spatial integration
of multiple single-cell modalities and understand the spatial regulation
of biological molecules. Other recently developed single-cell spatial
techniques include spatial transcriptomics methods like 10
x
Genomics Visium and Slide-seq, which enable high-resolution
mapping of gene expression in tissues.
Furthermore, barcoded imaging techniques like multiplexed error-robust
fluorescence in situ hybridization (MERFISH) and codetection by indexing
(CODEX) provide additional layers of molecular resolution at the single-cell
level.
,
While these approaches have been extensively
applied to mammalian research, the application in honey bee work remains
limited.
Integrating single-cell omics with spatial technologies
can significantly advance honey bee biology, facilitating the development
of reference cell atlases. High-performance computing (HPC) and cloud
computing platforms will enable researchers to analyze large-scale
multiomics data sets using more sophisticated algorithms. Together,
they can provide valuable information about how to prevent infection
spread and better treatments for bee health and colony resilience.
4
Computational Strategies for Omics
Analyzing
the complexity of honey bee omics data requires robust
computational strategies. Advanced statistical and machine learning
approaches are essential for uncovering biological insights. This
section provides an overview of key computational methods used to
explore honey bee molecular mechanisms.
4.1
Preprocessing
Methods
Before conducting
omics data analysis, systematic preprocessing and quality control
are essential to ensure the reliability and comparability of data
across different experimental batches and omics platforms.
Low-abundance
peptides or proteins below the instrument detection limit are typically
filtered out to reduce technical noise and improve downstream statistical
power.
Signals from known contaminantssuch
as human keratin or polymer-related background signals introduced
by laboratory consumablesare typically removed during post-database
search filtering using curated contaminant databases such as the common
Repository of Adventitious Proteins (cRAP), to avoid false biological
interpretations.
,
Log
2
transformation
is commonly applied to intensity
values to stabilize variance, reduce skewed distributions, and make
the data more amenable to parametric analyses.
Log
2
-transformed mass spectrometry intensities
enhance the analysis of low-abundance proteins in honey bee samples.
Missing values are common in mass spectrometry-based proteomics due
to technical limitations, such as low peptide abundance, ion suppression,
and the stochastic nature of peptide selection during data acquisition.
Common approaches include k-nearest neighbors
(k-NN), random forests (RF), Bayesian models, or low-rank matrix approximations.
,
To correct technical bias, intensity distributions are normalized
(e.g., quantile normalization) and batch effects adjusted using ComBat
or limma.
,
In metabolomics analyses, researchers
commonly use probabilistic quotient normalization (PQN) combined with
data transformation and Pareto scaling to correct sample variability.
After rigorous quality control and normalization,
technical biases are minimized, ensuring that observed differences
more accurately reflect the biological states of honey bees.
4.2
Differential Expression Analysis
After preprocessing,
identifying differentially expressed proteins,
metabolites, or genes is a crucial step in understanding honey bee
biology. Software packages such as prolfqua,
DEP2,
PBLMM,
or limma
have been utilized for identifying
differentially expressed proteins.
,
For two-group
comparisons, statistical tests, such as the
t
-test
or Wilcoxon rank-sum test, are appropriate. When dealing with multiple
groups, Analysis of Variance (ANOVA)
is commonly employed. For more complex experimental designs, linear
mixed models (LMMs)
,
are utilized. To control the
false discovery rate (FDR) and minimize false positives, the Benjamini–Hochberg
or other controlling procedure is applied.
,
These rigorous statistical frameworks ensure that differentially
expressed molecules identified are biologically relevant rather than
artifacts of noise or sample variability.
4.3
Functional
Enrichment and Pathway Analysis
and Visualization
Once differentially expressed molecules
are identified, functional enrichment and pathway analysis provide
biological context by linking the data to the underlying cellular
processes. Biological interpretation of differentially expressed proteins
can be achieved through Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway enrichment
analyses using tools like DAVID,
g:Profiler,
ClusterProfiler,
and Metascape.
Protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks
can be constructed using databases such as STRING,
Cytoscape,
and BioGRID to
identify molecular complexes or subnetworks. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis
(GSEA)
provides pathway-level insights
without requiring arbitrary expression cutoffs.
Visualization
tools such as ggplot2, pheatmap, and ComplexHeatmap
are commonly used to generate volcano plots and heatmaps,
providing intuitive representations of differential expression results.
Dimension reduction techniques such as Principal Component Analysis
(PCA),
t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor
Embedding (t-SNE),
and Uniform Manifold
Approximation and Projection (UMAP)
are
utilized for sample clustering and quality control. Partial least-squares
discriminant analysis (PLS-DA),
a supervised
learning method, is used to identify key variables that distinguish
different treatment groups. In honey bee metabolomics, PLS-DA is applied
to select the most influential metabolic features, computing variable
importance in projection (VIP) scores to filter potential biomarkers.
These computational approaches offer a clear view of the biological
pathways and processes influenced by environmental stressors, diseases,
and genetic variations.
5
AI for Multiomics Integration
Strategies
As the complexity of honey bee omics data grows,
integration of
multiple layers of information is essential for gaining comprehensive
biological insights. AI offers powerful tools for multimodalities
integration, enabling researchers to uncover complex relationships
that would be difficult to identify using traditional approaches.
Traditional ML algorithms are used to extract useful information
from the honey bee proteomic data. For instance, support vector machines
(SVMs)
and random forests
have been applied for classifying protein fingerprints.
Marcell Veiner et al. (2022) applied random forest, Lasso regression,
and SVMs to honey bee brain transcriptome data, successfully identifying
key genes like boss and
hnRNP A1
associated with
waggle dance behavior.
Meanwhile, multiomics
integration has provided valuable insights into physiological responses
to environmental stressors. For example, a study on Varroa mite parasitism
revealed consistent transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic alterations,
including heightened immune responses and energy depletion.
These approaches demonstrate how AI and multiomics
integration complement traditional gene expression analysis, enabling
the discovery of molecular markers linked to complex social behaviors.
To gain insights from multiomics data, researchers use joint modeling
and network-based integration.
Joint
modeling methods like multiomics factor analysis (MOFA)
simultaneously analyze multiple omics matrices
to identify shared patterns in the latent space. Network-based integration
represents omics layers as network levels using molecular interactions
for association analysis. For example, a multilayer network can link
genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolites through gene–protein
or enzyme–metabolite interactions, providing a clearer view
of interomics relationships.
With
the advancement of deep learning, sophisticated models now
integrate diverse multiomics data, extracting meaningful biological
insights, with a focus on functional capabilities and interpretability
rather than strict model classifications.
Unsupervised machine learning models have become pivotal in enhancing
proteomics, metabolomics, and lipidomics data exploration by uncovering
intricate biological patterns without relying on predefined labels.
,
,
Furthermore, the integration
of lipidomics with transcriptomics through unsupervised methods has
provided insights into the crosstalk between multiomics.
−
A notable example is the Multi-Omic Synthetic Augmentation (MOSA)
framework, which improves multiomics representations through synthetic
data generation.
In supervised
contexts, architectures such as feed-forward neural
networks (FNNs) and graph-based models have demonstrated high efficacy
for phenotype prediction and subtype classification tasks. For instance,
DeepMoIC and similar deep graph convolutional models have shown success
in cancer subtype identification across multiomics layers.
−
Meanwhile, autoencoders and contrastive learning frameworks have
emerged as powerful tools in unsupervised integration, particularly
for learning shared latent representations across diverse omics modalities.
Beyond representation learning, generative
models, such as variational autoencoders (VAEs)
and generative adversarial networks (GANs),
offer powerful capabilities for data denoising,
imputation, and synthetic augmentation (
Figure
). Recent studies show that such models can
enhance subtype discovery and resilience to missing data in noisy,
real-world omics data sets.
,
Moreover, transformer-based
architectures using Multi-Head Self-Attention (MHSA) have introduced
new flexibility and precision in modeling complex intra- and interomics
relationships.
Models like DMOIT and
DeepKEGG demonstrate how attention-based mechanisms improve feature
interaction learning and cancer recurrence prediction.
,
More deep learning models other than traditional machine learning
approaches have been applied to the multiomics data, including discriminative
models (e.g., Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), Convolutional Neural Network
(CNN), and Recurrent Neural Network (RNN)), generative models (e.g.,
AE, Deep Belief Networks (DBN), Restricted Boltzmann Machines (RBM),
and GAN), reinforcement learning (e.g., Deep Reinforcement Learning
(DRL) and model-based or model-free approaches), and hybrid/transfer
learning models (e.g., AE + Support Vector Machine (SVM), GAN + CNN,
CNN + Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), Diffusion, and Domain Transfer
Learning (DTL)) (
Figure
).

These models mitigate
data sparsity, infer missing
omics layers,
and enhance the classification performance. Success in multiomics
integration depends on model choice, biological question framing,
data availability, and balancing predictive performance with interpretability.
While still emerging, AI-driven deep learning frameworks are increasingly
recognized as essential for future honey bee health surveillance and
precision pollinator management.
6
Challenges
and Future Perspectives
6.1
Experimental and Computational
Challenges
in Bee Multiomics
Applying multiomics technologies in honey
bee research presents significant experimental and analytical challenges.
These challenges stem largely from the small body size and complex
anatomy of bees, which limit the amount and consistency of biological
material extractable for proteomics, metabolomics, and lipidomics.
Simultaneous extraction of different omics layers is difficult because
each molecular classproteins, lipids, or metabolitesrequires
distinct and often incompatible protocols. For instance, many solvent
extraction methods, such as chloroform/methanol extraction, disrupt
sample integrity by causing large-scale denaturation of proteins,
leading them to precipitate, and hindering enzymatic digestion needed
for bottom-up proteomics.
Optimized
protocols like the MTBE-based Matyash method allow for phase separation
of lipids, metabolites, and proteins, enabling multiomics analysis.
Protein pellets are recovered from the interphase
and resolubilized using MS-compatible buffers (e.g., RapiGest, SDC).
These protocols are validated by assessing recovery yields, proteome
coverage, and reproducibility across omics layers as reported in multiomics
studies.

Single-cell and spatial workflows
further compound these issues, requiring gentle tissue dissociation,
high cell viability, and ultralow-input extraction methodsall
of which are difficult to optimize in insects like bees.
On the computational side, data integration
across omics types introduces additional complexity. Converting heterogeneous
omics data into meaningful biological insights is still a major obstacle.
Integrating multiomics data to derive meaningful biological insights
presents several challenges due to the heterogeneous nature of the
data.
,
Different omics introduces features that
vary in scale, structure, and complexity.
Each data type requires tailored preprocessing steps, including
batch effect correction, normalization, filtering, and dealing with
missing values properly to ensure accuracy and comparability.
,
Inadequate preprocessing can lead to discrepancies between omics
layers, resulting in biased or incomparable integration outcomes.
Therefore, meticulous optimization from sample preparation to data
preprocessing is essential to maintaining data integrity across different
omics platforms.
High dimensionality
and small sample sizes in multiomics studies pose significant statistical
challenges, increasing the risk of overfitting and false discoveries.
Integration methods like multilayer network analysis, which combine
gene regulatory networks, protein–protein interactions, and
metabolic pathways, often fail to capture cross-layer associations.
Machine learning and statistical modeling approaches, including coexpression
analysis, principal component analysis, and Bayesian networks, offer
potential solutions, but challenges remain in identifying key molecular
modules. Balancing technical noise and biological signals remains
a critical issue, underscoring the need for robust fusion strategies
in small-sample studies.
,
Technological
innovations are helping to bridge some of these gaps.
In proteomics, the advent of single-cell and spatial proteomic techniques
now allows researchers to profile protein expression in individual
cells and even map their spatial distributions within tissues. For
example, next-generation workflows like Deep Visual Proteomics combine
high-content microscopy, laser microdissection, and ultrasensitive
MS to resolve the in situ proteome of cells in a tissue context.
,
Such spatial single-cell proteomics approaches can reveal how protein
networks vary between brain regions of a honey bee or between different
cell types in the gut, shedding light on cell-specific responses to
stress or infection. These cutting-edge methods greatly extend traditional
bulk proteomics by capturing cellular heterogeneity and microenvironmental
context that would otherwise be lost.
Parallel advances are
occurring in metabolomics and other MS-based
domains. Spatial metabolomics, enabled by techniques like imaging
mass spectrometry (IMS), can visualize the distribution of metabolites
and lipids directly on honey bee tissue sections.
Unlike conventional LC–MS metabolomics on homogenized
samples, spatial MS imaging preserves the location of each metabolite
within an organism, converting “invisible” chemical
information into spatially resolved maps. This approach has been applied
even to insect specimens such as bees, allowing researchers to localize
key molecules (e.g., pheromones, pesticides, or nutritional metabolites)
in different organs. The ability to map metabolic and chemical cues
within the bee, down to cellular or subcellular resolution, offers
new insights into phenomena like pathogen spread through tissues or
the organ-specific effects of agrochemicals.
6.2
AI and
Multimodal Integration: From Omics
to Ecology
Advanced ML or AI methods are transforming how
researchers analyze multiomics data in honey bee studies. As omics
data sets grow in dimensionality and complexity, traditional statistical
models struggle to capture nonlinear interactions, integrate diverse
data types, or extract predictive biomarkers. AI toolsincluding
deep learning, graph-based algorithms, and federated learningnow
offer new solutions to these long-standing limitations (
Figure
b).
In particular, graph
neural networks (GNNs) have emerged as powerful tools to model biological
systems.
GNNs can represent multiomics
data (genes, proteins, microbes, etc.) as interconnected networks,
capturing nonlinear relationships and interactions that classical
analyses might miss. Such models can reveal key regulators of immunity,
nutrition, and disease resistance that remain hidden in linear models.
Federated learning (FL) and collaborative computing frameworks offer
a powerful solution for capturing regional differences in bee populations.
FL enables machine learning models to train
on data from multiple sources without centralizing the raw data, making
it ideal when data sharing is restricted.
Transformers use self-attention mechanisms to capture long-range
dependencies and complex interactions, making them ideal for high-dimensional
omics data.
Recent transformer models
integrate multiple omics layers and embed biological knowledge like
pathways and networks, achieving state-of-the-art accuracy in tasks
like cancer subtyping. They could similarly be applied to honey bee
data sets to predict pathogen resistance or nutritional status from
integrated omics profiles. Additionally, diffusion models, a class
of generative deep learning models, are gaining traction in omics
research.
For example, the hybrid model
Precious2GPT generates realistic synthetic data from multiomics inputs,
simulating complex biological patterns. These models can augment small
data sets, simulate scenarios like dietary changes, and help identify
gene signatures by analyzing how synthetic alterations affect predictions.
Self-supervised models pretrain on unlabeled data using tasks like
cross-omics prediction, contrastive learning, or denoising, extracting
meaningful features without explicit labels.
These models significantly enhance downstream predictions with minimal
labeled data and can even perform zero-shot classification in some
cases.
Equally important is integrating omics with behavioral
and environmental
data to form a multimodal view of bee health. For instance, studies
combining genomics and transcriptomics with quantitative behavior
tracking have begun to illuminate the molecular basis of social behavior
in bees. In one study, researchers monitored the complete activity
profiles of individual workers in colonies and simultaneously profiled
their brain gene expression and chromatin accessibility.
Population omics and landscape genomics have
linked genomic variants to adaptation across climates and land uses.
Such integration will benefit from emerging
multimodal learning frameworks that can process genomics, sensor signals,
acoustic features, and image data simultaneouslyusing transformer-based
or hybrid deep learning models. Crucially, the focus is shifting not
only toward accuracy but also toward interpretability, sustainability,
and accessibility, so that these models are useable by broader ecological
and agricultural stakeholders (

Conclusion
Honey
bee health is crucial
for global food security and biodiversity
and requires comprehensive molecular analysis. While proteomics provides
valuable insights, single-omics approaches alone are insufficient.
Multiomics integrationincluding genomics, transcriptomics,
and metabolomicshas become essential for understanding honey
bee responses to pathogens, pesticides, and environmental stressors.
Despite advancements in mass spectrometry and single-cell technologies,
technical challenges persist such as tissue complexity, protocol incompatibilities,
and limited sample availability. Integrating heterogeneous omics data
also presents analytical hurdles, requiring tailored preprocessing,
normalization, and robust validation.
Artificial intelligence,
particularly deep learning, is revolutionizing
bee omics by enabling complex data analysis, biomarker discovery,
and predictive modeling for colony health. Looking ahead, combining
AI with multiomics will pave the way for “precision apiculture,”
integrating molecular, environmental, and behavioral data for real-time
colony monitoring.
As experimental methods
and computational tools advance, these frameworks will be central
to protecting one of nature’s most vital pollinators.
