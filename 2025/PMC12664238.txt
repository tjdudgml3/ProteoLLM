Introduction
Background and rationale
Worldwide, more than 900,000 individuals are diagnosed with a new head and neck cancer yearly, and approximately 70–80% undergo radiation therapy (RT) alone or in combination with chemotherapy [
1
,
2
]. Although hypofractionated and intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) maximises delivery to target tissue while reducing toxicity to healthy tissue, patients still experience xerostomia and hyposalivation as significant long-term side effects following RT [
3
,
4
]. Radiation damages the salivary glands and induces both acute and late responses including chronic inflammation, loss of acinar and progenitor/stem cells, and fibrosis leading to salivary gland hypofunction and the risk of dry mouth syndrome, xerostomia [
5
–
7
].
Radiation-induced xerostomia is associated with oral infections, impaired speech and swallowing, oral discomfort, sleep disturbances, and severely negatively impacts on the overall quality of life in cancer survivors [
8
,
9
]. Current available treatment strategies for xerostomia merely alleviate the symptoms, highlighting the need for new, innovative treatment opportunities [
10
–
12
].
Mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) have shown encouraging results in improvement of the salivary gland function following radiation in both preclinical in vivo studies [
13
] and in human clinical trials [
14
–
16
]. We have previously shown that intraglandular adipose-derived mesenchymal stromal cells (ASCs) have the potential to improve the salivary flow rate (SFR) and increase the quality-of-life (QoL) in humans in both a randomised, controlled trial with autologous ACSs [
15
], in a safety study with allogeneic ASCs [
17
], and most recently in a randomised, controlled trial with allogeneic ASCs [
16
]. The studies demonstrated that intraglandular therapy with ASCs was safe without serious adverse events, and that patients receiving ASCs significantly increased their salivary production with 33–50% resulting in increased QoL [
15
,
17
].
However, the mode of action is not fully understood, but mass spectrometry saliva analysis from the MESRIX-II study indicates that intraglandular ASC therapy changes the stimulated salivary proteome with increases in proteins related to tissue regeneration, angiogenesis, immune system functions and cell growth [
18
]. Specifically, the study showed that 99 proteins were differentially expressed 4 months post ASC therapy compared to baseline, although not restored to healthy conditions [
18
]. No differences were observed in the functional expression of the oral microbiota. However, the study was small (
n
= 10), was not a randomised, placebo-controlled trial and did not investigate the unstimulated saliva proteome, hampering the possibility to draw any definite conclusion from these data.
Therefore, the purpose of this MESRIX-III sub study is to evaluate the changes in the salivary proteome and microbial proteome in unstimulated whole saliva (UWS) following intraglandular treatment with allogenic ASCs compared to placebo for radiation-induced hyposalivation in previous head and neck cancer patients in a randomised, double-blinded, placebo-controlled trial. Second, we wish to evaluate the changes in the salivary proteome and microbial proteome in the group receiving ASCs from baseline to 4 months and to characterise the salivary proteome and microbial proteome following radiation therapy at baseline in both groups.
Objectives
The present sub study aims to characterise the changes in the salivary proteome 4 months post intraglandular ASC therapy compared to placebo in previous head and neck cancer patients with radiation-induced hyposalivation. The research hypothesis is as follows: Does intraglandular ASC therapy induce changes in the unstimulated salivary proteome compared to placebo?
Study design
The study is a sub study in the randomised, placebo-controlled, double-blinded trial investigating the salivary proteome in UWS following intraglandular ASCs treatment for radiation-induced hyposalivation in previous head and neck cancer patients, MESRIX-III study. Details on the study design, setting, population, sample size, randomisation, blinding and intervention are previously described [
16
,
19
].
The study will be conducted in coherence with the study protocol and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and monitored by the Good Clinical Practice (GCP) unit at the University of Copenhagen. The study protocol follows the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) [
20
] and the BMC Trials guidelines (
https://trialsjournal.biomedcentral.com/submission-guidelines/preparing-your-manuscript
).
Methods: participants, interventions and outcomes
Study setting
The study was be conducted at the Department of Otorhinolaryngology, Head and Neck Surgery and Audiology, Rigshospitalet, Denmark. All patients with previous head and neck cancer treated with RT in Denmark were eligible for inclusion.
Study population
Patients with clinically observed salivary gland hypofunction of UWS 0.05–0.25 ml/min, previous head and neck cancer treated with RT, 2 years without recurrence, between 18 and 75 years and informed consent were eligible for inclusion. Patients were excluded if they have had another cancer in the last 4 years, have other diseases in the salivary glands, receive xerogenic medicine, previous received ASCs, are pregnant or breastfeeding, are smoking or have an alcohol abuse. Details on the population and eligibility criteria are previously described [
16
,
19
].
Interventions
Patients were randomised to intraglandular ASC therapy or placebo in a 1:1 ratio. The intraglandular injections were done ultrasound-guided by free-hand without anaesthesia in both submandibular glands. The ACSs were provided by the Cardiology Stem Cell Centre (CSCC) according to good manufacturing practice (GMP). Placebo consisted of CryoStor10 (Biolife Solutions) containing 10% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). Patients received 0.5 mL of ASCs (25 × 10
6
ASCs per gland) or placebo into each submandibular gland. Details on the interventions are previously described [
16
,
19
].
Outcomes
The main endpoint of this sub study is comparison of the unstimulated salivary proteome evaluated by mass spectrometry analysis in the group receiving ASCs compared to placebo at 4 months.
Key secondary objectives are as follows:
Comparison of the unstimulated salivary microbial proteome evaluated by mass spectrometry analysis in the group receiving ASCs compared to placebo at 4 months.
Changes in the salivary proteome evaluated by mass spectrometry analysis in the group receiving ASCs from baseline to 4 months.
Changes in the salivary microbial proteome evaluated by mass spectrometry analysis in the group receiving ASCs from baseline to 4 months.
Assessment of the unstimulated salivary proteome at baseline in patients with previous head and neck cancer and radiation-induced hyposalivation.
Assessment of the unstimulated salivary microbial proteome at baseline in patients with previous head and neck cancer and radiation-induced hyposalivation.
Comparison with the stimulated salivary proteome identified in MESRIX-II [
18
].

Sample size
One hundred twenty patients were included. This relied on a 33% increase in the unstimulated whole saliva flow rate with a power of 0.8 and an alpha of 0.05 and an expected dropout rate of 20% [
15
,
17
]. These parameters were derived from the primary endpoint of the MESRIX-III study, which was the unstimulated salivary flow rate [
16
,
19
].
Recruitment
All patients with previous head and neck cancer treated with RT in Denmark were eligible. Patients were recruited through the Department of Otorhinolaryngology, Head and Neck Surgery and Audiology, Rigshospitalet or by referral from other clinical departments in Denmark. Patients could also be self-referred, e.g. through patient associations.

16

Methods: data collection, management and analysis
Saliva collection
As previously described [
16
,
17
], unstimulated whole saliva was collected by sialometry using the spitting method over 10 min at baseline and 4 months post intervention. Participants were instructed to drink a minimum of 2 L the day before and to refrain from eating including oral manipulation (e.g. tooth brushing) 1 h (h) before and from drinking 2 h before the testing. The sialometry was performed at the same time of the day at subsequent collections throughout the study. The saliva samples were frozen in dry ice immediately after the collection and stored in cryotubes at − 80 °C within 15 min and until analysis.
Sample preparation
The sample preparation is as previously described [
18
]: 500 µL unstimulated UWS will be lysed with 500 µL 95 °C warm lysis buffer (8 M guanidinium hydrochloride (GuHCl), 5 mM tris(2-carboxyethyl) phosphine (TCEP), 10 mM chloro-acetamide (CAA), 100 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.5) and by additional heating at 95 °C for 10 min. Subsequently, the lysates will undergo sonication with micro tip probe for 30 s with pulses of 1 s at an amplitude of 50%. Two hundred micrograms of protein from each sample will be diluted 5 × with 25 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.5 and digested with trypsin (MS grade; Thermo) overnight at 37 °C in a 1:50 w/w ratio. Ten percent trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) will be added to a final concentration of 1%, to stop the digestion. The peptide-containing supernatant will be desalted and concentrated with Sep-Pak C18 (Waters) with activation by 100% acetonitrile (ACN) and equilibration with 0.1% TFA. The sample will be washed two times with 0.1% TFA and eluted in 40% ACN followed by 60% ACN. The peptides will then dry in a SpeedVac (Eppendorf concentrator plus) for 1 h at 60 °C and resuspended in 50 µL 5% can in 0.1% TFA.
Mass spectrometry analysis
Each sample will be analysed by online nanoscale liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) in turn. Peptides will be separated on a 50-cm C18-column (Thermo EasySpray ES804A) using an Ultimate 3000 system (Thermo Scientific) at 40 °C. A flow rate of the gradient of 250 nl/min will be used, and in 80 min the gradient will go from 1% MS buffer B to 40% MS buffer B. Subsequently, samples will undergo a 10-min step going to 90% MS buffer B and stay for 5 min before going to 5% MS buffer B in 5 min and equilibrate for 10 min. The Q Exactive plus instrument (Thermo Scientific, Bremen, Germany) will run in data-dependent acquisition mode using a top 20 Higher-energy Collisional Dissociation (HCD)-MS/MS method (scan range 375–1500 m/z, full-scan resolution 70,000 m/z, AGC target of 3e6 and a maximum injection time (IT) value of 15 ms). Peptides will be fragmented with a normalised collision energy of 30 and a dynamic exclusion of 10 s, excluding unassigned ions and those with a charge state of 1, 6–8. MS/MS resolution will be set at 17,500 m/z, with an AGC target of 1e5 and a maximum IT of 45 ms. The mass spectrometry analysis is previously described [
18
].
Statistics
The raw LC–MS/MS data files will be processed with MaxQuant version 2.1.4.0 with default settings and label-free quantification. Oxidation (M), protein N-termini acetylation and met-loss will be used as variable modifications in the processing step and cysteine carbamidomethyl as static modification. A 1% false discovery rate will be used together with match between runs. Output from MaxQuant will be analysed with Perseus version 1.6.5.031. For quality assurance, proteins with less than two peptides will be excluded. The study personnel will be blinded to the intervention groups until the analyses are finalised. Two distinct searches will be executed. One search against the reviewed human database from Uniprot, and the other against the Human Oral Microbiome Database (
http://www.homd.org/
) primarily focusing on functional annotation for significant proteins. Protein expression will be evaluated with Venn diagram, principal component analysis (PCA), volcano plots, heat maps and cluster analysis. The relative intensity abundance at genus and species level will be calculated as the fraction of the total intensities for the individual samples compared to the total intensity. The bacterial proteins will undergo functional annotation analysis and mapped by SEQID’s from the Human Oral Microbiome Database to Uniprot ID by similarity using PROKKA [
21
]. The association between the salivary proteome and microbial proteome will be evaluated by Spearman’s rank correlation test and cluster analysis.
The statistical method is previously described [
18
].
Methods: monitoring
Data monitoring
The study adheres to the Good Clinical Practice (GCP) guidelines and was monitored by the GCP unit at the University of Copenhagen twice a year.
Harms
Adverse event including serious adverse events were monitored throughout the 4-month clinical trial period. Definitions on adverse events, serious adverse events and the reporting to the Danish Medical Agency and the National Health Committee are previously described [
16
,
19
].
Ethical considerations and risk assessment
From our previous studies, none of the patients experienced any serious adverse events and the risk of infection and bleeding is estimated to be lower than 0.5% [
15
,
17
]. The intraglandular injections are associated with brief pain [
15
,
17
].
Auditing
The trial was monitored by the independent GCP unit. The trial investigators were responsible for auditing the trial and will meet weekly to discuss the execution.

Research ethics approval
The MESRIX-III study is approved by the National Ethics Committee (1,802,872), the Danish Medical Agency (2018–000348-24) and the Danish Data Protection Agency (P-2020–1164). The study protocol is registered at the ClinicalTrials.gov database (

Any protocol amendments will be submitted to the National Ethical Committee and the Danish Medical Agency for approval. Amendments will be communicated to trial investigators afterwards.

Written consent was obtained before inclusion in the study by a designated trial investigator (a doctor).

Collected data will be kept confidential and documented in REDCap. Only designated trial investigators will have access to the database. Participants will have an individual identification number. UWS samples will be analysed at the Roskilde University (RUC), which is outside of the trial facility. Samples will be labelled with the trial identification number only and no clinical data will be shared.

Discussion
This is the largest study investigating the unstimulated salivary proteome and microbial proteome following intraglandular ASC therapy in patients with previous head and neck cancer and radiation-induced hyposalivation. The study will gain valuable insights into the potential mode of action of this innovative and disease-modifying therapy. Additionally, it will establish a comprehensive dataset detailing the chronic alterations in the salivary proteome following radiation therapy in the head and neck area.
Trial status
The MESRIX-III study was initiated in February 2021 and recruitment of participants was finished in February 2023. Last patient/last visit was medio February 2024. The trial followed protocol version 2.10, 25th of January 2023. The MESRIX-III study protocol was published earlier [
19
] as well as the primary endpoints [
16
]; however, this sub study has not been published prior, due to method amendments.
