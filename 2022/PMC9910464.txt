Structural valve disease is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in patients with cardiovascular diseases worldwide (
1
). With a growing elderly population, the burden of valvular heart disease is expected to grow (
2
). No pharmaceutical therapies exist in the treatment of valvular diseases, and therefore, surgical valve replacement and catheter-based interventions with mechanical or bioprosthetic devices are first-line therapies. Bioprosthetic heart valves (BHVs), implanted either via surgical or percutaneous intervention, are more commonly used than mechanical valves in replacement surgeries, owing to their favorable hemodynamics and reduced need for lifelong anticoagulation (
3
). However, BHVs engineered from porcine and bovine biomaterials are a form of xenograft capable of provoking an aggressive form of host immune system response. Processing of BHVs with glutaraldehyde fixation deprives BHVs of native living cells, reduces immunogenicity by masking antigens, and improves biomaterial performance with cross-links (
4
,
5
). While benefits outweigh risks for properly selected patients, BHVs suffer from unavoidable structural valve degeneration (SVD) in the long term, defined as pathological modifications with resultant compromised hemodynamic function (
6
). The expected valve timespan is 12 to 18 years until the need for replacement surgery (
7
,
8
), and BHV reoperation is required even sooner with diabetes mellitus (DM) (
9
). Multiple meta-analyses reveal risk factors for accelerated SVD, including young age of graft recipient, arterial hypertension, hyperparathyroidism, DM, and dyslipidemia (
10
–
12
). Despite these findings, mechanisms of SVD remain under continued study. Several works have implicated the degeneration of the extracellular matrix (ECM) and fibrocalcific remodeling by dystrophic calcification as major contributors to the pathogenesis of SVD (
9
,
13
–
20
).
Here, we focused on patients with metabolic syndrome (MetS) based on the latest definition published by the National Cholesterol Education Program (
21
). This definition refers to a prevalent phenotype in valvular surgery patients with a well-recognized cluster of overlapping comorbidities with multiorgan involvement, including Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM), elevated triglycerides, diminished high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL), obesity, and systemic hypertension. In addition, MetS is associated with increased cardiovascular disease mortality and all-cause mortality.
Our recent work demonstrated that infiltration of advanced glycated end-products (AGEs), posttranslational nonenzymatic protein-glycosylation, and human serum albumin (HSA) could negatively impact the organization of the collagen fiber microstructure. This occurred via the formation of AGE-cross-links (
20
) and the incorporation of glycated serum proteins. In addition, AGE and serum protein infiltration impaired the hydrodynamic functions of BHVs, with reduced effective orifice area, increased peak velocity and mean gradient, thus significantly contributing to SVD, even in the absence of calcification (
20
). In patients with T2DM and MetS, higher circulating levels of AGE have been associated with hyperglycemic states (
22
). AGE modification of serum proteins, however, only represents one category of overall protein absorption into BHVs, and T2DM or MetS might instigate accelerated failure of BHV. Without a functional endothelial barrier, BHVs are susceptible to unregulated infiltration from circulating host factors. Protein adsorption is a key mechanism of both calcific and non-calcific structural degeneration as it drives the accumulation of calcium-binding proteins, the formation of glycation and oxidation products, and the inflammatory response in BHV biomaterials (
23
). However, systematic analysis of protein adsorption in BHV leaflets is relatively unexplored.
Given the role of circulating protein infiltration in initiating and directing BHV leaflet degeneration, we formulated and characterized a BHV biomaterial modified with poly-2-methyl-2-oxazoline (POZ) (
19
) aimed at reducing protein absorption. POZ is a novel compound in a class of antifouling polymers that blocks protein infiltration and resists oxidative stress. We have shown that POZ-modification of BHV leaflets leads to reduced serum albumin and AGE accumulation. Further, POZ modification confers thromboresistance and anti-inflammatory properties of BHV tissues (
19
). The protective effect of POZ modification of BHVs in patients with a high risk of accelerated SVD is yet to be studied.
In the present study, it was hypothesized that MetS represents a significant and severe pathophysiologic state that contributes to serum protein infiltration-related SVD mechanisms and that POZ-modified BHV leaflets would demonstrate mitigation of MetS-related serum protein effects. We first conducted a clinical retrospective multicenter analysis of BHV implant duration in patients with and without MetS. We then performed a global proteomic assessment of leaflets from patients undergoing valve reoperation for SVD. To evaluate the mitigation strategy for protein absorption, we tested POZ modification of BHV biomaterials in an ex vivo patient serum incubation model with serum from patients with or without MetS. A MetS rat model was also studied, and clinical-grade glutaraldehyde-fixed bovine pericardium (BP) was implanted subcutaneously to assess SVD pathophysiology. In both models, calcification, collagen alignment, and collagen cross-linking density were primary endpoints.
Overall, our proteomics study of clinical BHV explants yields comprehensive insights into the proteome that contributes to the hallmarks of SVD in a cohort of patients with and without MetS, a prevalent cluster of comorbidities in patients undergoing cardiac interventions. Subsequent in vivo and ex vivo experiments using sera from patients and rodent models demonstrate a potential protective effect of POZ-modified BHV biomaterials in subjects at risk of accelerated SVD due to MetS.

Bovine Pericardia Incubated with Serum from Patients with or without MetS.
Sera collected from a prospectively enrolled cohort of 128 patients were pooled based on their MetS status (control vs. MetS serum). First, a selected list of serum markers was quantified based on their known involvement in MetS and/or cardiovascular disorders (
Table 3
). The two groups had similar serum calcium concentrations and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity. Patients with MetS were observed to have higher serum insulin (2,862 ± 4,138 vs. 419 ± 116 pg/mL,
P
= 0.014) and leptin (19,984 ± 19,452 vs. 7,802 ± 4,472 pg/mL,
P
= 0.012) which are consistent with insulin resistance. Further, progranulin, a protein associated with MetS and other known cardiovascular risk factors, (
25
) including valve calcification (
26

P
= 0.02). Fatty acid-binding protein 4 (FABP4) (16,902 ± 8,950 vs. 35,669 ± 19,365,
P
= 0.0005) and growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF-15) (1,480 ± 718 vs. 753.9 ± 340 pg/mL,
P
= 0.0003) have been associated with obesity, insulin resistance, and cardiovascular disease (
27

28
), unmodified glut-BP or POZ-modified glut-BP were incubated with either MetS serum or control serum for 8 wk (
Fig. 3
A
). After 8 wk of ex vivo incubation, von Kossa staining revealed that BP incubated with MetS serum had elevated calcification when compared to those treated with control serum (
Fig. 3
B
). This observation was further confirmed by calcium quantification on the incubated disks (20.99 ± 2.30 µg/mg vs. 8.62 ± 3.56 µg/mg,
P
< 0.01, one-way ANOVA) (
Fig. 3
C
). Further, POZ modification of glut-BP had a tendency to reduce calcification although the difference was not significant (20.99 ± 2.30 µg/mg vs. 15.88 ± 3.32 µg/mg,
P
= 0.09). The effects of serum incubation on BP tissue cross-linking were measured by thermal denaturation temperature and advanced glycation end-product accumulation. As expected, all serum-treated disks had increased thermal denaturation temperatures when compared to untreated BP tissues (85.78° ± 0.38°,
P
< 0.01, one-way ANOVA) due to accumulation of cross-links. Importantly, POZ modification led to reduced thermal denaturation temperature of BP irrespective of serum type (88.27° ± 0.36° vs. 89.92° ± 0.39°,
P
< 0.01, one-way ANOVA) (
Fig. 3

). No significant difference was found between MetS serum-incubated BP and control serum-treated BP (88.92° ± 0.39° vs. 89.93° ± 0.26°,
P
> 0.05). In addition, POZ modification reduced the accumulation of generalized AGE (

No difference was observed between MetS serum-incubated BP and control serum-treated BP in terms of AGE and CML staining consistent with the analysis of BHV explants. In terms of the collagen microstructure, POZ modification retained the collagen structure in BP by increasing the collagen alignment coefficient compared to unmodified BP, irrespective of when treated with control or MetS serum (
Fig. 3

and
F
).

Discussion
According to recent estimates from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, the age-adjusted prevalence of MetS in US adults is 34.3% (
29
). Among Americans over 60 y old, who represent the vast majority of BHV recipients, the prevalence is increased to 54.9%. To date, SVD in patients with MetS has been understudied. In this study, we sought to understand the structural dege
neration
of BHV biomaterials in a cohort of patients and present a tenable mitigation strategy. Herein, first, in our repository on explanted valves, we showed that patients with MetS are at significantly higher risk of accelerated BHV degeneration and require reoperation sooner with valve replacement. Following, we performed global quantification of the differential proteome from leaflets of BHV explants affected by SVD from MetS and non-MetS patients to assess the different protein absorption in patients with or without MetS. Given the significance of protein infiltration in MetS-induced SVD, we then examined the protective effects of our POZ modification of glut-BP against MetS-induced BHV biomaterial degeneration in both ex vivo serum and in vivo subcutaneous implant models.
With strict adherence to the latest definition of MetS (as described in
Material and Methods
), we identified 76 MetS patients in our multicenter cohort of 251 patients requiring valve reoperation for SVD. Our cohort shows a relatively more severe phenotype from the five accepted criteria of MetS (clinically diagnosed T2DM, elevated triglycerides, diminished high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, obesity, and systemic hypertension). Our findings are consistent with prior literature reporting on the risk of accelerated SVD in patients with MetS and its related diseases. Lorusso and colleagues reported on a large multicenter cohort study with propensity score matching in 2,226 patients that showed patients with T2DM were at increased risk of earlier BHV reoperation (
11
). Farivar and colleagues also reported that hypercholesterolemia is a significant risk factor for bioprosthetic valve calcification (
30
). In another relevant single-center study, Briand demonstrated that MetS is associated with faster BHV degeneration (
12
). These epidemiological observations highlight the crucial need to understand the mechanisms of accelerated SVD in this cohort of patients and to develop effective mitigation strategies. In the present study, patients with diagnosis of dyslipidemia were prescribed statin therapy. Adherence to statins was not assessed in the study and, to our knowledge, statin use has not been characterized as a conclusive prognostic indication of native valve degeneration or BHV durability although it may be an interesting opportunity for further study.
BHV biomaterials are inherently collagen-rich tissues (
1
). Collagen is the major load-bearing ECM protein supporting the valve leaflet’s motion, and its properties are highly associated with its structure (
2
). The exposure of BHV biomaterials to circulating proteins and minerals leads to infiltration, permanent incorporation, and biochemical modifications, such as tissue cross-linking. The interaction of implantable biomaterials with blood is a complex process that triggers a cascade of events (
3
). Among others, circulating protein absorption is an important event that might be impacted by hydrophobic interactions, charged groups, and changes in protein conformations (
4
). Protein adsorption initiates foreign body reactions in BHVs, mineral binding such as calcium, and catalyzes further reactions, such as glycation (
31
–
33
). We observed a variety of degeneration modes of these explanted BHVs involving calcific and/or non-calcific mechanisms. For explants from patients with MetS, both calcific and non-calcific mechanisms existed. We did not observe significant differences in terms of the percentage of calcified degeneration when comparing explants between MetS and non-MetS cohorts (
P
= 0.09).
Recent advances in tandem mass spectrometry (MS–MS) have made it possible to quantify thousands of proteins in complex biological samples (
34
,
35
). Next-generation proteomics with diaPASEF achieves improved efficiency without loss of sensitivity (
36
). To date, this technology has not been applied to the study of protein absorption as a culprit in the mechanisms of SVD in cardiac biomaterials. First, our results reveal distinct BHV leaflet proteomes from patients with and without MetS. Our data show proteins associated with diseases characterizing MetS are overrepresented in explanted BHV leaflets, namely CRP, serum amyloid A (SAA), matrix metalloprotease 9 (MMP9), and leptin. Notably, the relative abundance of SAA (log2FC = 4.384, q-value = 4.04E−05) isolated from BHV leaflets in patients with MetS suggests the propagation of an acute phase response and the relevance of inflammation (
37
). Indeed, our GSEA is the first of its kind to report significant differentially expressed metabolic pathways between BHV explanted from patients with and without MetS. Our data show an overrepresentation of ECM remodeling proteins, which has been shown to be associated with BHV leaflet degeneration (
38
). MMPs retaining ECM‐degrading enzymatic activity have been shown both to persist in BHV tissues through glutaraldehyde treatment and to infiltrate into clinically or experimentally implanted BHV tissue (
13
). Moreover, Vyavahare and colleagues found that cyclic fatigue caused a progressive loss of collagen helicity and glycosaminoglycan content in the leaflets of glutaraldehyde‐fixed porcine aortic valves (
39
). This work is also consistent with prior work characterizing the proteome that exists on bioprosthetic valve leaflets in continuous contact with serum laden with circulating proteins. Indeed, Senage and colleagues recently reported on antibodies against xenoantigens, galactose-α1,3-galactose (αGal), and
N
-glycolylneuraminic acid (Neu5Gc), in BHVs (
40
). These findings not only support the conclusions offered in the present study but also promote the future study of improving clinical outcomes in patients requiring interventions with biomaterials.
Calcification has historically been considered a major pathophysiologic mechanism responsible for SVD, irrespective of patient risk factors (
5
,
6
). Macroscopically, dystrophic calcification was also observed in 15 out of 24 BHV in our study; yet, the incidence of calcification was not significantly different between MetS and non-MetS groups (
P
= 0.089). In view of the blood-contacting nature of BHVs, elevated calcification marker levels could lead to more aggressive calcification either via direct calcium deposition or via the incorporation of calcium-binding proteins. Interestingly, patient serum analysis revealed no significant difference in Ca
2+
or ALP concentration between the two groups. In our proteomics analysis, we identified several notable calcium-binding proteins that may contribute to this phenomenon, such as SPARC, a protein previously studied in atherosclerosis and valve calcification (
41
–
43
). In addition, mineralization-regulating proteins such as COMP were overrepresented in BHVs from patients with MetS (log2FC = 1.509, q = 2.02E−03). Recent work by Du and colleagues highlights the interaction of COMP and bone morphogenetic protein-2 as important regulators of calcification in vascular smooth muscle cells. It is also worth noting that in both ex vivo and in vivo models, MetS led to higher amounts of calcification, and these calcification-related proteins were also detected.
These clinical and proteomic findings prompted us to investigate SVD mitigation strategies via reducing protein adsorption into BHV leaflets. We previously reported POZ modification of glut-BP and evaluated this technology in both in vitro and in vivo experiments. Our prior work has shown that POZ modification reduces serum albumin and glycation accumulation in BP (
19
). Specifically, POZ-modified glut-BP resisted oxidative stress and retained its antifouling properties. Importantly, clinical-grade SAVR modified with POZ had better hydrodynamic function than unmodified control materials when challenged by glycation and serum albumin. Herein, we examined whether POZ modification might inhibit biomaterial degeneration induced by MetS. Although POZ was previously shown to not affect calcification in the juvenile rat model, here we discovered that in both ex vivo and in vivo models, POZ modification reduced the elevated BP calcification due to MetS. Given that the baseline calcium content and ALP activity in the control and MetS pooled sera were not significantly different, we speculate that POZ reduced the incorporation of mineralization-associated proteins and osteogenic proteins. An overall reduction of protein infiltration was also observed in our IHC experiments conducted with multiple markers. Furthermore, in both models, we observed increased tissue cross-linking density due to the MetS phenotype and reduced tissue cross-linking conferred by POZ modification. Therefore, POZ modification, by reducing protein adsorption, can mitigate both calcified and non-calcified SVD in the MetS phenotype. In summary, POZ modification of BHV biomaterials offers an opportunity for engineering novel BHV technologies specifically for those at high risk of accelerated SVD, \ such as patients with MetS.
Taken together, our results demonstrate that accelerated SVD leading to BHV failure requiring reoperation in patients with MetS is a combination of host and implant factors. The interaction between host-circulating protein biochemistry, glycation conditions secondary to MetS, and the inherent properties of heterograft biomaterials contribute to BHV deterioration. Key mechanisms of failure, such as calcification and structural degeneration, are shared among all tissue-engineered biomaterials routinely used in the reconstruction and replacement of native cardiovascular tissues. Our proteomics methodology and biomechanical testing offer an innovative approach to future precision-guided interventions tailored to mitigating the absorption of deleterious biomarkers. The protective effect of POZ modification on glut-BP in mitigating protein absorption and the resultant reduction in cross-link formation and calcification in this at-risk cohort of patients with MetS may spur investigations of novel implantable biomaterials with improved durability. Clinically, the goal of this work is to reduce the need for high-risk cardiac reoperations by mitigating mechanisms responsible for accelerated device failure seen in BHVs.
Study Limitations.
There are some inherent limitations of this study. First, in our clinical data analysis, we followed the latest guidelines and definitions of MetS by categorizing patients who met at least three out of five criteria for MetS based on the best available data extracted from clinical records, but missing data are possible. There is an intrinsic selection bias in the study design because it is unknown how many patients with BHVs ultimately did not require reoperation due to SVD, may have died prior to needing reoperation due to other causes, or were deemed too high risk to undergo reoperation. Future large-scale prospective registries may address this limitation. Further, since data were extracted at the time of reoperation, it cannot be assured that all comorbidities present at the time of reoperation were also present at the time of implant. The ex vivo incubation model used in the current study lacks the flow and biomechanics aspects which are important for valve degeneration processes. Also, our study focused primarily on characterizing BHV biomaterials, but the MetS-related effects of infiltrating immune cells on SVD also warrant future investigation.
Materials and Methods
Chemicals and Supplies.
Fresh bovine pericardia were purchased from Animal Technologies (Tyler) and were shipped in a refrigerated, fresh state within 24 h of harvest, as a manufacturing-grade heterograft material. Monofunctionalized POZ (10 kDa) was purchased from Ultroxa. All chemicals were purchased from Millipore Sigma unless stated otherwise.
Patient Data and MetS Definition.
Four medical centers participated in enrolling patients for this study. Patient data refer to bioprosthetic heart valve replacement surgeries performed from January 1991 to June 2021 (N = 251). Data abstracted from patient charts included preoperative counseling, laboratory results, imaging, including echocardiography with attention to valve function and computed tomography scans, and intraoperative assessment of the valve of interest. A retrospective chart review was performed on all patients, and data were sent to the core laboratory (Cardiothoracic Surgery Research Program, Columbia University) for statistical analysis. Patients were followed up according to each institutional protocol. This study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Boards at Columbia University Irving Medical Center, The Valley Hospital, the Hospital of the University of Pennsylvania, and Ottawa Heart Institute. All patients provided informed consent. Explanted valve tissue and blood were collected intraoperatively. (
Table 1
)
Patients with MetS (
21
,
24
) had a medical history of at least three of the following five comorbidities at the time of valve reoperation: 1) T2DM, diagnosed based on the recommendations of the American Diabetes Association (
44
); 2) elevated triglycerides (>150 mg/dL); 3) diminished high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (men <40 mg/dL; women <50 mg/dL); 4) central obesity (
45
); and 5) systemic hypertension (≥130/≥85 mmHg) (
46
).
Clinical BHV Explants and Serum Collection.
Clinically explanted surgical BHV replacements were retrieved from patients at the time of reoperation. SVD was defined as dysfunction or deterioration that involved the implanted BHV (excluding infection or thrombosis), such as wear, fracture, calcification, leaflet tear, stent creep, and suture-line disruption of components of a prosthetic valve. Explanted BHVs were rinsed in PBS before fixation in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for 24-to-72 h, followed by dehydration in graded ethanol. A total of 24 BP valves with individual intact leaflets were assessed for their proteomic profiles, and a one-centimeter by one-centimeter square sample of tissue at the midpoint from the leaflet-free edge to the base was excised (N = 12 Met and N = 12 non-MetS,
Table 2
). Human serum was collected at Valley Hospital before cardiac procedures and pooled based on the MetS status (N = 140,
SI Appendix
, Table S1
).
Bovine Pericardia Collection and Preparation.
Pericardia were processed as previously described (
20
,
28
). Briefly, pericardial sacs were immersed in glutaraldehyde (0.6% v:v; Electron Microscopy Sciences) in HEPES solution (50 mM; pH 7.4; Fisher Scientific) for 7 d at room temperature. Pericardia were then rinsed in cold saline and stored in glutaraldehyde (0.2% v:v) in HEPES solution (50 mM; pH 7.4) at 4°C.
POZ Modification of Bovine Pericardia.
POZ modification has been previously reported by us (
19
). Briefly, BP samples (1 × 1 cm) were removed from 0.2% glutaraldehyde storage solution, rinsed three times with PBS solution, and placed in 1 mL of 50 mM MES buffer (pH 5.5) solution with 10 mg of POZ overnight at 37°C. To initiate the reaction, 43 mM NHS and 65 mM of EDC were added in 1 mL MES to a total volume of 2 m. The reaction proceeded for 24 h at room temperature under mild shaking.
Global Quantitative Proteomic Analysis.
Clinically explanted BHV were washed 5 times in 100 mM Tris pH 8.5, minced into small pieces, and transferred to 1.5-mL Eppendorf tubes. Briefly, tissues were resuspended in 200 μL of solubilization buffer (5% SDS/300 mM Tris pH 8.5/protease inhibitors). Samples were sonicated in a water bath and boiled at (90°C / 90 min) on a heating block. Next, samples were briefly centrifuged, and sonication was repeated in a water bath. The samples were returned to the heating block for 10 min. The lysate was centrifuged at 16,000 × g in a benchtop centrifuge for 10 min and collected in a new Eppendorf tube. Cleared samples were precipitated with the salt method, as previously described (
47
), and the pellets were solubilized in 1% SDC buffer (
48
) (1% SDS/40 mM CAA/10 mM TCEP/100 mM Tris-pH8.0). Protein denaturation–reduction and alkylation were performed at (45°C, 10min). Protein digestion was performed overnight at 37°C and 1,400 rpm by addition of 1 μg of trypsin and LysC. On the following day, samples were shortly centrifuged and digestion was stopped by adding trifluoroacetic acid (1% TFA) and subjected to StageTip clean-up via SDB-RPS (
49
). Peptides were loaded on one 14-gauge StageTip plug. Peptides were washed two times with 200 µL of 1% TFA 99% ethyl acetate followed by 200 µL of 0.2% TFA/5%ACN in a centrifuge at 3,000 rpm, followed by elution with 60 µL of 1% ammonia/50% ACN into Eppendorf tubes and dried at 45°C in a SpeedVac centrifuge. Samples were resuspended in 10 μL of LC buffer (3% ACN/0.1% FA). Peptide concentrations were determined using NanoDrop, and 200 ng of each sample were used for diaPASEF analysis on timsTOFPro. Peptides were separated within 120 min at a flow rate of 400 nl/min on a reversed-phase C18 column with an integrated CaptiveSpray Emitter (25 cm × 75 µm, 1.6 µm, IonOpticks). Mobile phases A and B were with 0.1% formic acid in water and 0.1% formic acid in ACN. The fraction of B was linearly increased from 2 to 23% within 90 min, followed by an increase to 35% within 10 min and a further increase to 80% before reequilibration. The timsTOF Pro was operated in diaPASEF mode (
49
), and data were acquired at defined 32 × 25 Th isolation windows from m/z 400 to 1,200. To adapt the MS1 cycle time in diaPASEF, set the repetitions to 2 in the 16-scan diaPASEF scheme. The collision energy was ramped linearly as a function of the mobility from 59 eV at 1/K0 = 1.6 Vs cm
−2
to 20 eV at 1/K0 = 0.6 Vs cm
−2
. The acquired diaPASEF raw files were searched with the UniProt Human proteome database in the Spectronaut Pulsar X (
36
), a mass spectrometer vendor-independent software from Biognosys. The default settings were used for targeted analysis of DIA data in Spectronaut except the decoy generation was set to mutate. The false discovery rate (FDR) was estimated with the mProphet approach and set to 1% at the peptide precursor level and 1% at the protein level.
Rat Subcutaneous Implantation Model.
The subdermal implantation model is a well-established animal model for assessing the fate of implanted devices. This model has been used by all bioprosthesis manufacturers to screen anti-calcification agents, and results have not disagreed with subsequent sheep valve replacements (
50
,
51
). Glutaraldehyde-fixed BP and POZ-modified BP discs (8 mm in diameter) were rinsed (3×) with and stored in saline (0.9% w:v, Rocky Mountain Biologicals) at 4°C until implantation. Thirteen-week-old (N = 24) male and female Zucker Diabetic Fatty (ZDF, Obese fa/fa) and control (ZDF Lean fa/+) rats (Charles River Laboratories) were utilized for all implantation procedures, with strict adherence to an Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee-approved protocol [AABP6552 (CUIMC)] and conformed to the NIH guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals. All rats arrived at 6 wk of age and were maintained on an ad-lib laboratory diet as recommended by the vendor. ZDF female rats were conditioned for 8 wk and maintained on a semipurified diet RD 12468 (Research Diets), and ZDF male rats were conditioned for 7 wk and maintained on Purina LabDiet #5008 (LabDiet) rat chow to induce diabetic phenotypes. Before the implantation procedure, blood from the tail vein was collected and glucose was measured with a glucose monitor (Roche Diabetes Care). Inhaled isoflurane (Henry Schein Inc.) was used for anesthesia induction (3%) and maintenance (1.5%). Once anesthetized, all animals were weighed and given identifying ear tags. Subcutaneous meloxicam (2 mg/kg; Henry Schein Inc.) was administered preoperatively for analgesia. The dorsal surface of each animal was clipped and prepped with 70% ethanol and chlorhexidine gluconate (Dyna-Hex 4; Xttrium Laboratories Inc., Mt Prospect, IL, USA). Four transverse incisions were made at the dorsal surface, and discrete subcutaneous pockets were formed via blunt dissection. Each animal received one glutaraldehyde fixed BP disc implant per subcutaneous pocket, for a total of four implants per animal (2 BP disks were POZ modified, and 2 disks were unmodified BP disks). After ensuring hemostasis, each incision was reapproximated using surgical clips, and the animals were allowed to recover from anesthesia. An additional dose of meloxicam was administered after 24 h. Surgical clips were removed after 10 d. The rats were euthanized by inhaled isoflurane and cardiac puncture at either 7 (N = 12) or 30 (N = 12) days, at which point the implants were retrieved and subsequently rinsed and stored in 0.9% saline at 4 °C.
Serum Analysis.
Patients were consented prior to cardiac surgery, and blood was collected by The Valley Hospital biobank. Blood was centrifuged, and the serum was stored at −80 °C for future analysis. The cytokine levels of BMP-2 (BR67), BMP-4 (BR72), cathepsin S (BR35), chitinase 3-like 1 (BR20), EpCAM/TROP1 (BR53), FABP4/A-FABP (BR25), GDF-15 (BR65), ICAM-1/CD54 (BR61), IL-1 alpha/IL-1F1 (BR38), IL-1 beta/IL-1F2 (BR28), IL-6 (BR13), IL-10 (BR22), insulin (BR14), leptin/OB (BR51), NCAM-1/CD56 (BR15), osteopontin/OPN (BR54), progranulin/PGRN (BR57), RAGE/AGER (BR45), P-selectin/CD62P (BR48), and TNF-alpha (BR12) in human serum samples were measured using Human Luminex Discovery Assay (R&D Systems, MN) and the Luminex 200 platform ((Luminex Corp, Austin, TX). The samples were processed according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and the cytokine concentrations were quantitated by the Luminex xPONENT v3.1 and MILLIPLEX Analyst v5 (MilliporeSigma, MA). Additionally, serum calcification concentration (Sigma-Aldrich) and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activities (Abcam) were quantified by commercially available kits.
Ex Vivo Serum Incubation.
Patient sera were pooled, and antibiotics (1% Penicillin–Streptomycin) were added to prevent potential bacterial infection. Glutaraldehyde-fixed BP and POZ-modified BP were extensively rinsed in PBS and subsequently incubated in either pooled human sera from MetS or non-MetS patients for 8 wk at 37°C under 100 rpm orbital shaking (N = 4 pieces of BP tissue per group). One mL solution was used per BP disk, and fresh serum was replaced weekly. After incubation, BP disks were rinsed in 3× fresh 0.9% saline and subject to analyses on calcification, microstructure, and cross-linking density.
SHG Imaging and Image Analysis.
SHG imaging was utilized to evaluate the collagen structure in BHV tissues. Individual BP samples were mounted on an 8-mm Fastwell incubation chamber (Electron Microscopy Sciences) and covered with a 0.17-mm coverslip. SHG microscopy was performed on a Nikon A1RMP multiphoton confocal microscope (Nikon; Minato). The SHG signal was generated using 860-nm excitation light from a Ti:sapphire femtosecond laser and detected using Nikon Apo LWD 25×/NA1.1 gel immersion as an objective with a 400 to 450 nm bandpass filter. All images were acquired at 1,024 × 1,024 resolution using NIS Elements software. For each piece of tissue, SHG imaging was taken on two or three nonoverlapping areas. Collagen alignment analysis was performed using CurveAlign software (
https://loci.wisc.edu/software/curvealign
), and the alignment coefficient was recorded (N = 12 images per group).
SEM.
BP samples were lyophilized and the surface of the smooth side (serous) of BP was sputter-coated with gold–palladium using a Cressington 108 auto sputtercoater (Cressington Scientific Instruments). Images were obtained from the surface of the tissue using an accelerated voltage of 2 kV using a Zeiss Sigma VP scanning electron microscope (Carl Zeiss Microscopy).
Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC).
A Perkin-Elmer DSC 6000 Autosampler (# N5370217) with Pyris software (version 11.0) was used to measure the thermal denaturation temperature (T
d
) of BP samples (
7

The samples (3 to 9 mg) were blotted dry and placed flat in preweighed 50-µL aluminum pan (Perkin-Elmer # B0169321) pans and were immediately hermetically sealed using a DSC pan sealing press (Perkin-Elmer # B0139005). The samples were equilibrated at 45°C and heated at 10°C/min until 110°C
8
. The denaturation temperature was recorded as the maximum value of the endotherm peak.
IHC.
BP samples are from the ex vivo serum incubation study; rat subcutaneous explants were fixed in formalin (10%, v:v) for 24 to 72 h and embedded in paraffin. Five-µm-thick sections were cut via microtome and mounted on high-adherence glass microscope slides. Antigen retrieval was accomplished by 30 min incubation in 1× sodium citrate buffer (Sigma-Aldrich) at boiling temperature. Anti-AGE (Abcam; #ab23722, 1:5,000 dilution), anti-CML (Abcam #ab27684, 1:1,000 dilution), anti-HSA (Abcam #ab10241, 1:2,000 dilution), and anti-osteopontin (Abcam #ab8448, 1:2,000 dilution) were diluted in DAKO primary antibody diluent (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and applied to sections overnight (16 to 18 h) at 4°C under gentle orbital shaking. DAKO HRP polymer-conjugated anti-rabbit secondary antibodies (Agilent, #ab214880) were applied at room temperature for 1 h. Washes were accomplished using DAKO Wash Buffer (Agilent). IHC stains were developed for 8 min using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride substrate (Abcam), and sections were counterstained for 3 min with Gill’s hematoxylin (Sigma-Aldrich). Slides were mounted using Permount mounting medium (Fisher Scientific) and visualized by 5× light microscopy.
Calcification Analyses.
Von Kossa staining (Polysciences #24633) was used for qualitative calcification assessment of microscopic sections, as per the manufacturer’s protocol. Quantitative calcium analyses of BP discs were conducted using a colorimetric calcium assay (Sigma-Aldrich MAK022), as per the manufacturer’s protocol. BP discs were rinsed in 0.9% saline three times followed by lyophilization. Dry weights were recorded prior to sample extraction in HCl solution (6 M overnight at 90°C). The solution was subsequently evaporated (overnight at 100°C) followed by reconstitution in 1 mL HCl (0.6 M) in preparation for the colorimetric assay.
Statistical Analysis of Clinical BHV Explants.
The sample size of n = 12 per group (n = 24 total) was selected based on similar prior studies on heart valves (
52
,
53
). This is further confirmed by power analysis showing that with regards to discovery-based proteomic analysis, N = 12/group reaches sufficient power when the confidence is set at 0.05 and fold change is over 1.2 (
54

t
-tests or Wilcoxon rank sum tests. Categorical variables are represented as counts/percentages and groups compared by χ2 tests of independence or Fisher’s exact tests. Statistical significance was established at
P
less than .05. Time-to-event analyses were performed with the use of Kaplan–Meier estimates and groups compared with the log-rank test. The effect of baseline variables was assessed with the univariate Cox proportional hazard regression model with baseline time at first valve implantation surgery. Following, significant variables were selected for a multivariable Cox regression model and proportional hazards assumptions were tested. Statistical analyses were performed with R v. 4.1.2 (Vienna, Austria) using R Studio v. 1.4.1106 (Boston, MA).
