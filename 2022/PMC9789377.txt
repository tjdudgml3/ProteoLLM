Introduction
Sepsis remains a major cause of death in intensive-care units worldwide [
1
]. Since December 2019, the world has encountered coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), which is characterized by endothelial damage and constitutive inflammatory cytokine storm and is currently a major pandemic and challenge for intensive-care units (ICUs) [
2
,
3
]. Sepsis is a multisystem disease with a complex pathogenesis involving systemic inflammatory responses, endothelial dysfunction, and multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS) in response to infections [
4
]. Following bacterial or viral infection, exogenous damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) trigger NF-κB signaling to generate a powerful inflammatory response, causing a collapse of endothelial function [
5
]. In addition to engagement of Toll-like receptors (TLRs), endothelial cells (ECs) will actively emit various molecules such as Notch receptors and ligands, TNF-α and TNFR1/2, IL-6R, the transmembrane chemokines CX3C-chemokine ligand 1 (CX3CL1) and CXC-chemokine ligand 16 (CXCL16), vascular endothelial cell cadherin (VE-cadherin/CDH5) and various cellular adhesion molecules (VCAM), as well as increase the extravasation of protein-rich fluid into the tissue space by glycocalyx deterioration and through induction of cell death and cell–cell junctional integrity loss [
5
,
6
]. In parallel, proinflammatory immune cells recruited into the vasculature are then excessively transported through a process involving cellular rolling, arrest, crawling, and transendothelial migration, and release large amounts of proinflammatory cytokines [
7
]. In addition, pathogen-induced platelet GPVI shedding will lead to detrimental aggregation of platelets, which caused thrombosis and coagulopathy during sepsis [
8
]. Most of these vascular shedding molecules are activated depending on a rapid irreversible posttranslational event—enzymatic cleavage of the extracellular domain [
9
]. A disintegrin and metalloproteinase (ADAM) is a family of enzymes often responsible for these vascular shedding events and are found on various cells, including neurocytes, leukocytes, platelets, and ECs [
10
]. ADAM10, one of the most important members of the ADAM family, shares a common zinc-dependent proteolytically active structure with those of 12 other ADAM family members [
11
]. ADAM10 has been identified as the main amyloid precursor protein (APP) α-secretase candidate that exhibits a protective effect against Alzheimer’s disease [
12
]. However, the excessive activation of ADAM10 may lead to a systemic inflammatory response and vascular endothelial injury following sepsis [
13
] (Fig.
1
). Our previous studies have demonstrated that functional genetic polymorphisms or intractable production of ADAM10 exacerbated sepsis-induced inflammatory responses and endothelial injury and conferred susceptibility to sepsis progression and poor prognosis [
14
,
15
]. In the PBMCs of sepsis patients, compared with the healthy controls, the expression of ADAM10 is significantly upregulated, with much higher serum concentrations of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β [
16
]. To date, all clinical trials targeting cytokine production or effects have failed [
17
]. Targeting ADAM10 is becoming a promising therapeutic strategy for sepsis.

Remarkably, ADAM10 not only mediates sepsis-related vascular shedding events but also acts as the cellular receptor for
Staphylococcus aureus
(
S. aureus
) α-toxin [
18
]. Since ADAM10 has been observed to be widely expressed in all cell types of the vasculature and rates of antibiotic resistance have increased, sepsis mediated by single bacterial toxins is often fast and fatal [
19
,
20
]. Although several antibiotics can be used to manage
S. aureus
effectively, most bacterial proteins or toxins from dying or lysed bacteria are still released into the bloodstream, leading to continuous endothelial damage and microcirculation disorders [
8
], implying that ADAM10 may act as a toxin neutralization-induced regulator during bacterial sepsis.
In this review, we aim to summarize cytokines, growth factors, chemoattractive proadhesion cues, and procoagulant molecules, which have been identified as substrates of ADAM10, with a particular focus on sepsis-related complex endothelium-immune cell interactions and microcirculation dysfunction (Table
1
) (Fig.
1
). In addition, we provide an overview of the regulation of ADAM10, from intracellular export, regulation at the cell membrane, endocytosis manner to extracellular release aspect, and their involvements in several aspects of sepsis pathophysiology, thereby implying a possible therapeutic target at each regulation stage (Table
2
) (Figs.
2
,
3
and
4
).

Different regulations of substrates shedding between ADAM10 and ADAM17
ADAM10 and its closest relative ADAM17, have a long list of potential substrates and overlapping substrate specificity [
21
]. Although considering it is possible that targeting ADAM10 activity could also affect ADAM17-mediated cleavage events such as TNF-α, IL-6R, and Notch, ADAMs-mediated shedding is seemingly not “random” events under certain pathophysiological conditions (Table
1
).
Crystal structure of the ADAM10 ectodomain
ADAM10, a single-pass transmembrane metalloproteinase, can be broadly divided into three parts: a carboxy-terminal SH3-binding cytoplasmic domain, a transmembrane domain, and an amino-terminal extracellular domain [
11
]. The ADAM10 ectodomain is one of the only ADAM ectodomains of known structure with a catalytic domain (PDB: 6BE6), containing an N-terminal signal sequence and an adjacent chaperone-like prodomain, followed by a zinc-binding metalloproteinase catalytic domain (MP domain), a disintegrin domain, a cysteine-rich domain (C domain), and a membrane-proximal stalk region [
22
] (Fig.
2
). After exiting from the ER and the Golgi network, in the absence of substrates, mature ADAM10 proteins are preferentially expressed as dimers rather than monomers at the cell membrane [
11
]. The C-terminal tail of one neighboring protein occupies the zinc-coordination catalytic site of its partner, simulating a peptide substrate, which putatively acts like a “lock” to block access of substrates to the enzyme pockets and exerts an autoinhibitory function providing an additional safeguard beyond that of the prodomain for the catalytic site [
22
] (Fig.
2
).
Different active sites between ADAM10 and ADAM17
The S1′ pocket is most important for ADAM10 substrate selectivity and is an enzyme pocket exhibiting a strong preference for large hydrophobic residues, in contrast to the shallower S1′pocket of ADAM17, which prefers smaller, nonaromatic hydrophobic residues [
22
]. Additionally, cleavage of some substrates like EGF is likely to be more dominated by exosite interactions involving non-catalytic domains within the ADAM10 protease to enhance substrate affinity [
23
]. Presumably, the C domain (present in most ADAMs but lacking in ADAM17) can contribute to substrate recognition [
21
].
Rapid regulation by phosphorylation and dimerization
With vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), a potent angiogenic and vascular permeability factor, many studies revealed its elevated levels in sepsis patients [
24
,
25
]. Indeed, ADAM10/17-dependent crosstalk between VEGF and extracellular signal-regulated kinase (Erk) has been reported. The binding of TIMP3 to both catalytic sites of the ADAM17 dimer was attenuated by the phosphorylation of ADAM17 at Thr735 mediated by VEGF/Erk axis, preventing TIMP3 from inhibiting the enzymatic activity of ADAM17 [
26
,
27
]. ADAM10 have a conserved threonine at Thr719 of the cytoplasmic domain as well [
11
]. And the cytoplasmic domain has been supposed to confer the ability of ADAM10 to form a homodimer [
28
], but whether Thr719 can authentically be phosphorylated mediated by VEGF/Erk or other cell signaling axis and whether phosphorylation at Thr719 controls ADAM10 dimerization and activity remains to be shown (Fig.
3
).
However, analysis of the mutant ADAM17Δ-cyto revealed that the protease requires its transmembrane domain but not the cytoplasmic domain for its rapid activation [
29
], suggesting that intracellular phosphorylation is presumably not a necessary step or the most rapid step for activity regulation of ADAM17. By comparison, Dreymueller, et al. considered ADAM10-mediated shedding events (e.g., VE-cadherin, CX3CL1, CXCL16, and CD44) occurred on the constitutive level and could be further enhanced by cell stimulation (e.g., ionomycin and APMA) [
21
,
29
]. Apparently, the different sensitivity to cell stimuli (e.g., lysophosphatidic acid, thrombin, and EGF, that clearly not stimulate ADAM10) [
30
], and more rapid processing of substrates by ADAM17 than by ADAM10 can explain, at least in part, why ADAM17 is the principal inducible sheddase for molecules, such as TNF-α and IL-6R, even though these can also be shed by ADAM10 in the absence of ADAM17.
Multiple consequences of ADAM10 in sepsis pathogenesis
Regarding its function as a “molecular scissor”, whether ADAM10 works for beneficial or harmful effects depends on the substrates it cleaves on different cell types under different pathological conditions (Table
1
). ADAM10, as a therapeutic target, may also cause toxic side effects due to the long and complex list of potential substrates [
21
]. For one hand, targeting ADAM10 is likely to make ADAM10 substrates (e.g., pro and anti-inflammatory cytokines) neutralize each other’s biological effects [
31
]. For another hand, ADAM10 does presumably not drive the simultaneous constitutive shedding of its large list of different substrates in response of different pathological stimulation [
11
]. As such, it may be necessary identifying how ADAM10 activity for specific substrates is regulated to minimize side-effect on cell types. To date, there is a lack of evidence of a specific cleavage motif recognized by the protease domain that can be used to predict which molecules could be sensitive to ADAM-associated cleavage, which prompted us to deeply study the non-catalytic domains of ADAM10 directly interacting with “partner proteins” like tetraspanin C8 (TspanC8) (but not directly interact with ADAM17) targeting of protease and substrates to particular membrane microdomains [
11
] (Fig.
2
).
Role of ADAM10-mediated shedding in vascular endothelial barrier dysfunction
Shedding of Notch
Endothelial barrier dysfunction is a hallmark of sepsis, and is at least partially mediated by molecular pathways that regulate endothelial barrier assembly during neovascularization. The Notch pathway has been shown to be critically involved in the regulation of developmental and pathological angiogenesis in sepsis [
32
,
33
]. In the plasma of sepsis patients, the soluble form of the Notch ligand Delta-like1 (sDLL1) is highly abundant. During lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-stimulated human umbilical endothelial cell (HUVEC) activation, DLL1-activated Notch signaling is closely associated with vascular permeability [
34
]. Vice versa, inhibition of Notch1 signaling is shown to improve posttraumatic septic non-unions or osteitis via the promotion on neovascularization and angiogenesis [
35
]. Cumulative evidence from gene knockout mouse models suggests that ADAM10 is a relevant Notch1 abscitase in vivo (Table
1
). Mice lacking endothelial ADAM10 (Adam10ΔEC) can survive into adulthood with defects in several organ-specific vascular beds, which can be rescued by endothelial-specific expression of Notch receptors in ECs [
36
]. In contrast, Adam17ΔEC mice have no evident defects in vascular development [
36
]. In mammals, the Notch pathway is comprised of the Jagged and Delta-like ligands, and the receptors Notch1–4. The ligand and receptor should be expressed in adjacent cells, so that DLL1 can provide the pulling force to open Notch1, which raise sensitive to cleavage by ADAM10 [
11
], while ADAM17 induces ligand-independent Notch signaling [
37
]. The
S. aureus
α-toxin can potently activate Notch to profoundly alter blood vessel sprouting in vascular cells in vivo, for which ADAM10 activation is essential [
32
]. However, the precise mechanisms by which α-toxin activates Notch in ECs remain unclear. In particular, the role of Ca
2+
influx in such pore-forming α-toxin-mediated Notch activation in ECs requires further study. Recently, shear stress, a key regulation in the initial stage of sepsis, has been further confirmed to activate Piezo1 channels to allow Ca
2+
influx, which activates ADAM10 (a Ca
2+
-regulated transmembrane sheddase) to play a potential role in the pathogenesis of sepsis by mediating Notch1 cleavage [
38
] (Fig.
3
).
HB-EGF/EGFR and VEGF/VEGFR2
Most of the vascular developmental defects can be explained by decreased levels of EGFR ligands, such as heparin-binding EGF (HB-EGF) and subsequent inadequate EGFR activation. Compared to healthy individuals, circulating levels of HB-EGF in patients with septic shock are much lower, associated with loss of endothelial barrier integrity [
39
]. HB-EGF gene KO has also been demonstrated to be detrimental to survival in a murine model of sepsis, increasing susceptibility to peritonitis-induced intestinal injury after exposure to CLP [
40
]. Although in most cases, mice lacking ADAM10 resemble Notch signaling-deficient mice, mice lacking ADAM17 resemble EGFR or EGFR-ligand knockout mice [
41
] (Table
1
). Dysregulated EGFR-ligand shedding has been shown to be induced by increased expression of constitutively active sheddases or activation of different sheddases by distinct stimuli. For example, phorbol esters predominantly stimulate ADAM17, thereby triggering large quantity of shedding of EGFR ligands from a late secretory pathway compartment, while Ca
2+
influx (a key regulation of sepsis-induced oxidative stress) activates ADAM10, requiring its cytoplasmic domain [
42
], implying that ADAM10 can be the dominate sheddase to HB-EGF shedding under the specific stimuli and its functional importance in pathological neovascularization.
Of note, ADAM10-dependent shedding of HB-EGF can stimulate VEGF-A-induced vascular permeability through HB-EGF/EGFR signaling in sepsis, while shedding of VEGF receptor 2 (VEGFR2) neuropilin-1 (NRP1) would not lead to VEGF binding [
21
] (Table
1
), which tips for us to explore more mechanisms of ADAM10 substrate specificity like TspanC8/ADAM10/HB-EGF or TspanC8/ADAM10/NRP1 complexes.
Shedding of cellular adhesion molecules
It is widely accepted that leukocyte trafficking regulated by ECs requires the production of chemokines and the expression of adhesion molecules, including the shedding of VCAM-1, ICAM-1 [
6
] (Table
1
). Plasma concentrations of adhesion molecules are increased in the serum of sepsis patients. The severity of sepsis is associated with markedly increased soluble ICAM-1 and VCAM-1 levels, indicating endothelial activation or damage [
43
]. In the case of ICAM-1, its ectodomain cleavage in ECs requires ADAM10 and ADAM17, contributing in part to neutrophil transendothelial migration, as neutrophils first firmly adhere to the endothelial surface using ICAM-1, while their actual transmigration requires release from ICAM-1 [
44
,
45
] (Fig.
1
). Interestingly, ICAM-1 has been reported to be recruited to tetraspanin-enriched membrane rafts (TEMs) as well as caveolin-rich lipid rafts upon clustering or leukocyte binding [
44
]. As specific “partner proteins” of ADAM10, however, it remains to be determined how members of the tetraspanins family TspanC8 specifically intersects with ADAM10-induced ICAM-1 shedding or whether the ADAM10-ICAM-1 interaction depends on membrane raft localization.
Shedding of VE-cadherin
Cadherins are almost exclusively shed by ADAM10 [
21
] (Table
1
). Upon lymphocyte arrest, endothelial VE-cadherin cleaved by ADAM10 is required for lymphocyte transendothelial migration. TNF-α and LPS-induced ADAM10-mediated VE-cadherin shedding injures endothelial barrier in vitro, and in patients with severe sepsis or septic shock with clinical signs of a microvascular leakage, circulating levels of VE-cadherin are significantly elevated [
13
] (Fig.
1
). Tspan5 or 17 is responsible for docking ADAM10 to endothelial tight junctions to induce the transmigration of human peripheral blood T lymphocytes across ECs dependent on VE-cadherin shedding [
46
]. It has emerged that, during bacterial infections, junctional clustered
S. aureus
α-toxin pores are shown to be more likely to cause cell death. Shah and his team explored a novel “dock-and-lock” mechanism—the cytoskeleton-related Tspan33–ADAM10–PLEKHA7–PDZD11–afadin multimolecular complex—through which ADAM10 clustering at junctions promotes α-toxin virulence and cadherin shedding [
47
] (Fig.
1
). In addition to the mechanism described above, activation of the acid sphingomyelinase/ceramide system by α-toxin is also mediated via ADAM10-induced VE-cadherin shedding, causes severe degradation of endothelial tight junctions in the lung and the development of lung edema [
48
].
Shedding of glycocalyx components
Inflammation-mediated “glycocalyx shedding” (GS) loss the normal functions of glycocalyx, leading to vascular hyper-permeability, tissue edema, augmented leukocyte adhesion, platelet aggregation, and unregulated vasodilation [
49
]. Many preclinical and clinical studies have demonstrated a decrease in the thickness of the glycocalyx and the clinical correlation between blood levels of glycocalyx components and organ dysfunction, severity, and mortality in sepsis [
50
,
51
]. During sepsis, the glycocalyx is shed by inflammation-associated sheddases such as metalloproteinases, heparanase, and hyaluronidase, which are activated by reactive oxygen species (ROS) and proinflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α and IL-1β [
49
]. Besides, overaggressive fluid therapy (leading to hypervolemia) may augment GS during early sepsis resuscitation [
6
]. Endomucin (EMCN), a component of the glycocalyx that interferes with the interactions between inflammatory cells and ECs, was recently confirmed to be cleaved from the cell surface by ADAM10/17 [
52
]. In the lung after systemic LPS injection in mice, EMCN was found to decrease to expression levels compatible with increased permeability, which has a potential negative impact in sepsis [
53
], implying ADAM10/17 can be the important contributor to GS.
Transmembrane glycoprotein CD44 is a well-known substrate of ADAM10 that, in its uncleaved form, mediates hyaluronic acid (HA) binding to inflamed vascular endothelium. The components HA and heparan sulfate (HS) together make up most of the luminal barrier, and the reduced thickness observed when both components are removed is comparable to what is observed after an inflammatory stimulus is present [
54
]. ADAM15 has been reported to contribute to glycocalyx shedding and vascular leakage through soluble CD44 release during sepsis [
55
]. However, the functional significance of ADAM10-induced CD44 shedding that mediates glycocalyx damage and further induces vascular barrier dysfunction is currently unclear (Fig.
1
). During early sepsis resuscitation, the volume of intravenous fluids administered is associated with septic endothelial glycocalyx degradation, which is especially sensitive to changes in plasma HS concentrations [
56
]. In fact, shedding of HA is faster than that of HS in response to the mechanical stimulus of blood flow [
57
,
58
], and transfers the shear stress signal to eNOS through the HA-CD44 axis [
59
]. Shedding of the glycocalyx decreases eNOS activity, which reduces NO bioavailability and generates ROS [
59
]. Notably, the generation of ROS is associated with Ca
2+
influx [
60
]. It seems the interaction between shear stress, glycocalyx, and Ca
2+
-regulated transmembrane sheddase ADAM10 occurs in a loop, the mechanisms of which with involvement in pathophysiological processes occurring during sepsis still have plenty of room to research.
Role of ADAM10-mediated shedding in systemic inflammatory response
Shedding of proinflammatory cytokines
It has been demonstrated that the evolution of proinflammatory cascades exhibits pivotal roles in sepsis pathogenesis. TNF-α is a potent proinflammatory cytokine, and proteolytically released from cells [
61
,
62
]. sTNF-α via binding its receptor TNFR1 induces cellular NF-κB activation, apoptosis, or necroptosis [
63
]. TNF-α levels are found elevated in patients with septic shock and positively correlate with sepsis severity as well as mortality [
63
]. Almost a quarter century ago, ADAM17 (also known as TNF-α converting enzyme), has emerged as the best candidate TNF sheddase [
64
] (Table
1
). Myeloid cell-specific ADAM17-deficient mice can survive into adulthood by preventing increased TNF serum levels offering strong protection from septic shock lethality in mice [
61
]. Likewise, in mice lacking TIMP3, TNF-α levels show to be increased in several inflammatory models including increased liver inflammation, sustained acute lung inflammation, and LPS-induced severe systemic inflammation [
21
], and it is not clear whether this involves also inhibition of other ADAMs and MMPs. A variety of in vitro and in vivo studies has demonstrated that but other proteinases than ADAM17 can also release TNF-α, including ADAM10 [
62
,
65
,
66
]. In mouse macrophages, ADAM17 is the primary sheddase, but ADAM10 is also involved in TNF-α shedding, while the involvement of ADAM10 and ADAM17 is equally in mouse vascular endothelial cell line [
67
].
Of interest was that resting neutrophils from irradiated mice reconstituted with ADAM17-deficient leukocytes expressed higher levels of surface TNFR1 [
68
]. TNFR1 shedding is also controlled by ADAM17 not only limiting cellular sensitivity toward TNF-α, but sTNFR1 also transmits cellular immune signaling itself by either acting as the decoy receptor for sTNF-α or binding to membrane-bound TNF-α to induce reverse signaling [
63
]. This can explain why ADAM17-mediated TNFR1 shedding reduces excessive inflammation and protects from multiple organ failure in clinically relevant mice model of sepsis [
69
], but elevated sTNFR levels positively correlate with organ failure and sepsis mortality of patients with severe sepsis [
70
].
It has been reported that human and murine IL-6R is shed by ADAM17 in a rapidly induced manner, whereas constitutively release of endogenous IL-6R is largely induced by increased expression of constitutively active sheddase ADAM10 [
71
]. Binding of IL-6 to sIL-6R produces an agonistic proinflammatory IL-6/sIL-6R complex, activating cells via gp130 irrespective of whether the IL-6R itself is expressed on the cell surface, which is termed trans-signaling [
72
]. In human, blood levels of sIL-6R are high, and IL-6 levels are extremely low under normal conditions. However, during inflammation, sIL-6R levels increase two- to fivefold, while IL-6 levels increase 100–1000-fold, particularly in septic states [
73
]. IL-6R shedding by ADAM17 has been shown to be rapidly induced in LPS-treated mice, but circulating homeostatic sIL-6R levels are not predominantly dependent on ADAM10/17 activity [
74
].
Shedding of chemokines
Chemokine CX3CL1 and CXCL16 have many functions such as chemotaxis and adhesion, and mediate immune injury. Almost any stimulus that alters cellular homeostasis can elicit the expression of inflammatory chemokine, such as infections and immune disorders [
75
]. CX3CL1 as well as CXCL16 plasma levels are elevated in sepsis patients admitted to the ICU and correlate with disease severity and increased mortality [
76
,
77
]. The release of membrane-bound chemokines CX3CL1 and CXCL16 by ADAM10 and ADAM17 attracts monocytes and T cells to the site of inflammation, which plays the crucial role in the local restriction of inflammatory processes [
63
]. It has been reported that ADAM10 but not ADAM17 is required for efficient chemokine-induced recruitment of monocytic THP-1 cells and neutrophils to the alveolar space in a murine model of acute pulmonary inflammation [
78
]. CX3CL1 KO improves survival of CLP mice, together with a reduction in the expression of intraperitoneal inducible NO synthase (iNOS) and bactericidal proinflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, TNF-α, IFN-γ, and IL-12 [
79
]. Likewise, administration of CXCL16 antagonists improves survival in the CLP mice model with less organ damage [
77
].
Shedding of CD44
The effects of CD44 shedding in the inflammatory response appear to be multiple, associated with diverse biological processes involving migrating cells, including inflammation, angiogenesis, wound healing, and bone metabolism [
80
]. Neutrophil trafficking also depends on neutrophil expression of CD44, which binds to HA. During
S. aureus
-induced sepsis, CD44-HA axis plays important roles in neutrophil trafficking in the liver, reflecting its more general role in the regulation of leukocyte behavior during inflammation [
81
]. Indeed, HA in a complex with CD44 competes with LPS for TLR4 activation to suppress the septic response to LPS [
82
]. Antagonistic monoclonal antibody targeting CD44 on neutrophils reduced neutrophil infiltration and lung damage in CLP mice, thereby implying the complexity of the effects of ADAM10-mediated CD44 shedding. Notably, the peritoneal but not plasma concentrations of TNF-α, IL-6, and CXC chemokines were significantly increased in CD44 KO mice during
E. coli
-induced abdominal sepsis, whereas inflammatory cell recruitment and organ damage were unaffected, which may be the result of impaired extravasation compensated by enhanced chemoattractive molecules [
83
]. Some chemoattractive substrates are shed by ADAM17 (e.g., TNF-α and L-selectin), others by ADAM10 (e.g., most cadherins), and some by both proteases (e.g., IL-6R and CX3CL1).
Shedding of Notch
As mentioned above, ADAM10 has been identified as the main protease involved in Notch processing and activation. Except for the involvement in neovascularisation, Notch1, 2 and 3 all play important roles in the increased Notch signaling detected in polarized activation of macrophages [
37
] (Fig.
1
). Bai et al. reported specifically knockout of Notch1 in macrophage reduced organ damage in the LPS-induced septic mice model [
84
]. TLR signaling has been shown to induce Tspan33 production in a Notch-dependent manner, which modulates macrophage activation via ADAM10-involved regulation of Notch signaling [
85
]. Besides, ADAM10 has been carefully described to be widely involved in the development and function of innate and adaptive immune cells via altered Notch signaling, and more detailed reviews on this topic are available [
11
].
Role of ADAM10-mediated shedding in platelet aggregation
Platelet aggregation could be an essential scavenging mechanism providing host benefit, but pathogen-induced detrimental aggregation of platelets in the circulation will lead to microvascular dysfunction, thrombosis, and organ injury during sepsis [
8
] (Fig.
1
). In a septic mouse model, platelet GPVI shedding was shown to impact local immunity and accelerate bacterial infection in the bronchoalveolar space [
86
]. In human platelets, ADAM10 is the critical scissor for released, soluble GPVI ectodomain, a plasma biomarker of platelet aggregation and venous thrombosis during inflammation, Tspan15/ADAM10 and Tspan33/ADAM10 cleave the platelet collagen receptor GPVI to damage vascular integrity [
87
]. Al-Tamimi et al. were first to find that exposure of platelets to high shear (that usually occurs in inflammation initiation) induced ADAM10-dependent GPVI cleavage as well [
88
].
Role of ADAM10 in pore-forming toxinic immunity
The
S. aureus
is a leading cause of bacterial infectious diseases such as sepsis. The
S. aureus
α-toxin–ADAM10 interaction at the cytomembrane is an internal pathogen–host interaction. First, ADAM10 activation contributes to cellular damage in bacterial sepsis. Upregulating the metalloprotease activity (such as Notch processing) of ADAM10 on vascular cells is required for the toxin’s full virulence against these cells, which is mediated by processes such as angiogenesis damage [
32
]. Second, ADAM10 protease activity is not required for α-toxin-mediated activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome in monocytes, but its cell surface expression. Short-term treatment of THP-1 cells (15–30 min) with GI254023X did not reduce α-toxin-induced cytotoxicity [
89
]. Hoven et al. confirmed that α-toxin binding and subsequent cytotoxicity have no requirement for the catalytic site of ADAM10 but require the disintegrin domain and the prodomain; however, the physical interaction between ADAM10 and α-toxin is still ill-defined [
90
]. Third, one group has shown that the content of an intact cell membrane that is therefore of importance to the strong interaction of α-toxin with ADAM10 [
91
]. The assembly of the α-toxin–ADAM10 complex in the cholesterol/sphingolipid-rich caveolar raft of the membrane seems to increase the local concentration of α-toxin, which subsequently results in the caveolin-1 (Cav1)-directed oligomerization of the toxin that mediate cytolysis and focal adhesion disruption [
91
]. Hence, in addition to the cytoskeleton affecting aggregation of toxins with ADAM10 at the adhesion junction [
47
], it was further proven that lipid rafts affect the subcellular localization of ADAM10 (Fig.
3
).
Initially, membrane lesions are caused by the metalloprotease activity of ADAM10 by directly interacting with the pore-forming toxin, which may further trigger activation of ADAM10 indirectly induced by uncontrolled flux of ions via cellular pore formation.
Pseudomonas aeruginosa
ExlA and
Serratia marcescens
ShlA have been shown to indirectly activate ADAM10 by promoting Ca
2+
influx as mentioned before, leading to cleavage of downstream substrates [
92
] (Fig.
3
). However, ADAM10 is not a cellular receptor of these toxins, and the receptor of them has not yet been identified [
92
].
Remarkably, the interplay between α-toxin and its receptor ADAM10 plays a crucial role in several cell types. Compound knockout of ADAM10 on myeloid cells and platelets has been reported to reverse mortality from lethal staphylococcal sepsis [
93
]. In animal models,
S. aureus
-induced platelets aggregated on the surface of Kupffer cells in the liver via α-toxin-dependent and complement-dependent mechanisms, which could be blocked by treatment with an α-toxin neutralizing antibody (MEDI4893*) [
8
]. ADAM10 knockdown produced the same result. In response to methicillin-resistant
Staphylococcus aureus
(MRSA) infection, macrophages have also been reported to functionally activate the expression of proinflammatory mediators in vitro, induce a cytokine storm in vivo, and increase mortality and bacteremia [
94
]. In addition to harming the host, α-toxin can activate specialized pro-resolving mediators (SPMs) in human M2-like macrophages to promote the resolution of infectious inflammation, which can be reversed by inhibition of the α-toxin receptor ADAM10 [
95
]. This finding shows that α-toxin is not always destructive to the host and hints at the latent beneficial function of ADAM10 in inflammation resolution.
Structure-based specific regulation of ADAM10 protein
Monoclonal antibody
Due to deep research into the structure of the ADAM ectodomain, monoclonal antibodies have become an attractive therapeutic [
96
]. A highly specific anti-ADAM10 mAb (8C7) can be generated as a “key” to fit the “lock”, which binds to sites on the ADAM10 C domain [
21
,
22
]. The X-ray structure (PDB: 5L0Q) showed that upon competitive binding to the C domain in vitro, the 8C7 heavy chain displaces the MP domain, leading to a distinct active form of ADAM10 via exposure of the catalytic site [
22
]. However, 8C7 preferentially targets active ADAM10 instead of the autoinhibitory conformation in vivo, leading to displacement of the MP domain relative to the bound substrate, which is dependent on disulfide isomerization of the ADAM10 D + C domain and redox conditions [
97
].
Its selective identification of the active ADAM10 population inspired us to determine whether 8C7 could mark overactive ECs or immune cells by targeting active ADAM10 during sepsis. Despite the gene expression of ADAM10 is proved upgraded in sepsis patients [
14
–
16
], a few studies have reported different species of ADAM10 prevalence in septic blood vascular system, for example, an immature form retaining the prodomain (proADAM10), a mature full-length form (ADAM10f), and a soluble form released from the membrane (sADAM10). On the basis of their apparent molecular weights, both two ADAM10 forms, both proADAM10 and mature ADAM10 dimers were presence at the cell surface [
27
]. Septic serum mediated vascular endothelial injury by changing redox conditions, promoting ROS production and MAPK and NF-κB activity [
98
]. Increased intracellular Ca
2+
concentration allowing the ADAM10 prodomain to be available for cleavage by furin [
92
], as well as VEGF/Erk-induced shedding activity of ADAM10 [
99
], which provides the possibility that the ADAM10 prevalent in sepsis is also readily activated (Fig.
3
).
ADAM10-specific synthetic inhibitors
The majority of synthetic ADAM10 blockers inhibit ADAM10 via a Zn
2+
-binding competitive mechanism [
96
]. While Zn
2+
-binding inhibitors can exhibit selectivity between ADAM10 and ADAM17 to a certain extent, they rarely selectively inhibit the shedding of substrates [
31
]. The differences in the S1′ pocket of the ADAM10 catalytic site between ADAM10 and ADAM17, as mentioned above, allowed the development of the hydroxamate-based ADAM10 selective inhibitor LT4 (also known as Compound 3), which shows more suitability to interact with the S1′ pocket of ADAM10 as well as higher specificity and efficacy for ADAM10 over ADAM17 by X-ray crystallography studies [
31
,
100
] (Fig.
3
). This finding may explain why LT4 is more potent than the commercial inhibitor GI254023X. To the best of our knowledge, LT4 has only been described to reduce ADAM10-induced shedding of the ligands of the NK cell receptor natural killer group 2D (NKG2D) regulating the cytotoxic activity of NK cells for the local restriction of inflammatory processes in Hodgkin’s lymphoma cell models thus far [
100
,
101
]. Perhaps, further studies are needed to verify the selective inhibition of LT4 by ADAM10 in an inflammatory model.
In contrast, GI254023X has been more studied in septic models. Upon LPS stimulation, GI254023X significantly decreased the production of proinflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6) and the apoptosis rate in THP-1 cells [
16
]. Inhibition of ADAM10-mediated VE-cadherin cleavage using GI254023X attenuates sepsis-associated endothelial barrier disruption [
13
]. GI254023X was shown to suppress α-toxin activity by interacting with the ADAM10 catalytic site in a murine model of
S. aureus
lung infection [
102
], which indicates that treatment with this inhibitor could represent a therapeutic option, especially in inflammatory or infectious lung diseases [
102
]. While there are no molecular modeling studies of the interactions between GI254023X and ADAM10, the compound has both a zinc-binding moiety and a bulky aromatic group that could interact with the ADAM10 S1′pocket, which could explain its selectivity for ADAM10 over ADAM17 [
31
,
102
] (Fig.
3
). GI254023X is widely used in academic research, but ClinicalTrials.gov registry has no sepsis-related clinical trials about it.
CID3117694, an example of a non-Zn
2+
-binder, does not have distinct zinc-binding groups [
32
]. As CID3117694 competes for nonactivity sites with substrates linked to ADAM10 by glycosylation, it exhibits apparent substrate selectivity [
31
,
103
]. CID3117694 inhibits the substrate TNF-α glycosylated at S80 but not the nonglycosylated substrate, which is in contrast with Zn
2+
-binders which have no differential inhibition for the glycosylated substrate and nonglycosylated variant. It has been demonstrated that the potency of CXCL16 shedding inhibition mediated by CID3117694 was similar to that of GI254023X, which was able to inhibit chemokine-induced migration of peripheral PBMCs and primary human neutrophils [
103
]. Accordingly, it can be seen that differences in the glycosylation of ADAM10 substrates are important foundations of the substrate selectivity of ADAM10.
Autoinhibitory prodomain
When mature ADAM10 that is activated upon proteolytic release is located at the cell surface, this natural autoinhibitory prodomain has been described as a potent competitive inhibitor of the catalytic/disintegrin domain of ADAM10 [
104
]. Its ability to selectively inhibit ADAM10 while weakly inhibiting other ADAM family members is certainly a more specific target to manipulate ADAM10-induced shedding events. More studies are needed in the future to validate the effect of the ADAM10 prodomain in inflammatory disease models.
Potential targets of ADAM10 posttranslational regulation for sepsis
Constitutive activity of ADAM10: regulation of intracellular export and protein processing
ADAM10 is originally synthesized in an inactive form. Newly synthesized ADAM10 could be endowed with catalytic activity by proteolysis during forward transport through the endoplasmic reticulum (ER)–Golgi secretory pathway to the plasma membrane [
105
] (Fig.
2
).
Arginine stretch of the cytoplasmic C-terminus
ER retention of ADAM10 to limit its export is a common mechanism used by many cell types to control the forward trafficking and surface expression of integral membrane proteins. An arginine stretch (
723
RRR) of the cytoplasmic C-terminus of ADAM10, the ER retention motif, has been reported to be responsible for this velocity-limiting step for surface trafficking, which governs surface density, and a second arginine mutation will end this limitation to allow ADAM10 ER exit [
105
] (Fig.
2
). However, the detailed molecular details of the mechanisms promoting arginine-rich region-mediated ER retention still need to be further elucidated.
N-glycosylation of the extracellular domain
ADAM10 is regarded as a glycoprotein with four potential N-glycosylation sites (N-X-S/T) including N551, N267, N278, and N439 [
106
] (Fig.
2
). Each potential N-glycosylation site was characterized individually through mutation years ago and each site is profoundly involved in many biological functions, such as the protein stability, folding, trafficking, and substrate recognition specificities of ADAM10 [
106
]. In addition, a very recent study reported that an N279 mutant version of ADAM10 prevented the high mannose terminal modification from limiting the ADAM10 EC-autonomous activity required for endothelial-to-hematopoietic transition (EHT) [
107
]. This finding suggests that restricting the N-glycan biosynthesis pathway to regulate the function of ADAM10 can be a new avenue to modulate inflammatory response and endothelial dysfunction in sepsis.
Proteolytic prodomain removal
During pro-ADAM10 maturation, the prodomain of the inactive proform is removed by furin protease and PC7, two proteases of the proprotein convertase (PC) family leading to the formation of the active, processed form in the secretory pathway, which is the necessary prerequisite for the constitutive activation of ADAM10 [
108
] (Fig.
2
). Intriguingly, the ADAM10 prodomain can not only specifically inhibit ADAM10 ectodomain shedding but also exerts molecular chaperone activity to facilitate the proper folding of other domains of the ADAM10 protein [
104
,
109
]. PC-mediated maturation of ADAM10 was suggested to be a rather slow process of cleavage that cooccurs with enzyme synthesis, while the rapid stimulation of ADAM10 does not significantly affect prodomain removal [
110
]. Pro-ADAM10, but not ADAM17, specifically associates with calmodulin (CaM) under low Ca
2+
concentrations [
111
]. More than being a prerequisite for furin activation, an ROS-induced increase in intracellular Ca
2+
concentration will lead to the separation of CaM and the ADAM10 prodomain, allowing the interaction between furin and the ADAM10 prodomain [
92
,
109
]. However, H
2
O
2
-mediated oxidation can disrupt the Ca
2+
-binding capacity of furin, resulting in defective activity [
112
] (Fig.
3
).
Recently, a study showed that furin expression can be induced by TLR signaling in a Notch-dependent manner. In COVID-19 patients, as one of the SARS-CoV-2 entry factors, serum furin levels could be used as a predictor of severe disease with a close positive relationship with IL-6, CD14 (the new biomarker for sepsis diagnosis), and disease severity [
113
,
114
]. A prospective cohort study showed that high plasma level of furin may be of use as an early stage infection biomarker of sepsis [
115
]. These evidences obviously indicate a significant role of furin in the modulation of sepsis-induced inflammation. PC7 and furin have been confirmed to trigger the activation of many hormones, neuropeptides, growth factors, and receptors, and these data suggested a major role in immunity, but targeting these processes still lacks specificity for ADAM10 as a method of inhibitory therapy [
116
].
SH3 domain partners
The cytosolic portion of ADAM10 proteases, which contains the two prominent proline-rich regions (PRRs), comprises amino acids 708–717 (PKLPPPKPLP) and 722–728 (RRRPPQP) [
117
] (Fig.
3
). They have been proven to directly interact with Src homology 3 (SH3) domain-containing interactors, including adaptor proteins, palmitoyltransferases, and nonreceptor protein tyrosine kinases (the Src family kinases [SFKs]: SRC, Yes, Fyn, Fgr, Lck, Hck, Blk, Lyn, and Frk) [
118
]. These SH3 domain proteins exert characteristic functions for transport, localization, and/or regulation of ADAM10 activity [
117
] (Fig.
3
).
With regard to SFKs, bioinformatics analysis showed that SRC was one of the key regulators in neonatal sepsis [
119
]. LPS-induced sepsis leads to decreased SRC in the brain, which decreases tyrosine phosphorylation of mitochondrial proteins to decrease ROS [
120
]. In CLP-induced polymicrobial sepsis, treatment with the c-Src-SH3 peptide in a complex with β-glucans (SPG) dramatically reduces the mortality involved in controlling the excessive production of ROS and inflammation [
121
]. SRC has been shown to bind directly to the cytoplasmic domain of ADMA10, limiting cell proliferation and migration to a great extent in pituitary adenoma [
122
]. As a putative ADAM10-interacting SH3 domain interactor, Lck can bind with both phosphorylated and unphosphorylated forms of ADAM10 [
117
,
123
]. Considering its localization in the cytosol, plasma membrane, and lipid rafts, its specific effect on ADAM10 needs further study [
87
]. It is worth noting that the lipid raft platforms-dependent substrates’ capture of ADAMs, including ADAM10, is confirmed to require activation of SFKs [
124
,
125
] (Fig.
3
).
With regard to ADAM family proteases, palmitoylation, as a posttranscriptional modification contributing to intracellular localization and membrane clustering has not been reported thus far. Fortunately, an ER-resident palmitoyltransferase, zinc finger DHHC domain-containing protein 6 (ZDHHC6), which acts as a switch for palmitoylation, was also identified as a putative ADAM10-interacting SH3 domain interactor [
117
]. Coincidentally, ZDHHC6 is related to sepsis. Myeloid differentiation primary response protein (MYD88), a commonly expressed adapter molecule in the Toll-like receptor (TLR) family, is palmitoylated by ZDHHC6 and aggravates sepsis by mitigating neutrophil migration [
126
]. In T cells, in the presence of 2BP, the shift of ADAM10, FasL and Lck (Src-related kinase) to light fractions was still visible [
124
]. Since then, researchers have speculated that the palmitoylation status of these regulatory proteins is not decisive for their raft positioning in T cells but changes the availability at the site of enzymatic activity.
Activation and repression regulators of ADAM10 at the cell membrane
TIMPs
TIMPs, which were initially identified as inhibitors of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), are currently the most studied endogenous downregulators of ADAM10 and ADAM17/TACE expression [
127
]. Among these TIMPs, TIMP1 exhibits a very restricted inhibitory profile against ADAMs and specifically inhibits ADAM10 but not ADAM17 [
128
]. TIMP3 can widely inhibit members of the ADAM family, including ADAM10, 12, 15, 17, 28, and 33 [
129
] (Fig.
3
).
Mice lacking TIMP3 show increased pulmonary microvascular barrier dysfunction under septic conditions [
130
], as well as TNF-α levels in several inflammatory models including increased liver inflammation, sustained acute lung inflammation, and LPS-induced severe systemic inflammation [
21
]. In contrast to ADAM10, TIMP3 is a more potent inhibitor of ADAM17 by binding of the catalytic domain. Because of the beneficial effects of TIMP3, a potential therapeutic treatment to increase TIMP3 levels in the tissue has been widely sought for diseases characterized by augmented proteolysis, such as sepsis [
129
]. Nevertheless, inappropriately high levels of the inhibitor can lead to mechanism-based side effects on tissue homeostasis [
26
,
129
]. Overexpression of TIMP3 increases TIMP3 binding to the endocytic receptor LRP1, while other LRP1 ligands, such as TIMP1, may trigger a positive feedback loop that inhibits ADAM10 to dramatically affect cell behaviors [
26
]. Recently, the TIMP1 gene has been identified as a novel biomarker involved in the occurrence and development of septic shock-associated AKI [
131
]. The plasma TIMP1 levels of patients upon ICU admission were significantly associated with 30-day mortality [
132
]. Since TIMP1 specifically inhibits ADAM10 but not ADAM17, TIMP1 seems to be a more promising way to interfere exclusively with ADAM10-mediated proteolysis than TIMP3 is, but its repressive effect on MMPs should still be noted.
RECK
Reversion-inducing cysteine-rich protein with Kazal motifs (RECK), a glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchored membrane-tethered protein, is a serine protease inhibitor-like molecule identified as a negative ADAM10 regulator (Fig.
4
). The plasma membrane enzyme glycerophosphodiester phosphodiesterase 2 (GDE2) can indirectly regulate ADAM10 activity by cleaving RECK at the GPI anchor and removing it from the cell surface [
133
]. However, the precise molecular mechanism of the RECK/ADAM10 interaction remains unclear.
RECK is ubiquitously expressed in almost all tissues and exerts anti-inflammatory effects but is downregulated in inflammatory diseases, such as periodontal disease, renal fibrosis, and myocardial fibrosis. Inflammatory mediators, such as IL-17A and IL-18, suppress RECK expression, while restoring RECK reverses IL-17A- or IL-18-induced vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC) or cardiac fibroblast migration and proliferation [
134
]. In comparison with TIMPs, RECK plays an irreplaceable role in vascular development, and RECK deletion is embryonically lethal [
135
]. In addition, defective expression of RECK in HUVECs causes altered vascular tube formation and cell senescence, and the main molecular pathways involved are hypoxia-related pathways, VEGF, angiopoietins (ANG), and Notch signaling [
135
], which are closely associated with the progression of sepsis. Early investigation suggested that RECK expressed in the Central Nervous System (CNS) shut down Notch signaling by inhibiting its proteolysis via direct interaction with ADAM10 [
136
], which implies the protective function of RECK in sepsis by inhibiting ADAM10.
Meprin β
ADAM10 is also susceptible to undergoing proteolytic attack by other proteases. The zinc-dependent metalloproteases meprin α and meprin β are extracellular proteases that are highly abundant in enterocytes and are widely expressed in the epidermis, blood vessels, kidney, lung, brain, and certain immune cells [
137
]. Meprins are responsible for the proteolytic processes of various inflammation-related chemokines, cytokines, and other bioactive peptides, which modulate the biological activity of immune cells involved in several inflammatory diseases, such as sepsis, inflammatory bowel disease and AKI [
137
]. ADAM10 contains a meprin β cleavage site within the 194 amino acid propeptide [
138
] (Fig.
3
). Since ADAM10 and Meprin β also share common inflammatory-related substrates, including IL-6R and IL-11R, induction of ADAM10 activity by Meprin β most likely has consequences for inflammatory pathologic processes [
139
–
141
]. Of note, considering that meprin α knockout mice were protected against LPS-induced AKI [
142
], further research is needed to confirm whether there are any meprin α cleavage sites beyond the already authenticated 194 amino acid propeptide.
The other ADAM family members
The functional crosstalk between ADAM family members is far more complex than initially thought. As a single-pass membrane-tethered protease, ADAM10 can accordingly be subjected to intramembrane proteolysis (RIP), similar to Notch receptors [
143
,
144
]. The residue remaining on the cell surface (ADAM10-CTF) after ADAM10 proteolytic cleavage can further release the ADAM10 intracellular domain (ICD) processed by presenilin and γ-secretase enzymes. ADAM10 ICD immunoreactivity was visible in a nuclear speckle-like compartment that contains interchromatin bodies that concentrate proteins involved in mRNA production, splicing, and maturation, similar to what has been described for Notch- and APP-ICDs in the nucleus, but its functional consequences have yet to be defined [
144
]. ADAM9/15 are thought to be the proteases responsible for releasing the ADAM10 ectodomain [
144
] (Fig.
3
). Specific inhibition of ADAM19 increases ADAM10 activity in the cell membrane, promotes its proteolytic release, and enhances ADAM10-induced shedding like the TNF family member CD40L shedding to regulate T-cell proliferation, activation, and survival [
145
]. ADAM10 seemingly performs a dual role in cells during inflammation, and ADAM9 has been implicated in this process.
Endocytosis by “scavenger” involved in ADAM10 regulation
Low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein-1 (LRP1) is an endocytic receptor for over 40 ligands, including TIMP1 and TIMP3. LRP1-dependent endocytosis manner may provide potential therapeutic targets against sepsis, depending on its ligands. Pharmacological blockade of LRP1 inhibited endocytic clearance of TIMP3, leading to an increase in macrophage surface levels of the inhibitor that blocked ADAM17-induced TNF release following LPS stimulation [
146
]. Conversely, with regard to ADAM10, an increased level of TIMP3 is likely to promote TIMP1/ADAM10 interaction via competitively binding to LRP1 [
26
].
When evaluating the affinity between LRP1 and ADAMs, TIMPs, or metalloproteinase/TIMP complexes, one group pointed out that ADAM10 and ADAM17 did not bind to LRP1 [
147
]. To a certain extent, TIMP1 was also found to increase the affinity of target metalloproteinase MMPs for LRP1, whereas the affinity of the ADAM10/TIMP1 complex for LRP1 was not shown [
147
]. However, TIMP1 directly binds LRP1 and displays MMP-independent activities—which include the promotion of cytokine-like activities—that influence the behavior of various cell types, including neurons during neuronal plasticity [
148
]. It will be interesting to determine whether LRP1 is more inclined to interact with TIMP1 or the ADAM10/TIMP1 complex to mediate cellular effects in an inflammatory environment (Fig.
4
).
Exosomal sorting, intracellular trafficking, and extracellular release of ADAM10
Extracellular release: ATG16L1
Given the important and unique role of ADAM10 as the α-toxins receptor, exosomes mediate a previously unknown mechanism of defense to neutralize toxins. This is a new innate immune response to defend against bacterial infection, which plays a significant role in sepsis. Keller et al. suggested that in response to MRSA DNA, ATG16L1 regulated the production of exosomes, prevented ADAM10 accumulation on the human lung epithelial cell line A549 cells, protected cells by trapping α-toxins on the exosome membrane, and improved the survival of mice infected with MRSA [
149
] (Fig.
4
). Based on the above studies, a new strategy to enhance the body's defenses can be envisaged by injecting exosomes into the body or promoting the production of exosomes to absorb bacterial toxins. Atg16L1 hypomorphic mice display increased lethality in both sepsis and pneumonia models of MRSA infection by reducing autophagy to increase damage to ECs caused by α-toxin rather than reducing bacterial burden [
150
]. It has been demonstrated that the ATG16L1 rs2241880 A > G functional polymorphism is associated of with ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) severity and septic shock development [
151
]. In addition to protecting ECs by increasing autophagic activity, another cytoprotective pathway may exist. With increased release of exosomal ADAM10, could downregulation of ADAM10 accumulation on cell membranes potentially protect host cells from further blows from bacterial endotoxin attack by reducing ADAM10-mediated cell shedding events during sepsis? Besides, ATG16L1 is expressed in many cell types including ECs. Both pro- and mature forms of ADAM10 are identified in secreted exosomes from various cell types including HUVECs, but clinically, it is unclear whether they are associated with the progression of sepsis [
152
]. And beyond that, both the origin and cargo selection process of exosomes remain poorly defined. Elucidating the mechanisms of molecular sorting systems for exosomes is the basis for the specific control of toxin receptors.
Molecular sorting for exosomes: proteins in invaginated membrane microdomains
Exosomes can be derived from invaginated membrane microdomains that are prone to internalization according to the lipid composition, and then, early endosomes (EEs) are generated via fission and scission of invaginated membrane domains [
153
]. These microdomains contain TEMs, cholesterol-based lipid rafts, proteolipid-enriched domains, clathrin-coated pits, caveolae, and low-density lipid-enriched regions [
154
].
ADAM10 had already been shown to predominantly localize to different tetraspanin microdomains (e.g., TspanC8s, CD9, CD81, and CD82) in different human cell types, of which CD9 and CD81 are paradigmatic markers of exosomal vesicles [
155
–
157
]. However, only Tspan5 and Tspan15 have been described to do so with ADAM10 endocytosis thus far, and whether Tspan5 and Tspan15 are expressed in inflammation-associated vesicles has not been studied [
158
] (Fig.
4
).
The formation of GPI-AP-enriched early endosomal compartments (GEECs) is independent of clathrin- or caveolae-dependent endocytosis. Interestingly, the ADAM10 inhibitor RECK has been reported to mediate the endocytosis pathway and to similarly affect GEECs [
159
,
160
]. Given the evidence that RECK can interact with ADAM10, an increasing number of studies have focused on whether this interaction influences their behavior and activity on the cell membrane. A study recently suggested that loss of proteolytic activity of ADAM10 by either inhibition or mutations of the catalytic site could cause the removal of mature ADAM10 from the cell surface and even the whole cell, which is associated with increased internalization, lysosomal degradation, or release of mature ADAM10 in extracellular vesicles [
102
]. These findings suggest that RECK suppression of ADAM10 involves endocytic pathways (Fig.
4
).
Endocytic trafficking: Rabs
Rab4 and Rab5 are mostly responsible for guiding EE transport toward multivesicular bodies (MVBs) [
161
]. Rabs are closely associated with sepsis-related inflammation. As an example, Rab5 is known to exert its function in the internalization of VE-cadherin and, as a result, it regulates endothelial barrier function and sepsis-related vascular inflammation [
162
]. Recently, the hematopoietic APPL1–Rab5 axis has been reported to restrict NLRP3 inflammasome overactivation in macrophages in the acute septic response [
163
]. Surprisingly, in endolysosomal vesicles released by Hodgkin lymphoma (HL) and stromal cells, specific fluorescent inhibitors of ADAM10 that bind to its active site colocalize with Rab5-positive early endosomes [
164
] (Fig.
4
).
In these contexts, exosomal protein selection and intracellular trafficking of ADAM10 may not be incidental, but still unclarified aspects of this process are still present.
Conclusions and future directions
As illustrated in the present review, ADAM10, as a “multitasker” in the homeostatic regulation of vascular inflammatory responses and pathogenesis of acute inflammatory diseases, including sepsis, exerts its enormous influence on the functions of almost every type of vascular inflammatory cell, especially when serving as the
S. aureus
α-toxin receptor. ADAM10 is undoubtedly an appropriate responder to future vasculature-targeted therapy for sepsis. The openness of the three-dimensional X-ray crystal structure of the ADAM10 ectodomain and full-length amino acid sequence of the ADAM10 structural domain provide the basis for the development of novel targeted therapeutic interventions in the future. However, little is known about posttranslational modifications (PTMs) of ADAM10, such as its phosphorylation or ubiquitylation, and their function, and mass spectrometry-based methods require large amounts of starting materials and easily miss rare proteins [
165
]. Fortunately, a proteomics tool capable of identifying single proteins—a nanopore-based single-molecule peptide reader with sensitivity to single-amino acid changes—has been developed recently [
166
]. The development of single-molecule proteomics constitutes a promising basis for measurements of small samples, as well as an accurate determination of cellular protein isoforms and variants, including their PTMs [
165
,
166
].
We also discuss how ADAM10 is regulated and activated, and manipulating these processes provides a promising way to potentially alter the process of inflammatory responses. Nevertheless, because of its multitasking nature, there are still several key issues that need to be noted. First, we emphasize the development of unique structure-based and substrate specificity-based antibodies against ADAM10. Second, the biological behaviors of ADAM10—including its constitutive activity, such as its cellular trafficking, dimerization, and conformational changes—are significantly complicated and usually regulated by multimolecular synergy or multiple pathways acting in concert or by one interacting protein regulating multiple regulatory pathways. Rapid activation of ADAM10 is cell type specific, stimulus-specific, and environment specific, and especially dependent upon the membrane environment. Third, ADAM10 activation is involved not only in the onset but also in the resolution of inflammation, more research on specific substrates for ADAM10 is needed in the future. Whether there are any other specific mechanisms remains unknown. Finally, the endothelial dysfunction discussed in our present review is limited to ECs. However, ECs are highly heterogeneous in morphology and function between different organs and even different vessels in the same organ. The differences in substrate expression and shedding events among different ECs need to be elucidated in the future, thus providing the possibility of targeted therapy for specific organ dysfunction induced by sepsis.
