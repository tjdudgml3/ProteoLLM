1
INTRODUCTION
Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a relatively common metabolic disease characterized by insulin secretion defects and peripheral insulin resistance, mainly involving skeletal muscle, adipose tissue, and liver.
1
Studies have shown that adipose tissue, liver, muscle, and pancreas were the inflammatory sites of T2DM, and the activation of inflammation and immune system was involved in the pathogenesis of T2DM.
2
Hepatic gluconeogenesis is an important part of hepatic glucose metabolism and plays a role in maintaining the homeostasis of glucose metabolism in the body, and insulin‐induced gluconeogenesis inhibition is impaired in T2DM.
3
,
4
Blood glucose control has been proven to be an important principle for improving T2DM and its complications. Although there are several clinically effective antidiabetic drugs for the treatment of T2DM, these antidiabetic drugs have certain limitations and defects.
5
,
6
Therefore, to explore the molecular mechanism of inhibiting liver gluconeogenesis is very important for the treatment of T2DM.
Sequence‐similar family 3 member D (FAM3D) is a new intestinal cytokine‐like protein, its secretion timing is contrary to that of glucagon. Whereas, it is still unclear about the character of FAM3D in metabolic disorders and its biological functions. A study showed that FAM3D inhibits glucagon secretion through mitogen‐activated protein kinase phosphatase 1 (MKP‐1, also known as dual specificity phosphatases 1, DUSP1)‐dependent extracellular‐regulated protein kinase 1/2 (ERK1/2) signaling pathway.
7
However, its function in diabetes needs further exploration. In animal models of diabetes and T2DM patients, macrophages can be observed to infiltrate tissues, and promote the expression of TNF‐α, IL‐6, and IL‐1β.
2
,
8
,
9
The Zinc finger protein 36 (ZFP36) is an RNA‐binding protein that can be rapidly induced by a variety of pro‐inflammatory stimuli such as IL‐1β.
10
,
11
It has been reported in the literature that the expression of ZFP36 was increased under the stimulation of IL‐1β, and overexpression of DUSP1 can down‐regulate the expression of ZFP36.
12
However, it has not been studied in diabetes. Many studies have shown that ZFP36 destroys the stability of target mRNA by combining with 3′ untranslated regions (3′UTR),
13
,
14
and the bone marrow specific deletion of ZFP36 stemmed insulin resistance and fatty liver in obese mice.
15
Therefore, we speculate that ZFP36 is related to T2DM.
Salt‐induced kinase 1 (SIK1) is a serine/threonine protein kinase, which serves to adenosine 5′‐monophosphate (AMP)‐activated protein kinase (AMPK).
16
It has been reported in breast cancer, SIK1 and SIK3 play a vital role in aerobic glycolysis and cell proliferation via p53/mTOR signaling pathways.
17
Studies have also found that phanginin A activates SIK1 to inhibit hepatic gluconeogenesis by increasing the activity of phosphodiesterases 4 and inhibiting the cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) signaling pathway.
18
According to RNA binding protein (RBP) map predictions, ZFP36 can be combined with SIK1, and this relationship has not been reported yet. Thus, we speculate that ZFP36 plays a role in diabetes by regulating the expression of SIK1.
In this study, we speculate that in an HG environment, FAM3D inhibits gluconeogenesis through the DUSP1/ZFP36/SIK1 axis and provides new diabetes treatment strategies.

MATERIALS AND METHODS
2.1
Cells culture and treatment
We purchased human hepatic carcinoma cell line HepG2 cells from the Cell Bank of Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China), and cultured in Dulbecco's modified eagle's medium (DMEM) with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco), 2 mM glutamine, penicillin, and 1% streptomycin, incubated at 37°C and 5% CO
2
.
In order to induce HepG2 cells in an HG environment, HepG2 cells were treated with 30 mM glucose for 24 h. We treated HG‐induced HepG2 cells with different doses of FAM3D (1000, 1500, and 2000 ng/ml; MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ) for 1 h, and then detected the relevant indicators. DUSP1 inhibitor BCI (8 μmol/L, MedChemExpress) was further used to explore the effect on gluconeogenesis.
2.2
Cell transfection
HepG2 cells were seeded in six‐well plates at 1 × 10
5
cells/well until 60%–70% confluent. According to the Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo) instructions, pc‐DNA3.1‐ZFP36 overexpression plasmids (OE‐ZFP36), OE‐DUSP1 and pc‐DNA3.1 (OE‐NC), and shRNA negative control (sh‐NC), ZFP36‐shRNA interference plasmids (sh‐ZFP36), and sh‐SIK1 were transfected into the cells, follow‐up experiments were performed after 48 h.
2.3
CCK
‐8 assay
According to the manufacture instruction, HepG2 cell viability in different HG treatments were detected by CCK‐8 assay kit (Abcam, Cambridge, MA). Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using micro‐plate reader (Thermo, Waltham, MA).
2.4
Glucose production assay
Glucose production assay was conducted by the colorimetric glucose assay kit (Catalog: K606‐100, BioVison). Briefly, after washing the cells, added non‐phenol red DMEM without glucose or glutamine, and incubated for 3 h with the addition of a gluconeogenic substrate (e.g., 2 mM sodium pyruvate and 20 mM sodium lactate). After treatment, the medium was analyzed for glucose and then normalized to total cellular protein.
19
2.5
Glucose‐6‐phosphatase and posphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase activity assay
Samples were collected from each group of cells. The activities of glucose‐6‐phosphatase (G6Pase) and posphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PEPCK) were determined by G6Pase assay kits (Sigma‐Aldrich) and PEPCK assay kits (Solarbio Life Sciences) following the manufacturers.
2.6
Quantitative reverse‐transcription polymerase chain reaction
We used the TRIzol® reagent (Takara, Tokyo, Japan) to extract total RNA and reversely transcribed into cDNA with PrimeScript reverse transcription (RT) kit. We performed quantitative real‐time polymerase chain reaction (RT‐qPCR) using TransStart Tip Green qPCR SuperMix (Transgen Biotech, Beijing, China) on an ABI 7500 system (Applied Biosystems, Carlsbad, CA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Ct values were normalized according to 2
−ΔΔCT
method. Table

2.7
Western blot
Total proteins were separated from cells cultured using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology) on ice. And the protein concentration was measured by the BCA protein assay (Beyotime). Sodium salt‐polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis was separated the proteins and then transferred to the polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Millipore, MA). At room temperature, blocked with 5% skimmed milk for 1 h, and the membranes were incubated by the primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. The following primary antibodies were used: anti‐PEPCK antibody (1:1000, #6924, Cell Signaling Technology), anti‐G6Pase antibody (1:1000, SAB1303137, Sigma‐Aldrich), anti‐phospho‐SIK1 antibody (1:1000, PA5‐105918, Invitrogen), anti‐SIK1 antibody (1:1000, ABE799, Sigma‐Aldrich), anti‐ZFP36 antibody (1:2000, Sigma‐Aldrich, T5327), and anti‐DUSP1 antibody (1:1000, sc‐373841, Santa Cruz). Washed three times and incubated with secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. After washing the membrane three times, the protein was developed with a chemiluminescence reagent, and the image was collected under the gel imaging system. ImageJ software was used to analyze the gray value of the protein band and calculate the protein expression.
2.8
RNA
immunoprecipitation assay
According to the manufacturer's instructions, use the EZ‐Magna RIP™ RNA Binding Protein Immunoprecipitation Kit (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) for RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) determination. Cells were lysed with RIP lysis buffer containing RNase inhibitors (Millipore) and protease inhibitors. Incubated 100 μL of whole cell extract with RIP buffer containing specific ZFP36 antibody‐conjugated magnetic beads. The expression of SIK1 was detected by RT‐qPCR.
2.9
Statistical analysis
We analyzed unpaired data using Student's t‐test and data comparisons between multiple groups by one‐way ANOVA by GraphPad version 5.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc, La Jolla, CA). All data are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three separate experiments. The difference was statistically significant at
p
< 0.05.

DISCUSSION
Hyperglycemia was an important clinical sign of T2DM, and the main culprit of many T2DM complications including cardiovascular disease, stroke, and cancer.
21
T2DM was a multifactorial metabolic disease characterized by defects in β‐cell and insulin function and abnormal liver glucose production.
22
Meanwhile, hyperglycemia was the most important factor resulting in the complications of T2DM.
23
The first condition for the treatment of this disease was to change the glucose and lipid metabolism disorders of the liver and other insulin‐sensitive tissues.
24
Therefore, exploring the mechanism of controlling hepatic gluconeogenesis is helpful for the treatment of diabetes mellitus. Here, an HG environment in vitro was simulated, and the potential molecular mechanism whether FAM3D inhibited gluconeogenesis through the DUSP1/ZFP36/SIK1 axis was explored.
As a member of a cytokine‐like family, FAM3D was previously proven to be associated with diabetes mellitus in pancreatic adenocarcinoma patients.
25
de Wit NJ et al. demonstrated that FAM3D was a gut‐derived protein secreted to the basolateral site of enterocytes in a nutritional status‐dependent manner.
26
Studies also revealed that FAM3D promoted liver adipogenesis and gluconeogenesis by inhibiting the activity of Akt and AMPK and activating the adipogenic pathway, and FAM3D could inhibit glucagon secretion.
27
An observational study evaluating the effect of glycemic levels in middle‐aged and elderly individuals with T2DM on salivary proteomics by liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) showed FAM3D a key candidate protein and one of the three proteins with the best differentiation, which combined with thioredoxin domain‐containing protein 17 (TXNDC17), zymogen granule protein 16B (ZG16B), and yielded an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.917 to distinguish good glycemic control and poor control.
28
In addition, FAM3D inhibited glucagon secretion via DUSP1‐dependent suppression of ERK1/2 signaling.
7
FAM3D inhibited glucagon secretion, ERK1/2 signaling, and induced DUSP1 expression; and knockdown of DUSP1 and inhibition of ERK1/2 abolished and potentiated the inhibitory effect of FAM3D on glucagon secretion, respectively. In Khadir A's study, DUSP1 upregulation is strongly linked to adiposity and physical exercise modulates its expression via mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway.
29
High DUSP1 protein expression was observed in obese subjects and correlated positively with various obesity indicators, such as glucagon, insulin. DUSP1 expression correlated with glucagon‐induced antagonization of MAP kinase phosphorylation by epidermal growth factor in hepatocytes.
30
Glucagon stimulates transient DUSP1 expression by activation of adenylate cyclase and PKA. PKG, Erk‐type MAP kinases and p38
MAPK
, Ca
2+
, PKC, as well as cell shrinkage, the generation of reactive oxygen intermediates, or tyrosine phosphatases are not involved in mediating the DUSP1 response to glucagon. The evidences above support our findings. In our study, the expression of DUSP1 was inhibited by the HG environment, while the expression and activity of gluconeogenesis‐related factors (PEPCK and G6Pase) and the production of glucose were promoted. Notably, FAM3D treatment promoted the expression of DUSP1, SIK1, and p‐SIK1, but inhibited the expression of ZFP36, PEPCK, and G6Pase, the activity of PEPCK and G6Pase, and the production of glucose under HG context. Meanwhile, the effect of FAM3D on gluconeogenesis was reversed by DUSP1 inhibitor BCI. Our results widely expanded the scope of FAM3D in gluconeogenesis development.
Though there were no researches on glucagon and ZFP36, the expression of ZFP36 can be down‐regulated by overexpression of DUSP1,
12
and the up‐regulated ZFP36 was proved to be related to lipid metabolism.
31
In our study, it was found that the expression of ZFP36 was up‐regulated in an HG environment. Overexpression of ZFP36 can reverse the inhibition of gluconeogenesis caused by FAM3D. Overexpression of DUSP1 could down‐regulate the expression of ZFP36 in an HG environment. Interestingly, after FAM3D treatment, overexpression of DUSP1 could promote the effect of FAM3D treatment on gluconeogenesis with the downregulated expression of ZFP36, PEPCK, and G6Pase, the decreased activity of PEPCK and G6Pase and the reduced production of glucose in our follow‐up experiment. Our result further revealed FAM3D modulated the DUSP1/ZFP36 axis to control gluconeogenesis.
SIK1 acts as a key modulator in regulating hepatic gluconeogenesis via cAMP signaling pathway.
18
In Patel K's study, LKB1‐SIK pathway functions as a key gluconeogenic gatekeeper in the liver and glucagon but not insulin promoted phosphorylation of SIK2.
32
The result revealed that liver‐specific ablation of SIK2 alone had no effect on gluconeogenesis and insulin did not modulate SIK2 phosphorylation or activity. In our study, ZFP36 was confirmed to bind with SIK1 by RIP assay and negatively regulated the expression of SIK1. The expression of SIK1 could be inhibited by HG, but promoted by ZFP36 knockdown. Gluconeogenesis was also suppressed by ZFP36 knockdown, which even exacerbated the effect of FAM3D treatment on gluconeogenesis. Meanwhile, knockdown of SIK1 could reverse the phenomenon of suppressing gluconeogenesis caused by ZFP36 knockdown. Our results further revealed that FAM3D may inhibit gluconeogenesis in regulation of DUSP1/ZFP36/SIK1 axis under an HG context.
There are still some limitations in our study. The current study focuses on the potential mechanism whether FAM3D could inhibit gluconeogenesis through DUSP1/ZFP36/SIK1 axis. The mechanism that FAM3D is affected by the inhibition of glucagon secretion is far more than enough. And there is a lack of corresponding in vivo experimental studies. FAM3D levels in human plasma samples with a postprandial elevation and a reduction after fasting in human plasma samples were also observed.
26
Significant differences in FAM3D expression were observed in human tissue distribution analysis, and were even greater when compared with FAM3D expression in mouse tissues.
26
FAM3 gene family, including FAM3A, FAM3B, FAM3C, and FAM3D, was proved promising therapeutical targets for non‐alcoholic fatty liver disease and type 2 diabetes.
27
,
33
Of course, the above will be our next steps and we will continue the corresponding research work in the future. Considering the results of our study and the evidences above, FAM3D may be a novel target for the treatment of T2DM and related metabolic disorders.
In summary, our findings demonstrated that FAM3D could inhibit gluconeogenesis through the DUSP1/ZFP36/SIK1 axis in an HG environment. FAM3D downregulated ZFP36 expression and inhibited gluconeogenesis by up‐regulated the expression of DUSP1 in an HG environment. At the same time, the down‐regulation of ZFP36 inhibited gluconeogenesis by promoting the expression of SIK1. The current study provides a new theoretical basis for exploring the pathogenesis and treatment strategy of T2DM.
5
CONCLUSION
For the first time, our study demonstrated that FAM3D restrained gluconeogenesis through the DUSP1/ZFP36/SIK1 axis in an HG environment, which could provide a new theoretical basis for exploring the pathogenesis and treatment strategy of T2DM.
