INTRODUCTION
The inflammation process plays a key role during disease development, including arthritis, cardiovascular disease, and cancer, and is often at the center of multiple investigations. As a counter reaction to inflammation, an immune response triggers mast cells to release increased amounts of histamine, tryptases, and eicosanoids, which are implicated in the heat and swelling.
1
,
2
Eicosanoids, in particular prostanoids, are of interest due to their pro‐inflammatory properties and increased presence in inflamed tissue. Prostanoids are derived from arachidonic acid after its release from the sn‐2 position of membrane phospholipids through the action of phospholipase A
2
. Prostaglandin G/H synthases (PGHS), often referred to as cyclooxygenase (COX), further catalyze the reaction towards the intermediate prostaglandin H
2
.
3
,
4
This intermediate prostaglandin is then, depending on the activity of the associated enzyme, converted into one of the active prostanoid end products (Figure
S1
): prostaglandin D
2
(PGD
2
), prostaglandin E
2
(PGE
2
), prostaglandin F
2α
(PGF
2α
), prostaglandin I
2
, or thromboxane A
2
.
5
,
6
This COX pathway can be separated into two different pathways, COX‐1 and COX‐2, due to the existence of different isoforms of PGHS. The end products of COX‐1 and COX‐2 have a similar structure but play a significantly different role in biology. With taking exceptions into account, one could state that in general the COX‐1 pathway is responsible for prostaglandin synthesis while COX‐2 is involved in prostaglandin production due to inflammatory response.
3
In bioanalytical and biomedical studies, this distinction between COX‐1 and COX‐2 is crucial.
Conventional liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC/MS/MS) profiling platforms have been developed for (high‐throughput) screening of eicosanoids in biological samples.
7
,
8
,
9
Most of these platforms require time‐consuming sample preparation steps and chromatographic separation for isomeric compounds (e.g. PGE
2
and PGD
2
). In addition, the free carboxylic acid groups of the eicosanoids offer very poor ionization efficiency in negative ionization mode.
10
Therefore, derivatization strategies of eicosanoids have been employed successfully to increase ionization efficiency for analysis in positive ionization mode.
11
Some of these derivatization reagents, such as Girard's reagent T (GT), target the carbonyl group present in prostanoids. GT has been previously used to derivatize both endogenous
12
,
13
,
14
and exogenous
15
,
16
compounds. The analysis of choice for eicosanoid screening requires efficient ionization, high sensitivity, and selectivity in complex biological samples, combined with powerful stereoisomeric separation. The latter is demanded due to the substantial impact of stereoisomerism on the biological activity of eicosanoids. For this reason, chromatography is often chosen for eicosanoid analysis.
17
,
18
As an alternative to chromatographic separation approaches, ion mobility spectrometry (IMS) offers a rapid separation technology in which ions are electrically driven through a buffer gas.
19
,
20
IMS has shown its potential for isomeric separation while removing background from complex biological samples.
21
There are several different IMS technologies available: drift tube IMS (DTIMS),
22
,
23
traveling wave IMS (TWIMS),
24
,
25
trapped IMS (TIMS),
26
,
27
and high‐field asymmetric waveform IMS (FAIMS; also known as differential IMS or DMS).
28
IMS is already widely used in the field of proteomics,
29
,
30
lipidomics,
31
and metabolomics.
32
,
33
Dwivedi and coworkers presented a screening method for a wide range of metabolites (including eicosanoids) using DTIMS.
32
This work proved to separate isobaric eicosanoids but still lacks sufficient ion mobility resolving power for isomeric separation (e.g. PGE
2
and PGD
2
). Higher ion mobility resolving power is necessary for the separation of structural eicosanoid isomers. Among different types of IMS, three IMS techniques have shown high ion mobility resolving power: cyclic IMS (
R
< 300), TIMS (
R
< 300), and FAIMS (
R
< 500).
34
Cyclic IMS (cIMS) is based on the TWIMS principle
24
but prolongs the path of the IMS cell by a loop configuration.
35
Ions can pass this IMS loop multiple times and, therefore, increase their total path length. The amount of passes can be adapted to the required separation. Researchers have recently investigated cIMS for polymer analysis,
36
RNA modifications,
37
and carbohydrates.
38
TIMS applies a reversed principle compared to cIMS. TIMS uses a buffer gas to introduce ions to the TIMS cell. These ions are trapped by applying a counteracting electric field while the buffer gas moves in the exit direction.
39
The ion trapping time is 10 ms up to 1 s dependent on the necessary separation power.
26
Several TIMS applications have been reported for proteins,
40
peptides,
41
and small molecules.
42
,
43
,

,
45
FAIMS
28
,
46
,
47
applies an asymmetric waveform (high and low electric fields) across two parallel plates to which gas‐phase ions are introduced by a carrier gas.
34
This waveform comprises short high‐field positive and long low‐field negative segments that integrate to zero over a period. Since the ion mobility (
K
) is different at
E
low
and
E
high
, an ion will tend towards one of the two plates.
48
A compensation voltage (CV) is superposed on the waveform to correct the ion's trajectory and to prevent collision with one of the plates (deionization). Alteration of this CV allows selection of ions, with specific ion mobility differentials, to be transferred to a mass spectrometer. Scanning the CV is often expressed by a compensation field (
E
c
) due to different plate distances used between FAIMS devices. FAIMS research has focused on protein analysis,
49
,
50
,
51
,
52
,
53
though applications can be found for analysis of glycans,
54
lipids,
55
,
56
,
57
and metabolites.
58
Advantages of cIMS, TIMS, and FAIMS are not only the isomeric separation power but also their ability to significantly reduce chemical noise from complex samples, which is of particular importance when analyzing low‐level endogenous eicosanoids. To our knowledge, only limited research has been published applying high‐resolution IMS to separate isomeric eicosanoids without incorporation of LC.
59
In the work reported here, we aimed to introduce optimal strategies to detect and separate prostanoids in complex biological samples. GT was utilized for derivatization of prostanoids and their isomers. The impact of GT derivatization on sensitivity by improving the ionization efficiency and reducing the ion suppression from the sample matrix was investigated. In addition, the influence of GT on ion mobility separation of stereoisomers was studied by means of three IMS systems, namely: linear cIMS, linear TIMS, and nonlinear FAIMS. We derivatized mini pig intestine extracts with GT and investigated the detection and separation of endogenous prostanoids to examine the applicability of the introduced strategies.

MATERIALS AND METHODS
2.1
Chemicals and reagents
HPLC‐grade water and HPLC‐grade methanol (MeOH) were purchased from Fisher Chemical (Fair Lawn, NJ, USA). Reagent‐grade (>95%) formic acid and GT were acquired from Sigma‐Aldrich (St Louis, MO, USA). All prostanoid standards were purchased from Cayman Chemical (Ann Arbor, MI, USA) and prepared to a 20 μM mixture in MeOH. Identification and FAIMS peak assignment was executed by direct infusion of individual standards at the same concentration (e.g. 20 μM in MeOH). Mini pig intestine tissue was provided by Janssen Pharmaceutica (Beerse, Belgium). Titan3, 17 mm filters with a pore size of 0.45 μm were purchased from Thermo Fisher (Rockwood, TN, USA).
2.2
GT derivatization
For the derivatization of prostanoids with GT, a GT stock solution was prepared at 17.5 mg/mL in MeOH–H
2
O–formic acid (79.9/19.9/0.2 v/v/v). The derivatized prostanoids were obtained by adding 500 μL of the 20 μM prostanoid mixture to 100 μL of the GT stock solution and 400 μL of MeOH. This mixture was incubated at room temperature for 5 min and resulted in 10 μM GT‐derivatized prostanoids in MeOH–H
2
O–formic acid (92/7.9/0.1 v/v/v). GT‐derivatized intestine tissue extracts were prepared by adding 50 μL of tissue extract with 5 μL of GT stock solution. Incubation at room temperature was executed for 5 min to obtain GT‐derivatized endogenous prostanoids in MeOH–H
2
O–formic acid (92/7.9/0.1 v/v/v).
2.3
Tissue extract preparation
Frozen mini pig intestine was received from Janssen R&D (Beerse, Belgium) and stored at −80°C. Eight, 500 μm thick sections were taken from the intestine sample at −21°C using a microtome cryostat (Thermo Scientific, Braunschweig, Germany) and collected in a 1.5 mL Eppendorf tube. For the prostanoid extraction, 500 μL of MeOH was added to the tube and three repeat freeze–thaw cycles (10 min on dry‐ice followed by 10 min sonication) were executed. After extraction, this sample was centrifuged (Eppendorf, 13 000 rcf, 10 min) to separate the supernatant from the lysate. Supernatant was collected in a clean 1.5 mL Eppendorf tube and stored at −80°C. Prior to the analysis, the thawed sample was centrifuged for 5 min and filtered over a 0.45 μm filter to prevent needle clogging during the nano‐ESI analysis.
2.4
Direct infusion MS
Directly infused prostanoids were concentrated to 1 μM in a MeOH–water solution (80/20 v/v). For analysis in positive ionization mode, 0.2% formic acid and 10 mg/mL GT was added to the prostanoid standard mixture. This mixture was incubated at room temperature for 5 min. Direct infusion experiments were performed using a high‐mass‐resolution (140 000 at
m
/
z
200) Q‐Exactive™ orbitrap mass spectrometer equipped with a heated ESI source (HESI‐II probe, Thermo Scientific). Negative and positive ions were generated at a 5 μL/min flow rate while applying a capillary voltage of 5.0 and 4.0 kV, respectively. The mass spectrometer was operated in normal MS mode and MS/MS spectra were acquired using collision‐induced dissociation (CID) fragmentation. The normalized collision energy was in the range 35–40 arbitrary units depending on the compound fragmented. Data were acquired at a mass range of
m
/
z
50–750 and recorded using Thermo Xcalibur Roadmap (Thermo Scientific, version 2.8).
2.5
High‐resolution IMS/MS experiments
Cyclic IMS
. Samples were infused at a 3 μL/min flow rate into a prototype cIMS/MS instrument based on a modified SYNAPT G2‐Si instrument (Waters Corporation, UK) after positive nano‐ESI. The cIMS separator consists of a multifunction array of electrodes forming an orthogonal closed loop. The cIMS chamber has a path length of 98 cm with a single‐pass resolving power of
ca

m
/
z
50–1000. Data were acquired and processed using a custom MassLynx data acquisition with new interactive instrument control software (Waters Corporation, UK).
TIMS
. Samples were introduced at a 3 μL/min flow rate into a timsTOF instrument (Bruker Daltonics, USA) and ionized using positive ESI. General ESI parameters were: capillary voltage of 3.5 kV, N
2
pressure of 0.8 bar (11.6 psi), drying gas flow rate of 4.0 L/min, and a drying temperature of 200°C. Voltages were applied at the entrance and exit of the TIMS tunnel, 255 and 55 V, respectively. The

m
/

FAIMS
. Nano‐ESI‐FAIMS/MS experiments were performed with a custom‐made FAIMS device
48
connected to an LTQ XL™ linear ion trap mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) equipped with an ion funnel (Heartland Mobility).
60
Negative and positive nano‐ESI occurred by infusion of the sample at a flow rate of 300 nL/min and a capillary voltage of 3.8 and 5 kV, respectively. FAIMS separation was carried out by two parallel plates distanced 1.88 mm apart, with a total path length of 4 cm. A bisinusoidal asymmetric waveform was created by an RF generator (Heartland Mobility) with a peak dispersion voltage of 4 kV. Carrier gas flow was produced and maintained by two digital flowmeters (MKS Instruments, Andover, MA, USA) in a He–N
2
mixture (60/40) at a rate of 1.4–2 L/min. The CV was scanned at a rate of 0.33–1 V/min for both positive and negative ionization modes, from 178 to 158 V and −210 to −190 V, respectively. Subsequent to the FAIMS device, an ion funnel technology (Heartland Mobility) allowed transmitting and focusing of the ions generated for maximized sensitivity. Data were acquired at a mass range of
m
/
z
100–600 and recorded by Thermo Xcalibur Roadmap (Thermo Scientific, version 2.2).

Multiple ion mobility peaks per isomer could be explained by their ability to form more than one three‐dimensional conformation. FAIMS spectra consistently showed more and baseline‐separated peaks throughout the spectra. This could be because the separation in FAIMS is based on nonlinear IMS while TIMS and cIMS are based on linear IMS. The latter two techniques operate at low electric fields leading to a linear relationship between the ion velocity and the electric field applied. FAIMS rapidly switches between a low and high electric field in an asymmetric manner which results in a nonlinear relationship between the velocity and the electric field. Nonlinear IMS is much more orthogonal to mass spectrometry than linear IMS hence the improved separation.
72
Therefore, the FAIMS spectra show a much larger number of peaks after infusion of the same isomeric standard mixture. Another significant difference of the FAIMS from TIMS and cIMS spectra is the peak intensity variations due to environmental conditions. Both cIMS and TIMS are performed under vacuum conditions while FAIMS operates at atmospheric pressure. Although FAIMS provides the largest resolving power (partly due to atmospheric conditions), this atmospheric environment leads to a significant loss in ion transmission. When comparing TIMS and cIMS, minor variations in isomeric separation (e.g. number of ion mobility peaks per isomer) could be observed due to the “reversed” mechanism of both IMS techniques. In cIMS, the electric field transports ions through the buffer gas while TIMS uses the electric field to trap ions as a carrier gas transports them through the IMS cell. This “reversed” principle between cIMS and TIMS explains that an ion's path length is generated differently by optimizing gas velocity and time. A longer path in cIMS is established by more passes and, as a result, a physically longer path (50–120 ms arrival time). In TIMS, a longer path is accomplished by a longer trapping time (450 ms trapping time). The IMS separation time scale should be considered when comparing cIMS and TIMS. In FAIMS, this is in the range of minutes (>20 min).

Not only is the identification of endogenous prostanoids of great biological relevance but also the quantification of these molecules could provide support in clinical and biomedical research. Due to the fast nature of our direct infusion IMS approach, the addition of isotope‐labelled prostanoids and/or isotope‐labelled analogues of GT could be used in the quantitative investigation of cIMS and TIMS detection for endogenous prostanoid analysis. For FAIMS, this approach would require the use of chromatographic separation prior to IMS detection due its ambient nature. CONCLUSIONS
This work demonstrates that high‐resolution IMS is a very powerful approach for the separation and identification of prostanoids present in complex matrices like biological tissues. Chemical derivatization with GT significantly improved the ionization efficiency of both standards and endogenous prostanoids, and thus improved sensitivity for their detection in tissue samples. The increase in signal intensity in the mass spectra by GT derivatization demonstrates the significant improvement in ionization despite the possible occurrence of ion suppression in the tissue samples. The comparison between cIMS, TIMS, and FAIMS revealed the strengths and weaknesses of all three IMS techniques in which a clear distinction is observed between linear and nonlinear IMS. Linear IMS provides the best sensitivity for endogenous analysis while nonlinear IMS delivers the highest IMS resolution. The combination of significantly improved ionization efficiency and isomeric separation power by high‐resolution IMS after GT derivatization confirms its applicability for low‐level prostanoid analysis without the need of expensive and time‐consuming sample cleanup. Our derivatization strategy offers high sensitivity and excellent stereoisomeric separation for screening of complex biological systems. High‐resolution IMS, in combination with other orthogonal analytical techniques, could further unravel the role of isomeric prostanoids in biology and disease.
