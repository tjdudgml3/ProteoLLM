Introduction
Chloroplasts are the prototype of all plastids and the trademark of algae and plants. They are best known for their role in photosynthesis, but they are also the sites of synthesis of many essential compounds, including amino acids, nucleotides, lipids, and phytohormones (
Chen et al., 2018
). Therefore, they are essential for plant development (
Pogson et al., 2015
;
Liebers et al., 2022
) and for sensing environmental stimuli (
Dopp et al., 2021
;
Kleine et al., 2021
;
Schwenkert et al., 2022
). Chloroplast biogenesis is a complex process that differs between monocotyledonous and dicotyledonous plants, developmental stages, organs, and plant tissues and is not fully understood (
Pogson et al., 2015
). In dicotyledons, such as
Arabidopsis thaliana
, chloroplasts develop in vegetative leaves from proplastids in the shoot apical meristem and in cotyledons from proplastids/eoplasts found in mesophyll tissue within the embryo or, when germinated in the dark, in etioplasts that develop from eoplasts (
Liebers et al., 2017
). In all cases, the development of photosynthetically competent chloroplasts in angiosperm seedlings is strictly dependent upon exposure to light (
Liebers et al., 2017
) and involves massive changes in the nuclear transcriptome (
Jarvis and Lopez-Juez, 2013
). As descendants of cyanobacterium-like progenitors, plastids have retained a reduced genome comprised of ∼100 genes (
Kleine et al., 2009a
), while approximately 3000 plastid proteins are encoded in the nucleus (
Christian et al., 2020
). Consequently, chloroplast development is further complicated by the need to coordinate the roles of plastid and nuclear genomes. Because most plastid proteins are encoded in the nucleus, it exercises anterograde control over the plastids (
Jan et al., 2022
). Conversely, chloroplasts signal their developmental and physiological status to the nucleus via a process called “retrograde signaling” to appropriately regulate nuclear gene expression (
Kleine and Leister, 2016
;
Dietz et al., 2019
;
Li and Kim, 2022
;
Liebers et al., 2022
).
Pogson et al. (2008)
coined the terms “biogenic” and “operational” signaling to refer to signals emitted by plastids during early steps of chloroplast biogenesis to ensure completion of the process safely and by fully active chloroplasts to adjust operation of the organelle in response to fluctuating environmental conditions, respectively.
The screen for
gun
(
genomes uncoupled
) mutants carried out by
Susek et al. (1993)
can be regarded as the starter’s gun for efforts to decipher the functions of retrograde signaling. Today, nearly 30 years later, the topic continues to generate a high level of interest, as exemplified by the large number of recent reviews (e.g.,
Dietz et al., 2019
;
Mielecki et al., 2020
;
Calderon and Strand, 2021
;
Dopp et al., 2021
;
Jan et al., 2022
;
Kleine et al., 2021
;
Wu and Bock, 2021
;
Li and Kim, 2022
;
Liebers et al., 2022
). Until now, considerable progress has been made in identification and elucidation of different classes of signals, including those originating from plastid gene expression, reactive oxygen species, the redox state, metabolites like 3′-phosphoadenosine 5′-phosphate, 2-C-methyl-d-erythritol 2,4-cyclodiphosphate, β-cyclocitral, and other (apo)carotenoids, which is excellently reviewed elsewhere (
Chan et al., 2016
;
Kleine and Leister, 2016
;
de Souza et al., 2017
;
Crawford et al., 2018
;
Li and Kim, 2022
). However, despite highly active research, the mechanisms of GUN-type signaling have remained opaque. GUN1 has aroused particular interest because its precise molecular function is still unknown. With the advent of novel technologies, new concepts have been proposed recently, but GUN1’s function remains a matter of intense debate (e.g.,
Sun et al., 2016
;
Tadini et al., 2016
;
Paieri et al., 2018
;
Wu et al., 2018
;
Marino et al., 2019
;
Pesaresi and Kim, 2019
;
Shimizu et al., 2019
;
Wu et al., 2019
;
Zhao et al., 2019
;
Tadini et al., 2020
;
Honkanen and Small, 2022
).
The background of the original
gun
mutant screen
The screen for
gun
mutants demonstrated nearly three decades ago that signaling from chloroplasts to the nucleus is genetically tractable (
Susek et al., 1993
). The screen was based on the observation that application of certain chemicals, such as the photobleaching herbicide Norflurazon (NF), which inhibits chloroplast carotenoid biosynthesis, and inhibitors of organellar protein synthesis, like lincomycin (LIN), results in reduced levels of transcripts derived from so-called photosynthesis-associated nuclear genes (PhANGs), especially the marker gene
LHCB
, which codes for light-harvesting chlorophyll a/b-binding proteins of photosystem (PS) II (
Figure 1
;
Oelmuller et al., 1986
). A mutant qualifies as a
gun
mutant when seedlings continue to express PhANGs even though plastid development has been blocked by NF. In the original screen, five mutants (
gun1
–
gun5
) were isolated (
Susek et al., 1993
), and it was later shown that overexpression of plastid ferrochelatase 1 (FC1) in
gun6-1D
also results in a
gun
phenotype (
Woodson et al., 2011
;
Table 1
). The
gun1
mutant is special in that it behaves as a
gun
mutant when grown on either NF or LIN (
Koussevitzky et al., 2007
), while
gun2
–
gun5
do so only in the presence of NF, and, to the best of our knowledge,
gun6
’s PhANG expression behavior on LIN has not been investigated so far. Furthermore, the PhANG
gun
phenotype can only be observed in seedlings; therefore, initially, most studies primarily concentrated on biogenic signaling in
gun
mutants.
Figure 1
The plastid-localized tetrapyrrole (tetpy) biosynthetic pathway.
GUN-related enzymes of the tetpy pathway and the subplastidial localization of the two isoforms of protoporphyrinogen oxidase (PPO) and ferrochelatase (FC) are shown. In the tetpy pathway, glutamate is converted into 5-aminolevulinic acid at the rate-limiting step. Eight 5-aminolevulinic acid molecules are required to form porphyrins. Protoporphyrin IX is the substrate for the two metal-chelating chelatases, Mg chelatase (MgCh) and FC, at the branching point between the pathways leading to chlorophyll and heme synthesis, respectively. The MgCh complex consists of the subunits CHLH/GUN5, CHLI1, CHLI2, and CHLD. GUN4 is a regulator of MgCh. The isoforms PPO1 and FC2 presumably provide the heme used in plastids. Heme is further converted into phytochromobilin by the heme oxygenases HO1/GUN2, HO3, and HO4 and phytochromobilin synthase (HY2/GUN3). PPO2 and FC1/GUN6 are thought to produce the heme required for heme-dependent proteins outside of the plastids; furthermore, the heme pool is postulated to act as a retrograde signal. A heme transporter is assumed to export heme into the cytoplasm. It is of note that, in
Arabidopsis
, mitochondrial localization of PPO and FC can be excluded (see main text). Therefore, here heme is exclusively produced in chloroplasts.

Heme as a signal?
The loci
GUN2
–
GUN6
encode enzymes and regulators of tetrapyrrole (tetpy) biosynthesis, which is essential for production of heme, chlorophyll, and other metabolites (
Terry and Smith, 2013
;
Figure 1
). In the context of chloroplast-nucleus signaling, these observations suggest that perturbations of tetpy biosynthesis might result in accumulation of an intermediate that leaves the chloroplast and modulates nuclear gene expression. In fact, Mg protoporphyrin IX has been proposed as such a signaling molecule, but the critical experiments on which this hypothesis is based could not be corroborated (
Mochizuki et al., 2008
;
Moulin et al., 2008
). Subsequently, heme was suggested as an alternative tetpy with signaling function originating from the chloroplast (
Woodson et al., 2011
). More recently, it has been suggested that a specific heme pool or a heme metabolite generated by flux through FC1 serves as a positive signal to promote the expression of genes required for chloroplast development (
Terry and Smith, 2013
). Similarly, heme is proposed to act as a retrograde signal in algae (
von Gromoff et al., 2008
), and plastid-produced bilins (heme-derived metabolites) have been reported to be essential for light-dependent greening and phototrophic growth in
Chlamydomonas reinhardtii
(
Duanmu et al., 2013
).
Interestingly, GUN1 has been reported to interact with proteins involved in tetpy biosynthesis (
Tadini et al., 2016
) and seems to directly bind tetpys, including heme (
Shimizu et al., 2019
). Indeed,
Shimizu et al. (2019)
found that GUN1 reduces flux through the tetpy biosynthesis pathway, thus limiting heme and protochlorophyllide synthesis, and these authors therefore proposed that the signaling role of GUN1 may be mediated by changes in tetpy metabolism. An earlier proposal suggested that a specific heme pool generated by flux through FC1 functions as a positive signal and that GUN1 restricts production of the heme signal (
Terry and Smith, 2013
), implying that heme would be the positive signal in a
gun1
mutant. However, the model of heme as a positive signal is not final because
Shimizu et al. (2019)
also showed that GUN1 enhances FC1 activity, which would be incompatible with assumed higher (local) heme levels in a
gun1
mutant.
Although direct experimental proof for heme’s involvement in retrograde signaling in plants is still missing, and it has to be noted that not all FC1-overexpressing lines behave like
gun
mutants (
Page et al., 2020
), heme currently seems to be the most promising retrograde-signaling candidate among the proposed tetpys and will be discussed in more detail in the following section.
Heme synthesis and its export to the cytoplasm for use by heme-dependent enzymes and intracellular signaling
While the tetpy products chlorophyll and siroheme are restricted to the plastids, phytochromobilin and heme are, respectively, required for photomorphogenesis via the photoreceptor phytochrome and insertion into heme-dependent proteins in all cellular compartments within and outside of plastids (
Wang et al., 2022
).
In particular, the existence of a heme synthesis pathway in the mitochondria of animals and yeast, as well as the requirement for heme in several protein complexes found in the respiratory electron transport chain of plant mitochondria, initially prompted searches for heme synthesis activity in these organelles. Indeed, multiple reports suggested that the last two enzymes of heme synthesis, protoporphyrinogen oxidase (PPO) and FC, are translocated into plant mitochondria (e.g.,
Jacobs et al., 1982
;
Smith, 1988
). Aside from that, in all higher plants, each of these two enzymes occurs in two quite distinct isoforms, which are encoded by different genes. Moreover, FC2 contains a C-terminal extension, which resembles the hydrophobic membrane-spanning α helix of Chl
a
/
b
binding proteins (
Sobotka et al., 2011
). These structural features reinforced earlier assumptions that the heme-synthesizing pathway branch is distributed between plastids and mitochondria in plants (
Lermontova et al., 1997
;
Papenbrock et al., 2001
;
Suzuki et al., 2002
;
Masuda et al., 2003
).
However, more recent studies suggested that
A. thaliana
FC is exclusively located within the plastids (
Fan et al., 2022
). The previous allocation of FC-mediated heme synthesis to plant mitochondria might be explained by technical limitations in purification of these organelles.
Fan et al. (2022)
present additional evidence showing that the two
Arabidopsis
FC isoforms are localized in different sub-compartments of chloroplasts. FC2, the dominant form, is localized in the thylakoid membrane and serves the need for heme-dependent proteins in plastids. FC1 is found in the stroma and, in lower amounts, in the thylakoids and, importantly, envelope membranes (
Fan et al., 2022
). Moreover, it is known that FC1 is expressed at higher levels in non-photosynthetic tissues and that
FC1
is induced in response to multiple abiotic stresses, when chloroplast functions are basically compromised (
Singh et al., 2002
;
Nagai et al., 2007
;
Scharfenberg et al., 2015
;
Zhao et al., 2017
). These findings favor the idea that FC1 is dedicated to the synthesis of heme for use in heme-dependent proteins in all cellular compartments except plastids, which would also account for its localization to the envelope membrane. The fraction of heme allocated to the cytoplasm most probably performs functions required for stress defense and intracellular signaling during acclimation to stress conditions.
But how does heme actually leave the chloroplast? It is obvious that allocation of FC1-synthesized heme to the cytoplasm implies its transport across the envelope membranes. However, the precise mechanisms involved are not well understood, and one innovative theory posits that heme mobilization is mediated by nucleic acids and membrane contact sites (
Chambers et al., 2021
). Other findings support the idea that heme is transported by members of the ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporter family. Indeed, in vertebrates, yeast, and worms, several candidate heme importers and exporters have been proposed. In yeast and mammals, members of the ABC transporter family have been proposed to be involved in intracellular heme transport (
Hamza, 2006
;
Krishnamurthy and Schuetz, 2006
). ABCB6, ABCG2, and ABCB10 have been extensively investigated in mammalian systems (
Krishnamurthy et al., 2004
;
Fukuda et al., 2016
;
Seguin et al., 2017
). Moreover, proteome analysis of plastid envelope membranes has revealed the presence of ABC transporters (
Bouchnak et al., 2019
;
Trentmann et al., 2020
). But no obvious plant homolog of known heme transporters has been detected so far, largely because of the structural diversity of this protein family (
Verrier et al., 2008
). Whether single proteins (perhaps acting as an oligomer) or hetero-oligomeric complexes are required for heme transport remains open. Because candidates belonging to the ABC transporter family have been found in a complex consisting of mammalian FC dimers (
Maio et al., 2019
), it is not unlikely that plant FC1 is also found in close proximity to heme transporters. Protein-protein interaction studies could contribute to elucidation of new candidates of FC1-interacting heme-binding proteins, such as
Arabidopsis
ABC transporters. However, heme transporters are expected to be present in low abundance, and it cannot be excluded that investigations of specific stress conditions, developmental stages, or times of the day will be necessary for identification of heme-binding transporter proteins.
While the function of heme (e.g., in the binding of gases and in redox reactions) by heme-requiring proteins has been intensively explored, open questions remain with respect to how heme acts as a potential intracellular signaling factor and what other proteins contribute to heme-mediated intracellular signal transduction. The identification of the
gun6
mutant (see above;
Page et al., 2020
;
Woodson et al., 2011
) sheds some light on this process. Because FC2 overproducers do not exhibit the specific
gun
phenotype (
Woodson et al., 2011
), these data are consistent with the different localization of the two FC isoforms in plastids. These findings substantiate several suggestions for heme being involved as signal molecule (see above;
Page et al., 2020
;
Shimizu et al., 2019
;
Terry and Smith, 2013
;
Wang et al., 2022
;
Woodson et al., 2011
). It can be interpreted that elevated levels of heme synthesized by overproduced FC1 can continue a modulated signal transmission when heme is transferred into the cytoplasm. It has been suggested that only free heme (designated “labile heme” in some publications) is responsible for this regulatory function (
Terry and Smith, 2013
). Thus, to trace the flow of labile heme during retrograde signaling, quantification of free heme in subcellular compartments would be of the utmost importance. As one possible approach to evaluation of free heme in yeast and mammals, a heme sensor designed by
Hanna et al. (2016)
has been successfully employed to determine amounts of free heme. It might be challenging, but also enlightening, to apply this powerful approach to plant systems. Other methods for detection of proteins, their modifications, and metabolites implicated in retrograde signaling, together with their spatial distribution, are discussed in the last section of this review.
Does GUN1 play a role in the editing of chloroplast transcripts?
The
gun1
mutant differs from the other classic
gun
mutants not only in its unique response to LIN treatment but also has no obvious connection with tetpy biosynthesis. In fact, its molecular function is still unknown. Strikingly, however, GUN1 is an ancient protein that evolved within the streptophyte clade of the algal ancestors of land plants before the first plants colonized land more than 470 million years ago. It has been suggested that GUN1’s primary role has to do with chloroplast gene expression and that its involvement in retrograde signaling probably evolved more recently (
Honkanen and Small, 2022
). Indeed, GUN1 contains two domains known to interact with nucleic acids: a pentatricopeptide repeat (PPR) and a MutS-related domain (
Koussevitzky et al., 2007
). Although GUN1 was found to interact with DNA
in vitro
(
Koussevitzky et al., 2007
), no such function has been detected for it to date. The PPR domain, on the other hand, points to an RNA-binding function of GUN1, and alterations in the efficiency of plastid-RNA editing (slight increases and decreases, depending on the target) have been reported in NF-treated
gun1
mutants; hence, GUN1 might regulate plastid RNA editing during retrograde signaling (
Zhao et al., 2019
). However, in this scenario, GUN1 does not directly bind to an RNA target but interacts with the protein MULTIPLE ORGANELLAR RNA EDITING FACTOR 2 (MORF2). Overexpression of MORF2 alters RNA editing and results in a
gun
phenotype, but only in seedlings treated with NF and not LIN, and has less of a phenotypic effect than that of
gun1–
9
(
Zhao et al., 2019
). Interestingly, the overexpressor of MORF2 (
MORF2OX
) was tested for a
gun
phenotype and not a
morf2
mutant because the
morf2-1
mutant is completely devoid of chlorophylls (
Takenaka et al., 2012
), making selection of
morf2
homozygotes under NF treatment impossible (
Zhao et al., 2019
). In the
morf2
mutant, editing of nearly all known sites is reduced (including
rpoB
), while some sites, such as
ndhF-290
and
psbZ-50
, are not edited at all (
Takenaka et al., 2012
). Therefore, it comes as a surprise that editing of these same targets is also reduced in
MORF2OX
seedlings. Moreover, they include the six sites (
ndhF-290
,
psbZ-50
,
rpoC1-488
, and
rpoB-338
/
551
/
2432
) where
Zhao et al. (2019)
found editing to be decreased in
MORF2OX
and
gun1–
9
seedlings under NF treatment. Editing of these sites was either suppressed more strongly or at least to the same level in
MORF2OX
than in
gun1–
9
, which would suggest a stronger
gun
phenotype for
MORF2OX
in the event that reduced editing of these sites under NF was indeed the cause of the
gun
phenotype. Clearly, the role of GUN1 in editing and its contribution to GUN signaling have not yet been satisfactorily resolved.
Is GUN1 involved in chloroplast protein homeostasis?
Remarkably, GUN1 has been shown to control the level of PLASTID RIBOSOMAL PROTEIN S1 (PRPS1), at least in the
Arabidopsis gun1 prps1-1
mutant (
Tadini et al., 2016
). However, GUN1 seems not to be involved in (PRPS1-mediated) heat-responsive signaling (
Tadini et al., 2016
;
Llamas et al., 2017
). Genetically, loss of GUN1 enhances, or in some cases (e.g.,
prprs1
) suppresses, the phenotypes of various other mutants with defects in plastid gene expression or protein homeostasis. Such mutations result in defects in transcription (RpoTp;
Tadini et al., 2020
), plastid transcript processing (mitochondrial transcription termination factor mTERF4, RESISTANT TO INHIBITION WITH FSM 10 [RIF10];
Llamas et al., 2017
;
Sun et al., 2016
), plastid ribosomal proteins (PRPS17, PRPL11, and PRPL24;
Llamas et al., 2017
;
Paieri et al., 2018
;
Tadini et al., 2016
), the translation elongation factor fu-gaeri1 (FUG1;
Marino et al., 2019
), chloroplast proteases/chaperones (FTSH2, FTSH5, caseinolytic proteases CLPC1, and CLPR1;
Llamas et al., 2017
;
Marino et al., 2019
;
Tadini et al., 2020
), and cpHSC70-1 (
Wu et al., 2019
;
Tadini et al., 2020
). The additive effects of introducing
gun1
into these mutants could be rationalized by assuming that GUN1 interacts with the nucleus-encoded plastid RNA polymerase (RpoTp) and promotes nucleus-encoded plastid RNA polymerase-dependent transcript accumulation in response to perturbation of chloroplast protein homeostasis (
Tadini et al., 2020
). In addition to interacting with enzymes of tetpy biosynthesis (see above;
Tadini et al., 2016
), GUN1 also associates with several chloroplast proteins involved in proteostasis and protein import (
Tadini et al., 2016
;
Wu et al., 2019
;
Li et al., 2021
), which makes it a candidate for participation in the chloroplast-specific unfolded protein response (cpUPR; see next section). However,
gun1
plants show a virtually wild-type (WT) profile of chaperone gene expression following LIN treatment (
Llamas et al., 2017
). Hence, GUN1 does not participate in regulation of the cpUPR response but more likely acts as an integrator of chloroplast protein quality control.
Chloroplast protein homeostasis and the UPR
Under homeostatic conditions, proteins in membrane-confined compartments are in the native, folded state. But protein homeostasis (proteostasis) can be disturbed by a plethora of triggers, including environmental changes. Unless corrected promptly, such alterations lead to accumulation of unfolded/misassembled proteins that can aggregate and eventually cause disease and death (
Balchin et al., 2016
). The topic of chloroplast proteostasis was recently excellently reviewed in more detail (
Gao et al., 2022
;
Llamas and Pulido, 2022
). Here, we focus on the retrograde signaling-related aspect and the cpUPR. Proteostasis is maintained by molecular chaperones and proteases. Chaperones of the HSP90 class prevent protein unfolding, while members of the HSP70 and HSP60 classes prevent protein aggregation and facilitate protein (re)folding (
Nathan et al., 1997
;
Hayer-Hartl et al., 2016
;
Rosenzweig et al., 2019
). While the chaperones assist with folding of newly produced or imported proteins, proteases degrade aberrant, damaged, or non-foldable proteins. Under severe stress conditions, when protein aggregates are formed in large amounts, synthesis of small heat shock proteins (sHSPs) and CLPB-type proteins of the HSP100 class is activated. sHSPs intercalate into developing aggregates, thus enabling HSP70 and CLPB to interact with individual polypeptides and allowing CLPB to extract them so that they can refold into the native state, assisted by HSP70s and HSP60s, if required (
Cashikar et al., 2005
;
Mogk et al., 2015
). Any disturbance in protein homeostasis must be sensed to elicit a UPR, which then results in upregulation of molecular chaperones and proteases to restore protein homeostasis and triggers responses that suppress the cause of the perturbation. This may require retrograde signals when the disruption occurs in the endoplasmic reticulum or in an organelle because molecular chaperones and proteases found in these compartments are encoded in the nucleus. First indications for a cpUPR came from the observation that chloroplast HSP60 (CPN60), HSP70, and HSP90 members were up-regulated in
Arabidopsis clpc1
mutants (
Sjogren et al., 2004
). CLPC, a chaperone of the HSP100 class, likely binds substrate polypeptides and threads them into the associated ClpP protease for degradation (
Shanklin et al., 1995
). Accordingly,
Arabidopsis
mutants lacking components of the hetero-oligomeric ClpP complex also show a strong up-regulation of various chloroplast proteases and chaperones (for example,
Zybailov et al. (2009)
). Similarly, depletion of chloroplast-encoded ClpP1 in
Chlamydomonas
results in up-regulation of chloroplast chaperones, including sHSPs (HSP22C/E/F), HSP70B, and CLPB3, and of chloroplast proteases, including DEG1C and FTSH2 (
Ramundo et al., 2014

GUN functions that extend beyond classic GUN signaling
Nuclear transcription factors that may be targets of GUN signaling have been identified. Perhaps most prominently, GUN1-mediated retrograde signals have been found to repress
GLK1
expression at the transcriptional level (
Kakizaki et al., 2009
) in response to PHYTOCHROME-INTERACTING FACTOR-mediated light signals (
Martin et al., 2016
). This may serve to mitigate light-induced activation of photosynthetic gene expression by GLK1 when light levels are high, helping to protect the chloroplast against photo-oxidative damage (
Martin et al., 2016
). Furthermore, it was suggested that regulation of GLK1 protein stability by GUN1 via ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis may be even more important than regulation at the level of transcription (
Tokumaru et al., 2017
). Recently, it was shown that GLK1 mediates GUN1 signaling in response to chloroplast damage, at least in part through the B-box-type zinc-finger transcription factor BBX16 (
Veciana et al., 2022
). Mutation of
bbx16
markedly suppressed the
gun1
phenotype upon LIN treatment as well as the
gun1
-like phenotype of GLK1OX plants. BBX16 has been reported to support cotyledon development associated with de-etiolation in the light, while excess light resulted in reduced photomorphogenesis (
Veciana et al., 2022
).
Moreover, tetpy biosynthesis is also vital for plants in later developmental stages, and
gun
mutants with defects in this pathway also show phenotypic effects in adult plants. For instance,
gun5
plants are pale green (
Mochizuki et al., 2001
) and cold sensitive (
Kindgren et al., 2015
). Similarly,
gun1
mutants display a defect in cold acclimation (
Marino et al., 2019
). Moreover,
gun1
mutants partially de-etiolate in the presence of LIN in the light, while
gun5
and
gun6
seedlings do not display this phenotype (
Martin et al., 2016
). In adult plants, inactivation of GUN1 causes a delay in flowering (
Wu et al., 2018
;
Marino et al., 2019
), while overexpression causes early flowering (
Wu et al., 2018
). In addition, the
gun1
mutant shows increased sensitivity to treatment with sucrose or abscisic acid (ABA) during early seedling development (
Cottage et al., 2010
), and
gun
mutants accumulate low amounts of anthocyanin when plastid biogenesis is perturbed (
Ruckle and Larkin, 2009
;
Cottage et al., 2010
;
Voigt et al., 2010
;
Richter et al., 2020
), as discussed in the next section.
Regulation of flavonoid biosynthesis by plastid-derived signals
Anthocyanins belong to the flavonoids, a group of specialized plant metabolites that accumulate in virtually all tissues and organs. In addition to fulfilling various eco-physiological functions, their strong (UV) light-absorbing and antioxidant properties help to protect vulnerable biomolecules when plants face adverse environmental conditions (
Davies et al., 2018
;
Ferreyra et al., 2021
). Consequently, flavonoid biosynthesis (FB;
Figure 3
) is transcriptionally and metabolically activated when external factors, such as light intensity and growth temperature, threaten cell integrity and necessitate plant acclimation. In recent years, it has become evident that chloroplast function and plastid-to-nucleus signaling are involved in regulating FB and anthocyanin synthesis, although the precise mechanisms have yet to be resolved. The proposed regulatory networks involve known triggers of signal transduction cascades, ranging from light, sugar, and phytohormones to reactive oxygen species (ROS; e.g.,
LaFountain and Yuan, 2021
).

Biogenic signals regulating FB
Biogenic signaling (see above;
Pogson et al., 2008
) not only ensures balanced expression of PhANGs but also stimulates activation of FB and anthocyanin synthesis when chloroplast biogenesis is perturbed. Strikingly, the impact of chloroplast-derived signals on regulation of FB was revealed through analysis of
gun

gun1
and
gun5

gun

.
Although this most probably excludes a signaling function for flavonoids (and their intermediates) in GUN signaling (
Xu et al., 2019a
;
Richter et al., 2020

Hildreth et al., 2022
). As in the context of biogenic signaling, FB genes and PhANGs are oppositely regulated targets that are subject to control by GUN proteins. Transcriptomics analyses have shown that expression of MYB (
MYELOBLASTOSIS
)-LIKE 2 (

), a repressor of the MBW complex (a transcription factor [TF] complex consisting of MYB, basic helix-loop-helix, and a WD40 component;

), is upregulated in
gun1

gun

Richter et al., 2020
). As in the case of PhANGs, the nature of the signal released or induced by GUN proteins to activate FB when chloroplast development is suppressed remains open.
Operational signaling components controlling FB
In the context of the acclimation response of fully developed plants and leaves, several “operational signals” may serve to activate FB in response to environmental changes (
Figure 3
). Intriguingly, activation of FB in HL is independent of the GUN pathway (
Ruckle and Larkin, 2009
;
Richter et al., 2020
). FB is usually activated under environmental conditions that typically enhance ROS production in chloroplasts (
Li and Kim, 2022
;
Mittler et al., 2022
). ROS have been associated with stimulation of FB-related transcripts and end-product accumulation (
Xu et al., 2017
;
Shi et al., 2018
), which is in general agreement with the light-shielding and antioxidant properties of the latter. However, while genetic perturbation of peroxisomal catalase, plastid-localized 2-cysteine peroxiredoxins, or ascorbate production also results in ROS accumulation, it is accompanied by transcriptional repression of FB genes and low anthocyanin accumulation in HL (
Vandenabeele et al., 2004
;
Vanderauwera et al., 2005
;
Page et al., 2012
;
Awad et al., 2015
). Therefore, a more detailed understanding of ROS as potential signals, particularly under acclimation-relevant conditions, is needed. In addition, the phytohormones jasmonate (JA), gibberellic acid (GA), ABA, cytokinin, strigolactones, and salicylic acid, whose biosynthesis takes place, at least in part, in chloroplasts (precursor formation), can be regarded as retrograde signals that affect FB. Among other mechanisms, phytohormone-mediated regulation of FB involves ubiquitin, and proteasome-dependent degradation of repressors of phytohormone signaling (e.g., the JA zing-finger inflorescence meristem domain for JA or DELLA for GA), which sequester components of the MBW complex or repressors of the pathway (e.g., MYBL2; reviewed in
LaFountain and Yuan, 2021
;
Araguirang and Richter, 2022
;
Figure 3
). However, whether these phytohormones are involved in FB regulation during acclimation to environmental changes remains to be elucidated.
Intriguingly, previous studies have demonstrated that phytohormones and signaling components act downstream of a sugar signal to promote FB under standard growth conditions (
Loreti et al., 2008
;
Li et al., 2014
). It is well documented that exogenous sugar supply (
Teng et al., 2005
;
Solfanelli et al., 2006
), but also high cellular sugar content because of genetic disturbance of carbohydrate sinks, stimulates anthocyanin production (
Lloyd and Zakhleniuk, 2004
;
Wippel and Sauer, 2012
;
Ragel et al., 2013
;
Schmitz et al., 2014
). Suppression of FB and anthocyanin accumulation through inhibition of photosynthetic electron transport (
Akhtar et al., 2010
;
Jeong et al., 2010
), which can be rescued by short-term sucrose feeding (
Creasy, 1968
;
Zirngibl et al., 2022
), connects FB with photosynthetic activity and sugar availability. In the case of acclimation to HL, increased light intensity results in higher photosynthetic electron transport, CO
2
assimilation, and (a transient) rise in cellular sugar content (
Schmitz et al., 2014
;
Schumann et al., 2017
). As part of the acclimation response to low temperatures, soluble sugar accumulation (e.g.,
Naegele and Heyer, 2013
) and the energy status of the cell (
Zhang et al., 2021
) also correlate with FB activation and anthocyanin production. Communication of the sugar/energy signal here may depend on phytohormone signaling pathways involving ethylene, GA, or JA (
Loreti et al., 2008
;
Das et al., 2011
;
Li et al., 2014
). Recently it was demonstrated that carbon fixation in chloroplasts, export of photosynthates by the triose phosphate/phosphate translocator, and sugar-induced inactivation of the protein kinase SnRK1 (sucrose-non-fermenting 1-related protein kinase 1) is required for activation of anthocyanin biosynthesis during acclimation to HL (
Zirngibl et al., 2022
). Hence, a chloroplast-derived metabolic signal serves to transcriptionally activate and stimulate anthocyanin production under HL conditions. Activation of FB in response to endogenous sugar content ensures the availability of sufficient resources for FB, which has a high demand for energy and carbon.
A number of metabolites, including carbohydrates, have been proposed previously to be associated with retrograde signaling (
Kleine et al., 2009b
), and SnRK1, which regulates metabolism by phosphorylation of metabolic enzymes, is thought to play a central role in retrograde signaling (
Wurzinger et al., 2018
;
Dietz et al., 2019
). This is supported by mathematical modeling, which shows that slight changes in SnRK1 activity induced by allosteric effectors may be sufficient to induce dramatic readjustments of metabolic homeostasis (
Nägele and Weckwerth, 2014
). In addition, chloroplast proteins like CHUP1 and CP29 have been discussed as possible targets of phosphorylation by SnRK1, which would directly link sensing and signaling networks in plastids, the cytosol, and the nucleus (
Nukarinen et al., 2016
).
Characterization of candidate signaling molecules and cascades in a multicompartmental space: Detection, quantification, and limitations
Elucidation of the spatial distribution of the metabolites and proteins involved would make a crucial contribution to the disentanglement of retrograde-signaling pathways, as exemplified by the need to quantify heme pools and identify putative heme transporters (see above). However, detection and interpretation of the metabolic readouts of inter-organellar or retrograde signaling networks is an extremely challenging task. Metabolic pathways and signaling cascades in eukaryotic cells frequently comprise transport and membrane receptor-mediated steps that connect molecular networks in different compartments. Particularly in plant cells, the presence of additional compartments like plastids and vacuoles complicates detection, quantification, and interpretation of signaling compounds. Hence, for experimental analysis of such networks, protocols and analytical platforms that are able to detect, quantify, and integrate data derived from diverse molecular compounds with multicompartmental distributions must be combined (
Weckwerth et al., 2020
). Chromatography coupled to mass spectrometry (MS), together with specific purification and enrichment protocols, has become a central approach for specific but comprehensive detection and quantification of diverse substance classes ranging from nucleosides to proteins and metabolites from the tissue to the single-cell level (
Jora et al., 2019
;
Rinschen et al., 2019
;
Kelly, 2020
).
Detection of proteins and their modifications
Environmental stimuli typically trigger chemical modifications; e.g., phosphorylation of membrane-associated kinases, channels, receptor-like kinases, or ion pumps. Such modifications of membrane proteins can be detected by MS, although integral membrane proteins may be more challenging to analyze than peripheral proteins because they require specific purification and enrichment protocols (
Savas et al., 2011
;
Collins et al., 2017
). Combination of such enrichment protocols with phosphoproteomics can reveal the temporal dynamics of membrane protein modification (
Niittylä et al., 2007
). However, membrane proteins, including those from chloroplasts, may exhibit fast dynamics (
Orädd and Andersson, 2021
), which can make interpretation of an experimental snapshot difficult. While this also applies to soluble proteins, particular enrichment and purification requirements of membrane proteins can delay quenching during experiments, which increases the ambiguity of experimental findings. Furthermore, detergents must be employed to increase membrane protein solubility. Such compounds need to be removed prior to liquid chromatography coupled to MS (LC-MS) analysis, which may again result in sample loss (
Chen and Weckwerth, 2020
). Despite such methodological limitations, protocols have been established that allow efficient enrichment and quantification of (plasma) membrane-bound proteins; e.g., combinations of differential centrifugation with non-ionic detergent treatment (
Collins et al., 2017
). Proteomes of organelles and compartments (e.g., the nucleus and the chloroplast), have also been profiled using quantitative MS, which yields quantitative information about the composition and dynamics of membrane proteomes (
Tang et al., 2020
;
Trentmann et al., 2020
).
Environmentally induced conformational changes in a receptor induce intracellular changes, such as alterations in ion concentrations and membrane potentials, which may cause posttranslational modifications. This, in turn, induces cascades of protein-protein interactions and modifications, finally modifying the activities of target enzymes or TFs (
Crepin and Rolland, 2019
). Recently, the regulation of signaling pathways and their effects on metabolism in Mut9-like kinase (
mlk
) mutants of
Arabidopsis
was elucidated (
Wilson et al., 2021
). MLKs are serine/threonine kinases that link light, circadian, and abiotic stress signaling. The authors used a combination of protein isolation, digestion, phosphopeptide enrichment, and labeling, which was followed by high-pH reverse-phase fractionation before LC-MS/MS analysis. With this setup, they were able to detect effects of MLKs on phosphorylation of nuclear proteins and modification of chromatin. Quantitative phosphoproteomics has also uncovered cascades of SnRK1 signaling (see above) involving potential phosphorylation targets in various subcellular compartments; i.e., in the cytosol, nucleus, chloroplast, mitochondria, and the plasma membrane (
Nukarinen et al., 2016
;
Li et al., 2022
).
Wolschin et al. (2005)
applied a metal-oxide-based affinity chromatography method for enrichment of phosphorylated proteins and peptides (
Wolschin et al., 2005
). Crucially, such enrichment enables detection and quantification of low-abundance proteins and phosphorylation sites. Combining enrichment protocols with targeted proteomics (i.e., selected/multiple reaction monitoring) is a promising approach for tracing signals within and between cellular compartments. By employing internal standards, one can even quantify absolute peptide concentrations down to the low femtomole/high attomole range (
Arsova et al., 2018
). Using such technologies, one can identify pathways, or even single metabolic reactions, whose signaling functions can be tested by integrated “omics” analysis of mutants (
Bjornson et al., 2017
).
Detection of organellar metabolomes
While similarly sophisticated and high-precision gas chromatography-MS (GC-MS) and LC-MS platforms are available for metabolite profiling and targeted analysis (
Liu et al., 2019
;
Perez de Souza et al., 2021
), it remains difficult to specifically quench, separate, and enrich organellar metabolomes. However, this is an obstacle of great importance to be able to explore putative signaling compounds involved in the retrograde pathway, which is needed to advance the understanding of this process. Compared with proteomics approaches, where predictions about subcellular localization of identified proteins are possible on the basis of signal sequences, such information is not available for metabolites. Furthermore, isolation and enrichment protocols for intact organelles (e.g., Percoll layers;
Kley et al., 2010
), are problematic for metabolomics because of leakage and ongoing enzymatic interconversion of metabolites during the isolation procedure. To overcome the effects of inefficient quenching of metabolism, the method of non-aqueous fractionation (NAF) applies the principle of separating components of cellular compartments within a density gradient replacing aqueous with non-aqueous solvents (
Elbers et al., 1974
;
Gerhardt and Heldt, 1984
;
Stitt et al., 1989
). After quenching and homogenizing plant tissue in liquid nitrogen, sample water (i.e., ice) is removed by lyophilization, which inhibits all enzymatic reactions. Suspension of freeze-dried plant material in mixtures of organic solvents, followed by sonication, then allows separation of compartment-specific compounds within a non-aqueous density gradient (
Gerhardt and Heldt, 1984
). Finally, quantification of specific marker enzyme activities or proteins together with metabolites enables estimation of subcellular compartment-specific metabolite concentrations by correlation (
Arrivault et al., 2014
;
Fürtauer et al., 2016
,
2019
). In addition to appropriate quenching of metabolism, NAF also separates the constituents of different organelles from each other. Simultaneously, this enriches for low-abundance compounds and reduces chromatographic overlap with highly abundant proteins (e.g., Rubisco subunits or metabolites; e.g., fumarate or sucrose), which limits chromatographic separation and identification by MS (
Ly and Wasinger, 2011
). Thus, NAF-based approaches are, in principle, able to characterize retrograde signaling and intercompartmental pathways comprising plastids, mitochondria, and the nucleus (
Fürtauer et al., 2019
). This methodology might also enable further insights into the metabolic regulation of SnRK1 signaling; e.g., by disentangling the cytosolic concentrations of sucrose and trehalose 6-phosphate. Sucrose in particular is likely to be strongly compartmentalized between plastids, cytosol, and the vacuole, and this factor must be accounted for when it is discussed in the context of energy signaling (
Figueroa and Lunn, 2016
;
Weiszmann et al., 2018
). Furthermore, serial NAF enrichments might improve the detectability of low-abundance and reactive compounds like acetyl-coenzyme A, which is required for lysine acetylation and histone modification (
Hartl and Finkemeier, 2012
;
König et al., 2014
;
Locato et al., 2018
).
Genetically engineered metabolite sensors (e.g., based on Förster resonance energy transfer) also represent suitable tools with which to trace spatial and temporal metabolic dynamics (
Caldana et al., 2012
), as exemplified by application of a heme sensor (see above;
Hanna et al., 2016
). Another example is determination of subcellular ATP compartmentalization by expressing cytosol and plastid-targeted versions of a Förster resonance energy transfer-based sensor protein (
Voon et al., 2018
). While such a targeted approach yields very detailed insights into the dynamics of specific metabolite, its effects on the (full) primary and/or secondary subcellular metabolome remain elusive. Thus, future studies that focus on metabolic regulation and the effects of inter-organellar signaling may combine sensor and NAF approaches (
Figure 4
). Together with quantitative proteomics and phosphoproteomics, this promises to provide detailed and quantitative information about subcellular cascades of retrograde information processing.

Concluding remarks and future directions
The GUN saga began with an elegant genetic screen (
Susek et al., 1993
), but subsequent analyses have often led to misleading, contradictory, and erroneous conclusions. How GUN signaling actually works remains unclear with respect to tetpy signaling (concerning
gun1
–
gun6
) and GUN1-related signaling (involving GUN1 and GUN7 [oeGLK1]). Because of its enigmatic characteristics, GUN1 continues to attract a great deal of attention; it appears to act as a tipping point that can dramatically enhance the effect of defects in chloroplast function. However, further efforts to determine GUN1’s true function will undoubtedly result in numerous future publications.
It is becoming increasingly clear that the ability of plants to acclimate to a changing environment is closely linked to proper chloroplast development and function. Chloroplast function, in turn, is strictly dependent on a reciprocal information exchange between chloroplasts and the other intracellular compartments. Here, characterization of extrachloroplastic proteins that have a strong impact on chloroplast development (e.g.,
Xu et al., 2019b
) will lead to a better understanding of the dependence of plastid transcription and biogenesis upon epigenetic regulatory processes, such as histone acetylation (
Barrero-Gil et al., 2021
). On the other hand, synthesis of compounds needed for epigenetic modifications is itself initiated in the organelles (
Lindermayr et al., 2020
), and information exchange is a two-way process. Besides this, it will be interesting to elucidate cell-type-specific signaling events in retrograde signaling in light of the partial functional specialization of plastids/chloroplasts in different plant cell types and their differential exposure to environmental factors. Methods such as spatially defined intracellular transcriptomics, along with measurements of cell-type-specific activities of regulatory elements under environmental stress, should provide further insights into this fundamental question.
Finally, the retrograde signaling field has reached a threshold where technical advancements must be cleverly combined for detection and identification of candidate signaling molecules with spatiotemporal precision. Clearly, interpretation of the metabolic readout of inter-organellar and retrograde signaling networks will be an extremely challenging task.
