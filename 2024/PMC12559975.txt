Introduction
Neurons are highly polarized cells, with a single axon and multiple dendrites deriving from the cell body with each neuron forming hundreds to thousands of synapses. Synapses are formed predominantly during development as axons establish contacts with dendrites and neuronal somata but continues in the mature nervous system and throughout postnatal life (Sudhof,
2018
). The past decades have witnessed enormous progress in our understanding of the mechanisms that underlie neurotransmitter release at the presynaptic compartment (Jahn & Fasshauer,
2012
; Südhof,
2017
) and information processing at the postsynapse (Groc & Choquet,
2020
). In contrast, we know surprisingly little about the formation of the presynaptic compartment, for example how synaptic vesicles (SVs) are generated from SV proteins synthesized in the neuronal soma. As the biosynthetic machinery, such as the rough endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and the Golgi apparatus, is largely restricted to the somatodendritic domain (Horton et al.,
2005
; Jan & Jan,
2010
), presynaptic vesicle proteins synthesized in the neuronal soma need to be exported into the axon before assembly into mature SVs (Fig.
1
). Estimates of protein synthesis
vs
. degradation from metabolic labelling and mass spectrometry suggest that ∼0.7% of synaptic protein content is degraded per hour in cultured neurons
in vitro
(Cohen et al.,
2013
; Truckenbrodt et al.,
2018
). Replenishment of this material may thus be accomplished by as few as 10–15 precursor vesicles (PVs) per hour in mature neurons in culture (Ahmari et al.,
2000
; Maas et al.,
2012
; Rizalar et al.,
2021
), posing challenges with respect to the detection and analysis of PVs in mammalian CNS neurons. Moreover, we know very little about the mechanisms that convert such PVs into mature SVs (Rizalar et al.,
2021
).

Once assembled into a functional presynaptic compartment, SVs are capable of undergoing calcium‐triggered exocytic fusion with the active zone (AZ) membrane. To prevent expansion of the presynaptic plasma membrane and a corresponding loss of lateral membrane tension (i.e. tension within the plane of the membrane), SV exocytosis is followed by compensatory endocytic membrane retrieval (Ogunmowo et al.,
2023
). Moreover, SVs of correct size and composition have to be reformed in order to sustain neurotransmission. If SVs (partially) lose their identity over multiple rounds of exo‐endocytosis (Fernandez‐Alfonso et al.,
2006
; Wienisch & Klingauf,
2006
), specific mechanisms must exist to orchestrate the fidelity of SV protein sorting and to ensure SV uniformity and size. Finally, synapses must be equipped with mechanisms that ensure the spatiotemporally controlled coupling of SV exocytosis and endocytic SV reformation to maintain presynaptic membrane homeostasis and, thereby, sustain brain function.
Here we review the pathways implicated in the formation and axonal transport of SV precursor organelles, review the mechanisms involved in the local reformation of SVs within nerve terminals in mature neurons, and discuss open questions as well as perspectives for future research.
Axonal transport of precursor vesicles and presynapse assembly
Since little protein translation occurs in the axon (Taylor et al.,
2013
) and rough ER is absent from axons and nerve terminals altogether (Horton et al.,
2005
; Jan & Jan,
2010
), SV proteins need to be synthesized in the neuronal soma from where they are exported via the Golgi complex and the trans‐Golgi network (TGN) to axons and eventually to the synapse (Fig.
1
A
and
B
). The mechanisms underlying this pathway are among the least understood aspects of synaptic biology [see also Acuna et al. (
2016
), Guedes‐Dias & Holzbaur (
2019
), Maeder et al. (
2014
) and Rizalar et al. (
2021
) for detailed reviews on presynaptic biogenesis]. Studies from invertebrates show that newly synthesized presynaptic proteins are transported in PVs along axonal microtubules to the nascent presynapse in a regulated manner to ensure its proper assembly, maintenance and function (Maas et al.,
2012
; Maeder et al.,
2014
). Different types of PVs that may carry presynaptic cargo to nerve terminals have been described. Early studies in the giant axon of the squid and ligation experiments in rat sciatic nerves had identified 50–60 nm anterogradely directed vesicles and tubulovesicular carriers (Allen et al.,
1982
; Tsukita & Ishikawa,
1980
) that based on immunoelectron microscopy labelling contained SV proteins (Li et al.,
1995
). Analysis by immunogold electron microscopy has shown that SV proteins are transported in proximal axons as assemblies of pleiomorphic vesicles (50–300 nm) devoid of peripheral SV proteins such as Synapsin or α‐Synuclein. These pleiomorphic vesicles may be held together by liquid–liquid phase separation and appear to undergo maturation during further axonal transport into 40 nm vesicles, akin to mature SVs (Tao‐Cheng,
2020
). In most recent work, a chemical–genetic strategy was devised to trap PVs in proximal axons of induced human glutamatergic neurons. In this approach, PVs marked by tagged Synaptophysin were acutely recruited to the surface of axonal mitochondria followed by correlative spinning disc confocal light and focused ion beam milling scanning electron microscopy (FIB‐SEM) analysis. Three‐dimensional reconstruction revealed the presence of vesicles and tubules with diameters of 50–400 nm and a size distribution clearly distinct from that of mature SVs. The largest population comprised vesicles with diameters between 50 and about 90 nm. These vesicles not only contained Synaptophysin but also a number of other SV (e.g. Synaptotagmin 1) and AZ proteins (e.g. Piccolo) (Rizalar et al.,
2023
). These studies suggest that presynaptic biogenesis in mammalian CNS neurons involves a somewhat heterogenous population of vesicular and tubular structures that carry SV and AZ proteins to nascent synapses to mediate presynaptic biogenesis and assembly. Whether and how these vesicles relate to the previously described Piccolo–Bassoon transport vesicles (e.g. distinct 80 nm‐sized dense core granules) suggested to harbour the large cytoplasmic multidomain AZ scaffolds Piccolo, Bassoon and ELKS requires further studies.
Anterograde axonal transport of PVs is mediated by the kinesin‐3 family members KIF1A (i.e. Unc104 in
Caenorhabditis elegans
, Imac in
Drosophila melanogaster
) and its paralogue KIF1Bβ in worms and fly motoneurons (Hall & Hedgecock,
1991
; Jin & Garner,
2008
; Pack‐Chung et al.,
2007
) and in mouse (Jin & Garner,
2008
; Okada et al.,
1995
) and human CNS neurons (Rizalar et al.,
2023
) (Fig.
1
C
). Mutations in human KIF1A are linked to epilepsy, hereditary spastic paraplegia, a rare gait disorder caused by axonal defects or axonal degeneration in the spinal cord, and the peripheral neuropathy Charcot‐Marie‐Tooth disease (Mahungu et al.,
2023
; Pareyson et al.,
2015
). Putative PVs have been found to accumulate in neuronal somata of
unc104
mutant worms (Hall & Hedgecock,
1991
), in neurons from KIF1A knockout (KO) mice (Yonekawa et al.,
1998
), and in
D. melanogaster
motoneurons lacking the small GTPase Arl8, a major recruitment factor of KIF1A/Unc‐104/Imac in worm (Klassen et al.,
2010
; Wu et al.,
2013
), fly (Vukoja et al.,
2018
) and human neurons (Rizalar et al.,
2023
). Consistently, motoneuronal somata from
arl8
mutant
D. melanogaster
display assemblies of 70–80 nm vesicles generally lacking dense cores, which are, thus, distinct from mature SVs (Vukoja et al.,
2018
).
These and other data suggest that axonal transport of PVs is accompanied by further maturation steps that may occur either during transport and/or concomitant with their assembly into the nascent presynapse (Fig.
1
). How the presynaptic material contained in PVs is eventually incorporated into nascent synapses is unclear. Work in
C. elegans
has revealed crucial roles for the KIF1A/Unc‐104‐associated small GTPase Arl8 and the c‐Jun N‐terminal kinase (JNK) pathway in regulating the balance between axonal transport via PVs and the stable assembly of newly synthesized SV and AZ proteins into the presynapse (Wu et al.,
2013
). It is likely that more than one type of PV mediates presynapse assembly. For example, knockout of Arl8A/B or KIF1A in human neurons selectively impairs axonal transport and assembly of SV and AZ proteins such as Munc13‐1 and Piccolo but does not appear to impact presynaptic delivery of voltage‐gated calcium channels (Rizalar et al.,
2023
), indicating that the latter use a distinct type of transport vesicle. Consistently, work in mutant mice has shown that presynapse assembly proceeds unperturbed in the absence of voltage‐gated calcium channels (Held et al.,
2020
). Further studies in invertebrates argue for a genetically encoded hierarchical model of presynapse assembly, in which delivery of synaptic cell adhesion molecules such as Neurexins is coordinated with the arrival of early‐acting AZ scaffold proteins, most notably Syd‐1 and Liprin‐α, to build nascent synaptic sites (Kittelmann et al.,
2013
; Owald et al.,
2012
) that serve as targets for the further assembly and integration of SV and AZ proteins. Elegant biochemical and genetic approaches argue that this process requires the co‐assembly via phase‐separation of Liprin‐α (i.e. Syd‐2 in worms and flies) with ELKS1 (also known as Erc1 in mammals) (McDonald et al.,
2020
), the closest relative of
D. melanogaster
BRP. Nascent presynapses initiated by Liprin‐α and associated synaptic cell adhesion proteins may then be further stabilized and expanded by PV‐mediated co‐delivery of SV and AZ proteins. In agreement with this model, it was recently shown that in human neurons lacking all four Liprin‐α isoforms, nascent synaptic contacts are formed but recruitment of SVs and AZ proteins is strongly reduced, resulting in ‘empty’ boutons and loss of synaptic transmission (Marcó de la Cruz et al.,
2024
). Similar albeit weaker presynaptic phenotypes have been reported for loss of Liprin‐α in
C. elegans
(Kittelmann et al.,
2013
) and
D. melanogaster
photoreceptors or motoneurons (Choe et al.,
2006
; Fouquet et al.,
2009
; Owald et al.,
2012
).
Conversion of PV into mature SVs
The above data suggest that SV proteins are initially transported as immature precursor PVs that undergo further maturation steps along their transport itinerary (Allen et al.,
1982
; Tao‐Cheng,
2007
,
2020
; Tsukita & Ishikawa,
1980
) and/or upon arrival at nascent presynaptic sites. Such maturation conceivably could occur by PV fusion with the plasma membrane followed by endocytic recycling and budding of mature SVs (Fig.
1
D
). Loss of key factors found critical for neuronal exocytosis (e.g. Munc18, Munc13‐1; Varoqueaux et al.,
2006
; Verhage et al.,
2000
) does not appear to cause severe defects in presynapse assembly or alter SV content, arguing against a role for the neuronal fusion machinery in the conversion of PVs into mature SVs. A fusion mechanism based on SNARE proteins other than neuron‐specific SNAREs and their associated machinery cannot be ruled out.
Another possible mechanism for the conversion of PVs into SVs is direct budding or fission of precursor organelles (Fig.
1
D
). At mature synapses, SVs following their exocytic fusion and endocytosis of SV membranes are reformed predominantly via clathrin‐mediated budding from membrane invaginations or internal endosome‐like vesicles (Kononenko et al.,
2014
; Watanabe et al.,
2014
) (see below). It is therefore conceivable that a similar budding process involving clathrin and clathrin adaptors such as AP‐2 and AP180/CALM mediates the formation of SVs from axonally transported PVs. Consistently, it has been shown that mutations in key components of the clathrin machinery alter SV size (Chanaday et al.,
2019
; Kononenko & Haucke,
2015
; Saheki & De Camilli,
2012
). Alternative budding processes, e.g. via the neuronal AP‐3 complex (Blumstein et al.,
2001
) and its binding partners ADP ribosylation factor 1 and/or VPS41 (Asensio et al.,
2013
), could conceivably contribute to the formation of SVs from PVs carrying newly synthesized SV proteins. Budding from synaptic PVs would require the dissociation of peripherally associated AZ proteins or Arl8‐kinesin complexes, e.g. via signalling cascades impinging on JNK (Wu et al.,
2013
), SRPK79D (Arancibia et al.,
2019
) or Par‐1/MARK1 (Barber et al.,
2017
).
Budding processes to generate small uniform SVs may be enhanced by the intrinsic properties of SV proteins, in particular Synaptophysin and related tetraspan transmembrane proteins. Studies in non‐neuronal cells have revealed a stunning ability of Synaptophysin to generate small vesicles similar in size to SVs found in mature nerve terminals. Co‐expression of Synaptophysin with the phase‐separating peripheral SV protein Synapsin promoted the formation of clusters of small vesicles that resemble SV clusters found at mammalian synapses (Park et al.,
2021
,
2023
). These experiments argue for a facilitatory role of tetraspan SV membrane proteins, possibly together with membrane lipids such as cholesterol and phosphatidylethanolamine (Takamori et al.,
2006
) in the formation of small homogeneously sized SVs.
Clearly, more work is needed to unravel the mechanisms that generate mature SVs from PVs during development.
Mechanisms of SV reformation in mature neurons
Important features of SVs are their morphological uniformity and precise average protein composition, which are efficiently maintained over repetitive rounds of exocytosis and endocytosis to maintain their fusion competence. A single SV contains about 600 transmembrane domains, roughly representing a quarter of the vesicle surface. Hence, sorting of SV proteins, many of which (e.g. the vATPase) are present in just a few copies (Takamori et al.,
2006
; Taoufiq et al.,
2020
), is a major task for the presynaptic endocytic machinery (Kononenko & Haucke,
2015
). Moreover, if SVs lose their identity over multiple rounds of exo‐endocytosis (Fernandez‐Alfonso et al.,
2006
; Wienisch & Klingauf,
2006
), specific mechanisms must exist to orchestrate the fidelity of SV protein sorting and to ensure SV uniformity. However, it should be noted that (1) distinct SV proteins display different levels of intervesicle variability in copy numbers (Mutch et al.,
2011
), (2) molecular heterogeneity of SVs may contribute to the generation of SV pools with different release properties (e.g. release probability) (Guillaud et al.,
2017
; Ivanova et al.,
2021
; Morgan et al.,
2013
) and (3) SVs from distinct types of synapses may differ in SV protein copy numbers or even contain unique SV proteins (Gronborg et al.,
2010
; Salazar et al.,
2004
). An example of the last is the excitatory synapse‐specific tetraspan membrane protein MAL2 (Gronborg et al.,
2010
).
There is a general consensus that clathrin and clathrin‐associated adaptors play a central role in SV reformation (Chanaday et al.,
2019
; Gan & Watanabe,
2018
; Heerssen et al.,
2008
; Heuser & Reese,
1973
; Kononenko et al.,
2014
; Maycox et al.,
1992
; Watanabe et al.,
2014
) (Fig.
2
) [see references Chanaday et al. (
2019
), Gan & Watanabe (
2018
) and Kononenko & Haucke (
2015
) for detailed reviews on the topic]. Clathrin‐coated vesicles isolated from nerve terminals are highly enriched in SV proteins (Maycox et al.,
1992
) and depletion of clathrin (Watanabe et al.,
2014
) or KO of AP‐2(μ) in cultured hippocampal neurons or in cortical neurons
in vivo
(Kononenko et al.,
2014
) resulted in the accumulation of postfission endosome‐like vesicles and a corresponding depletion of properly shaped SVs, while SV membrane retrieval was largely unaffected (Kononenko et al.,
2014
; Lopez‐Hernandez et al.,
2022
). Moreover, a plethora of studies have indicated that disruption of clathrin or clathrin‐associated adaptors, while dispensable for compensatory endocytosis of SV proteins
per se
(Lopez‐Hernandez et al.,
2022
), leads to defects in sorting SV cargoes and/or to alterations in SV size (Kononenko et al.,
2013
; Koo et al.,
2015
; Petralia et al.,
2013
; Zhang et al.,
1998
). Loss of clathrin‐associated cargo‐specific sorting adaptors such as AP180 and CALM increased the diffusional spread, impaired the efficacy of SV protein reclustering, and eventually resulted in the partial redistribution of their corresponding SV cargo (i.e. Synaptobrevin/VAMP2) to the axonal surface (Gimber et al.,
2015
; Koo et al.,
2015
). Surprisingly, loss of SV protein sorting adaptors (e.g. AP180, Stonin 2) typically has only marginal effects on the kinetics of SV protein endocytosis (Kononenko et al.,
2013
; Koo et al.,
2015
), suggesting that distinct mechanisms govern presynaptic membrane retrieval and SV reformation (Kononenko & Haucke,
2015
). In addition to the direct sorting of SV proteins by cargo‐selective adaptors, SV proteins are also sorted by their association with each other. For example, sorting of the SV calcium sensor Synaptotagmin is chaperoned by its association with SV2A (Kaempf et al.,
2015
), which itself can bind to endocytic proteins, and Synaptobrevin/VAMP2 is sorted in a complex with synaptophysin (Gordon et al.,
2011
; Harper et al.,
2021
). These data are most consistent with a model whereby SVs are reformed primarily via clathrin/AP‐2‐mediated vesicle budding either directly from the plasma membrane or via endosome‐like intermediates generated for example by ultrafast or bulk endocytosis (Chanaday et al.,
2019
; Kononenko et al.,
2014
; Watanabe et al.,
2014
) (Fig.
2
). In line with this model, studies on synaptosomes
in vitro
have shown that the generation of clathrin coats on endosome‐like vesicles might occur via mechanisms and components similar to those mediating endocytic vesicle budding from the plasma membrane (Takei et al.,
1996
). It is likely that during this process endosome‐like vesicles generated from the plasma membrane preserve their plasma membrane identity [e.g. enrichment of the signalling lipid phosphatidylinositol 4,5‐bisphosphate (PI(4,5)P
2
)] to allow for endocytic clathrin/AP‐2 coats to assemble (Kaksonen & Roux,
2018
).

In spite of the prominent phenotypes resulting from interference with clathrin function, data using chemical stimulation indicate that synapses, in addition to clathrin‐mediated endocytosis (CME), are equipped with other clathrin‐independent pathways of SV reformation that may operate preferentially under conditions of high sustained activity (Y. Wu et al.,
2014
) (Fig.
2
). Evidence from various systems including genetic studies in mice suggests that one possible compensatory pathway for SV reformation involves SV budding from endosomes via assembly protein 3 (AP‐3) coats. Mutations in AP‐3δ in
D. melanogaster
garnet mutants (Ooi et al.,
1997
) and in
mocha
mice cause pigmentation defects due to impaired biogenesis of lysosome‐related pigment granules (Kantheti et al.,
1998
), in line with the notion that AP‐3 sorts transmembrane cargo to lysosomes and lysosome‐related organelles to which SVs and their precursor organelles (i.e. PVs) belong (Rizalar et al.,
2023
; Vukoja et al.,
2018
). AP‐3 has also been implicated in SV recycling (Cheung & Cousin,
2012
; Voglmaier et al.,
2006
) and the neuronal form of AP‐3 is able to produce SVs from endosomes
in vitro
(Blumstein et al.,
2001
).
Mocha
mice display neurological deficits, including balance and hearing problems, but show a normal SV pool size, suggesting that the AP‐3 pathway is not essential for SV reformation
in vivo
, although it may be of importance in subsets of neurons (i.e. inhibitory neurons) (Nakatsu et al.,
2004
) and/ or under conditions of sustained high‐level activity when bulk endocytosis is activated (Chanaday et al.,
2019
; Gan & Watanabe,
2018
). The role of AP‐3 in SV reformation from endosomes may partially overlap with that of other coat complexes such as AP‐1, a clathrin adaptor implicated in traffic to and from endosomes (Cheung & Cousin,
2012
; Glyvuk et al.,
2010
). While conventional endosomes marked by the phosphoinositide lipid phosphatidylinositol 3‐phosphate [PI(3)P] are unlikely to be essential for SV reformation (Liu et al.,
2022
), the observation that mutations in the endosomal small GTPase Rab5 impair neurotransmission at
D. melanogaster
neuromuscular junctions (Wucherpfennig et al.,
2003
) suggests that endosomes and/or endosomal fusion events may be important for the maintenance of functional SV pools. Consistently, presynapses have been found to harbour various endosomal proteins (e.g. Rabs, SNARE) with conceivable functions in SV reformation or other forms of endosomal quality control (Asensio et al.,
2013
).

Recent work shows that PVs carrying newly synthesized SV and AZ proteins in axons of human excitatory neurons are marked by the rare endo‐lysosomal signalling lipid phosphatidylinositol 3,5‐bisphosphate [PI(3,5)P
2
]. Sustained genetic (i.e. KO of PIKFYVE) or acute pharmacological interference with PI(3,5)P
2
synthesis or local depletion of PI(3,5)P
2
from PVs inhibited anterograde axonal transport of PVs and reduced the presynaptic amounts of SV and AZ proteins (Rizalar et al.,
2023
). Further molecular analysis showed that one important function of PI(3,5)P
2
is to promote the recruitment of KIF1A to PVs via a coincident detection mechanism involving its direct association with the pleckstrin homology (PH) domain of KIF1A and the Arl8A/B binding to KIF1A (Rizalar et al.,
2023
). Hence, PI(3,5)P
2
controls the formation of newly synthesized SVs at the level of axonal PV transport via KIF1A.
Strong evidence implicates the plasma membrane‐localized signalling lipid PI(4,5)P
2
in SV endocytosis and reformation. Many endocytic proteins including the GTPase Dynamin, several clathrin adaptors such as the AP‐2 complex, and Dynamin‐binding bin‐amphiphysin‐rvs (BAR) domain proteins directly associate with PI(4,5)P
2
and require this lipid for membrane targeting (Kaksonen & Roux,
2018
; Kononenko & Haucke,
2015
; Saheki & De Camilli,
2012
). Moreover, genetic inactivation of PIPK type Iγ, the major PI(4,5)P
2
‐synthesizing enzyme at synapses (Di Paolo et al.,
2004
), or of the PI(4,5)P
2
‐phosphatase Synaptojanin (Cremona et al.,
1999
) cause severe defects in SV cycling in various models and neurotransmission and are associated with neurological diseases and/or neurodegeneration (e.g. Parkinson's and Alzheimer's disease) (Cao et al.,
2014
,
2017
,
2020
; Krebs et al.,
2013
). Most recent work has uncovered an important lipid‐based mechanism for the coupling of SV exocytosis and endocytic SV reformation. Specifically, it was demonstrated using optical probes that neuronal activity triggers presynaptic PI(4,5)P
2
synthesis. This mechanism involves the nanoscale recruitment of PIPKIγ to sites at or around the AZ membrane via exocytosed Synaptotagmin 1. Interference with this pathway, e.g. by mutational inactivation of the binding site for PIPKIγ on the Synaptotagmin‐C2B domain, impaired PI(4,5)P
2
‐triggered SV membrane retrieval but not exocytic SV fusion (Bolz et al.,
2023
). These findings suggest an important function for PI(4,5)P
2
in exo‐endocytic coupling and provide a molecular explanation for the long‐standing observation that loss of Synaptotagmin in
C. elegans
,
D. melanogaster
and mice perturbs SV recycling (Jorgensen et al.,
1995
; Nicholson‐Tomishima & Ryan,
2004
; Poskanzer et al.,
2003
) and/or reformation.
A seemingly opposite role is executed by the endosomal lipid PI(3)P, which can act as an activity‐dependent homeostat. Elevated neuronal activity was found to reduce neuronal and synaptic PI(3)P levels by repressing the activity of the lipid kinase VPS34. Depletion of PI(3)P impaired neurotransmission as a consequence of perturbed SV recycling via a pathway involving Calpain 2‐mediated hyperactivation of the kinase Cdk5 downstream of receptor‐ and activity‐dependent calcium influx (Liu et al.,
2022
). Hence, endosomal PI(3)P is part of an autoregulated cell‐intrinsic pathway that controls neuronal network activity via Calpain 2 and Cdk5‐mediated regulation of VPS34 activity.
The regulatory role of signalling lipids in SV biogenesis during development and SV reformation in mature neurons is paralleled by and partially intertwined with the function of protein kinases and phosphatases (Table
1
). Among the best studied of these factors are cyclin‐dependent kinase 5 (CDK5) and the calcium‐regulated phosphatase calcineurin. Compelling evidence in multiple systems suggests that SV pool size is controlled by the activities of CDK5 and calcineurin (Marra et al.,
2012
). Inhibition of CDK5 activity potentiates presynaptic function, including unmasking of previously ‘silent’ synapses and via tuning Cav2.2 (N‐type) voltage‐gated calcium channel activity (Kim & Ryan,
2010
,
2013
). Conversely, loss of calcineurin suppressed exocytosis at nerve terminals and calcineurin was required for the effects of CDK5 on neurotransmission. Among the best studied substrates of calcineurin are endocytic proteins including Dynamin (Armbruster et al.,
2013
; Raimondi et al.,
2011
). Consistently, acute or sustained inhibition of calcineurin function has been shown to impact multiple if not all forms of SV endocytosis and reformation in neurons and other types of neurosecretory cells (X. S. Wu et al.,
2014
). Other kinases implicated in SV recycling are cyclin‐dependent kinase‐like 5 (CDKL5), a serine/threonine kinase in severe early‐onset epileptic encephalopathy (Kontaxi et al.,
2023
), glycogen synthase kinase 3 (GSK3), which specifically regulates activity‐dependent bulk endocytosis via Dynamin 1 (Smillie et al.,
2013
), and LRRK2, a kinase implicated in Parkinson's disease and in the regulation of axonal transport of PVs as well as in the switch between autophagy and SV recycling in various models (Dou et al.,
2024
; Nadiminti et al.,
2024
; Soukup et al.,
2016
). It is likely that other kinases and phosphatases such as JNK (Wu et al.,
2013
) or SRPK79D (Arancibia et al.,
2019
) control SV biogenesis and/or reformation, e.g. during development or homeostatic scaling. Future studies will be needed to address such regulatory pathways.
Perspectives and open questions
Research in the recent past has provided important insights into the mechanisms that control presynaptic biogenesis including the identification of a conserved machinery for axonal transport and delivery of SV proteins to nascent synapses as well as a first glimpse at the PVs that mediate such transport (Rizalar et al.,
2023
). Many key questions remain unresolved (Rizalar et al.,
2021
): How many types of PVs exist and what are their cargos? How are PVs formed in neuronal somata and what is the machinery that controls their formation? Which genetic factors control the order and hierarchy of presynapse assembly (Marcó de la Cruz et al.,
2024
; Owald et al.,
2012
) during development? How do newly made synapses ‘inform’ the soma where protein synthesis occurs and PVs probably are assembled of their assembly status and which factors exhibit such feedback control? Furthermore, it is conceivable that the same or overlapping machinery that mediates presynaptic biogenesis and SV formation during development regulates synaptic strength and homeostatic scaling in the mature nervous system.
Moreover, in spite of decades of research on the topic, no consensus view on the mechanisms and modes of SV reformation in mature neurons has emerged. It seems possible that different types of neurons capitalize on a large repertoire of endocytic proteins and their associated signalling lipids to recycle exocytosed SV proteins to regenerate functional SVs (Chanaday et al.,
2019
; Soykan et al.,
2017
). Distinct phenotypes following genetic perturbations in this model may reflect different abundance and thus different rate‐limiting steps in the SV recycling and reformation pathway. Moreover, we know comparably little about the quality control pathways that must exist to ensure that only functional SV proteins are incorporated into reformed SVs at synapses. Studies have shown that in cultured hippocampal neurons ageing SVs are inactivated after about 200 cycles of exo‐ and endocytosis and it has been suggested that the misincorporation of the plasma membrane SNARE protein SNAP‐25 into aged SVs may serve as a trigger to induce degradation (Truckenbrodt et al.,
2018
). How such degradation is initiated, whether ageing SVs are degraded as an entity and how SV proteins are eventually turned over remain unresolved. Recent work (Zhou et al.,
2024
) suggests a possible regulatory role for autophagy and/or the endolyosomal system in SV protein degradation, but clearly further work is needed to resolve the physiological importance of neuronal autophagy (Kuijpers et al.,
2021
) and the mechanisms of SV turnover in detail.
Finally, cumulative evidence links defects in SV biogenesis and recycling to neurological disorders such as epilepsy (Smillie et al.,
2021
) and neurodegeneration, most notably Parkinson's disease (Cao et al.,
2014
,
2017
,
2020
; Krebs et al.,
2013
). Understanding the underlying mechanisms may be beneficial for the development of therapies to target these and related diseases.
