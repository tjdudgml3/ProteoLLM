Introduction
True morels (
Morchella
spp., Pezizales, Ascomycota) are worldwide famous edible and medicinal mushrooms for their unique appearance, delicate taste and outstanding biological activities (e.g. anticancer, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, immune modulation, and liver protection)
1
–
4
. The field soil cultivation of
Morchella
mushrooms characterized by the use of cultivable morel species (e.g.
M. importuna
,
M. sextelata
and
M. eximia
) and exogenous nutrition aiding has been realized in China since 2012
5
,
6
. In production season of 2023–2024, the total cultivation area exceeded 26,000
ha
covering most areas of China. However, along with the rapid expansion of morel production, the increasing problem of continuous cropping obstacle (CCO) has been becoming the major restrictive factor for healthy development of morel industry
7
. Similar to plants, the main symptoms of morel soil sickness are also those of stunted growth and development, serious occurrence of fungal disease, significant drop in production and even total crop failure
8
,
9
. The decrease in α-diversity of soil fungal communities and the increase in abundance of pathogenic fungi and certain dominant fungal genus may be partially responsible for morel CCO
10
–
12
. Furthermore, our previous study found that the phenolic acid extract from morel continuous cropping soil may exhibit certain extent of toxicity due to the existence of morel-specific allelochemicals
9
.
Allelopathy is the important factor conducing to plant consecutive monoculture problem
13
. The allelochemicals responsible for allelopathy are secondary metabolites and/or decomposition products of those metabolites, such as phenolic acids, aldehydes, coumarin, quinines, alkaloids and terpenoids. Among which, phenolic compounds are a class of the most important and common plant allelochemicals in the ecosystem. The principal phenolic compounds extensively studied in plants include chlorogenic acid, protocatechuic acid, gallic acid, 3,4-dihydroxybenzaldehyde,
p
-hydroxybenzoic acid, caffeic acid and 3,5-dinitrobenzoic acid
14
–
16
. However, the mushroom allelochemicals in phenolic acid extracts are unclear. In this study, the allelochemicals in phenolic acid extract of morel continuous cropping soils were identified by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) combined with in vitro bioassay. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report on discovery of morel allelochemicals. The work will be helpful for further study of the allelopathic mechanism responsible for morel replant disease and exploitation of precautionary measures to lower the losses of morel replanting.
Results
GC-MS analysis of the differential compounds
GC-MS analysis was performed on extracts of 12 soil samples (6 tested soils and 6 control ones). A total of 61 chemical substances were identified, including 10 phenolic acids, 36 acids, 3 aldehydes, 2 alcohols, 2 amides, 2 ketones, 1 amine, 1 ester, and 10 other compounds (Fig.
1
; Supplementary Information
I
,
II
). Acids (17.00-50.58%) and phenolic acids (19.35-70.52%) constitute a substantial proportion in all soil samples (Fig.
1
). Overall, the contents of total phenolic acids and acids in samples of continuous cropping soil were higher than those of control, while the contents of each major class of compounds exhibited variations across 12 tested soil samples (Fig.
1
; Supplementary Information
II
).

Meanwhile, a total of 44 compounds, including 5 phenolic acids, 27 acids, 3 alcohols, 2 ketones, 1 amide, 1 amino acid, and 5 others were identified in samples of morel static culture broth through our GC-MS analysis. Among which, 28 compounds were shared with those found in samples of continuous cropping soil, including 17 acids, and 5 phenolic acids (4-hydroxybenzoic acid, 4-coumaric acid, vanillic acid, ferulic acid, and caffeic acid) (Supplementary Information
III
). Moreover, the contents of 4-coumaric acid and vanillic acid in morel static culture broth were lower than those in samples of morel continuous cropping soil (Supplementary Information

OPLS-DA analysis of potential allelochemicals
An ideal separation was observed in the OPLS-DA score scatter plots between samples of continuous cropping soil and control (Fig.
2
a). In the OPLS-DA model, R
2
X (cum) and R
2
Y (cum) are used to evaluate the cross-validation and goodness of fit, while Q
2
(cum) describes the validity. The values of the three parameters are from 0 to 1. Values close to 1 indicate that a reliable model is obtained and values exceeding 0.5 imply the ideal fitting accuracy of the model. Our OPLS-DA model exhibited high value of R
2
X (0.769) and R
2
Y (0.886), indicating a stable and reliable model. Furthermore, this OPLS-DA model had good predictive power as indicated by the goodness of the prediction parameter (Q
2
= 0.827). In addition, a replacement test (evaluation of the risk of over-fitting) was used to verify the validity and predictability of the model. The intersections of the Q
2
regression line and the vertical axis were below 0 after 200 permutation tests, which indicated that the established model exhibited a good fit and strong predictability
17
,
18
(Fig.
2
b). The results suggested that our OPLS-DA model can effectively discriminate the components that may be responsible for the differences between samples of continuous cropping soil and control. Additionally, the importance of variables in projection (VIP) method ranks the total contribution of each variable to the OPLS-DA model. Variables with VIP > 1 will be considered to be relevant for group discrimination. In this study, the differential compounds between samples of continuous cropping soil and control were filtered by means of VIP > 1. Consequently, 15 potential allelochemicals were selected: 4-coumaric acid, vanillin, 2-hydroxyheptanoic acid, 3-hydroxypropionic acid, ferulic acid, acetovanillone, fumaric acid, octanoic acid, glycolic acid, malic acid, 2-hydroxydecanedioic acid, azelaic acid, 2-hydroxypropanoic acid, vanillic acid, and phenylacetic acid (sorted by VIP values from high to low) (Fig.
2
c, Supplementary Information
IV
).

Toxic effect of potential allelochemicals
The bioassays of potential 15 allelochemicals with VIP > 1 suggested that except for 4-coumaric acid, vanillic acid and ferulic acid, the other 12 tested compounds showed promotional effects on mycelial growth of three tested
Morchella
strains under 3 tested concentrations. However, at concentrations equivalent to the highest soil contents and under decuple concentrations, 4-coumaric acid and vanillic acid demonstrated the inhibitory effects on mycelial growth of three tested morel strains, while they promoted the morel mycelial growth at one tenth concentrations. Meanwhile, ferulic acid inhibited the mycelial growth of
M. eximia
SM at decuple concentration, while the other assays showed promotional effects (data not shown). The results suggested that 4-coumaric acid, vanillic acid and ferulic acid are potential morel allelochemicals. Further bioassays with 4-coumaric acid, vanillic acid and ferulic acid indicated that 4-coumaric acid inhibited the mycelial growth of three tested morel strains at soil native content in a concentration-dependent manner (

KEGG pathway annotation indicated that
p
-coumaric acid and ferulic acid are associated with the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (ko00940) pathway which begins with phenylalanine. The latter can be synthesized from glucose through the glycolysis/gluconeogenesis (ko00010) pathway (Fig.

Morchella

Morchella

Morchella

Discussion
We used the model of OPLS-DA to discriminate the allelochemicals between samples of continuous cropping soil and control. As an extension of PLS-DA (partial least squares-discrimination analysis) with built-in orthogonal signal correction, OPLS-DA not only allows addressing of multicollinearity and information redundancy issues like PLS-DA, but also improves the interpretability of constructed models by removing variance orthogonal to the variation of interest
19
,
20
. As a supervised recognition pattern technology, OPLS-DA has been widely used to determine the difference between sample sets and screen out the main molecular differences responsible for classification, such as analyzing data of gene expression, proteomics, and metabolite profiling
21
–
23
. Moreover, OPLS-DA constructs more concise and interpretable models and is commonly utilized to discern metabolite differences between two similar groups
24
,
25
, just like our samples of the continuous cropping soil and control. Comparatively, as an unsupervised multivariate technique, principal component analysis (PCA) is capable of providing an overview of complex multivariate data and is used to reveal an association between variables and relations between sample patterns
26
. However, PCA cannot ignore intra-group errors, eliminate random errors that are irrelevant to target research, and thus unable to distinguish differences and differential compounds between groups
27
.
Phenolic acids are common secondary metabolites of
Morchella
spp
4
,
28
. Our study determined minor 4-coumaric acid and vanillic acid in static culture broth of
M. sextelata
(Supplementary Information
IV
). Moreover, they also have been detected in many wild and cultivated morels
28
–
30
. In microorganisms including bacteria and fungi, 4-coumaric acid is derived mainly from (a) acetate and malonate via the polyketide pathway or from (b) phenylalanine, tyrosine, 3-dehydroquinate and chorismic acid produced in the shikimate pathway (phenylpropanoid biosynthetic pathway)
31
,
32
. Vanillic acid can be de novo synthesized from precursor protocatechuic and caffeic acid, and via the biotransformation from lignin-derived monomers (including syringol, isoeugenol, ferulic acid, and vanillin) by some bacteria, e.g.
Pseudomonas
spp.,
Halomonas
spp.,
Bacillus
spp.,
Streptomyces
spp., and
Serratia
spp
33
–
35
. However, our analysis suggested that
Morchella
mushrooms cannot solely synthesize the phenolic acid allelochemicals. The minor content of 4-coumaric acid and vanillic acid in acocarp and culture of
Morchella
spp. may be produced by morel closely related microbes, including endosymbiotic bacteria, endophytes, and hyphosphere microbiome in soil. The endosymbiotic bacteria may be popular in cultivated
Morchella
mushrooms. Key functions performed by the intracellular endosymbionts may be chemosynthesis and diet supplementation like
Pseudomonas putida
36
. Morel endophytes exist at a certain or the entire stage of the host and can facilitate the growth and enhance the stress resistance of
Morchella
mushrooms
37
–
39
. As geophilous mushrooms,
Morchella
spp. are cultivated in soil and have complex interactions with other organisms during their lifetime. To sustain continuous cropping, exogenous nutrition bags and morel mycelial spawn were consecutively added to the soil
12
. The relationships between morel cultivation, soil properties and soil hyphosphere microbiome are reciprocal owing to complex networks of causation and feedback effects that can have eco-evolutionary consequences
40
–
42
. Morel endophytes may be closely related with soil hyphosphere microbiome. Although confirmation of the common core microbial community at genus level is difficult due to the heterogeneity and complexity of morel cultivated soil, some bacteria were recognized as morel beneficial endophytes and members of hyphosphere microbiome, including
Bacillus
,
Paenibacillus
,
Pseudomonas
,
Pedobacter
,
Stenotrophomonas
and so on
39
,
41
,
42
. Additionally, 4-coumaric acid and vanillic acid are common plant allelochemicals or autotoxins, the possibility of their plant derivation cannot be ruled out. Anyway, the two phenolic acids can only be defined as morel allelochemicals rather than autotoxins due to their inability to be synthetised by
Morchella
mushrooms. The traceability of 4-coumaric acid and vanillic acid needs further study.
In Nature, the allelopathic activities are largely due to the combination and interaction of different allelochemicals
43
–
46
. The effects of allelochemicals may be synergistic or antagonistic, depending on the action target sites (kinds of target host species), number and ratio of allelochemicals and the concentration of the compounds in the mixture (the mixture may perform additively at low concentrations and antagonistically at high concentrations)
44
,
47
. Our bioassays indicated that 4-coumaric acid and vanillic acid did not exhibited any significantly synergistic allelopathic effect on mycelial growth of three tested morel strains. On the contrary, vanillin (aldehyde) and the mixture of 10 organic acids with VIP above 1 under their native soil concentrations (Table
2
) showed different degree of synergistic allelopathic effects with two morel allelochemicals on mycelial growth of three morel strains (data not shown). The results imply the complexity of soil allelochemicals on growth and development of
Morchella
mushrooms. The underlying mechanism of the synergistic actions among distinct allelochemicals needs further study.
The morel endophytes and soil hyphosphere microbiome are very sensitive to the interference of abiotic environmental factors, including soil nutritional status, soil tillage, temperature, heavy metals, and so on
48
. Thus the composition and content of phenolic acids may be very complex due to the sophisticated cultivation management and the soil management after morel planting. On the other hand, the soil factors may also influence the allelopathic activity. The fate of many allelochemicals in the soil environment is relatively uncertain. After their release by morels and plants, the allelochemicals are exposed to physical (e.g. sorption), chemical (e.g. direct oxidation or oxidation by metal ions) and biological (e.g. microbial degradation) soil factors
49
–
52
. Studies on the residence time, degradation and transformation of allelochemicals in soil, and their qualitative and quantitative differences in the rhizosphere versus bulk soil are needed
53
. In addition, the synergistic actions among distinct allelochemicals make their fate more complicated. The evaluation and assessment of allelochemical dynamics in soil settings of morel cultivation need further study.
Conclusion
Continuous cropping obstacle has been becoming the restrictive factor for stable development of morel industry since the field soil cultivation of
Morchella
mushrooms was realized in China in 2012. The Allelopathy exerted by allelochemicals (mainly phenolic acids) may play an instrumental role in morel soil sickness. Using comparative GC-MS combined with in vitro bioassays, 4-coumaric acid and vanillic acid were identified as morel allelochemicals. The study will be beneficial to further mechanism study responsible for morel continuous cropping obstacle and exploitation of precautionary measures to lower the losses of morel replanting, and thus will be conducive to the stable advancement of morel industry. Moreover, traceability of 4-coumaric acid and vanillic acid, the toxic effect of two phenolic acids on cultivated
Morchella
mushrooms, the underlying mechanism of the synergistic actions among distinct allelochemicals and the evaluation and assessment of allelochemical dynamics in soil settings of morel cultivation need further study.
Methods
Sampling
Soil sampling was conducted on April 15th, 2021, when the morel planting was nearly over, in four sites of Luanchuan County, Henan Province, China, and one site of Xingyang, Henan Province, China (Table
1
). The soils were clay, and the morel planting mode was that of ‘plastic house’
54
. The soil samples of continuous cropping group were collected by the five-point sampling method in each plot
55
. Control soils were collected by the equidistant sampling method outside the plastic house without morel growing
9
. After removing the surface sundries, approximately 100 g of topsoil (0–15 cm depth) was collected. Five soil samples from the same plot were uniformly mixed to create a composite sample of approximately 500 g. Subsequently, the sample was placed and sealed in a ziplock bag, transported to the laboratory immediately, and was refrigerated at -20 °C until further use.

Extraction of phenolic acids
The refrigerated soil samples were subjected to freeze-drying at -50 °C for approximately 48 h until constant weight. Then they were smashed and sieved through a 4-mm mesh. Phenolic acids were extracted according to our modified protocols of alkali liquor-ethyl acetate method
9
. The resultant extracts were pooled and evaporated to dryness using a rotary evaporator at 60 °C. The residue was dissolved in 5 ml ethyl acetate and kept in the dark at 4 °C for further GC-MS analysis.
For static culture broth, the pH was adjusted to 1.5 with 5 mol l
− 1
HCl, let stand for 2 h at room temperature (around 22 °C), and subsequently extracted with ethyl acetate for five times. The resultant extracts were combined and evaporated to dryness. The residue was dissolved in 5 ml ethyl acetate, and stored at 4 °C until GC-MS analysis.
GC-MS analysis
One milliliter of phenolic acid extract was evaporated to complete dryness in fume hood at room temperature (around 25 °C), then 0.4 ml of derivatization reagent [99% N, O-bis (trimethylsilyl) trifluoroacetamide + 1% trimethyl-chlorosilane] was added and heated for 1 h at 90 °C in water bath. The derivatives were filtered with 0.22 micron membrane after cooling, and the obtained filtrate was used for GC-MS analysis on GC7890A/MSD5975C (Agilent Co. Ltd., USA). The process from silylation to analysis was completed within 6 h. The analysis conditions were as follows: a DB-5ms (30 m × 250 μm × 0.25 μm) chromatographic column was used; the carrier gas was high purity helium (> 99.999%) with the gas flow rate of 1.2 ml min
− 1
; the temperatures of injection-port and interface were 250 °C and 290 °C, respectively; the injection volume was 2 µl, and the mode of sample injection was splitless. The MS conditions were as follows: an electron impact of 70 eV, the temperatures of ion source and quadrupole were 230 °C and 150 °C, respectively. The GC temperature programming was: 60 °C kept for 1 min, increased to 250 °C at a rate of 6 °C per minute, and held 250 °C for 3 min
15
.
Identification and quantitation of allelochemicals
Each chromatographic peak was identified by searching the NIST11 mass spectral databases. Compounds with a matching index above 800 were identified. Peak area normalization was conducted to calculate the relative percentage content of each component. We dissolved the three phenolic acid samples (4-coumaric acid, vanillic acid, and ferulic acid) in ethyl acetate and prepared them as standard solutions of phenolic acids with concentrations ranging from 0 to 100 µg ml
− 1
. These solutions were then analyzed using the aforementioned GC-MS method. The standard curves were constructed by plotting peak area versus concentration of the solutes. The concentration of 4-coumaric acid and other compounds (except vanillic acid and ferulic acid) was established by comparing the peak areas with those of 4-coumaric acid. The calibration curves for 4-coumaric acid, vanillic acid, and ferulic acid were y = 21,226,944 × (r
2
= 0.999), y = 20,723,254 × (r
2
= 0.998), and y = 17,945,284 × (r
2
= 0.999), respectively. The linear range of all three standard curves is 0-100 µg ml
− 1
. The result was expressed in ×10
− 3
g l
− 1
.
Bioassays of potential 15 allelochemicals
Based on the orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) model comprehensive analysis of the GC-MS results of soil samples, the top 15 compounds with values of variable importance for the projection (VIP) exceeding 1 were selected as the potential allelochemicals for further bioassays with an equal volume of distilled water as control (Table
2
; Supplementary Information
IV
).
M. sextelata
13,
M. eximia
SM, and
M. importuna
Y2 are all productive strains domesticated from wild morels. Their taxonomic status were all confirmed using sequence analysis of ribosomal DNA internal transcribed spacer
9
. All strains are available from Peixin He in Zhengzhou University of Light Industry. The highest contents of the potential allelochemicals in 6 samples of continuous cropping soil based on our GC-MS analysis and their one tenth and decuple concentrations were tested (Table
2
). A mycelial plug (4 mm diameter) from respective activated culture was placed on the center of tested CYM plate (diameter 9 cm) with different content of potential allelochemicals. The inoculated plates were incubated at 22 °C in the dark. The mycelial growth was observed every 12 h. The colony diameters were recorded when the mycelia of any treatment near the side of a CYM plate. The response index (
RI
) was utilized to assess the impact of the potential allelochemicals on mycelial growth of three tested morel strains.

− 1
) and vanillic acid and ferulic acid (1.0, 2.0, 5.0, 10.0, 20.0, and 50.0 µg ml
− 1
) were assayed with an equal volume of distilled water as control. The response index (
RI
) was used to assess the impact of the potential allelochemicals on mycelial growth of three morel strains.

Statistical analysis
Supervised orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was used to identify variations of metabolites between samples of continuous cropping soil and control. OPLS-DA and differential metabolite screening were performed using SIMCA14.1, Sartorius Stedim, Sweden. Variable important for the projection (VIP) value and Fold change (FC) were used to identify the differential levels of potential allelochemicals between continuous cropping soil and control. The variables with VIP > 1 and FC > 1.5 (or < 0.67) were suggested to be potential allelochemicals
56
,
57
.
The response index (
RI
) was utilized to assess the impact of the potential allelochemicals on mycelial growth of three morel strains. The
RI
is defined as 1-(C/T) if T ≥ C, and (T/C) − 1 if T < C, where T represents the colony diameter of the treatment group, and C represents the colony diameter of the control group. The
RI
scale ranges from −1 to +1, with a positive value indicating a promotional effect compared to the control group, and a negative value indicating an inhibitory effect of the treatment
9
,
58
.
The data are presented as mean ± standard error (S.E.) (
n
= 3). Data were subjected to one-way analysis of variance followed by the Duncan’s multiple range test. Probability values (
p
) < 0.05 and < 0.01 were regarded as statistically significant and highly significant, respectively. All analyses were carried out used IBM SPSS Statistics 22.
