INTRODUCTION
Owing to its fast growth rate, genetic tractability, ease and safety of use, and our extensive knowledge of the organism,
Escherichia coli
is one of the most widely used hosts for protein overexpression (Rosano and Ceccarelli
2014
; Schütz et al.
2023
). Post‐overexpression, protein purification is typically facilitated by affinity tags (e.g., poly‐histidine, glutathione transferase) encoded as either N‐ or C‐terminal fusions to the protein of interest. To produce a protein in
E. coli
, the corresponding gene is often cloned under the control of a T7 RNA polymerase promoter and expressed in a strain whose genome encodes this phage polymerase (Rosano and Ceccarelli
2014
). While this system allows very high expression, the foreign protein is often insoluble and present in inclusion bodies. This crippling issue has been mitigated by employing solubility‐enhancing tags. For example, Smt3, the small ubiquitin‐like (Ubl) modifier (SUMO) from
Saccharomyces cerevisiae
(lacking Ala‐Thr‐Tyr at the C‐terminus), is a popular solubility‐enhancing tag used either alone or in conjunction with other solubility‐enhancing tags (Guerrero et al.
2015
; Johnson et al.
1997
; Meluh and Koshland
1995
). The tertiary structure of SUMO is recognized by Ubl‐specific protease (Ulp) that cleaves after the P1 position of a C‐terminal Gly‐Gly↓Xxx (P2P1↓P1′) motif (Li and Hochstrasser
1999
; Li and Hochstrasser
2000
; Mossessova and Lima
2000
; Schwienhorst et al.
2000
). Because the Ulp protease tolerates any amino acid (except proline) at position P1′, its use allows generation of the native N‐terminus of the target protein; moreover, the Ulp protease is active under wide‐ranging cleavage conditions that include denaturants, salt, and detergents (Butt et al.
2005
; Malakhov et al.
2004
; Panavas et al.
2009
; Zhang et al.
2022
). These factors attest to the appeal of the SUMO tag‐Ulp protease pair in basic/applied research. When we sought to leverage this system for producing the
Salmonella
FraB deglycase, we made an unexpected discovery and describe it in this report.
In many bacteria (e.g.,
E. coli
), translation initiation depends on the interaction between the Shine–Dalgarno (SD) sequence, a component of the ribosome binding site (RBS) that is present upstream of the open reading frame (ORF), and the anti‐SD sequence, located at the 3′ end of the 16S rRNA in the 30S ribosomal subunit (Estrada et al.
2024
; Shine and Dalgarno
1974
; Shine and Dalgarno
1975
). SD‐like (SDL) sequences within ORFs are generally deleterious and appear to be subject to negative selection in Enterobacteriales (Hockenberry et al.
2018
). Indeed, SD sequences, which are not necessary for eukaryotic translational initiation, occur more frequently in eukaryotic coding sequences (CDSs) compared to prokaryotic counterparts (Whitaker et al.
2015
). As expected, introducing a non‐prokaryotic ORF with internal RBSs (iRBSs) in an
E. coli
host generates truncated proteins (Jennings et al.
2016
; Kim et al.
2008
; Swaminathan et al.
1996
; Whitaker et al.
2015
). The production of truncated proteins during heterologous gene expression decreases yield of the desired full‐length recombinant protein and undermines any purification step that requires the N‐terminal affinity tag. Here, we demonstrate that cryptic SDL sequences present within the widely used yeast SUMO tag promote internal translation initiation events (Figures
1
and
S1
, Supporting Information). To circumvent this limitation, and guided by native mass spectrometry, we designed, tested, and validated SUMO
NIT
(no internal translation), a sequence variant that eliminates the possibility of truncated SUMO fusion proteins. SUMO
NIT
will be useful to all researchers who seek to improve the yield, purity, and homogeneity of SUMO fusion proteins.

RESULTS
2.1
Truncated
FraB
expressed in
E. coli
upon fusion to yeast Smt3
The tobacco etch virus (TEV) protease recognizes the TEV recognition sequence (TRS) ENLYFQ↓X, where X represents any amino acid but proline (Kapust et al.
2002
). Previously, we purified His
6
‐TRS‐FraB
331
, where FraB was 331 amino acids, and digested it with TEV protease to obtain FraB (Sengupta et al.
2019
). However, a proteomics study revealed that FraB was only 325 amino acids inside the cell and not the 331 version predicted computationally (Brown et al.
2012
). For subsequent studies, we purified the 325‐long version, hereafter referred to as His
6
‐TRS‐FraB. Due to the repeated inability of TEV protease to cleave His
6
‐TRS‐FraB (Figure
S2
), we replaced the TRS with a SUMO tag to generate His
6
‐SUMO‐FraB (Figures
1
and
S1
and Tables
S1
and
S2
). Upon overexpressing His
6
‐SUMO‐FraB, we observed a 36‐kDa protein (later confirmed as untagged FraB) along with the 48‐kDa full‐length protein (Figure
S3
). However, no His
6
‐SUMO (12 kDa) was observed despite the preponderance of untagged FraB (36 kDa), suggesting that the presence of the latter may not be due to proteolysis. Moreover, since there is no endogenous SUMO protease in
E. coli
, we reasoned that the shorter polypeptide must arise from internal initiation of translation.
Internal RBS (iRBS) or cryptic SDL sequences in CDSs that can result in the production of truncated or frame‐shifted proteins expressed in
E. coli
have been previously reported (Jennings et al.
2016
; Jin et al.
2006
; Kim et al.
2008
; Whitaker et al.
2015
). Using the RBS Calculator, higher‐strength iRBS predictions were found at substantially greater (>200 fold) frequencies in
Saccharomyces cerevisiae
CDSs compared to
E. coli
CDSs (Whitaker et al.
2015
). Since SUMO (i.e., ubiquitin‐like protein Smt3) originates from
S. cerevisiae
, it seemed possible that an iRBS within SUMO results in production of untagged FraB. Therefore, the RBS Calculator (version 2.1) was used to determine the translation initiation rates for every start codon of the mRNA transcript encoding His
6
‐SUMO‐FraB (from the T7 promoter to the T7 terminator). Start positions at nucleotides 316, 319, 325, 349, and 394, all located within
fraB
, were predicted to have translational rates of 573, 803, 186, 19, and 0.7 arbitrary units, respectively, and yield proteins with molecular weights ranging from 33 to 36 kDa (Table
S3
). All other predicted start positions were discounted from further consideration as they would not account for the truncated product that we observed. Given the high expression levels of untagged FraB and the relatively low usage of near‐cognate start codons (i.e., GUG and UUG) compared to AUG, we focused on start positions 316 (Met106), 319 (Met107), and 325 (Met109) at the 5′ end of the FraB CDS (Hecht et al.
2017
); note that Met106 corresponds to the first Met in FraB.
2.2
Mutation of cryptic
SDL
sequences eliminates translation of untagged
FraB
We reasoned that mutation of start codons encoding Met106, Met107, and Met109 should drastically reduce, if not eliminate, the translation of untagged FraB. A multiple sequence alignment of FraB homologs by DeepMSA2 (Zhang et al.
2020
; Zheng et al.
2024
) revealed that leucines are found at positions corresponding to Met106, Met107, and Met109 (Figure
S4
). Due to this observation and the similar size and properties of methionine and leucine, we decided to mutate all three Met residues to Leu (FraB
ML
mutant with Met106Leu, Met107Leu, and Met109Leu substitutions). Indeed with His
6
‐SUMO‐FraB
ML
, no appreciable expression of untagged FraB was observed (Figure
S3
). Encouraged by this observation, we next sought to identify Shine–Dalgarno‐like (SDL) motifs (i.e., GGNGGN) within the CDS of the SUMO tag that might account for the truncated protein observed during expression of His
6
‐SUMO‐FraB.
We observed two SDL sequences, hereafter referred to as SDL1 and SDL2, that are located 40 and 0 nt, respectively, upstream of the AUG codon for Met106. SDL1 (GGAGGA) is a part of an AT
G
‐
GAG
‐
GA
T sequence that encodes Met‐Glu‐Asp and SDL2 (GGTGGA) encodes the conserved C‐terminal Gly‐Gly motif required for Ulp1 (SUMO protease) cleavage (Mossessova and Lima
2000
). Although a GAG → GAA mutation for Glu would disrupt the canonical SD motif in SDL1, we opted to introduce nonsynonymous mutations to reduce the possibility of weak anti‐SD:SD interactions. Because we were concerned about disrupting the 3D structure of SUMO and thereby preventing recognition and cleavage by Ulp1, we sought to identify candidate replacement residues for Met‐Glu‐Asp. DeepMSA2 was used to generate a multiple sequence alignment of SUMO (Zhang et al.
2020
; Zheng et al.
2024
). Based on a sequence logo (Figure
S4
), generated by WebLogo 3 using the DeepMSA2 results, we chose to mutate Met‐Glu‐Asp (ATG‐GAG‐GAT) to Leu‐Gln‐Asn (CTC‐CAG‐AAC) and tested this mutant for expression (Crooks et al.
2004
; Schneider and Stephens
1990
). Compared to His
6
‐SUMO‐FraB, there appears to be a modest, if any, reduction of untagged FraB upon expressing His
6
‐SUMO
ΔSDL1
‐FraB (Figures
1
and
S3
; see below for additional comments on the ΔSDL1 variant).
Next, we sought to mutate the Gly‐Gly motif in SDL2 such that SUMO could still be recognized and cleaved by Ulp1 while also eliminating internal translation initiation events. Zhang et al. (
2022
) demonstrated that the Gly‐Gly motif is not strictly obligatory for Ulp1 cleavage and other amino acids, depending on P1 and P1′, can be accommodated at the P2 position without altering the cleavage site. We mutated Gly‐Gly↓Met (GGT‐GGA‐ATG) to Ala‐Gly↓Met (GCT‐GGC‐ATG) to retain the native sequence of FraB following Ulp1 cleavage. Like His
6
‐SUMO‐FraB
ML
, expression of His
6
‐SUMO
ΔSDL2
‐FraB led to a reduction in untagged FraB and a slower‐migrating band was evident (Figure
S3
). Both SDL1 and SDL2 mutations were then introduced into SUMO to generate His
6
‐SUMO
NIT
‐FraB (no internal translation), whose expression showed neither untagged FraB nor a slower migrating band (Figure
S3
).

To establish the identity of the truncated proteins, we took advantage of the fact that

‐SUMO‐FraB monomer, the truncated monomer would be isolated. Therefore, we purified each His

To confirm that the mass change was due to oxidation, we performed surface‐induced dissociation (SID) (VanAernum et al.

These key insights from mass spectrometry on protease inhibitor adducts as well as oxidation events associated with sonication enabled us to make informed changes to our experimental design to eliminate covalent modifications that would have otherwise gone undetected (see Technical notes section in Data

3
DISCUSSION
Here, we demonstrate that at least two SDL sequences within SUMO facilitate internal translation initiation and contribute to considerable unanticipated sample heterogeneity. Affinity purification followed by SDS‐PAGE analysis revealed that at least two truncated products of different molecular weights are present. For His
6
‐SUMO‐FraB and His
6
‐SUMO
ΔSDL1
‐FraB, the faster migrating band predominates whereas the slower migrating band is only visible for His
6
‐SUMO‐FraB
ML
and His
6
‐SUMO
ΔSDL2
‐FraB. While we consider below the reasons underpinning generation of the truncated products, we emphasize that mutation of two cryptic SDLs within SUMO (SUMO
NIT
mutant) eliminated the production of all truncated products.
Native MS revealed molecular masses for undigested His
6
‐SUMO‐FraB and
Cth
Ulp1‐digested His
6
‐SUMO‐FraB, His
6
‐SUMO‐FraB
ML
, and His
6
‐SUMO
ΔSDL2
‐FraB dimers that deviated from their expected masses by +1222/1236 Da. These aberrant masses were absent from protein samples that were expressed from constructs with mutated iRBS1/SDL1. A partial sequence from the C‐terminus of SUMO [IIEAHREQI(G/A)G; Figure
1
] would account for 1204/1218 Da (i.e., smaller by 18 Da compared to observed masses). During translation, initiation factor 3 (IF3) prevents initiation from non‐canonical start codons, but AUU is known to serve as a start codon in
E. coli
with mutated IF3 (Lateana et al.
1993
; Singh et al.
2022
). It is conceivable that initiation with fMet‐tRNA
fMet
from non‐canonical start codons may be possible due to limiting IF3 concentrations during robust overexpression of a recombinant protein. Substitution of the first Ile in IIEAHREQI(G/A)G with Met would account for the 18 Da difference between the expected and observed masses. Therefore, we postulate that the slower migrating band is likely FraB fused to this partial sequence of SUMO and reflects use of iRBS1/SDL1 and an AUU codon that is decoded by fMet‐tRNA
fMet
(Figure
1a
). Surprisingly, the RBS Calculator did not predict translation initiation occurring from the non‐canonical start codon AUU, but instead predicted an upstream, canonical AUG (Hecht et al.
2017
). For optimal translation initiation, the sequence spacing between the SD and the A of the AUG start codon is 4–9 nt (Chen et al.
1994
; Hartz et al.
1991
); when the spacing between SD and the P‐site codon is greater than 6–9 nucleotides, the ribosomal translocation rate is decreased (Wakabayashi et al.
2020
). Consistent with these previous studies, the spacing between SDL1 and the Ile codon (AUU), which can act as a cryptic start codon, in SUMO is 7 nt (Hecht et al.
2017
).
Since FraB has three start codons (AUG) at its 5′ end, it is possible that three different versions of FraB are being made because of internal translation initiation. However, the three AUG start codons in FraB encoding Met106, Met107, and Met109, respectively, are 0, 3, and 9 nt away from SDL2, and 40, 43, and 46 nucleotides away from SDL1. Given the optimal spacing between SDL2 and the P‐site codon for initiating at Met109, it is not surprising that the Met109 variant predominates in the heterodimer formed with the full‐length His
6
‐SUMO‐FraB (Figure
S8
). We were surprised by the minor contribution from a species that initiated at Met106. Aside from the spacer length, however, the position and strength of secondary structures and A/U‐rich sequences proximal to the SD site can modulate translation (Evfratov et al.
2017
; Komarova et al.
2020
; Osterman et al.
2013
; Saito et al.
2020
); a weak SDL upstream of SDL2 is, perhaps, somehow utilized, once again highlighting limitations in our predictive capabilities.
Our MS data revealed untagged FraB starting from Met106 (minor) and Met109 (major), and surprisingly from Gly108 as well (not Met107). The species starting from Gly108 is, however, unlikely to be the result of internal translation initiation given that GGU is rarely used as a start codon (Hecht et al.
2017
). Prokaryotes initiate protein synthesis using formylmethionine where the formyl group is removed by formylmethionine deformylase followed by methionine removal by methionine aminopeptidase (MAP) (Wingfield
2017
). The radius of gyration for the side chain of the residue (P1′) adjacent to the N‐terminal methionine (P1) was proposed to determine MAP specificity; if P1′>1.3 Å, then MAP would not cleave P1 (Sherman et al.
1985
). The adjacent (P1′) residues for Met106, Met107, and Met109 (P1) are Met, Gly, and Lys with radii of gyrations of 1.8, 0, and 2.1 Å, respectively (Wingfield
2017
). Therefore, it is possible that untagged FraB starting from Met107 was generated but Met107 was subsequently removed by MAP since P1′ is Gly.
Our results reinforce the previously articulated theme that truncated proteins shortchange the goal of overexpressing and purifying recombinant proteins (Whitaker et al.
2015
). In addition to dampening yield of the full‐length protein, the erroneous output could result in proteins with partial or impaired function; however, the latter problem is circumvented by using N‐terminal affinity tags that ensure removal of (non‐oligomerizing) truncated species. While this simple purification strategy might account for why this problem of truncated proteins has not been described before with N‐terminal SUMO fusions, it is also conceivable that sequence/context‐dependent changes in mRNA structure may have occluded anti‐SD:SD interactions or start codon recognition. In fact, a non‐exhaustive literature review revealed some instances of potential truncations of proteins fused to SUMO at their N‐terminus, an inference based solely on SDS‐PAGE analyses (Hartwig et al.
2015
; Kuo et al.
2011
; Lai et al.
2021
; Tileva et al.
2016
). Nevertheless, with the correct confluence of factors, this issue presents a challenge in the case of homooligomers as exemplified by our FraB studies. Fusing the SUMO tag with FraB inadvertently resulted in internal translation initiation events that spawned four truncated proteins, in addition to the full‐length protein, thus leading to 15 different dimer variants, only two of which can be used for functional studies of the homodimer (Figure
S13
). Moreover, at least one of the truncated variants was caused by a ripple effect: internal initiation followed by the adventitious action of MAP.
To summarize, we mutated the two cryptic SDLs within SUMO (SUMO
NIT
) and showed that truncated proteins can be eliminated. There were no amino acid constraints that prevented us from disrupting iRBS activity. In fact, mutating the C‐terminal Gly‐Gly to Gly‐Ala in SUMO
ΔSDL2
and SUMO
NIT
did not impair Ulp1 cleavage. Thus, we propose SUMO
NIT
as a viable variant for recombinant protein purification because it eliminates internal translation initiation events even while permitting cleavage by Ulp1.

4.2

Heat‐shock
E. coli
SixPack cells were prepared as described by Yang et al. (
2022
), but the large‐scale cultures were grown in non‐autoclaved Dynamite media to avoid the formation of Maillard reaction products, which can inhibit growth (Byrd et al.
1999
; Lipinszki et al.

; Taylor et al.
2017
; Yang et al.
2022
). After thawing in ice for 10 min, 25 μL of cells were mixed with 18 μL of water, 5 μL of 5× KCM solution (0.5M KCl, 0.17M CaCl
2
, 0.25M MgCl
2
) and 2 μL (1–50 ng) of either pLANT‐2b–His
6
‐TRS‐FraB or the His
6
‐SUMO‐FraB variant and then incubated in ice for 30 min. Cells were then heat‐shocked at 42°C (in a water bath) for 90 s and chilled on ice for 2 min prior to the addition of 300 μL of SOC media. Following outgrowth in an Innova 4230 refrigerated incubator shaker (New Brunswick Scientific) at 37°C and 250 rpm shaking for 60 min, 350 μL of transformed cells were spread on a 37°C‐pre‐warmed SOB agar plate containing 35 μg/mL kanamycin and incubated for 20 h at 37°C. Three colonies were seeded in 5 mL of Dynamite media containing 100 mM dextrose and 35 μg/mL kanamycin at 37°C for 20 h.
For “small‐scale” expression of the His
6
‐SUMO‐FraB variants, 50 μL of seed culture was subcultured into two 25‐mL glass tubes each containing 5 mL of autoclaved Dynamite media supplemented with 35 μg/mL kanamycin; these two tubes served as the “uninduced” and “induced” cultures. All cultures were grown at 37°C and with 250 rpm shaking to an OD
600
(optical density at 600 nm) of 0.6–0.8, and then cooled in a 20°C water bath for 10 min. After addition of 1 mM IPTG (final) to only the induced cultures, both uninduced and induced cultures were grown for 20 h at 20°C and with 250 rpm shaking. Cells from 4 mL cultures were harvested in 1.5 mL tubes by centrifugation at 5000
g
for 5 min and the supernatants were discarded. The cell pellets were then resuspended with 1 mL of buffer A (50 mM Tris‐HCl [pH 7.5 at 20°C], 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT) by vortexing, and cells were chilled in an ice‐water bath for 10 min. Cells were lysed by sonication with a Vibra‐Cell Ultrasonic Processor (GEX130) using a small bit (Vibra‐Cell 630‐0422) for a total of 60 s: 2 s ON (pulse), 4 s OFF (interval); amplitude 50%. Aliquots of the whole cell lysates were saved in separate tubes. The lysate supernatants were clarified at 27,000
g
at −10°C for 5 min prior to transferring to new tubes. Each whole cell lysate and lysate supernatant (8 μL each) was mixed with 2 μL of 5× SDS‐loading buffer (Cold Spring Harbor recipe) and boiled for 5 min. All samples were loaded on a 15% (w/v) polyacrylamide‐SDS gel, electrophoresed for 45 min at 220 V, and then visualized with glacial acetic acid, methanol, water mixture (1:3:6) either containing (to stain) or lacking (to destain) 3 mM Coomassie Brilliant Blue R‐250.
For “mid‐scale” expression of the His
6
‐SUMO‐FraB mutants, each 1 mL seed culture was added to a 500‐mL Erlenmeyer glass flask containing 100 mL of 0.22 μm filter‐sterilized Dynamite media and 35 μg/mL kanamycin. Cultures were incubated at 37°C and with 250 rpm shaking in an Innova 4230 refrigerated incubator shaker (New Brunswick Scientific) to an OD
600
of 0.6–0.8 and induced as described above. Each culture was split into two 50 mL tubes, centrifuged at 2500
g
for 15 min, the supernatants were discarded, and all pellets were weighed (typically ~1 g per pellet) prior to storage at −80°C.
For “large‐scale” expression of His
6
‐TRS‐FraB, 5 mL of seed culture was added to a 2‐L baffled Erlenmeyer plastic flask containing 500 mL of non‐autoclaved Dynamite media and 35 μg/mL kanamycin. Post‐induction, cells were grown in a Lab Companion IS‐971R Shaker‐Incubator (Jeio Tech) for 20 h at 20°C and with 100 rpm shaking. The culture was split into two 50‐mL tubes, centrifuged at 2500
g
for 15 min and the supernatants were discarded. The harvested cell pellets were weighed (typically ~3.3 g per pellet) prior to storage at −80°C.

4.6
Identifying Shine–Dalgarno‐like sequences
The nucleotide sequence coding for His
6
‐SUMO‐FraB, including upstream sequences up to the T7 promoter and downstream sequences up to the T7 terminator, was submitted to the RBS Calculator (version 2.1) to predict translation initiation rates per start codon (Cetnar and Salis
2021
; Espah Borujeni et al.
2014
; Espah Borujeni et al.
2017
; Espah Borujeni and Salis
2016
; Reis and Salis
2020
; Salis et al.
2009
). Start codons below nucleotide 316 and above nucleotide 325 were not considered for potential iRBSs because they would generate translated products that were larger and smaller, respectively, than what was observed on SDS‐PAGE (~35 kDa). The Expasy Translate tool (
https://web.expasy.org/translate/
) was used to translate each sequence from the putative start position to the end of the T7 terminator to identify products with molecular weights ~35 kDa, as determined by the Expasy ProtParam tool (
https://web.expasy.org/protparam/
). For start positions that translated into ~35 kDa products, the upstream sequence was manually examined for Shine–Dalgarno‐like sequences (i.e., “GGNGGN” motifs).
4.7

2020

2024

4.8
Native mass spectrometry
His
6
‐TRS‐FraB (undigested and TEV‐digested) and His
6
‐SUMO‐FraB variants (undigested and
Cth
Ulp1‐digested) samples were solution exchanged into 200 mM ammonium acetate (AmAc) (Sigma‐Aldrich) using micro‐Bio‐spin P6 spin columns (BioRad). The pH was adjusted to 8 by adding ammonium hydroxide solution (Sigma Aldrich); we empirically determined that high‐quality mass spectra for FraB were pH dependent. All protein samples were diluted to a final concentration of 3 μM (monomeric concentration) prior to native MS measurements. MS experiments were performed on a Thermo Q Exactive ultra high mass range (UHMR) Orbitrap™ instrument, which was modified with a 4‐cm wide surface‐induced dissociation (SID) device located between the quadruple and C‐Trap (VanAernum et al.
2019
). Samples were introduced into the MS instrument by nano‐electrospray ionization, using glass capillary tips pulled in‐house using a P‐97 micropipette puller (Sutter Instruments). Three to five microliter of each sample was loaded into the tips, and the electrospray voltage was set between 0.5 and 1.0 kV. The mass spectrometer was operated in positive mode using the following tuning settings: source temperature: 200°C; detector optimization: low
m
/
z
; ion transfer target: high
m
/
z
; in‐source trapping: 60 V; HCD trap gas flow: 5–7; resolution: 25,000. SID experiments were performed after isolating the FraB dimer using the quadrupole, with a voltage of 45 V to dissociate dimer to monomer. Top‐down electron‐capture dissociation (ECD) experiment was performed on another Thermo Q Exactive UHMR Orbitrap instrument, which was modified with a custom ExD‐SID cell (Shaw et al.
2024
). Mass spectra were examined by using Xcalibur 4.1 (Thermo Scientific), and deconvolved using UniDec software (Marty et al.
2015
). Top‐down data were processed using ExDViewer (

) from Agilent Technologies, Inc.
