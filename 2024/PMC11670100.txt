Tumors have a complex organization that relies not only on the intrinsic characteristics of its primary cells, but also on their cross talk with immune cells and the tumor’s microenvironment. These interactions differ among patients because of their unique individual biological characteristics (e.g., age, sex, hormones, genetics, and epigenetics), environmental and lifestyle factors (e.g., stress, smoking, diet, physical activity), and tumor stromal composition, such as cancer-associated fibroblasts and extracellular matrix (
1
). Tumor stromal cells, along with immune cells in the tumor niche, secrete numerous cytokines, and chemokines (
2
) that can alter the repertoire of immune cells in the tumor microenvironment (
3
). During the evolution of a tumor, cytokines, among other factors, can modulate various tumoral pathways, including cell death, proliferative signals, growth suppressors’ effects, invasion, metastasis, and angiogenesis (
4
). These pathways, described as hallmarks of cancer (
5
–
7
), can promote tumorigenicity and enable evasion from the immune response. One mechanism of immune evasion adopted by cancer cells is the expression of surface inhibitory ligands, such as PD-L1 (
8
). It is well established that the anticancer activity of T cells and macrophages is modulated through the PD-1/PD-L1 and the CTLA4 pathways (
9
).
Cytokines can be either pro- or anticancerous, and it is hard to attribute this behavior to them being pro- or anti-inflammatory (
10
). Some cytokines have a dual role and can act to promote either cancer progression or suppression, depending on various factors. For instance, in prostate cancer, the proinflammatory cytokine interleukin 6 (IL-6) inhibits proliferation of LNCaP cells in a paracrine manner by activating signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3). In striking contrast, it stimulates the growth of PC-3, DU145, and TSU cells in an autocrine manner, leaving STAT3 inactive (
11
). In an as yet a different example, IL-6 elevates the expression of cyclooxygenase 2 (COX-2) and prostaglandin E(2) [PGE(2)] in the prostatic intraepithelial neoplasia cell model. This results in activation of STAT3. In this case, the activation of STAT3 by IL-6 increases the proliferation of cells (
12
).
Stromal cell-derived factor 1 (SDF1), known also as chemokine CXCL12, was initially described as a cytokine expressed in the bone marrow where it orchestrates the localization and retention of hematopoietic progenitors and stem cells (
13
). Later, it was shown to be an essential factor in numerous normal and pathological pathways, such as embryogenesis, angiogenesis, inflammation, etc. (
14
). In human cells, CXCL12 is expressed in six isoforms (α, β, δ, γ, ε, ϕ) generated through alternative splicing (
15
). They are differentially expressed in various cells, tissues, and organs and can affect both physiological and pathophysiological outcomes (
16
). As for its cell surface receptors, CXCL12 primarily interacts with the G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) C-X-C chemokine receptor type 4 (CXCR4), which when activated, regulates several intracellular cascades responsible for migration, proliferation, and cell survival (
17
). CXCR4 can exist as both monomer and dimer, resulting in different outcomes in different cellular processes. Binding of the CXCL12 ligand to its receptor shifts the equilibrium toward dimer formation (
18
). Additionally, CXCL12 interacts with the atypical chemokine receptor 3 (ACKR3, CXCR7), which leads to its scavenging along with its bound ligand (
19
). Furthermore, CXCR4 can form a heterodimer with CXCR7 (CXCR4/CXCR7), resulting in constitutive recruitment of β-arrestin and enhanced signaling through ERK1/2, p38, and MAPK. This interaction results in faster migration of the cells compared to the cellular expression of the homodimers (
20
). In another example, in colorectal cancer Caco-2 cells, CXCL12 promotes metastasis by upregulating the transcription factor homeobox B5 (HOXB5) via increased phosphorylation of ERK1/2 (
21
).
Unlike the most common activities of CXCL12 that promote progression of tumors, it also displays less common opposite characteristics. In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, the expression of CXCL12 was significantly repressed compared to healthy exocrine ducts (
22
). Both in vitro and in vivo studies showed that the overexpression of CXCL12 in human pancreatic cancer cell lines decreased their migration ability, metastasis, and tumor growth (
22
). The finding that CXCL12 exists in mono-, di-, and oligomeric species, may provide an explanation for the finding that it regulates signaling processes in distinct and at times opposing ways. For instance, in studies using colorectal carcinoma or murine melanoma-bearing mice, the administration of CXCL12 prolonged their survival by restricting metastasis. The dimeric form caused rapid activation of ERK1/2 but recruited β-arrestin to a small extent which did not promote migration significantly (
23
,
24
). In contrast, following interaction with CXCR4, only the monomeric form of the cytokine was able to recruit β-arrestin-2 and inhibit cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) signaling, positively regulating actin polymerization and cell migration (
23
,
24
).
The variety of CXCL12 isoforms and their different dimerized forms, along with the numerous possible combinations of their receptors, result in a complex regulation of signaling pathways and their effects on cellular processes.
As mentioned, binding of CXCL12 to CXCR4 alters diverse G protein-coupled signal transduction pathways which involve phosphatidyl-inositol-3-kinase (PI3K), mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), and Ras-MEK1/2-ERK1/2 pathways (
25
,
26
). For example, it was shown that in HEK-293 cells, CXCL12 activates CXCR4, causing oscillated phosphorylation of ERK1/2. In contrast, its binding to the heterodimer CXCR4/ACKR3 significantly slows it down (
27
). Another group demonstrated in a
35
S-GTP-γS binding assay, that following stimulation of HEK-293 T cells with CXCL12, there is reduced activation of G proteins after overexpression of CXCR4/CXCR7 compared to cells expressing CXCR7 alone (
28
). In addition, it has been shown that the CXCL12/CXCR4 axis promotes the growth of malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumors via stimulation of the PI3K and β-catenin signaling pathways, leading to subsequent Cyclin D1 expression and G1/S cell cycle transition (
29
).
In our previous study on the tumor suppressive effect of the p50 subunit of the NF-κB transcription factor, we showed that part of the effect is due to secretion of cytokines by the p50-expressing tumoral cells and recruitment of macrophages and NK cells to the tumor (
30
). One interesting cytokine known to be involved in regulation of tumorigenesis is CXCL12. In this study, we are adding yet additional lines of experimental evidence that CXCL12 can be tumor suppressive. That, in contrast to its more “canonical” tumor-promoting activities. We show that the cytokine inhibits activation of the Ras oncogene and the phosphorylation of ERK1/2, and leads to a reduction in c-Myc levels. Moreover, in our cell models, excess of CXCL12 promotes cell cycle arrest. In animal models, we showed that CXCL12 expression leads to shrinkage of xenografts derived from glioblastoma and breast cancer cells via downregulation of PD-L1 and recruitment of cytotoxic T cells. Further, it resulted in suppression of the metastatic process.

Overexpression of CXCL12 Inhibits Tumor Growth in Mice Via Two Independent Mechanisms.
The finding that overexpression of CXCL12 leads to cell cycle arrest in cultured cells, prompted us to study this effect in a mouse model. For this purpose, we generated human U87-MG and U87-MG CXCL12 xenografts in Balb/c nude mice (which lack T cells) and in Rag2/IL2RG double knockout (R2G2) mice (which lack almost all immune cells). Remarkably, despite significant shrinkage of U87-MG CXCL12 tumors compared to control, the decrease of CXCL12-expressing xenografts was similar in both species of mice (
Fig. 4

This limitation would impede studying the involvement of the complete immune system in the tumor suppressive effect of CXCL12. To evaluate the involvement of the intact immune system in the effect of CXCL12 beyond the effect of the cytokine on cell cycle, we generated mouse PyMT V0 or PyMT CXCL12-derived xenografts in wild type C57/Bl mice. Indeed, immunohistochemical staining of C57/Bl tumors demonstrated strong infiltration of cytotoxic T cells (CD3/CD8/Granzyme positive) into the xenografts that express CXCL12. To further elucidate the contribution of the host’s T cells to the tumor suppressive effect of CXCL12, we monitored the expression of PD-L1 in U87-MG and PyMT cells. We found that in both cell lines, the level of PD-L1 was significantly reduced following expression of CXCL12 (

We suggest that overexpression of CXCL12 reduces expression of PD-L1 in U87-MG and PyMT cells, enhancing the host’s immune response to the inoculated tumors.
Discussion
An unsolved enigma in cancer research is the Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hyde behavior of certain proteins that under some circumstances act as tumor promoters and under other as tumor suppressors. In the case of p53, the WT protein is a bona fide tumor suppressor, whereas certain mutants display a tumor-promoting characteristics (gain of function) (
35
). While a mutation in a protein can explain its dual behavior, for other proteins it is the same molecule that in different cell types or microenvironments display opposing effects (see also under
Introduction
). Little is known on the mechanisms that underlie this phenomenon. For the cytokine CXCL12, in most cases, it was described as a tumor promoter (
36
,
37
), yet, in fewer cases, it was shown to restrict tumor growth. In the current study, we show that in three cultured cell lines of which two were also inoculated in mice to generate xenografts, the cytokine is a tumor suppressor. Importantly, we dissected the suppressive mechanisms. Using both U87-MG glioblastoma cells and PyMT breast cancer cells, we showed that when they express CXCL12, the secreted cytokine suppresses anchorage-independent growth in an autocrine manner. Proteomic analysis revealed that the suppressive effect is most probably due to modulation of cell cycle-regulating pathways (
Fig. 1
). Another and independent characteristic of malignant cells that was shown to be suppressed by the cytokine is migration and seeding of metastases (
Fig. 2
). Deeper analysis of the affected pathways revealed suppression at both transcription and modification levels. Thus, we observed, for example, reduced expression of c-Myc and Cyclin D1, and reduction in the level of phosphorylated MAPK ERK1/2 (
Fig. 3
). In animal model, expression of the cytokine by the tumoral cells attracted cytotoxic T cells that exerted their suppressive effect on the tumors. Augmenting the effect of the T cells was probably the suppression that CXCL12 exerted on the expression of the PD-L1 immune checkpoint protein (
Fig. 4
).
Thus, it appears that the cytokine exerts its tumor suppressive effect by employing different and importantly independent mechanisms. In isolated cultured cells, it suppresses cell cycle. However, in a tumor grown in the intact animal, it exerts a chemoattractant effect to recruit immune system cells and facilitating their attack on the tumoral cells by suppressing the expression of immune checkpoint proteins. In an already growing tumor, activation of expression of the cytokine results in its regression (
Fig. 5
).
In this study, we shed a mechanistic light on the tumor suppressive effect of the cytokine CXCL12 which is a much less known characteristic of the protein than its tumor-promoting activity. At the same time, we have failed, thus far, to solve the enigma of the mechanisms that underlie the opposing behavior of the protein under different microenvironments and cancer types. This will require a systematic search for differences in critical growth-regulating pathways in cells in which CXCL12 is tumor suppressive compared to cells in which it is a tumor promoter. Regardless of the mechanisms that underlie the dual effect, as CXCL12 (and probably other factors that have a similar behavior) has become a target for cancer therapy (
36
,
37
) – it will be important, for each cancer patient, to identify the role the cytokine plays in his/her tumorigenesis in order not to target an effective tumor suppressor. Attesting to the different activities of even a single cytokine, is the use of different inhibitors to CXCR4. While AMD3100 is being used mostly to mobilize stem cells from the bone marrow to the peripheral blood so they can be collected for transplantation, another inhibitor – Mavorixafor – is used to treat WHIM syndrome which is an immune system disorder that affects the ability of the patient to combat bacterial and viral infections, in particular human papillomavirus.

Migration Assay.
The growth medium of exponentially growing U87-MG or PyMT cells that expressed either an empty vector (V0) or CXCL12, was changed to a medium that contains only 1% FBS. After 24 h, cells were placed in the upper chamber, and allowed to migrate (at 37 °C for 3 h) into the lower compartment that contained DMEM supplemented with serum (10%). Antibody that neutralizes CXCL12, or NUCC-390 (250 μM) were added to the cells 30 min before they were transferred to the upper chamber. 2 × 10

The cell cycle modeling software FlowJo was used for analyzing the cell cycle stages.
qRT-PCR.
RNA was extracted from U87-MG cells that overexpressed either CXCL12, or an empty vector (V0) using an RNA purification kit. Subsequently, the RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using a cDNA Synthesis Kit. qRT-PCR was then carried out using the TaqMan Gene Expression Assay. The assay was performed in triplicates, using TaqMan primers designed for CXCL12, CyclinD1, c-Myc, and PD-L1 genes. HPRT gene served as a control.
Cytokine Profiling.
U87-MG or PyMT cells that overexpress either an empty vector (V0), or CXCL12 were grown in DMEM supplemented with FBS (1%). After 48 h, the conditional media were collected and subjected to incubation with the Proteome Profiler Human XL Cytokine Array, or the Proteome Profiler Mouse XL Cytokine Array membranes following the manufacturer’s protocol.
Immunohistochemistry.
Immunohistochemical staining of formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded, 5 µm tissue sections of U87-MG-, or PyMT-derived tumors grown in mice, was conducted using a Ventana BenchMark ULTRA IHC/ISH system. The sections were subjected to immunostaining for CD3 (1:100), CD8 (1:200), Granzyme B (1:100), or ki-67 (1:100) antibodies. Visualization was performed using the Ventana iVIEW DAB detection system in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols.
Inhibition of CXCR4 Receptor.
U87-MG V0 or U87-CXCL12 cells were grown in DMEM, supplemented with Doxycycline (0.5 μg/mL) for 5 h, in the presence or absence of AMD3100 (15 μM).
Proteolysis and Mass Spectrometry Analysis of U87-MG V0 and U87-MG CXCL12 Cells.
Cells were lysed in urea (8.5 M), ammonium bicarbonate (400 mM), and DTT (10 mM); sonicated twice; and centrifuged. Protein concentration was measured using Bradford reagent (Pierce). The samples were reduced (60 °C for 30 min), modified with iodoacetamide (35.2 mM) in ammonium bicarbonate (100 mM, room temperature for 30 min in the dark), and digested (overnight at 37 °C) with modified trypsin (Promega) in a 1:50 (weight) enzyme-to-substrate ratio dissolved in Urea (1.5 M) and ammonium bicarbonate (66 mM). An additional second digestion with trypsin was carried out for 4 h at 37 °C in a 1:100 (weight) enzyme-to-substrate ratio. The tryptic peptides were desalted using C18 (Top tip, Glygen) tip, dried and resuspended in formic acid (0.1%). The peptides were resolved by reverse-phase chromatography on 0.075 × 300 mm fused silica capillaries (J&W) packed with Reprosil reversed phase material (Dr. Maisch GmbH, Germany). They were eluted (at flow rate of 0.15 μL/min) with linear 180 min gradient of 5 to 28% acetonitrile with formic acid (0.1%) in water, 15 min gradient of 28 to 95%, and 25 min at 95% acetonitrile with formic acid (0.1%) in water. Mass spectrometry was performed by the Q Exactive HFX mass spectrometer (Thermo) as described elsewhere (
41
). The mass spectrometry data were analyzed using the MaxQuant software 1.5.2.8 (
42
) for peak picking and identification using the Andromeda search engine, searching against the human proteome from the Uniprot database with mass tolerance of 6 ppm for the precursor masses and 20 ppm for the fragment ions. Oxidation on methionine and protein N-terminus acetylation were accepted as variable modifications, and carbamidomethyl on cysteine was accepted as static modification. Minimal peptide length was set to six amino acids and a maximum of two mis cleavages was allowed. Peptide- and protein-level false discovery rates were filtered to 1% using the target-decoy strategy. The data were quantified by label free analysis using the same software with “match between runs” option. Protein tables were filtered to eliminate the identifications from the reverse database and common contaminants. Statistical analysis of the identification and quantification results were carried out using Perseus 1.6.10.43 software (
43
). Annotation enrichment was done by the string tool (
https://string-db.org/
) and the David bioinformatics package (
https://david.ncifcrf.gov/
). Data are available via ProteomeXchange with identifier PXD054783.
