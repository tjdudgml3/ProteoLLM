Introduction
Fusion gene results from the amalgamation of segments from two separate genes due to chromosomal translocations or deletions [
1
]. Fusion genes are capable of generating abnormal transcripts or proteins that may initiate or promote tumorigenesis [
2
]. The relationship between fusions and tumor phenotypes renders these genetic anomalies critical for both the diagnosis and subclassification of tumors, enhancing the precision of risk stratification and providing targeted therapeutic opportunities. In 2018, Gao et al. showed the prevalence and diversity of gene fusions by identifying 25,664 fusions in 9,624 tumor samples from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), which not only highlighted the diagnostic and prognostic value of fusions but also pointed to their significant therapeutic implications. For example, BCR-ABL1 fusion leads to constitutive protein tyrosine kinase activity and downstream signaling of the PI3K and MAPK pathways, which enables cells to evade apoptosis and achieve increased cell proliferation [
3
–
7
].
Tumor neoantigen is a peptide sequence generated by genetic variations across the tumor genome, the binding of neoantigens to human leukocyte antigen (HLA) is crucial for antigen presentation, which is a necessary prerequisite for effective T cell recognition [
8
–
10
]. Neoantigens in tumor treatment extend beyond conventional therapeutic targets and minimize the risk of autoimmune reactions [
11
], enhancing the diversity of neoantigens accessible for immunotherapeutic strategies (immune checkpoint inhibitors [
12
], personalized tumor vaccines [
13
], and adoptive T cell therapy [
14
]). Current studies primarily focus on neoantigens derived from single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) and insertions or deletions (Indels). Fusion genes generate novel open reading frames that produce peptides distinctly different from self-antigens [
15
,
16
], offering new avenues for immunotherapy. Compared to SNV&Indels, gene fusions can generate more neoantigens [
17
]. The heightened immunogenicity of fusion-derived neoantigens, suggests that gene fusions may represent promising targets for the next generation of cancer immunotherapies [
17
]. BCR-ABL in chronic myelogenous leukemia and SYT-SSX1 in synovial sarcoma underscore how fusion-derived neoantigens can significantly augment the tumor neoantigen repertoire and provide potent targets for therapeutic development [
18
].
Gene fusion neoantigens have been applied in tumor immunotherapy. Pinilla-Ibarz [
19
] demonstrated neoantigen-specific T-cell responses in some CML patients. A phase II trial showed evidence of safety and immunogenic response but not clinical efficacy [
20
]. Another trial involved patients with Ewing sarcoma or alveolar rhabdomyosarcoma receiving peptide-pulsed vaccinations [
21
]. In 2008, a study on patients with Ewing's sarcoma (ESFT) or alveolar rhabdomyosarcoma (AR) used autologous T cells, influenza vaccinations, dendritic cell (DC)-loaded peptides, and recombinant human interleukin-2 [
22
]. Translocation breakpoint peptides were generated and some patients had E7-specific responses. Patients had minimal toxicity and better survival with immunotherapy [
22
]. Jennifer [
23
] used TMPRSS: ERG type VI (2:4) fusion peptides on dendritic cells to expand specific T cells, which occurs in 13% of prostate tumor patients. Yang identified neoantigens from MYB-NFIB gene fusions that bind to HLA-A02:01 and generate cytotoxic T-cell responses for many patients. Yang[
16
] identified DEK-AFF2 gene fusion as a driver in metastatic head and neck cancer, and its neoantigens elicited a specific cytotoxic T-cell response. A peptide from CBFB-MYH11 fusion protein is immunogenic in HLA-B40:01 + donors [
24
]. In vivo, CD8 + T cells specific to CBFB-MYH11 can kill AML cell lines and samples and control AML in a mouse model [
24
]. High-avidity CBFB-MYH11 epitope-specific T cell receptors (TCRs) in CD8 + T cells have antileukemic activity in vitro[
24
]. The FusionNeoAntigen platform has cataloged approximately 500,000 fusion-specific neoantigens, establishing a critical resource for the identification of highly immunogenic peptides suitable for vaccine development or adoptive cell therapies [
13
,
25
–
33
]. However, only a limited percentage of these gene fusions involve druggable targets like ALK, RET, and FGFR [
2
], so there is an urgent need to develop new tumor-specific fusion neoantigen.
Breast cancer (BRCA) is the most frequently diagnosed malignancy and the fifth leading cause of cancer-related death among women worldwide [
34
]. While advancements in current immunotherapy offer promising treatment options for BRCA, challenges such as limited efficacy, significant side effects, and resistance remain. Targeting neoantigens is a beneficial new immunotherapy in BRCA therapy. Hashimoto et al. evaluated the use of RNA-seq data from six breast cancer patients for neoantigen detection [
35
]. Animesh et al. also employed a multi-omics approach combining WES and RNA-seq to assess the neoantigen burden in breast cancer[
36
]. Their pipeline predicted 434 unique neoantigens from 237 genes, and most of them were patient-specific and MHC class I-specific. Mistretta et al. developed a framework to detect neoantigens from chimeric RNA sequences in cancerous tissue [
37
]. They mined RNA sequence reads from 75 breast tumors, analyzed MHC I binding affinity, and verified immunogenicity. Barroso-Sousa et al. [
38
] found high mutational load in breast cancer patients correlates with higher neoantigen burden, increased cytolytic activity, and expression of GZMA and PRF1 genes. Supports TMB-neoantigen burden relationship in breast cancer. Narang et al. [
39
] reported the highest potential neoantigen number in TNBC. High neoantigen load in TNBC increases chemosensitivity and immunotherapy response via PD-1 upregulation. Ren et al. [
40
] predicted neoantigen distribution in HLA genotypes. This study systematically analyzed fusion genes in BRCA and their potential in prognosis and immunotherapy, revealing their significant value as diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic markers, enhancing the understanding of BRCA fusion genes and their functions, laying a foundation for neoantigen discovery and immunotherapy development.
Methods
Data collection and preprocessing
Gao et al. [
2
] performed fusion detection using STAR-Fusion [
1
] (
https://github.com/STAR-Fusion/STAR-Fusion/wiki
, default parameter, the human genome: build 38) for RNA-seq data from 9,624 tumor samples and 713 normal samples from TCGA, then filtered fusions reported in normal samples (TCGA normal samples, GTEx tissues, and non-cancer cell [
41
]). We downloaded the Fusion list from Gao et al. [
2
] (
https://doi.org/
10.1016/j.celrep.2018.03.050.) and extracted these fusions (n = 5410, Table S1) that occur in breast cancer (BRCA) patients (n = 864).
Fusion gene analysis
Statistics were performed on the chromosomal breakpoints for partner genes of each fusion gene (n = 5410, Table S1), distinguishing between intrachromosomal (within the same chromosome) and inter-chromosomal (between different chromosomes) fusion events.
Prognostic analysis
The DeepSurv [
42
] model was utilized to assess the prognostic significance. DeepSurv (
https://github.com/jaredleekatzman/DeepSurv
) is a Cox proportional risk deep neural network and a state-of-the-art survival analysis approach for modeling the interaction between patient covariates and treatment outcomes [
42
]. It implements a deep learning generalization of the Cox proportional hazards model using Theano and Lasagne [
42
]. This approach has advantages over traditional Cox regression as it does not require a priori selection of covariates but instead learns them adaptively [
42
].
We employed the clinical data (including age, gender, tumor stage, survival time, and survival status) of BRCA patients (n = 827) and the PCA dimension reduction data of gene expression for prognosis prediction. The PCA dimension reduction data of gene expression is derived from the first 10 latitudes after PCA dimension reduction of FPKM data in the RNAseq dataset (from
https://tcga-xena-hub.s3.us-east-1.amazonaws.com/download/TCGA.BRCA.sampleMap%2FHiSeqV2.gz
). Using DeepSurv predict the survival risk of patients and determine which fusion genes or fusion proteins have a significant impact on patient survival.
We randomly divided the patient data into a training set (60%, n = 497), a test set (20%, n = 165), and a validation set (20%, n = 165). The model parameters are set as follows: the number of layers is 32, the number of nodes is 32, and the learning rate of the neural network is 0.01. Predict a survival risk table of the survival probability of each patient at 458 time nodes. The concordance index (C-index: 0.87) to evaluate the performance of the model. Calculate the average survival risk of patients with or without fusion genes and use the T-test as the significance test. The Kaplan–Meier curve shows the survival rate of patients with different fusion genes.
Protein sequence
AGFusion [
43
] (
https://github.com/murphycj/AGFusion
) was utilized to annotate gene fusions, which required inputs included the reference genome (hg38), the name of partner genes, and the fusion junction coordinates (breakpoints). The outputs by AGFusion [
43
] included: (1) FASTA files of cDNA, CDS, and protein sequences; (2) Visualization of protein domains and exon structures of the fusion transcript; (3) Tables of protein features and exon coordinates in the fusion; (4) Visualization of exon structures and protein domains of the wild-type versions of the fusion gene partners. This process identified 3,866 proteins expressed by the fusion genes. Then we compared this fusion protein list (n = 3,866) with UniProt/PFAM domain database [
44
] (
http://www.uniprot.org/database/
) to annotate the protein domains encoded by the fusion genes to understand their potential functions.
Neoantigen prediction
Peptides were extracted from the fusion proteins by taking a maximum of 8 amino acids upstream and downstream of the fusion junction point (breakpoints), considering the longest peptide to bind MHC molecules is 15 amino acids. Sequence filtering involved deleting sequences with a 100% match in the UniProtKB/Swiss-Port [
45
] database to ensure neoantigen peptides are sequences, resulting in 3,198 new fusion peptide segments.
The netMHCpan-4.1 [
46
] (
https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/service.php?netMHCpan-4.1
) was employed to predict the binding of peptide sequences to 112 HLA alleles (Table S5), which are sorted out the most frequent HLA alleles around the world summarized by Alicia et.al [
47
] in their research. Strongly binding peptides (p-MHCI, n = 7,988) were filtered using Rank_EL < 0.5. TransPHLA [
48
] (
https://github.com/TransPHLA
) is a transformer- and attention mechanism-based model to predict peptide–HLA class I. TransPHLA consists of four modules, the embedding block generates embeddings for polypeptides and HLA; the Encoder block with a masked multi-head self-attention mechanism and a feature optimization block processes these embeddings; the output feature representations are connected to form p-MHCI embedding; the projection block predicts the binding score of p-MHCI after the p-MHCI embedding passes through the Encoder block. Taking HLA-A*02:01 and the peptide QLVDFQWKL as an example, TransPHLA predicts it as Binding (the value is 1 in the table), and the Predicted score (predicted as the probability of binding) is 1.0 (The threshold to define a predicted binder is at 0.5). The brighter the color (yellow), the more important the site is to the prediction result. Further using the TransPHLA tool predicted 7,971 Rp-MHC I peptides binding to MHC, forming a comprehensive dataset of BRCA neoantigens for vaccine development.
Mass spectrometry (MS) proteomic data of BRCA patients from the Proteomic Data Commons (PDC000173, n = 950) were acquired to confirm the presence of predicted neoantigen peptides. We employed the R language to perform mass spectrometry data analysis, focusing on the extraction and identification of peptide peaks. Using the mzR (
https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/mzR.html
) packages, we processed mzML format data to extract peaks within specified m/z and retention time ranges.
Structure prediction
The three-dimensional structure is predicted using the AlphaFold2 online computing platform in Google Colab (
https://colab.research.google.com/github/sokrypton/ColabFold/blob/main/AlphaFold2.ipynb
), and then the structure is visualized using PyMOL (Version 3.0), and measure the hydrogen bond length at the docking site of the docked structure and label the amino acid sites involved in docking.
Statistical analysis
Data analysis and visualization were conducted using R and Python. This included analyzing data distribution, fusion gene frequencies, and their impact on patient prognosis.

43

Seventy-four fusion proteins demonstrated a significant correlation with patient survival (Table S3) by the T-test which employed as the significance test to examine patients’ survival rate with or without fusion proteins. Among them, the top 20 significant prognostic fusion proteins are listed in Fig.

Generate potential neoantigens library from fusion peptides
Immunotherapy targeting tumor-specific neoantigens holds significant potential in cancer treatment [
51
]. Gene fusion events in tumor cells generate fusion peptides that can serve as neoantigen candidates [
52
]. To ensure that the fusion peptide is a novel sequence produced by the patient's fusion gene, the following steps were employed (Fig.
3
a, b): (1) Peptide Extraction: Since the longest peptide that binds to MHC molecules is 15 amino acids, a maximum of 8 amino acids upstream and 8 amino acids downstream of the fusion protein joining point were extracted; (2) Sequences with a 100% match in the Swissprot database [
45
] were deleted, resulting in unique fusion peptide lists (n = 3,198, Table S4). Using netMHCpan-4.1 (predicted by artificial neural networks), we predicted the binding between fusion peptide sequences and MHC class I (Table S5). Extract peptides that strongly bind to MHC (HLA) class I (p-MHCI, n = 7,988) (filtered by Rank_EL < 0.5) (Fig.
3
b, Table S6). Further prediction by TransPHLA (based on the transformer) resulted in credible Rp-MHCI peptides (n = 7,971, Table S7), forming a BRCA neoantigens library (Fig.
3
b). We analyzed the distribution of potential neoantigens across BRCA patients to assess the treatment scope, there are 88.41% of BRCA patients had Rp-MHCI peptides.

Discussion
Our study highlights the crucial role of fusion genes and their derived neoantigens in the prognosis and treatment of BRCA, offering new avenues for personalized medicine and immunotherapy.
Fusion genes in BRCA prognosis and treatment
The significant correlation of 74 fusion proteins with patient survival suggests that these biomarkers can stratify patients based on their prognosis. For instance, the TDRD3-DIAPH3 fusion protein is associated with better survival outcomes, while the TNRC18-RNF216 fusion protein correlates with poorer prognosis. The use of AGFusion to generate full-length cDNA, CDS, and protein sequences, and to annotate them with Pfam domains [
44
], provided a comprehensive view of the functional implications of these fusion genes. The identification of the most active fusion partners, such as SHANK2, FBXL20, and BCAS3, further illustrates the potential targets for therapeutic intervention.
Neoantigens derived from fusion genes
The exploration of neoantigens derived from gene fusions in BRCA patients is particularly promising for immunotherapy. Neoantigens, being tumor-specific, offers a unique target for immune system activation. Our study utilized advanced prediction tools like netMHCpan and TransPHLA to identify and validate neoantigen peptides that can bind to MHC class I molecules. The subsequent validation with mass spectrometry data confirmed the presence of these neoantigens, making them viable candidates for vaccine development. A notable example is the neoantigen derived from the TBC1D4–COMMD6 fusion protein, which significantly improved median patient survival. This finding is pivotal as it demonstrates that targeting fusion gene-derived neoantigens can enhance patient outcomes, a strategy that could be extended to other cancers with similar genomic alterations.
Implications for BRCA treatment
The therapeutic implications of our findings are multifaceted. First, the identification of fusion genes as prognostic markers can guide treatment decisions, allowing for more personalized and effective therapeutic strategies. For example, patients with poor prognosis fusion proteins like TNRC18-RNF216 might benefit from more aggressive treatment regimens or novel therapeutic approaches. Second, the development of neoantigen-based vaccines offers a promising avenue for BRCA treatment. The validation of 15 neoantigen peptides in mass spectrometry data provides a robust foundation for designing personalized cancer immunotherapy.
Limitations and future directions
Due to technical limitations, our current understanding of gene fusion neoantigens has certain deficiencies. All neoantigen prediction tools are limited. Possible reasons include biases in currently available epitope data towards past prediction methods and in vitro experimental validation techniques. For instance, multimer staining for neoantigen-specific T cells cannot cover all MHC types, and there are biases in mass spectrometry (MS) experiments where the data known as eluted ligands are not shared with T-cell epitopes [
53
]. While 13–15 mer peptides presented by MHC II are potential neoantigens with the ability to induce cytotoxic T-cell responses [
54
], current major studies mainly focus on the most common 9–11 mer fusion neoantigens presented by MHC I. This is because the binding of peptides by MHC II is more promiscuous than that by MHC I, making it more challenging to identify binding motifs [
55
]. There is a lack of experiments to verify the impact of fusion genes on tumors and the immunogenicity of antigen peptides, and there is no one-to-one corresponding proteomic data to confirm the expression of fusion proteins. Multi-dimensional omics data for exploring the specific antigen presentation process is also lacking, relying solely on mass spectrometry proteomics data to predict neoantigen candidates cannot determine whether they can truly play the role of neoantigens. Neoantigen candidates in mass spectrometry data cannot determine whether they can be effectively processed and presented on HLA molecules or recognized by tumor-specific T cells. For neoantigens to exert antitumor effects, they need to be presented on HLA molecules and recognized by tumor-specific T cells [
56
–
58
]. Relying only on mass spectrometry proteomics data for neoantigen prediction is insufficient, there is needful immunoprecipitation and flow cytometry to detect the binding of neoantigens to HLA, as well as T cell cloning and functional assays to verify T cell recognition of neoantigens should be combined.
Future research should focus on further elucidating the functional roles of these fusion genes and their derived proteins in BRCA pathogenesis. Additionally, clinical trials assessing the efficacy of neoantigen-based vaccines in BRCA patients are essential to translate these findings into clinical practice. The integration of fusion gene analysis with other omics data could also provide a more comprehensive understanding of BRCA biology and treatment resistance mechanisms. Finally, binding-based methods ignore gene expression levels and protease cleavage signatures, which play an important role in HLA presentation. Developing more accurate methods to predict and verify the function of neoantigens, including improving mass spectrometry technology, integrating multi-omics data, and developing new experimental models. In-depth research on the mechanism of neoantigens in tumor immunity should be conducted to better guide the design and implementation of tumor immunotherapy. In the future, with the development of sequencing techniques and MS experiments, the evaluation of the immunogenic potential of peptides will become more accurate and objective.
This study provides a comprehensive analysis of BRCA fusion genes and their potential as sources of highly immunogenic neoantigens. By employing advanced computational tools and neural network models, we identified and validated a robust set of neoantigens that hold promise for improving patient outcomes. These findings lay a solid foundation for the development of personalized cancer vaccines and precision medicine approaches in oncology. The integration of genomic, proteomic, and computational methodologies underscores the potential of neoantigen-based immunotherapies to revolutionize cancer treatment and enhance patient survival.
