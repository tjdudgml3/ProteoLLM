Introduction
According to the latest statistics from GLOBOCAN, there were approximately 1.1 million new cases of gastric cancer (GC) in 2020, accounting for 5.6% of all new cancer cases. GC led to about 800,000 deaths, representing 7.7% of all cancer-related mortality, ranking it fourth in terms of mortality rates (Sung et al.
2021
). To date, there is no gold standard treatment for GC. Treatment options are primarily based on the stage of the disease, biomarkers, and physician preference, with surgical and endoscopic resection remaining the mainstays for curative treatment of gastric cancer. Various chemotherapy drugs have a definite therapeutic effect in advanced GC, improving patients’ survival and quality of life. Despite progress in multimodal therapy for GC, the prognosis remains poor. Except for Japan and South Korea, the 5-year survival rate for GC in most regions is about 20–30% (Brenner
2002
). Due to the high invasiveness of GC, many patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage of the disease. For metastatic GC, the median overall survival is still less than one year (Patel and Cecchini
2020
). Therefore, further exploration of the molecular mechanisms of GC and the search for new therapeutic targets is of great clinical significance.
The Notch signaling pathway is involved in a variety of biological processes, including cell fate determination, embryonic and tissue development, tissue function, and repair. Abnormal Notch signaling can lead to the occurrence and development of tumors. Studies have shown that Notch signaling is essentially related to all characteristics of malignant tumors (Aster et al.
2017
). Dysregulation of Notch receptors and ligands is often found in GC. The activation of Notch signaling has been widely reported to be involved in the occurrence and development of gastric cancer. A study analyzed the gene expression data and survival information of 876 GC patients followed up for 20 years and found that high expression of Notch1-4 mRNA was associated with a shortened overall survival (OS) in GC patients (Wu et al.
2016
). It was also found that increased Notch1 expression is related to non-cardia location, >5 cm size, diffuse type, lymphovascular invasion, and distant metastasis, and Notch1 and Jag1 are independent poor prognostic factors for GC (Du et al.
2014
). The Notch signaling pathway is regulated at the transcriptional or post-translational levels. The extracellular domain of Notch is mainly modified by glycans (or sugar residues), while the intracellular domain is modified by various other post-translational modifications (PTMs), including glycosylation, acetylation, hydroxylation, phosphorylation, and ubiquitination (Antfolk et al.
2019
). An increasing number of new pieces of evidence support that PTMs can have a broad impact on the state and function of Notch, suggesting the future of PTM-targeted Notch therapy. However, the PTMs of the Notch1 protein are largely unknown, especially its role in the occurrence and development of gastric cancer, and thus deserve further exploration.
AKT is a serine/threonine kinase that plays a key role in the PI3K signaling pathway. Activated AKT can regulate the function of many downstream proteins, including cell survival, proliferation, migration, metabolism, and angiogenesis (Revathidevi and Munirajan
2019
). Abnormal activation of AKT is highly oncogenic, and its overexpression and phosphorylation activation are widely found in human cancers (Cerami et al.
2012
). In GC, AKT is also closely related to tumor occurrence and progression (Kang and Chau
2020
). It is reported that about 78% of gastric cancers have abnormal activation of AKT, which is associated with poor prognosis in gastric cancer (Nam et al.
2003
). As mentioned earlier, the activation of Notch1 plays an important role in the occurrence and development of gastric cancer, and the Notch pathway can be regulated at the post-translational level, including phosphorylation. Given this, this study proposes the hypothesis that AKT may affect the biological function of Notch1 protein by phosphorylation. This study will explore the above hypothesis in the hope of providing new ideas for the prevention and treatment of gastric cancer.
Materials and methods
Experimental animals and cell lines
Cell lines: MGC-803 and HGC-27 cell lines were purchased from Shanghai Yu Chi Biotechnology Co., Ltd. All cell lines have been identified by STR profiling.
16 male SPF-grade Balb/C nude mice aged 6–8 weeks and weighing 16–18 g were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (SCXK(Jing) 2021-0006), and were housed in SPF-grade animal rooms with a 12 h/12 h light cycle, with free access to food and water.
Experimental reagents
Fetal bovine serum (FBS, Beijing Cellmax Company, SA111.02); RPMI-1640 (Gibco, 42401042); DMEM (Dalian Meilun Biology, MA0212-2); LipofiterTM Transfection Reagent (Hanheng, HB-TRCF-1000); RIPA Lysis buffer (Beijing Solarbio Company, R0010); SDS Lysis Buffer (Beyotime, P0013G); Membrane Protein Extraction Kit (Solarbio, EX1500); Nuclear Protein Extraction Kit (Solarbio, EX1470); Notch1 Antibody (CST, 3447); Recombinant AKT1 Protein (Abcam, ab79792); Notch1-IC Antibody (CST, 4147); AKT1 Antibody (Abcam, ab238477); phospho-Akt substrate RXX(S*/T*) Antibody (CST, 9614); IRS-1 Antibody (Abcam, ab131487); Flag Antibody (Abcam, ab205606); E-cadherin Antibody(Abcam, ab231303); Snail1 Atibody(CST, 3879); Vimentin Antibody(Abcam, ab92547); Co-IP Kit (Thermo, 26149); Kinase Buffer (CST, 9802); ATP (CST, 9804); Recombinant human AKT1 protein (Active) (Abcam, ab62279); Recombinant Human NOTCH1 protein (hFc Tag) (Sinobiological, 10954-H02H); TRIzol (Ambion, USA, 15596026); PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Perfect Real Time) (TaKaRa, Japan, RR047A); TB Green
®

Cell culture
MKN-28 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 complete medium containing 10% FBS, and GES-1, SGC-7903, MGC-803, HGC-27 cells were cultured in DMEM complete medium containing 10% FBS, both at 37 °C in a CO2 incubator. Penicillin-streptomycin (1%) was added to all culture media. All cell lines were authenticated using short tandem repeat (STR) profiling within the past 3 years. All experiments were performed with mycoplasma-free cells.
Preparation and transduction of overexpression lentiviral vectors
The overexpression human Flag-Myr-AKT1 and Notch-IC lentiviral vectors were purchased from Shanghai Hanheng Bio-Tech Co., Ltd. The recombinant lentiviral vectors was produced by co-transfection of 293 T cells with lentiviral vectors, plasmids pSPAX2 and pMD2G with LipoFiterTM. The successfully constructed lentiviral vectors were transduced into target cells using polybrene, and stable cells were selected by puromycin.
Synthesis and transduction of IRS1 shRNA lentivirus
3 specific shRNAs for the IRS-1 gene and one negative control (NC) were designed. The related lentiviruses were designed and synthesized by vectorbuilder Biotechnology (Guangzhou) Co., Ltd. The transduction steps are the same as in 2.4. The shRNA sequences are as follows: hIRS1[shRNA#1] GCCGCTCAAGTGAGGATTTAACTCGAGTTAAATCCTCACTTGAGCGGC; hIRS1[shRNA#2] GGCATCAAACTACCGATTTAACTCGAGTTAAATCGGTAGTTTGATGCC; hIRS1[shRNA#3] GGGTTTGGAGAATGGTCTTAACTCGAGTTAAGACCATTCTCCAAACCC; NC CCTAAGGTTAAGTCGCCCTCGCTCGAGCGAGGGCGACTTAACCTTAGG.
Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay
Cells were lysed utilizing IP lysis buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% NP-40 and 5% Glycerol), and the protein concentration was determined. Subsequently, the Pierce
®
Co-IP Kit was employed to perform the co-IP experiment, which was succeeded by Western blot analysis to evaluate the protein-protein interactions.
Western blot
Total protein was extracted from cells and tissues using RIPA lysis buffer (50 mM Tris (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 1% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS, sodium orthovanadate, sodium fluoride, EDTA and leupeptin), followed by SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresis. The primary antibodies (Notch1, Notch1-IC, AKT1, phospho-Akt substrate RXX(S*/T*), IRS-1, all diluted at 1:2000) were incubated overnight at 4 °C, and the secondary antibodies were incubated at room temperature for 1 h. The ECL reagent was added, and the film was scanned using a scanner to analyze the OD values.
In vitro kinase assay
The mixture of 2 µg recombinant Notch1, 200 µM ATP, 400 ng AKT1, and kinase buffer (containing 25 mM Tris-HCl at pH 7.5, 5 mM beta-glycerophosphate, 2 mM DTT, 0.1 mM Na3VO4, and 10 mM MgCl2) was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. The reaction was then halted by adding 4× loading buffer (0.2 M Tris-HCl, 0.4 M DTT, 277 mM, 8.0% SDS, 6 mM Bromophenol blue, 4.3 M Glycerol) and heating at 100 °C for 10 min. The samples were analyzed by SDS-PAGE and immunoblotted for Notch1, AKT1, and phospho-Akt substrate using specific antibodies.
Notch1 phosphorylation mass spectrometry analysis
Protein was extracted as in the co-IP experiment, and the extracted protein was subjected to SDS-PAGE. After electrophoresis, the gel band near 110 kDa of the target protein was cut for mass spectrometry identification, which was tested by Shanghai Oebiotech Co., Ltd.
Immunofluorescence
GC cells were seeded in 6-well plates and treated with interventions. After fixation and PBS washing, cells were incubated with BSA, then with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by secondary antibodies at room temperature. Nuclei were stained with DAPI, and slides were sealed for fluorescence imaging with a confocal microscope. Image J was used for semi-quantitative analysis.

After fixation and permeabilization of cells, the Click reaction was used for cell labeling, and Hoechst 33,342 was used to stain the cell nucleus. Cells were observed and imaged using a fluorescence microscope.
Transwell invasion assay
Matrigel was added to the upper chamber of the Transwell plate, followed by the addition of cell suspension. 500 µL of 20% FBS-supplemented medium is added to the lower chamber. The plate was then incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. After incubation, the inserts were removed, and the upper chamber’s non-invading cells were gently wiped off with a cotton swab. The inserts were stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 30 min, rinsed with PBS, and the invading cells were counted under a microscope to assess the invasiveness of cells.
Scratch assay
When cells cover more than 80% of the bottom of the flask, scratches are made with a pipette tip aligned to a ruler, ensuring they are as perpendicular as possible to the horizontal lines on the background. The cells are then rinsed twice with PBS, followed by the addition of serum-free medium, and continued cultivation in a 37 °C incubator with 5% CO
2
. Photos are taken at 0 h and 24 h to compare the wound healing capabilities of each group.

Results
AKT1 phosphorylates notch1 to promote its entry into the cell nucleus
The PhosphositePlus database indicated potential phosphorylation of serine (S), threonine (T), and tyrosine (Y) residues in the Notch1 protein. This study showed that Notch1 interacted with AKT1 in GC cells (Fig.
1
A). The GPS 5.0 database predictions identified possible phosphorylation sites on Notch1 that might interact with the AKT1 binding domain. Using the CST website, we found the consensus motifs RXRXX(S*/T*) and RXX(S*/T*) for AKT1 phosphorylation, where R is arginine, X is any amino acid, and S*/T* suggests phosphorylation at serine or threonine. Although ScanProsite tool predictions with RXRXX(S*/T*) did not yield specific sites, the RXX(S*/T*) motif predicted phosphorylation sites on Notch1.
In vitro kinase assays demonstrated that upon treatment with AKT1 and ATP, Notch1 protein could be distinctly detected by the phospho-Akt substrate RXX(S*/T*) specific antibody, while faint phosphorylation signals were observed in the two control groups (Fig.
1
B). To investigate the influence of AKT1 on Notch1 phosphorylation in gastric cancer cells, the study selected the MGC803 cell line and established a stable cell line with overexpression of AKT1. Immunoprecipitation with a Notch1 antibody showed that AKT1 did not affect the expression level of endogenous Notch1 protein, yet endogenous Notch1 could be phosphorylated by AKT1, and this phosphorylation could be inhibited by LY294002 (Fig.
1
C, D). Phosphoproteomic analysis identified the phosphorylation site on Notch1 as S2183 (Fig.
1
I), and the sequence surrounding the phosphorylation site, RKKS, matched the consensus motif for AKT1 phosphorylation, RXX(S*/T*).
Further investigation into the effect of AKT1 phosphorylation on Notch1 translocation was illustrated in Fig.
1
E-H. In the oe-Myr-AKT1 group, the expression of Notch1 in the cell membrane decreased, while the expression of the activated form of Notch1, Notch1-IC, significantly increased in the cytoplasm and nucleus, indicating that AKT1-induced phosphorylation promoted the cleavage and release of the transmembrane protein Notch1 and the translocation of the activated Notch1-IC into the nucleus, which could be inhibited by the addition of LY294002. Immunofluorescence results similarly indicated that AKT1-induced phosphorylation promoted the activation of Notch1 and facilitated the translocation of Notch1-IC into the nucleus (Fig.
1
J, K).

IRS-1, as a target gene of notch1-ic, is significantly elevated in gastric cancer and reduces clinical prognosis
To further elucidate the impact of Notch1 nuclear translocation on downstream molecules, this study utilized an online database to screen for 50 target genes of the transcription factor RBPJ, which interacts with Notch1 (Fig.
2

Cellular experiments confirmed that overexpression of Notch1-IC significantly increased the transcriptional levels of the five predicted molecules, with IRS-1 showing the most pronounced effect (Fig.
2
B). Consequently, IRS-1 was selected as a target molecule of Notch1-IC for subsequent research. Utilizing the GEPIA platform, tissue microarrays, and clinical samples, it was found that IRS-1 is highly expressed in gastric cancer tissues (Fig.
2

2
G). Subsequently, the clinical data of 97 gastric cancer patients in the tissue microarray were analyzed. The expression of IRS-1 is associated with the clinical stage, N stage, and M stage of gastric cancer, but not significantly related to the T stage (Table
1
). Survival analysis from tissue microarrays indicated that the high expression group of IRS-1 had a significantly shorter survival time compared to the low expression group (
P
<0.05, Hazard ratio = 2.944) (Fig.

AKT1-mediated notch1 phosphorylation promotes the growth of gastric cancer cells in vivo through the regulation of IRS-1
In vivo experimental results revealed that the addition of LY294002 or knockdown of IRS-1 partially reversed the impact of AKT1 overexpression on the tumorigenic ability of MGC803 cells in nude mice (Fig.
5
A, B). Further findings indicated that compared to the oe-NC group, the expression of E-cadherin significantly decreased, while the expression of Snail and Vimentin significantly increased in the oe-Myr-AKT1 group, and the oe-Myr-AKT1 + LY294002 group or the oe-Myr-AKT1 + shIRS-1 group showed a marked increase in E-cadherin and a significant decrease in Snail and Vimentin expression compared to the oe-Myr-AKT1 group (Fig.
5
C-J). Figure
5
K-M demonstrated the transfection efficiency of the transplanted tumors in each group, and the addition of LY294002 effectively inhibited the increase in p-AKT1 caused by overexpression of AKT1. This study further found that compared to the control group, the expression of IRS-1 significantly increased in the Myr-AKT1 overexpression group. The addition of the protein kinase inhibitor LY294002 or knockdown of IRS-1 reversed the regulation of IRS-1 by Myr-AKT1 overexpression (Fig.
5
O-Q). At the same time, this study found no significant difference in the expression of Notch1 in the transplanted tumors among the groups. However, overexpression of AKT1 significantly increased the content of activated Notch1-IC in the transplanted tumors, and the addition of LY294002 reversed the impact of AKT1 overexpression on the content of Notch1-IC (Fig.
5
I, S).

Discussion
Activated Notch1 was highly expressed in GC tissue and was correlated with numerous clinical parameters. Moreover, the Notch pathway was found to interact with classical pathways such as Wnt, Ras, and NF-κB, promoting the occurrence and development of GC (Yao et al.
2017
). Phosphorylation modification, one of the primary post-translational modifications of Notch, affects the complexity of the Notch signaling pathway (Walsh et al.
2005
). In fact, Notch proteins contain many phosphorylation sites that affect activity and stability. The Notch extracellular domain generated after NICD and S2 cleavage was proven to be phosphorylated, while the full-length Notch was phosphorylated to a lesser extent (Foltz and Nye
2001
). Significant progress has been made in research on the phosphorylation of Notch1, but identifying its phosphorylation sites and clarifying the biological roles of these phosphorylations has only just begun. In this study, we discovered through predictive software that Notch1 could be phosphorylated by AKT1. Subsequent in vivo and in vitro experiments confirmed that AKT1 could phosphorylate Notch1, with the phosphorylation site being S2183. Mammalian proteins containing a large number of AKT phosphorylation motifs RXRXX(S*/T*) or RXX(S*/T*) are substrates of AKT. This study predicted the phosphorylation site sequence of Notch1 by AKT1 to be RXX(S*/T*), which was confirmed by cell experiments and mass spectrometry analysis. Studies have confirmed that AKT can phosphorylate and activate multiple oncogenes, promoting the occurrence and development of cancer (Ozes et al.
1999
). This study confirmed that AKT1 could phosphorylate Notch1 in GC. As mentioned earlier, protein phosphorylation modification is crucial for protein activity, complex formation, protein degradation, and subcellular localization. This study further found that AKT1-induced Notch1 phosphorylation could affect the subcellular localization of Notch1, promoting the nuclear translocation of Notch1-IC. Phosphorylation may promote nuclear import by enhancing the binding affinity for importins or exposing nuclear localization signals, or it may inhibit nuclear translocation by acting on a component of the nuclear transport mechanism or by disrupting nuclear localization signals (Nardozzi et al.
2010
). However, the specific mechanism still requires further research. Transmembrane Notch proteins, after cleavage, are activated, transferred from the cell membrane to the nucleus, and regulate the transcription of downstream genes by forming transcriptional complexes, which has been observed in multiple cancers (Ramakrishnan et al.
2015
; Santio et al.
2016
). The above confirms that Notch1 is a substrate of the AKT1 kinase. AKT1 in GC could induce the phosphorylation of Notch1 at the S2183 site, thereby changing the subcellular localization of Notch1-IC and promoting its nuclear translocation.
This study further explored the downstream molecules targeted by the intracellular domain of activated Notch1 (Notch1-IC) that entered the nucleus. In the classical Notch1 signaling pathway, Notch1-IC, acting as a transcriptional regulator, binds with the transcription factor RBPJ to form a transcriptional complex, regulating the transcription of target molecules. This study identified IRS-1 as a potential transcriptional target gene of Notch1-IC. Previous research has found that IRS-1 is related to malignant transformation of tumors and plays an important role in the occurrence and development of digestive tract tumors (Bergmann et al.
1996
; Gao et al.
2020
; Reiss et al.
2012
; Ye et al.
2020
). However, research on IRS-1 in GC has been relatively lacking. This study found that IRS-1 was highly expressed in GC tissue and was associated with the clinical staging, lymph node metastasis (N), and distant metastasis (M) of GC, and was an indicator of poor prognosis for GC patients. Subsequent findings revealed that Notch1-IC could regulate the expression of IRS-1. Yamaguchi K et al. found that in Notch1 heterozygous mutant (N1+/-) mice, the expression of Notch1 in white fat decreased, leading to decreased expression of IRS-1 (Yamaguchi et al.
2021
), further illustrating a positive regulatory relationship between Notch1 and IRS-1. This study found that Notch1-IC could promote the proliferation, invasion, and metastasis of GC cells. Knockdown of IRS-1 partially inhibited the aforementioned effects of Notch1-IC overexpression on GC cells, indicating that the influence of Notch1-IC on the malignant behavior of GC cells is partly dependent on IRS-1. Early studies have found that elevated Notch1 is associated with lymph node metastasis of GC and can promote the proliferation and migration of GC cells (Ma et al.
2022
). Inhibition of Notch1 can inhibit the proliferation of GC cells (Wang et al.
2015
). RBPJ recruits NICD to the recognition site of the promoter region and the super-enhancer located further away (Hurtado et al.
2019
). This study found that Notch1-IC could bind to IRS-1 through the transcription factor RBPJ, targeting the regulation of IRS-1 transcription, thereby regulating the proliferation, invasion, and metastasis of GC cells. In diffuse GC, researchers have found that activated Notch1 can directly bind to RBPJ in the 5’ promoter region of the CD133 gene, thereby regulating the expression of CD133 and affecting the stemness of GC cells (Konishi et al.
2016
). The above confirms that IRS-1 is highly expressed in GC, activated Notch1 can bind to the IRS-1 gene through RBPJ, directly regulating IRS-1 transcription, thus affecting the invasion and metastasis of GC cells, indicating that IRS-1 may be a potential therapeutic target for GC. This study further confirmed using the CDX GC model that AKT1/Notch1/IRS-1 can affect the size of GC transplanted tumors and the expression of EMT-related molecules. EMT is a major factor in promoting the transfer of tumor cells (Babaei et al.
2021
), and is related to various tumor functions, including tumor initiation, malignant progression, tumor stemness, cell migration, metastasis, and resistance to treatment (De Craene and Berx
2013
; Nieto et al.
2016
; Pastushenko and Blanpain
2019
). The above suggests that AKT1-induced Notch1 phosphorylation can regulate the EMT process of GC through IRS-1, thereby promoting the progression of GC.
In summary, AKT1 induced the phosphorylation of Notch1 and promoted the activation and nuclear translocation of Notch1-IC, which in turn targeted the regulation of IRS-1 to promote the progression of GC (Fig.
6
). However, this study did not further verify through experiments mutating the amino acid site, nor did it clarify the role of this phosphorylation site in GC with experiments. At the same time, after Notch1 is cleaved, it becomes activated Notch1-IC, and whether the activated Notch1-IC is also regulated by AKT1 within the cell requires further exploration.
