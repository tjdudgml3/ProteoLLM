Introduction
The metabolic conversion of L-carnitine to trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) from gut microbiota–host interactions has been recently highlighted as a potential connection between red meat consumption and cardiovascular diseases.
1–5
L-carnitine, an abundant nutrient in red meat, can be metabolized by gut microbes to produce trimethylamine (TMA) which is subsequently converted to TMAO by the liver enzyme flavin monooxygenase (FMO3).
1
Excessive TMA production resulting from the metabolism of L-carnitine by gut microbiota leads to increased levels of circulating TMAO. This elevation in turn raises the risk of cardiometabolic diseases by exacerbating insulin resistance and atherothrombosis.
6
,
7
Circulating TMAO can also be derived from the microbial metabolism of other food substrates such as choline and betaine, resulting in a mixed pool of complex metabolic pathways. To better understand the gut microbiota contributing to L-carnitine-mediated TMAO formation, we developed an oral carnitine challenge test (OCCT) that measures TMAO production more specifically related to microbial metabolism of L-carnitine under a designed dosage.
8
Through the OCCT, we identified various TMAO producer phenotypes among individuals, primarily dependent on the composition and function of the gut microbiome. Among them, high-TMAO producers exhibited unique microbiome characteristics, including an increased presence of
Emergencia timonensis
, a gut anaerobe previously discovered to be responsible for TMA production from L-carnitine metabolism.
9
,
10
In more recent studies,
E. timonensis
has been observed to contribute to around 25% of the high-TMAO producer phenotype, suggesting that a significant portion of the gut microbiota contributing to this phenotype remains unclarified.
9
,
11
While a microbial pathway involving the Rieske-type monooxygenase (CntA) paired with a reductase component (CntB) was initially suggested to convert L-carnitine to TMA,
12
this enzymatic reaction requires oxygen for its catalytic action and might be challenging to function in the anaerobic segments of the intestine. Recent investigation has indicated that L-carnitine can be transformed into TMA via two distinct, sequential gut microbe-mediated pathways: (1) L-carnitine ➙ γ-butyrobetaine (γBB), and (2) γBB ➙ TMA.
10
The initial transformation can be undertaken by gut commensals possessing the
cai
operon (
caiTABCDE
), which is prevalent in the human gut.
13
The subsequent step is catabolized by the recently identified γ-butyrobetaine utilization (
gbu or bbu
) gene cluster (
gbuABCDEF or bbuABCDEF
).
14
,
15
Unlike the
cai
operon, the
gbu
gene cluster is present only in a limited number of gut bacterial species that exist in low abundance.
14
,
15
It is speculated that the
gbu
gene cluster may serve as the rate-limiting step in L-carnitine-mediated TMAO production within the entire gut microbiota. However, this hypothesis still requires clinical investigation, which we believe can be addressed by the OCCT.
In the current study, we aim to investigate the role of gut microbial genes (
cntA/B
, the
cai
operon, the
gbu
gene cluster) in L-carnitine-mediated TMAO production in the human gut through an integrative multiomics analysis using metagenomic and metabolomic datasets from the OCCT. With this approach, we hope to uncover the crucial gut microbes responsible for TMAO formation specifically from L-carnitine consumption in humans.
Results
The gbu gene cluster, rather than cntA/B and the cai operon, is significantly correlated with L-carnitine-specific TMAO production
While circulating TMAO measured in the blood can be derived from gut microbial conversion of various nutrient substrates, such as choline, L-carnitine, or betaine, as well as directly from fishery products, fasting plasma TMAO levels might not precisely reflect the gut microbiota’s ability to transform L-carnitine into TMA and TMAO (
Figure 1a
). To address this issue, we developed an OCCT which measures plasma and urine TMAO serially under a controlled dose of L-carnitine administration. The OCCT has been demonstrated to reliably assess the TMAO-producing capacity from the metaorganismal metabolism of dietary L-carnitine, with a strong correlation between urine and blood TMAO levels when measured concurrently (
r
= 0.92,
p
< 0.0001).
8
Individuals were categorized as high-TMAO producers when the maximal plasma TMAO (pTMAOmax) exceeded 10 µM, which is equivalent to uTMAOmax >162.8 mmole/mole Cre, under the OCCT (
Figure 1b
).
9
The microbial enzymes encoded by
cntAB
have been identified to directly convert L-carnitine to TMA.
11
The alternative route for transforming L-carnitine to TMA involves two sequential metabolic pathways contributed by two microbial gene clusters:
caiTABCDE
(L-carnitine ➙ γBB) and
gbuABCDEF
(γBB ➙ TMA), possessed by different gut microbes (
Figure 1c
).
14
,
15
To determine which microbial gene cluster is crucial for TMAO production from L-carnitine metabolism by the gut microbiota, we performed OCCT and shotgun metagenome sequencing of fecal samples from subjects in three separate cohorts, comprising healthy adults and cardiovascular patients (
Figure 1d
).

In the present study, three separate cohorts were recruited from different projects, each involving participants who underwent OCCT. These cohorts included an interventional healthy cohort (healthy cohort 1 or HC1,
n
= 55) from the community, which received L-carnitine supplementation for 1 month; a cross-sectional healthy cohort (healthy cohort 2 or HC2,
n
= 68) recruited from the Health Management Center at National Taiwan University Hospital; and a cardiovascular cohort (CV cohort,
n
= 50) recruited from the outpatient department of National Taiwan University Hospital, where participants received a plant-based diet intervention for 2 months. All participants were categorized as high-TMAO producers (HP) and low-TMAO producers (LP) using the OCCT uTMAOmax value. In the HC1 cohort, we found that the fecal abundance of both
cntA
and
cntB
genes, which encode Rieske-type monooxygenase and reductase, did not show a significant difference between the HP and LP groups. For the initial oxygen-independent metabolic pathway that converts L-carnitine to γBB, encoded by the
cai
gene operon (
caiTABCDE
), the abundance of all
cai
genes showed no significant difference between the HP and LP groups. For the subsequent pathway that convert γBB to TMA encoded by the
gbu
gene cluster, the abundance of
gbu
genes, including
gbuABCDEF
, was all significantly increased in the HP group as compared with the LP groups (
Figure 1e
). To corroborate the findings, we conducted further investigations on these microbial genes in two additional independent cohorts undergoing OCCT, encompassing HC2 and CV cohorts. Consequently, the fecal abundance of
cntAB
genes and
cai
operon showed insignificant difference between HP and LP, whereas the abundance of
gbuABCDEF
was consistently increased in HP when compared with LP in the HC2 and CV cohorts (
Figure 1f,g
).
TMAO production via the microbial gbu pathway can be modulated by L-carnitine supplementation and plant-based diet intervention
To further understand whether the microbial
gbu
gene cluster in the gut microbiota could be altered by dietary intervention, we analyzed the L-carnitine-specific TMAO-producing capacity and the abundance of
gbu
genes under dietary interventions in the HC1 and CV cohorts. For HC1, 55 participants underwent L-carnitine supplementation for 1 month (500 mg/d), with OCCT and metagenome sequencing conducted before and after the intervention (
Figure 2a
). The capacity for TMAO production from L-carnitine, as measured by OCCT uTMAOauc, and the abundance of
gbu
genes significantly increased after L-carnitine supplementation (
Figure 2b
). In contrast, the abundance of
cntAB
genes and the
cai
operon remained unchanged (Figure S1A). Notably, the omnivores in HC1 showed no difference in both uTMAOauc and
gbu
levels (
Figure 2c
), while vegetarians demonstrated a significant increase in OCCT uTMAOauc and
gbu
abundances after the L-carnitine supplementation (
Figure 2d
). For the CV cohort, 32 patients with cardiovascular diseases received dietician-supervised dietary intervention with whole plant-based foods for 2 months and were followed up 3 months later after resuming their regular diets (
Figure 2e
). Food frequency questionnaires and 24-h dietary records were collected during the intervention to assess adherence to the interventional diet. The OCCT and metagenome sequencing were performed at baseline, seconnd month and fifth month for each subject. Initially, we observed a decreasing trend in
gbu
genes following the implementation of the plant-based diet intervention, with a significant increase noted between the second and fifth months among all participants (
Figure 2f
). However, we noticed that some participants did not adhere well to the plant-based diet during the intervention. We thus separated the participants into high-adherence and low-adherence groups based on a plant-based diet adherence scoring (supplemental methods). The results showed that the high-adherence group showed a significant decrease in uTMAOauc and
gbu
genes after the plant-based diet intervention. Interestingly, a rebound in uTMAOauc and
gbu
genes was noted 3 months later when the patients returned to their regular diets (
Figure 2g
). In contrast, no significant difference in uTMAOauc and
gbu
levels was observed among patients with low adherence during the plant-based dietary intervention (
Figure 2H
). On the other hand, the abundances of
cntAB
and the
cai
operon showed no significant change during the plant-based diet intervention (Figure S1B).

The gbu genes mapped to JAGTTR01 sp018223385 and E. timonensis correlate significantly with L-carnitine-mediated TMAO production
Given the aforementioned findings, we verified that the
gbu
gene cluster serves as the pivotal microbial pathway in determining TMAO production from L-carnitine consumption. Within the
gbu
gene cluster, the four essential genes for the conversion of γBB to TMA are
gbuA
,
gbuB
,
gbuC
, and
gbuE
.
15
In this study, we observed that the abundances of
gbuABCE
demonstrated stronger correlations with OCCT uTMAOmax (Spearman
r
= 0.318 to 0.410,
p
< 0.0001) and uTMAOauc (Spearman
r
= 0.337 to 0.436,
p
< 0.0001) compared to the fasting plasma TMAO (Spearman
r
= 0.137 to 0.185,
p
< 0.05) (
Figure 3a
). In addition, the
gbu

gbu

FMO3
mutation, such as Glu158Lys and Glu308ly, was not observed to be associated with TMAO production from L-carnitine metabolism (Figure S3).

JAGTTR01 sp018223385 is significantly enriched in high-tmao producers and positively correlated to L-carnitine-mediated TMAO production
We next seek to explore whether gut microbes, enriched in high-TMAO producers, were correlated with the
gbu
gene cluster, as identified from the binning of metagenome-assembled genomes. In this study, species-level genome bins (SGBs) were constructed using sequences from the 292 fecal samples. These were profiled into 18 phyla, 284 families, 897 genera, and 1857 SGBs. Increased alpha diversity, including the number of observed species, the Shannon index and the Simpson’s index, was observed in the HP group compared with the LP group (
Figure 4a
). A significant difference in the composition profiles of gut microbiota was also noted between the HP group and the LP group (
Figure 4b
). Of the 1857 SGBs, 12 were significantly more enriched in the HP group compared to the LP group (ANCOM-BC, FDR < 0.05) (
Figure 4c
). Among these SGBs, JAGTTR01 sp018223385 (SGB4778) stands out as the sole SGB that contains the
gbu
gene cluster and shows a significant correlation with OCCT TMAOauc. We also identified five SGBs belonging to
gbu
-containing bacteria and found that JAGTTR01 sp018223385 and
E. timonensis
were significantly correlated with OCCT uTMAOmax and uTMAOauc (
Figure 4d,e
; Table S3). The combination of the levels of JAGTTR01 sp018223385 and
E. timonensis
further showed an additive value for the correlation efficient with the uTMAOauc (
Figure 4f
). These findings suggest that JAGTTR01 sp018223385 and
E. timonensis
serve as major contributors to L-carnitine-mediated TMAO production and the prevalence of
gbu
gene clusters in the gut microbiome.

Isolation and functional validation of novel gbu-containing bacteria

gbu
-containing bacteria, including the JAGTTR01 sp018223385, are unavailable from public bioresource centers, we sought to isolate these pivotal
gbu

gbu

gbu
genes from the fecal samples of high-TMAO producers. These include JAGTTR01 sp. NTUH-002-81 (with
gbu
genes close to those of JAGTTR01 sp018223385) (Figure S5),
Intestinibacillus

gbu

Marseille-P4005),

A qPCR-based fecal gbuB quantification predicts TMAO production from L-carnitine consumption
Given the verified correlations between the
gbu
genes and L-carnitine-specific TMAO production, as measured by the OCCT, and armed with ample
gbu
-containing bacteria as reference genomes, we next sought to develop a qPCR-based method for quantifying
gbu
genes in the gut microbiome. Consequently, we designed a pair of degenerate primers targeting the
gbuB
gene using genome templates from six
gbu
-containing bacteria. This includes three type strains:
E. timonensis
SN18,
Eubacterium minutum
ATCC 700,079,
Agathobaculum desmolans
ATCC 43,058, and three new isolates: JAGTTR01 sp. NTUH-002-81,
Agathobaculum
sp. NTUH-O15-33,
Intestinibacillus
sp. NTUH-41-i26 (
Figure 6a
). We subsequently used the qPCR method to quantify the
gbuB
gene in 292 fecal samples from our study cohorts. Additionally, 91 fecal samples from an external study, which utilized a different fecal sample processing protocol, served as our validation cohort (
Figure 6b
). In the study cohort, the results demonstrated that the abundances of the
gbuB
gene, as measured by qPCR, were significantly higher in the HP group compared to the LP group (
Figure 6c
). Furthermore, the qPCR-based
gbuB
abundances exhibited stronger correlations with OCCT uTMAOmax (Spearman
r
= 0.357,
p
< 0.0001) and uTMAOauc (Spearman
r
= 0.360,
p
< 0.0001) compared to fasting plasma TMAO (Spearman
r
= 0.142,
p
= 0.016) (
Figure 6d
). The qPCR-based
gbuB
abundances were also significantly correlated with the OCCT uTMAOauc for each separate cohort in this study (Figure S7). In the validation cohort, the qPCR-based
gbuB
abundances were notably elevated in the HP group in comparison to the LP group (

). Additionally, these qPCR-based

abundances correlated significantly with OCCT uTMAOmax (Spearman

Discussion
The formation of TMAO from the gut microbial metabolism of L-carnitine has emerged as one of the most plausible explanations for the link between red meat consumption and cardiovascular diseases. Despite the proposal of several microbial genes, such as
cntA/B
, the
cai
operon, and the newly identified
gbu
gene cluster, as being responsible for converting L-carnitine to TMA, the primary microbial pathway contributing to TMAO production from L-carnitine intake in humans still requires to be confirmed.
14
,
15
In this study, we employed metagenome sequencing and OCCT to pinpoint the crucial microbial genes associated with the L-carnitine-specific TMAO production. We demonstrated that the
gbu
genes, rather than the
cai
operon or
cntAB
, were significantly associated with TMAO production from L-carnitine administration in three separate cohorts. In addition, two dietary interventional studies revealed that the abundance of the
gbu
gene cluster can be enriched by L-carnitine supplementation or decreased by a high-adherence plant-based diet. Taxonomic analysis of the
gbu
genes revealed that those mapping to the JAGTTR01 sp018223385 and
E. timonensis
have the most significant correlations with L-carnitine-mediated TMAO production. JAGTTR01 sp018223385 also stands out as a significantly enriched SGB in high-TMAO producers in the differential abundance analysis. Additionally, the combined levels of JAGTTR01 sp018223385 and
E. timonensis
exhibited a synergistic effect in their association with TMAO production from L-carnitine metabolism. We next isolated six in-house gut microbes possessing the
gbu
gene cluster and confirmed their role in converting γBB to TMA both
in vitro
and

Over the past decade, there has been growing interest in studying how gut microbes utilize L-carnitine to produce TMA. However, a large proportion of studies have focused on the Rieske-type monooxygenase CntA, overlooking the fact that oxygen is essential for its catalytic reaction. This makes it unlikely to function in the anaerobic gut environment. In the present study, we demonstrated that the microbial
gbu
genes, rather than
cntAB
and the
cai
operon, in the human gut exhibit significant correlations with TMAO production specific to L-carnitine intake, as measured by the OCCT. These findings ascertained that TMAO originated from the anaerobic route in the human gut and the gut microbes containing
gbu
gene cluster determine the capacity of TMA/TMAO production from L-carnitine consumption.
Our research findings also corroborated the results reported by Buffa et al., who demonstrated that the abundances of fecal
gbuA
were significantly correlated with fasting plasma TMAO levels in a dietary intervention study (113 participants, 398
gbuA
/pTMAO pairs, Spearman
r
= 0.17,
p
= 0.0005).
15
Similarly, our results revealed a significant, moderate correlation between
gbuA
and fasting plasma TMAO (173 participants, 292
gbuA
/pTMAO pairs, Spearman
r
= 0.140,
p
= 0.016). Notably, the abundances of
gbuABCE
displayed stronger correlations with OCCT uTMAOmax (Spearman
r
= 0.318 to 0.410,
p
< 0.0001) and uTMAOauc (Spearman
r
= 0.337 to 0.436,
p
< 0.0001) than with fasting pTMAO. This implies that the OCCT offers more specificity in measuring L-carnitine-mediated TMAO production, whereas fasting pTMAO levels reflect mixed effects from the intake of various TMAO precursors like choline and betaine or TMAO directly from fishery products.
Additionally, in our dietary intervention studies (HC1 and CV cohorts), changes in fecal
gbu
genes were correlated with changes in TMAO-producing capacity, further indicating that
gbu
genes may serve as biomarkers for predicting TMAO production.
Leveraging the specificity of OCCT for TMAO production from L-carnitine consumption, our study revealed that the
gbu
genes mapped to JAGTTR01 sp018223385 and the SGB of JAGTTR01 sp018223385 were significantly associated with TMAO production from L-carnitine metabolism in the human gut. In this study, JAGTTR01 sp018223385 and
E. timonensis
were the only two

in silico

gbu
(
bbu

E. timonensis
, suggesting that all genomes of these taxa may exhibit this pathway.
11
To confirm the catabolic function of JAGTTR01 sp018223385 in converting γBB to TMA, we purified six new isolates containing the
gbu
gene cluster from fecal samples of high-TMAO producers. Among these isolates was JAGTTR01 sp. NTUH-002-81, a native strain closely related to JAGTTR01 sp018223385. In our study, both JAGTTR01 sp. NTUH-002-81 and
E. timonensis
consistently demonstrated their ability to convert γBB to TMA
in vitro
using isotope tracer precursors and
in vivo
using germ-free mice. This is likely because these two
gbu
-containing gut microbes have narrow nutritional niches, making the conversion of γBB to TMA essential for their energy generation.
11
Interestingly, we found that JAGTTR01 sp. NTUH-002-81 requires two synergy bacteria,
Bacteroides ovatus
and
Flavonifractor plautii
, for successful colonization in the gut of germ-free mice. Whether JAGTTR01 sp018223385 also requires these synergy bacteria for colonization in SPF and humans warrants further investigation.
Armed with a collection of
gbu
sequences from various gut microbes proven to convert γBB to TMA, we subsequently developed a qPCR-based method to quantify the
gbuB
gene from the gut microbiome. The qPCR-based
gbuB
levels were significantly correlated with uTMAOauc, and these results were validated with an external cohort. Notably, the validation cohort had a smaller sample size, used a different fecal collection method, and employed a different DNA extraction kit, yet still exhibited a significant correlation between the qPCR-based
gbuB
levels and uTMAOauc. In addition,
gbuB
amplicon sequencing revealed a taxonomic composition similar to that profiled by shotgun metagenome sequencing, further supporting the use of this qPCR-based method as an alternative approach for measuring
gbu
genes in the human gut.
This study did have a few limitations. First, although the abundances of
gbu
genes were confirmed to correlate with L-carnitine-mediated TMAO production, the correlation coefficients between
gbu
genes and uTMAO were moderate. This could be because the levels of
gbu
gene expression might not be adequately reflected by DNA abundance. Further study, possibly measuring
gbu
mRNA or Gbu protein, may be required to optimize the measurement of functional
gbu
gene cluster. In addition, we observed that high-TMAO producers tend to have higher blood creatinine levels and are older than low-TMAO producers (Table S1). This may suggest that other factors such as reduced renal function and older age may contribute to elevated blood TMAO levels beyond the presence of
gbu
-containing bacteria and an L-carnitine-rich diet. Second, this study is a retrospective investigation of the correlations between the
gbu
gene cluster and L-carnitine-mediated TMAO production measured by OCCT. A prospective study may be required to confirm the predictive efficacy of the
gbu
genes for TMAO production from L-carnitine-rich food consumption. Third,
gbuB
was selected as the biomarker to predict L-carnitine-mediated TMAO production in the qPCR-based method due to its higher correlation coefficient with uTMAOauc. However, the potentially higher performance of other
gbu
genes cannot be excluded and may require future investigation.
In summary, we employed an integrative multi-omic analysis using metagenome and metabolome datasets from OCCT to interrogate which microbial factor is crucial for L-carnitine-mediated TMA(O) production in humans. We identified essential gut microbes with the
gbu
gene cluster, particularly JAGTTR01 sp018223385, that primarily contribute to the conversion of γBB to TMA in the human gut. Additionally, we developed an efficient qPCR-based method to quantify fecal
gbuB
, which may be used to predict TMAO production from L-carnitine consumption and serve as a biomarker for personalized nutrition.

Description of oral carnitine challenge test (OCCT) and research participants
To measure individual’s TMAO producing capacity from L-carnitine intake, we previously developed the OCCT and investigated its potential use in personalized nutrition.
8
,
9
For each OCCT, urine samples were collected before the oral administration of 1500 mg L-carnitine (GNC®) and subsequently at the 24th and 48th h after the challenge. An individual’s TMAO producing capacity from L-carnitine intake was determined by the maximum level of urine TMAO (OCCT uTMAOmax) and the area under the curve of TMAO levels (OCCT uTMAOauc). In the present study, we applied the OCCT in three separate study cohorts which include an interventional healthy cohort from the community (HC1,
n
= 55) (
NCT02838732
), an observational healthy cohort recruited from Health Management Center, National Taiwan University Hospital (HC2,
n
= 68) (
NCT06175260
) and an interventional cardiovascular cohort recruited in Outpatient Department of NTUH (CV cohort,
n
= 50) (
NCT03781011
). Participants were divided into high-TMAO producers (uTMAOmax > 162.8 mmole/mole creatinine) and low-TMAO producers (uTMAOmax < 162.8 mmole/mole creatinine) based on the OCCT results.
9
In total, 292 OCCTs were performed across the three study cohorts, accompanied by metadata from the shotgun metagenomics of fecal samples, biochemistry, and
FMO3
genetic SNPs (Table S1). In addition, fecal DNA samples from 91 subjects who underwent OCCT were obtained from Microbio Co., Ltd., serving as an external cohort for validating the qPCR-based
gbuB
quantification (
NCT04674839
). All study protocols and informed consents received approval from the Institutional Review Board of National Taiwan University Hospital. All participants provided signed informed consent waivers, and all clinical investigations were conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration.
L-carnitine supplementation and plant-based diet treatment in the interventional cohorts
The participants in HC1 had daily L-carnitine supplementation (500 mg/tablet, GNC®) for 1 month and received OCCT after the intervention. Subjects identified as high-TMAO producers in the CV cohort were invited to participate in a plant-based diet intervention study supervised by a registered dietitian. Thirty-two participants in the CV cohort completed the plant-based dietary intervention for 2 months and resumed their regular diet for 3 months subsequently. The OCCT was conducted at baseline, the second month and at the fifth month. Food frequency questionnaires (FFQ), 24-h dietary records and blood low-density lipoprotein were recorded during the plant-diet intervention to evaluate dietary adherence of the participants (supplemental methods).
Plasma and urine TMAO measurement by LC-MS/MS
Briefly, 50 μL plasma and 50 μL urine samples were extracted with 450 μL and 950 μL methanol containing 200 ng/mL isotopically labeled internal standards (d9-TMAO), respectively, and the extraction was performed using the Geno/Grinder 2010 (SPEX SamplePrep., Metuchen, NJ, USA) at 1000 rpm for 3 min. The extracts were then centrifuged by using the Eppendorf Centrifuge 5810 R at 12,000 g for 5 min at 4°C. The supernatants were subjected to liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis. Target metabolites and their corresponding internal standards were analyzed by using Agilent 1290 UHPLC coupled with an Agilent 6460 triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Separation was performed using a MicroSolv Cogent Diamond Hydride column (150 mm × 2.1 mm, 4.2 μm, MicroSolv, Eatontown, NJ, USA), and the column was thermostated at 40°C during analysis. The mobile phase was composed of solvent A (10 mm ammonium acetate and 0.2% formic acid in water) and solvent B (10 mm ammonium acetate and 0.2% formic acid in 90% ACN). A 0.4 mL/min linear gradient elution was used: 0–1 min, 90–75% solvent B, 1–2 min, 75–65% solvent B, 2–4 min, 65–55% solvent B, 4–5 min, 55–40% solvent B; and column re-equilibration with 90% solvent B for 1 min. The injection volume was 5 μL. The positive electrospray ionization mode was utilized with the following parameters: 325°C for drying gas temperature, 7 L/min for drying gas flow, 45 psi for nebulizer pressure, 325°C for sheath gas temperature, 11 L/min for sheath gas flow rate, and 3500 V for capillary voltage. Nozzle voltage was set at 500 V. The mass spectrometer was configured in multiple reaction monitoring mode, and the monitored transitions for TMAO were
m/z
76.1→58.1 and 76.1→59.1; d9-TMAO were
m/z
85.1→66.3 and 85.1→68.3. The concentration of each analyte in the samples were determined from calibration curves by using the peak area ratio of the analyte to its corresponding isotope internal standard.
Flavin monooxygenase 3 (FMO3) polymorphism genotyping
Determination of
FMO3
genetic variants among the study participants was performed using PCR and Sanger sequencing. The primer set used for
FMO3
polymorphism is listed in Table S5. The PCR conditions were as follows: 95°C for 10 min, followed by 35 cycles of 95°C for 30 s, 55°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 30 s, and a final extension at 72°C for 10 min. PCR products were purified from agarose gel using Zymoclean Gel DNA Recovery Kits (Zymo Research, USA). The purified DNA fragments were then sequenced by Sanger sequencing. Two common genetic polymorphisms of

Fecal sample processing and fecal DNA extraction
Fecal samples were transported to the laboratory under cold chain conditions (4–7 °C) within 24 h of collection. Once received, they were aliquoted and stored at −80°C. Fecal genomic DNA was extracted using the QIAamp PowerFecal Pro DNA Kit, following the manufacturer’s instructions. The isolated DNA was then quantified with the NanoDrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The isolated DNA was stored at −20°C until further analysis. For the external cohort used to validate the PCR-based
gbuB
quantification, fecal samples were collected by Sigma-Transwab (Medical Wire), stored at −80°C, and extracted using the QIAamp DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen).
Library preparation and shotgun metagenome sequencing
DNA degradation degree and potential contamination were monitored on 1% agarose gels. DNA quantification was checked using Qubit® dsDNA Assay Kit in Qubit® 4.0 Fluorometer (Life Technologies, CA, USA). A total amount of 1 μg DNA per sample was used as input material for the library preparations. Sequencing libraries were generated using NEBNext® DNA Library Prep Kit (NEB, USA) following manufacturer’s recommendations and index codes were added to attribute sequences for each sample. The library quality was assessed on the Qubit 4.0 Fluorometer and Agilent bioanalyzer system. At last, library was sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, and paired 150‐bp reads were generated.
Preprocessing, assembly, and analysis of metagenome sequencing
The quality assessment of raw reads was performed using fastp (v0.23.0)
16
with default parameters, which involved trimming of low-quality bases and adaptor sequences, filtering out short-length reads (<15bp) and eliminating reads with low read-level quality. To eliminate host-derived sequences, the qualified reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg38) using BBMap (v38.93) and reads with a length coverage exceeded 16% and an identity greater than 90% were discarded. Metagenome contigs were assembled using MEGAHIT (v1.1.3)
17
with default parameters, and contigs with a minimum length of 2,500 base pairs (bps) were retained. Subsequently, the sequencing reads were mapped to the assembled contigs using minimap2. The function jgi_summarize_bam_contig_depths implemented by MetaBAT2 was utilized to estimate the depth of each contig. The contigs, alone with their associated depth information, underwent binning with VAMB (v3.0.9).
18
The genome completeness and contamination of all bins were assessed using CheckM2 (v.1.0.1) with default parameters, and bins with a size ≥200 kbp were considered as MAGs. The taxonomic classification of MAG was conducted using GTDB-Tk (v2.1.1)
19
with reference to the Genome Database Taxonomy (r207). In addition, the profiling of microbial community composition was conducted using MetaPhlAn4 with SGBs marker database vOct22, employing default parameters.

Bacterial isolation, culture, and identification
The
gbu
genes were selected as markers for isolation of key microbes involved in the conversion of γBB to TMA from the human gut, and specific primers were designed (Table S5) based on the sequences of
gbu

gbu

gbu

These samples were then subjected to serial dilution using the fcBHI medium under anaerobic conditions. After incubating for 16–24 h at 37°C, the enrichment of the target bacteria in the culture medium was confirmed by PCR with amplification of
gbu
genes. Subsequently, an appropriate bacterial suspension was plated on blood agar plates. After 24–48 h of incubation, single colonies were selected from the plate, and their identification was confirmed using PCR amplification of
gbu
genes and Sanger sequencing of 16S rRNA gene. All anaerobic procedures were carried out in an anaerobic chamber (Coy Laboratory Products, USA) maintained in an atmosphere of 80% nitrogen, 10% hydrogen, and 10% carbon dioxide, at 37°C. Bacteria containing
gbu

Statistical analysis
All graphic and statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v9 and R software v4.3.1. Student’s t-test or a Mann-Whitney U test were used for group comparisons of continuous variables as deemed appropriate and a chi-square test was used for categorical variables. Two-tailed Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient ρ values were calculated for correlation analysis.
p
values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini – Hochberg method to control the False Discovery Rate (FDR). The abundance of species-level genome bins was employed to analyze alpha diversity and beta diversity and to conduct differential abundance analysis. Beta diversity was evaluated by determining the Bray – Curtis dissimilarity index. Ordinations were developed utilizing Non-Metric Multidimensional Scaling (NMDS) analysis, and heterogeneity in gut microbial composition across sample groups was tested using ADONIS (permutational multivariate analysis of variance using distance matrices). Differential abundance analysis was performed using Analysis of Compositions of Microbiomes with Bias Correction (ANCOM-BC) using R.
