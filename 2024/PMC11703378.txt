Introduction
Pancreatic β-cell destruction is the underlying cause of Type 1 Diabetes (T1D), a chronic autoimmune illness that has seen a concerning rise in occurrence in recent years. While this increase has been particularly notable among children, affecting approximately one out of every 300 [
1
], it is important to acknowledge that the rise of T1D is not restricted to this age group. There is also a significant and growing trend of late-onset T1D, where the disease develops after the age of 30 [
2
,
3
]. Despite significant progress in comprehending diagnosis, medical care, and the prevention and treatment of complications, the burden of T1D remains substantial for both patients and their caregivers. As of now, insulin continues to be the standard of care. Their effectiveness does not prevent the challenges associated with daily compliance, secondary complications of T1D, or metabolic control improvements (
Figure 1
). In order to create novel treatment approaches for T1D, a comprehensive understanding of its cellular and molecular mechanisms is crucial. Simultaneously, there is an increasing need for robust and individualized resources to comprehend and manage intricate and diverse conditions such as T1D. This demand arises due to the rise of precision medicine, which offers promising prospects for distinct patient results. New technology, experimental techniques, and bioengineering models of pancreatic (patho)physiology have been rapidly advancing in recent years. This review (see
Flowchart
) examines the pros and cons of state-of-the-art bioengineering research methods and omics currently employed in the study of T1D. Additionally, we inquire about the potential of these techniques to predict a patient’s response to treatment.

Extracellular matrix: foundations for tissue bioengineering
The rapid advancement of technology has greatly enhanced our comprehension of the extracellular matrix (ECM). In the pancreas, a dense network of ECM molecules surrounds the islets of Langerhans. These components are found in separate locations: the inner matrix (IM) offers pliability and resilience, while the basement membrane (BM) acts as a barrier, restricting the mobility of both soluble substances and cells [
4
]. The primary components of pancreatic ECM include collagen type IV, laminin, fibronectin, and vitronectin, although there is some disagreement regarding the composition of islet ECM.
Figure 2
highlights the main pancreatic ECM proteins and their respective functions.
Figure 2.
An overview of the most prevalent pancreatic ECM components and their functions.
Although the precise roles of cell-matrix interactions in pancreatic structure and function remain incompletely comprehended, multiple investigations have demonstrated the importance of the ECM in islet function. These studies have emphasized the ECM’s role in organizing the interactions among immune cells, endocrine cells, and vascular endothelial cells. For instance, the binding of laminin-5 to the α6β1 integrin receptor leads to increased cell growth and enhanced insulin release [
5
]. Another example is that the viability, proliferation, and insulin secretion of INS-1 cells are stimulated by a mixture of collagen type IV and β1-integrin [
6
]. As a result of these interactions, oxygen, nutrients, metabolites, and signaling hormones, including insulin and glucagon, are exchanged rapidly. Moreover, the connection between the cell and the ECM is linked to improved islet performance and β cells differentiation in laboratory settings [
7
,
8
].
Having a framework like this seems to be necessary for the successful and efficient treatment of T1D by implanting pancreatic islets or induced pluripotent stem cells
in vivo
, as the ECM plays a vital role in the pancreas, including the prevention of pancreatic cell death [
9
,
10
]. A study shows that decellularized human pancreas-derived hydrogel supports human islet survival and function, emphasizing the importance of natural pancreatic ECM in maintaining islet health and recapitulating the native islet environment [
11
]. Nevertheless, limited research has been conducted in this domain, and the current availability of scientific literature is scarce. Restoring the ECM micro-architecture may help β cells operate better by creating an environment more similar to that found in their natural environment.
Islet transplantation (IT) remains a promising therapy for T1D, yet outcomes are suboptimal due to disrupted ECM microenvironment post-isolation and transplantation. Investigating ECM-islet interactions and optimizing implantation sites may reveal therapeutic interventions to improve IT and stem-cell derived βcell therapy [
12
]. Moreover, alternative transplant sites such as prevascularized subcutaneous sites have shown success in reversing diabetes without the need for permanent encapsulation devices, highlighting the importance of ECM support in islet function and survival [
13
]. These advancements underscore the critical role of ECM in pancreatic bioengineering and suggest avenues for ­further research to optimize islet-based therapies for diabetes.
In conclusion, the ECM serves as a foundation for tissue bioengineering, particularly in the context of pancreatic islets. Restoring or mimicking the ECM micro-architecture is crucial for improving the survival and function of transplanted islets, as well as for developing novel
in vitro
models. The knowledge of ECM biology has positively impacted the field of pancreatic bioengineering, leading to the development of ­innovative therapeutic strategies and
in vitro
models (
Figure 3
).

Pancreas 3D cell clusters: spheroids and organoids
Compared with conventional 2D cultures, 3D culture systems can better represent cellular responses
in vivo
[
14
]. Spheroids and organoids, which are most frequently produced on scaffold-based systems, are the two most widely utilized 3D cell architectures. Spheroids are 3D microtissues generated from monodispersed cells that are created by self-assembly. They replicate the morphogenesis, organogenesis, and embryogenesis processes that occur naturally [
15
]. Within 3D organoids, cells have the ability to autonomously arrange themselves into appropriately specialized functional cell types and precursors, mirroring their
in vivo
equivalents and replicating certain organ functions [
16
]. Pluripotent stem cells, neonatal tissue stem cells, or adult stem cells/adult progenitors can all give rise to these cells to construct organoids [
17
].
The study of T1D can benefit from the promising application of islet 3D spheroids, which are created with uniform size and incorporate stress conditions relevant to T1D. Researchers can analyze the initial phases of immunopathogenesis and evaluate the effectiveness of anti-inflammatory drugs [
18
]. In detail, using islets from human donors, islet spheroids were cultured under conditions mimicking the autoimmune milieu in T1D. All the models exhibited a decrease in β-cell function, such as reduced insulin secretion in response to glucose stimulation, along with other characteristics of T1D. The use of primary human islet spheroids in combination with proinflammatory cytokines, activated peripheral blood mononuclear cells, or HLA-A2-restricted preproinsulin-specific cytotoxic T lymphocytes in these
in vitro
models of islet-immune injury presents a potential approach for conducting detailed, efficient, and extended studies on the immunopathogenesis of T1D.
Kim Y. et al. [
19
] derived human pluripotent stem cells in 2016 and outlined the production of 3D islet-like organoids that can be effectively utilized for glucose detection both in laboratory settings and within living organisms. Interestingly, upon ectopic transplantation of these islet-like organoids into the renal capsules of immunodeficient streptozotocin-injected diabetic mice, glucose homeostasis was successfully reinstated without eliciting any immune system rejection [
20
]. These findings have offered a potential new method for studying regenerative medicine and applying these methods to the care of T1D patients. Furthermore, insights into pancreatic development and its endocrine cells were obtained through the analysis of single-cell RNA sequence (scRNA-seq) data obtained from organoids derived from the fetal pancreas of humans [
21
]. Building upon these insights, an open-label phase 1/2 study evaluating pancreatic endoderm cell implants for T1D treatment reported notable improvements, including increased C-peptide levels and meal-regulated insulin secretion, underscoring the feasibility and promise of stem cell-derived therapies in addressing T1D [
22
]. Surprisingly, Amin J. et al. [
23
] introduced a reliable and streamlined 384-well configuration for high-throughput screening of diabetes drugs. This technique holds the potential to reduce expenses and the high attrition rate associated with the discovery and development of novel diabetes medications. Additionally, the encouraging outcomes of a phase 1/2 study investigating device-encapsulated pancreatic precursor cells derived from human embryonic stem cells for T1D treatment further bolster the optimism surrounding stem cell-based therapies. Specifically, three out of ten patients displayed improved glucose control and reduced insulin dosing, underscoring the potential of stem cell-derived organoids T1D management [
24
]. Rapidly progressing techniques enable the generation of human organoids using adult or fetal pancreas cells or pluripotent stem cells, facilitating the study of pancreas physiology and pathology for a deeper comprehension of T1D [
25–27
]. In a significant advancement, researchers leveraged ready-made microvessels from adipose tissue to enhance the subcutaneous engraftment of human embryonic stem cell (hESC)-derived pancreatic progenitors and human islets. This approach accelerated diabetes reversal and glycemic normalization in mouse models of T1D, paving the way for innovative therapeutic strategies [
28
]. Specifically, organoids derived from fetuses enable the assessment of specific genes’ involvement in the development of endocrine cells [
29
], while those from adult pancreatic tissues maintain the patients’ genetic composition, leading to personalized disease models and facilitating detailed investigation into T1D pathogenic mechanisms [
30
]. Expanding on the potential of stem cells, a groundbreaking first-in-human phase 1/2 study revealed that pluripotent stem cell-derived pancreatic endoderm cells, engrafted in T1D patients, released insulin in a physiologically regulated manner. This finding hints at their potential as a scalable and renewable substitute for traditional islet transplants [
31
]. Furthermore, there are currently a wide variety of pancreas organoid models available from mice, canines, and human beings to study the pathophysiology of the pancreas and T1D [
32–34
].
To sum up, it has been shown that organoids can serve as a connection between cellular and tissue/organ models, improving comprehension of spatial arrangement and interactions among cellular and extracellular elements within
in vivo
tissue, all while preserving certain physiological capabilities. Therefore, these models possess significant potential in the representation of islet development and function, comprehending the mechanisms underlying the onset of T1D, screening for therapeutic purposes, and performing autologous islet transplantation.
Pancreas/islet-on-a-chip
The pancreas/islet-on-chip (PoC/IoC) has been suggested as a deep-level
in vitro
model utilizing organ-on-chip technology. Organoids acquire notable benefits by integrating microfluidic systems, enabling the provision of biomimetic microenvironments for cells or tissues [
35
]. The creation of these
in vitro
tools that resemble the pancreas is influenced by the biology, structure, and flow dynamics of β cells. However, each research team brings their own perspective to the design, including the appearance, size, fabrication methods, costs, and integration of microfluidics. As a result, there is considerable variation in the final product.
Over the past few years, there has been the development of disease-specific PoC devices that imitate certain key causal features of T1D. The representation of dynamic insulin secretion, activation of mitochondria, and composition of lipid in vesicles that secrete insulin, which happens in diabetes, has been shown in PoC [
36
]. Uniquely, this proof-of-concept model integrates Raman microspectroscopy, a chemical analysis method that aids in the real-time observation of insulin release. This innovative approach offers a novel means to investigate the kinetics of insulin secretion in various cell models, encompassing primary human islets and nonimmortalized human β cell cultures. Recently,
in vitro
settings have suggested that palmitic acid hydroxy stearic acid (PAHSA), a newly identified group of lipids, may have therapeutic properties for diabetes [
37
]. The study used an islet-on-a-chip microfluidic model of pancreatic islets to examine the effects of two isomers of PAHSA on cell growth, insulin expression, and insulin secretion. The results showed that PAHSA, especially 5-PAHSA, improved pseudoislet growth, increased cell count and mass, and enhanced insulin secretion in response to glucose stimulation. These results suggest that PAHSA, specifically 5-PAHSA, could possess therapeutic properties worth considering. Moreover, Glieberman and colleagues [
38
] have suggested an immunofluorescent glucose tracking and
in situ
insulin immunoassay to automatically stimulate pancreatic islets in a thermoplastic, microfluidic-based pancreas-on-chip with synchronized stimulation and continuous insulin sensing. The microfluidic arrangement enables the evaluation of islet viability, composition, and the release of metabolites directly on the chip. Crucially, Jun and colleagues [
39
] reported that a platform based on chips has the capability to imitate interstitial flow levels by utilizing an osmosis-driven control at low speeds, thereby decreasing the shear stress on islets. With enhanced insulin secretion, restoration of ECM, and maintenance of endothelial structure, this platform enables islet culture for a maximum of 30 days. Furthermore, a development has taken place where PoC devices were created to culture genetically altered and recombined native human islet cells, enabling the simultaneous acquisition of multiple functional parameters [
40
]. Differences in GPCR signaling pathways between human β cells and α cells were observed through monitoring calcium signaling and hormone secretory profiles. By examining cell-cell communication, the pancreas-on-a-chip model revealed a correlation between pancreatic endocrine function and the function of the transmembrane conductance regulator channel. Moreover, these
in vitro
systems derived from patients could be utilized for the advancement of personalized medicine by assessing the functions of pancreatic epithelial or endocrine cells. The unique proof-of-concept configurations can be effortlessly adapted for clinical use, enhancing the accuracy of existing islet tests conducted before transplantation. This will broaden our understanding of human islet biology and disease progression while also enabling innovative approaches to T1D research.
Despite the remarkable increase in PoC complexity from the original models and prototypes, the overall intricacy of the pancreas remains inadequately portrayed. In this context, numerous researchers directed their attention and dedicated their studies to particular processes or structures. The available PoC systems now incorporate the fundamental characteristics of the Langerhans islets that contain primary rat β cells [
41
], the distinct vascular arrangement of the pancreas [
42
], and the paracrine interactions within islets following the localization of secreted soluble factors or drug metabolism [
39
].
Furthermore, T1D has been widely documented as a systemic condition with significant implications beyond the pancreas [
43
]. As a result, the integration of proof-of-concept devices has advanced to the point where disease-specific models are being merged with additional ones like liver, muscle, and brain to provide a more accurate representation of pancreatic functions and their effects on the entire system [
35
,
44–46
]. To comprehend the interaction between organs in T1D, multi-organ chips like pancreas-, adipose-, and gut-on-a-chip connected could be especially appropriate. In a multiorgan-on-a-chip system, the evaluation of the connection between human micro-islets and liver spheroids was conducted in 2017 [
47
]. The researchers were able to examine islet reactions to glucose and insulin release, as well as glucose absorption by the pancreas spheroids, using the perfusable arrangement on this platform. This indicates the existence of effective communication between the two organs. Additional research is underway to develop a ‘physiome-on-a-chip’ platform that includes a wider range of organs and is applicable to diabetes [
47
,
48
]. Although multi-organ and etiology-centered approaches have advanced, there remains a lack of consensus regarding cellular sources and mechanobiological cues within the various PoC models (For example, whether endothelial, endocrine, and mesenchymal cells perform optimally on multi-organ systems). Moreover, the duration of maintaining cultural conditions on chips is reduced. Due to the limited duration of this period and the lack of adequate proof regarding the effectiveness of the system, it is necessary to enhance and optimize multi-organ systems in order to conduct future analysis on the formation and function of β cells.
Tissue-based approaches
Slices of pancreatic tissue serve as a natural
ex vivo
model resembling islets, maintaining intact interactions between cells and the ECM [
49
]. Pancreas tissue slice systems utilize
ex vivo
pancreas explants of a specified thickness. In contrast to primary islets, which have a limited lifespan and experience significant loss of function in culture, pancreatic tissue slice cultures can be sustained for a duration of 10 days under ideal circumstances [
50
]. Islets are sliced to preserve tissue morphology using a less harmful and quicker preparation method, unlike isolated islets that lose their anatomical polarity post-isolation [
51
]. In this artificial environment, a significant portion of the cellular structure of the organ is maintained, enabling the examination of islet function and the relationships among endocrine, exocrine, neural, vascular, and immune cells within their anatomical framework. The establishment of this system involves the utilization of mouse, porcine, and human donors, enabling morphological and functional examinations of the endocrine and exocrine pancreas [
51–55
].
Pancreatic tissue slices from patients undergoing pancreas transplantation or surgical biopsy or resection are obtained from explanted, resected, or nontransplantable human tissues. Slices of the pancreas can also be acquired through the Network for Pancreatic Organ Donors with Diabetes (nPOD) program, which focuses on donors with diabetes [
56
,
57
]. The Krumdieck (currently Alabama) tissue slicer is typically used to produce pancreas slices with a diameter of 150 μm and a thickness ranging from 100 to 200 μm [
51
]. This is important as it impacts the amount of oxygen and nutrients absorbed during
in vitro
experiments. The slices are subsequently cultured using William’s E medium in conventional tissue plates under static, dynamic, or bioreactor-based culture conditions. Under stagnant circumstances, cultures of pancreas slices experience a reduced lifespan of 48 h due to the presence of hypoxia/hyperoxia and heightened cell mortality. In order to create quasi-physiological conditions for islets in culture, various techniques have been employed, including the utilization of artificial oxygen carriers, agitation of cultures, and the implementation of perfusion bioreactors, all aimed at improving the oxygen and media supply [
50
,
58
]. A single investigation documented that human pancreatic slices maintained their viability and retained both endocrine and exocrine function for a minimum of 10 days after being cut using an oxygen-enhancing rocking platform during
ex vivo
cultures [
50
]. By combining this technique with adenoviral-mediated lineage tracking, researchers were able to conduct lineage tracking experiments in a natural organotypic culture environment. This allowed them to monitor the formation of β cells in real-time as a response to a known stimulus for regeneration. Preserving organotypic pancreatic tissue for an extended period provides a distinct chance to investigate the regeneration of human endocrine cells in a cultured environment while maintaining the structural integrity of the original organ.
In recent times, there has been a focus on examining immune cells within and surrounding islets using live pancreatic slice models [
53
,
59
]. Imaging techniques can ascertain the infiltration, phenotype, and functionality of immune cells. The pancreatic slice models, while being processed, maintain the original structure and function of the islets. Additionally, this kind of model facilitates the investigation of autoimmune cell behavior during the progression of T1D, encompassing the evaluation of islet macrophage functionality and calcium flux [
60
,
61
]. Studies requiring the preservation of the structural organization and integrity of human lymph nodes, tonsils, or thymus have utilized additional tissue sections [62–64]. The examination of the control of T-cell reactions by lymph node stroma and the movement of thymocytes between the cortex and medulla was permitted. Although the identification of antigen-specific T cells in thymic and lymph nodes has not been accomplished at present, these sections are crucial for investigating the immune mechanisms of T1D.
Culturing slices of the human pancreas has been essential for disease modeling and studying the dynamic islet physiology, cell biology, and function [
65–68
]. It also helps explore the intricate interplay between the neurovascular networks of tissues and organs in both healthy and diseased states [
69
,
70
]. The significant advancements in technology and the prolonged existence of culture have opened up immense possibilities for studying the cellular diversity of patient-specific pancreatic tissues and for testing new drugs for diabetes.
Decellularized pancreas scaffold-based approach
The development of a suitable scaffold/matrix that accurately imitates a pancreatic microenvironment similar to
in vivo
conditions is crucial for engineering
in vitro
models and achieving functional cells that are realistic. The efficiency of a scaffold in supporting the survival and function of pancreatic cells is influenced by a multitude of factors, such as its structure, mechanical characteristics, pore size, degradation rate, porosity, elasticity or stiffness, and overall design [
71–74
]. Thus, recreating the natural ECM microenvironment requires careful consideration of substrate design and biomechanical characteristics. To this end, a wide array of techniques and materials, encompassing synthetic, natural, and hybrid compositions, have been devised to produce scaffolds with the ability to accommodate cells for the generation of biomimetic tissue constructs. These constructs serve as valuable models for both healthy and impaired pancreases [
75–77
]. The pancreatic matrisome, which has been produced, described, and confirmed in recent research, includes ECM markers other than collagen (as demonstrated earlier). This matrisome holds promise as a suitable matrix for
in vitro
models aimed at investigating pancreatic diseases. In this review, particular emphasis is placed on the decellularized pancreas scaffold method, primarily due to the inherent natural biochemical and structural cues found in the decellularized ECM. Synthetic materials and 3D bioprinting techniques have not been able to precisely replicate these cues [
78
,
79
], despite the considerable progress made in islet transplantation [
80
].
The decellularization process removes the cellular and nuclear elements of the tissue, leaving only the ECM scaffold intact [
81
]. To remove cells from an organ, a variety of techniques, including physical, chemical, and enzymatic methods, are employed for decellularization [
82–86
]. The ECM structure and components are susceptible to harm from severe conditions. Hence, it is crucial to meticulously fine-tune the decellularization procedures for every individual organ. Different studies have reported the use of various substances such as sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), Triton X, per-acetic acid, ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), and trypsin for the decellularization of different organs [
81
]. The viability and functionality of intact islets infused through the pancreatic duct were proven by comparing different perfusion routes, including the artery and portal vein. The application of temperature is another crucial aspect of the decellularization process. Perfusing the porcine pancreas with sodium deoxycholate and Triton X-100 at 4 °C is able to effectively remove cells while maintaining the integrity of the ECM [
87
,
88
]. Moreover, it has been demonstrated that the enzymatic activity responsible for ECM degradation is suppressed at a temperature of 5 °C [
89
].
Successful decellularization is typically achieved when the decellularized organ has less than 50 ng of DNA per mg of dry weight, shows no visible nuclear material after staining with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) or 4′, 6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI), and contains DNA fragments shorter than 200 bp [
90
]. Ideally, the decellularized framework should preserve an undamaged vascular network structure and matrix-bound proteins like growth factors and cytokines, which are capable of enhancing recellularization and cellular activity. Discarded human pancreata, initially collected for transplantation but later considered unsuitable, serve as a potential source for pancreas decellularization. Pigs, which are sizable creatures, can also serve as pancreas donors for decellularization due to their similar anatomy and proximity in size to the human pancreas [
87
,
91
]. Recently, a decellularized ECM derived from caprine pancreas has also been found to be capable of cultivating islets, which can grow for up to 9 days
in vitro
, affording a safe alternative as a transplantable organ from a xenogeneic source for T1D [
85
].
Typically, an assessment of the physical characteristics of the decellularized matrix is conducted following the process of decellularization. To assess the microstructure of the matrix, techniques such as scanning electron microscopy, transmission electron microscopy, and corrosive casting are employed [
75
,
85
,
92
]. Give a few examples: atomic force microscopy and mechanical testing are used to assess the biophysical characteristics. By using a quantibody human growth factor array, one can determine whether the growth factor is being retained. On the basis of the composition analysis of ECM, an analysis of the proteome of the matrix can be performed using proteomics [
93
]. Additionally, quantifying the DNA content in the decellularized matrix is crucial due to the immune responses triggered by foreign DNA [
85
]. Recently, a new method has been suggested to evaluate the quality of the decellularized pancreas through a scoring system [
94
]. This system primarily emphasizes the efficiency of decellularization and the integrity of the pancreas’ vascular system.
Following the process of decellularization, the original organic framework undergoes repopulation. Perfusion recellularization through the portal vein or pancreatic duct is the prevailing approach, although there have been reports of using perfusion through the splenic artery with direct injection of cells into the parenchyma and perfusion through the Left gastric artery [
95–97
]. Organoids are commonly used to re-perfuse, often along with another cell type like fibroblasts, endothelial cells, or mesenchymal stem cells [
98
]. Furthermore, vascularized organoids have demonstrated the ability to reassemble into larger interconnected channels to facilitate perfusion. This phenomenon has been utilized in the context of islet transplantation to achieve integration with the vasculature of the recipient [
99
,
100
]. In addition, it has been observed in pre-clinical studies that alternative vascularization units, specifically adipose-tissue-derived microvascular fragments (MVFs), exhibit enhanced efficacy in the reassembly of microvessels during the initial phase following islet transplantation [
101
,
102
]. Co-transplantation of MVFs with islets in both the kidney capsule and subcutaneous space has been shown to enhance islet engraftment and successfully restore normoglycemia in individuals with diabetes [
101
]. Successful seeding of other important cell types onto whole decellularized pancreata has also been achieved through perfusion or static recellularization, including insulinoma cells [
103
], acinar cells [
104
], mesenchymal stem cells [
105
], and pluripotent stem cell-derived pancreatic progenitor cells [
106
] and β-like cells [
107
]. Perfused cells infiltrate and attach to the scaffold framework, replenishing the decellularized organ formations. One way to accomplish this is by utilizing custom-made bioreactors and creating mediums that imitate the fluidics and environment found in the body [
108
]. Afterwards, the organ must also undergo revascularization through the introduction of endothelial cells prior to transplantation into a living organism. The purpose of this is to guarantee a properly working circulatory system that has enough oxygen and nutrient transfer to sustain cellular activities and to avoid blood clotting when connecting the donated organ to the recipient’s blood vessels. So far, human umbilical vein endothelial cells [
109
] alone or in combination with human fibroblasts [
110
], rat endothelial progenitor cells [
111
], human primary pancreatic endothelial cells [
108
], as well as normal aortic endothelial cells [
97
] have been utilized to vascularize the decellularized matrices through the application of co-seeding techniques [
110
] or heparin immobilization [
112
].
In 2013, the creation of decellularized scaffolds from mouse pancreas were capable of being recellularized with acinar and β cell lines, culturing for up to 5 days [
113
]. Following this, porcine pancreatic islets and human amniotic fluid-derived stem cells were seeded onto cell-free scaffolds from the pig pancreas [
91
]. Then, the repopulation of Langerhans islets into a decellularized rat liver was carried out [
114
]. The engrafted islets were incubated with both endothelial cells and mesenchymal stromal cells. This process also resulted in the development of a neo-pancreas with endothelialization capable of secreting insulin. Due to the presence of shared key elements in the pancreatic and pulmonary ECM and a significant similarity in the vascular structure of the pancreas and lung, decellularized rat lung received human pancreatic islets and endothelial cells, resulting in increased insulin release for a period of 30 days, surpassing the insulin release of standard islets [
115
]. These platforms completely transformed the idea of regenerative medicine, where a bioengineered organ is typically produced using ECM obtained from an identical organ. Exploring and capitalizing on the resemblances among various organ ECMs could open up possibilities for innovative organ-bioengineered collaborations.
In general, pancreas decellularization presents a possible remedy for the scarcity of donor organs, inadequate rates of islet engraftment, and deterioration of islet function and viability after islet transplantation. This is achieved by expanding the availability of donor pancreas suitable for transplantation. Therefore, the use of decellularized scaffolds demonstrated a hopeful bioengineered framework for producing a synthetic pancreas or islet that closely resembles the natural one, which is particularly crucial for managing T1D. In addition, mass spectrometry and multiplex ELISA data from pancreatic decellularized tissue help us understand the molecular footprint of the human pancreas, and further understanding of the pancreas’ physiology and pathology can be gained through this approach [
92
].
Harnessing omics analyses to study T1D
In 2012, a worldwide analysis of miRNA expression in pancreatic islets of female non-obese diabetes (NOD) mice aged 4 and 8 weeks (representing control and pre-diabetic conditions, respectively) was conducted to investigate the involvement of miRNAs in the death of β cells linked to the development of T1D [
116
]. In the past few years, the progress in omics techniques has resulted in an increased number of findings regarding the genetic and molecular changes associated with pancreas/islet biology, as well as the comprehensive analysis of diseases. The use of these advanced technologies has also made it possible to gain a comprehensive understanding of T1D in the medical environment, and they are producing noninvasive methods for diagnosing the condition, which allows for more accurate treatment choices. The integration of solitary omics and/or various omics opens up opportunities for understanding the causes, identification, and management of T1D.
Recently, transcriptomic profiles of human adult islet α and β cells have been depicted [
117
]. According to this report, β cells that expressed both MAF bZIP transcription factor A (MAFA) and MAF bZIP transcription factor B (MAFB) showed higher gene expression related to glucose sensing and had increased electrical activity compared to cells expressing only one of the transcription factors. Understanding the pathways responsible for coordinating hormone secretion within pancreatic islets can provide us with assistance. ScRNA-seq data from the human islet can determine cell identity/heterogeneity or disease status by examining the cellular landscape and various insulin secretion profiles [
117–119
]. Another seminal study delves into the cellular and molecular basis of β-cell functional heterogeneity, identifying CD63 as a novel marker associated with enhanced glucose metabolism and insulin secretion, offering promising avenues for diabetes diagnosis and therapy [
120
].
Unraveling the intricate web of gene regulatory networks that coordinate the determination of different cell types remains an ongoing obstacle for ­scientists in the field of developmental biology. The utilization of single-cell examinations presents possibilities to tackle these difficulties and expedite the revelation of infrequent cell lineage connections and the mechanisms that underlie hierarchical lineage choices [
121
]. The research reveals networks of transcription factors that define the lineages of β, α, and δ cells. By conducting genomic footprint analysis, the researchers discover DNA interactions between transcription factors and regulatory elements that control the development of pancreatic cells with an unprecedented level of detail. They also provide a molecular analysis of the differentiation of mouse pancreatic endocrine cells using single-cell transcriptomic data, indicating that the transcription factor Neurog3 might function as a pioneering factor in determining the pancreatic endocrine lineage. The discovery has the potential to enhance procedures for producing substitute endocrine cells for diabetes treatment using sustainable sources such as stem cells.
The involvement of the exocrine pancreas in T1D has been initially clarified through the combination of scRNA-seq and cytometry by time of flight, mass cytometry [
122
]. The findings indicate that the exocrine compartment’s cells exhibit alterations in transcription and gene ontology in the context of T1D, implying that pancreatic ductal cells might have a role in inhibiting CD4
+
T cells within pancreatic tissues.
RNA sequencing is unable to capture modifications that happen after mRNA translation into proteins. Therefore, mass spectrometry becomes a crucial instrument for identifying these significant organisms. Yau and colleagues [
123
] aimed to identify significant alterations in the entire islet proteome linked to metabolic well-being through the utilization of mouse models induced by genetic modifications or dietary interventions. The results suggest that genetic factors are the main contributors to the observed variations in protein expression within pancreatic islets. Subsequently, the comparative examination of proteomics demonstrated a robust correlation between proteins connected to mitochondrial activity and metabolic well-being, emphasizing the significance of mitochondria in the compensation of β-cells to excessive nutrients. This analysis unveiled the causal connections between alterations in cellular metabolism and the compromised function of β-cells. In a separate investigation, after Mary and her colleagues [
124
] evaluated 4,955 plasma proteins in a group of 8,923 individuals participating in the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) Study, they found 47 plasma proteins that can accurately predict the development of diabetes. In addition, pathways associated with inflammation and lipid metabolism were identified, which may have implications for diagnosis and therapy. Furthermore, different altered proteomic techniques have also been extensively employed to investigate the underlying mechanisms of diabetes. A report utilized phosphoproteomics to present a comprehensive diagram illustrating the alterations in crucial signaling pathways within the diabetic phosphorylation network [
125
]. It also identified the primary regulatory signaling axes responsible for insulin secretion. The authors, Syed et al. [
126
], conducted a discovery-based proteomics approach on islets obtained longitudinally from NOD mice and NOD-SCID mice. Commonly modulated pathways in these T1D mouse models included EIF2 signaling, the unfolded protein response, mTOR signaling, mitochondrial function, and oxidative phosphorylation. In the meantime, an upregulation of a specific protein called protein disulphide isomerase A1 (PDIA1) was observed in both NOD islets and pancreatic sections obtained from human organ donors who tested positive for autoantibodies or had T1D. The research yielded significant findings regarding the time-limited increase in proteins associated with stress management and the preservation of β cell functionality. Additionally, MHC class I-associated peptidome exocrine proteins could be the biomarker of T1D identified by proteomic profiling [
127
]. Recently, the utilization of targeted proteomics has enhanced our comprehension of HLA allele-specific antigen presentation, potentially aiding in the discovery of immunopeptides for therapeutic purposes in T1D [
128
]. Alongside various endeavors, Kolic and colleagues [
129
] recently conducted a groundbreaking study leveraging advanced proteomics techniques to decipher the intricate proteome landscape of pancreatic β cells in the context of diabetes. Collectively, Kolic et al.’s research complements and enhances the growing body of knowledge surrounding the role of proteomics in diabetes research. It underscores the importance of continued exploration into the complex proteome of β cells and their interactions with the broader metabolic landscape, ultimately aiming to improve the diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment of diabetes.
Metabolomics is the study of tiny compounds and metabolic substances, including amino acids, lipids, and sugars. Metabolic differences can distinguish between different (sub)types of diabetes. To differentiate maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) from healthy controls and distinguish MODY3 from T1D, glycerophosphocholine was chosen as a diagnostic biomarker [
130
]. In individuals at a heightened risk for T1D, metabolomic investigations additionally indicated a rise in the levels of pyruvate in the bloodstream [
131
]. The buildup of pyruvate caused by inflammation is a distinguishing feature of T1D, impeding additional oxidation in the Krebs cycle. The study additionally found a decrease in alanine concentrations in individuals who are susceptible to T1D, which could potentially act as a compensatory mechanism. In addition, the study has shown that inhibition of the AMP-kinase pathway is a method employed to counteract hypoglycemia associated with T1D.
The use of (multi)-omics technologies will persist in producing extensive datasets that offer new ­mechanistic understandings of the causes of T1D (

). Moreover, by increasing usage and expanding the scale, these methods also open up the potential for individualized diagnoses and treatment approaches.

Implications and prospects for the future
Researchers face numerous challenges in improving treatment options and developing more precise models due to the intricate nature of T1D. Nevertheless, it is through these difficulties that numerous engineering possibilities emerge. As shown previously (also summarized in
Table 1
), a number of successful approaches exist for identifying the genetic, molecular, and cellular occurrences that precede the clinical initiation of T1D, investigating and analyzing the performance of isolated islets with great precision in live conditions, and creating
in vitro
environments for islets that better replicate their natural surroundings. These methods have been expanded beyond individual cell types placed in a three-dimensional setting to microenvironments created to promote blood vessel formation and decrease immune reactions after transplantation. Furthermore, there is ongoing development of biomaterial systems to facilitate the integration of other cell varieties like mesenchymal stem cells, which can help decrease the inflammatory reaction, or pancreatic stem and progenitor cells, which can promote islet-like differentiation and provide sustained glycemic regulation.

To create an authentic and informative disease model for T1D, more work is needed to enhance the heterogeneity and functionality of islet organoids, as the currently existing ones are typically less developed than native islets. Anticipated advancements in technology are also predicted to offer expansion tools that allow for increased adaptability, screening-friendly multiwell formats, and microfluidic systems. The utilization of microfluidic systems would allow for the simultaneous retrieval of various physiological parameters and the integration of organoid models from diverse human organs. Although there are obstacles to achieving stable conditions for prolonged periods, these systems have the potential to facilitate the replication of inter-organ communications within organoid systems. Emerging are hybrid cultural approaches that offer guiding geometric limitations while facilitating the self-organizational characteristics of organoids due to the connections between architecture, differentiation, and function.
There are still a few obstacles to overcome in order to widely adopt pancreas-on-a-chip systems, which include the complex handling process and the less-than-ideal physical properties of the materials used in their creation. Enhancing pancreas/islet on-chip systems should involve the inclusion of biomechanical stimuli commonly associated with T1D research, including environmental factors and possibly the pertinent immune cells. Also, it is expected that the incorporation of biochemical or biological parameter sensors will greatly enhance the ability to analyze cells in real-time in response to new drugs. Furthermore, despite the significant progress made by researchers, the realization of a ‘body-on-chip’ remains a lofty aspiration. A multi-organ-on-a-chip could be used to simulate the entire human physiology, resulting in excellent accuracy and model complexity, which would be a solid foundation for T1D research.
Pancreas tissue slices enable comprehension of the pancreatic reaction to novel substances, albeit within a restricted timeframe. Enhancing the feasible duration of incubation could prove highly advantageous, potentially achieved through the integration of multiple
in vitro
systems such as slices as well as tissue obtained from individuals or models with T1D.
The immense potential of decellularized pancreas systems, predominantly employing matrices derived from nature, offers the significant benefit of emulating the native microenvironment of the human pancreas. However, this also presents challenges in terms of standardization and widespread adoption. Expanding the utilization of matrices could be facilitated by developing and validating them with a specified composition, potentially incorporating the predominant constituents in appropriate proportions. Likewise, it is important to standardize the experimental factors that imitate the biomechanics of the blood circulation, such as shear stress, pressure, stiffness, and others. This standardization will enable easier comparison of findings across various research teams. In addition, biomedical scientists are currently confronted with the task of effectively and efficiently repopulating these organ scaffolds, particularly under conditions involving multiple cell types and perfusion. An appropriate framework for functional tissue engineering must possess the following characteristics: (1) it should be simple to remove and decellularize; (2) it should maintain the original structural connections and vascular channels; (3) it should allow repopulation through immersion or perfusion; and (4) it should offer the necessary signals for cellular adhesion and proliferation.
The application of omics in T1D, as well as other biomedical fields, is vast, making it challenging to maintain up-to-date literature and utilize cutting-edge methods. The utilization of fixed tissue in the ongoing prototyping of single-cell analysis will revolutionize our current understanding of spatial omics. Nevertheless, mapping tissue necessitates substantial monetary commitment and exceptional experimental planning. Hence, collaborations between public and private entities involving researchers and doctors would be highly intriguing in order to promote the progress of understanding.
In general, the methods discussed in this analysis and those that are emerging have the potential to greatly aid in comprehending T1D, creating novel treatments, and promoting individualized healthcare for this persistent condition. Naturally, it is essential to amalgamate them in a righteous manner, incorporating tissue/cells derived from humans whenever feasible and enhancing our ability to replicate human ailments in a laboratory setting. Success is guaranteed if multidisciplinary teams continue to develop future work.
