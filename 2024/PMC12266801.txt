INTRODUCTION
HCC is the most common type of liver malignancy and represents the third most common cause of cancer death.
1
HCC typically arises in the context of liver damage resulting from excessive inflammation associated with risk factors such as metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), infection with HBV or HCV, and excessive alcohol consumption.
2
MASLD has become the fastest growing etiology for HCC in the United States due to the increasing prevalence of obesity.
3
Metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis (MASH), the inflammatory component of MASLD, is associated with increased cell death and regeneration that can cause liver injury, fibrosis, and/or cirrhosis that can progress to HCC.
4
However, the molecular mechanisms by which MASH contributes to hepatocarcinogenesis remain poorly understood.
5
The hypoxia-associated factor, HAF (encoded by
SART1
), is a multifunctional molecule that was initially identified as a transcriptional coactivator of
EPO
.
6
We have shown that HAF similarly binds and activates hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF)-2α–dependent transcription of
VEGFA
and
OCT3/4
.
6
–
8
In addition to its DNA binding cotranscriptional activity, HAF also functions as an E3 ubiquitin ligase that ubiquitinates and degrades HIF-1α and the tumor suppressor protein, neurofibromin.
9
,
10
Homozygous HAF loss is embryonic lethal in mice, suggesting a nonredundant role for HAF during development.
11
Male C57BL/6 mice heterozygous for HAF (
SART1
+/−
) presented with steatosis at age 6 months that progressed to MASH and HCC without any further dietary or other intervention.
11
At age >18 months, >90% of male
SART1
+/−
mice presented with histologically confirmed MASH-HCC.
11
HAF haploinsufficiency was associated with marked upregulation of HIF-1α and increased secretion of the HIF-1α regulated chemokine, RANTES, by KCs isolated from preneoplastic
SART1
+/−
livers, implicating KC-derived RANTES with HCC progression in the
SART1
+/−
mice.
11
However, no HIF-1α upregulation was observed in preneoplastic hepatocytes from
SART1
+/−
mice, and the cell type driving hepatocarcinogenesis associated with HAF haploinsufficiency is unclear.
11
In this study, we investigate the impact of HAF insufficiency in hepatocytes and cells of the myeloid lineage to identify their involvement in hepatocarcinogenesis associated with HAF loss. We show that HAF depletion in hepatocytes is sufficient for MASH-HCC initiation. Significantly, we reveal HAF as an essential mediator of NF-κB activity through the transcriptional regulation of TRADD and RIPK1, which has important implications in the pathogenesis of MASH and MASH-HCC.
METHODS
Mice, cell lines, and reagents
C57BL6/J mice were purchased from Jackson Labs (JAX #000664) for the generation of SART1-floxed mice. Floxed mice were crossed with Alb-Cre (JAX #018961), LysM-Cre (Jax #004781), or Alf-p-Cre (a generous gift from Tom Luedde at University Hospital Dusseldorf). All animal studies were reviewed and approved by the University of Utah Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. THLE3, HepG2, and SNU475 cells were purchased from ATCC, whereas HUH7 were from JCRB Cell Bank (NIBIO). The NF-κB Luciferase Reporter HepG2 stable cell line was purchased from Signosis. HepG2 and HUH7 cells were grown in DMEM (ThermoFisher Scientific), while SNU475 cells were grown in RPMI-1640 (ATCC), both supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco). Cell lines were authenticated by STR testing upon receipt and were routinely tested and verified as mycoplasma negative using the MycoAlert kit (Lonza). Human recombinant TNF was from BioLegend Inc., whereas MG132 and cycloheximide were from Sigma-Aldrich.
Human tissue
Human tissue microarrays (TMAs) containing deidentified patient liver samples were purchased from Protein Biotechnologies (TMA1427/1428) or from Xenotech (TMA.NASH 2210347, TMA.NAS 1910265). Additional large tissue sections containing livers with steatosis, MASH, HCC, and adjacent non-HCC tissue sections were from archival samples from deidentified patients seen at Huntsman Cancer Institute (HCI). The protocol was reviewed by the Institutional Review Board at HCI and determined to be exempt from human subjects research (exemption number IRB_00091019). Slides were stained using a previously validated monoclonal HAF antibody.
12
Slides were scanned using the Aperio AT2 digital scanning system (TMA1427/1428), and HAF nuclear staining intensity quantitation was performed using Aperio digital imaging software (Leica Biosystems) or was quantitated manually (TMA.NAS, TMA.NASH, large tissue sections) by a pathologist (Kimberley J. Evason) using H-score quantitation, which is a product of intensity (1, 2 or 3+ for mild, moderate, and strong) × the percentage of cells stained.
13
Statistical analysis
Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software versus 10.2.2. Results are representative of at least 3 independent experiments, and data are expressed as mean ± SEM. Normal distribution was determined using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Statistical significance between the 2 groups was analyzed by unpaired
t
test when data followed a Gaussian distribution or Mann-Whitney test as a nonparametric alternative. Comparisons between more than 2 groups were determined by 1-way or 2-way ANOVA with a Bonferroni or Tukey post hoc test to detect significant differences as indicated.
All other methods are described in Supplemental Methods,
http://links.lww.com/HEP/I644
.

Hepatocyte-specific HAF deletion is associated with lipid dysregulation
To characterize the metabolic changes associated with progression to HCC, we performed lipidomics profiling of livers from male control and
hepS
−/
−
mice at ages 6, 12, and 18 months (5 mice per group), and liver tumors from 3 male 18-month-old
hepS
−/
−
mice. We found significant enrichment of triglycerides in
hepS
−/
−

2

1

20
At 18 months, the tumor-bearing livers of the
hepS
−/
−

2

hepS
−/
−
tumors compared with livers from age-matched wild-type littermates (Figures

21
We also observed a significant decrease in C22:0 and trending increases in C16:0 and C24:1 ceramides (Cers) in the
hepS
−/
−
tumors compared with normal liver from nontumor-bearing hepS
−/
−
mice and wild-type control mice (Supplemental Figure S2A,
http://links.lww.com/HEP/I646

Although the specific function of C22:0 Cer is unclear, the alteration of C16/C22–C24 ratios has been proposed to markedly impact cellular function, whereas upregulation of C16:0 Cer in the liver is associated with altered glucose and lipid metabolism, increased oxidative stress, and enhanced susceptibility to apoptosis and HCC.
22
,
23
In this regard, both ACars and Cers can cause increased cell death and/or reactive oxygen species that drive inflammation, in part through activation of the NF-κB pathway, thus driving the lipotoxicity associated with MASH.
24
,
25

Hepatocyte-specific HAF deletion is associated with downregulation of the NF-κB pathway
To investigate the potential association of HAF with the NF-κB pathway, we examined the phosphorylation of the transcriptional subunits of NF-κB: p65 and p50. Phosphorylation of p65 at S536 (P-p65) promotes p65 nuclear translocation and transactivation, whereas phosphorylation of p50 at S337 (P-p50) is required for its DNA binding activity.
30
,
31
IκB is transcriptionally induced by p65/p50 through a negative feedback mechanism, thus serving as a surrogate indicator of NF-κB transcriptional activation.
32
HAF depletion in the
hepS
−/
−
and
LPC
-S
+/−
mouse models was associated with a significant reduction of P-p65 and P-p50, and in reduced TNF-induced IκB in both isolated hepatocytes and in whole livers (Figures
3
A–C, Supplemental Figures S3A, B,
http://links.lww.com/HEP/I647
), with trending decreases in P-p65 in the
macS
−/
−
mice (Supplemental Figure S3C,
http://links.lww.com/HEP/I647
). In addition, there was a trend of decreased total p65 in all mouse models (Figures
3
A, B, Supplemental Figures S3A–C,
http://links.lww.com/HEP/I647
). By contrast, no consistent changes were observed in the activation of mTOR, AMPK, or autophagy pathways (Supplemental Figure S3D,
http://links.lww.com/HEP/I647
). To investigate the effect of HAF on the NF-κB pathway independent of any potential liver pathology, we performed HAF small interfering RNA (siRNA) knockdown in a panel of cell lines, which resulted in a marked reduction in P-p65, P-p50, and IκB in the absence or presence of TNF treatment (Figure
3
D, Supplemental Figure S3G,
http://links.lww.com/HEP/I647
), and markedly depleted many upstream component including TRADD, TAK1, RIPK1 and NEMO (Supplemental Figure S3E, F,
http://links.lww.com/HEP/I647
). This was associated with significantly decreased TNF-induced luciferase activity of cells stably expressing the NF-κB response element fused to a luciferase reporter (Figure
3

3

HAF promotes transcriptional activation of TRADD and RIPK1
We next sought to elucidate the mechanism by which HAF regulates NF-κB activation. HAF siRNA markedly depleted TRADD, RIPK1, TAK1 and NEMO, and P-p50 and P-p65 (Supplemental Figures S3E, F,
http://links.lww.com/HEP/I647
, Figure
4
A), which were also reduced in isolated hepatocytes from
LPC-S
+/−
mice (Supplemental Figure S4A,
http://links.lww.com/HEP/I648
). Treatment with the proteasome inhibitor, MG132, attenuated the siHAF-induced decrease in NEMO, but did not prevent decreases in TRADD or RIPK1 suggesting that HAF knockdown may promote the degradation of NEMO, but not of TRADD and RIPK1 (Figure
4
B). Cotreatment of HAF siRNA-transfected cells with the lysosomal inhibitor, chloroquine, only partially attenuated the siHAF-induced decrease in RIPK1 and did not affect the siHAF-induced decrease in TRADD, TAK1, or NEMO (Supplemental Figure S4C,
http://links.lww.com/HEP/I648
). In addition, although cotreatment of HAF siRNA-transfected cells with cycloheximide (inhibitor of mRNA translation) resulted in a trend of decreased stability of these proteins (particularly TAK1 and NEMO), these changes were not significant, suggesting that decreased protein stability is not the primary mechanism driving their decrease in the context of HAF siRNA (Figure
4
C, Supplemental Figure S4B,
http://links.lww.com/HEP/I648
). Indeed, quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction analysis demonstrated that
RIPK1
and
TRADD
mRNA levels were significantly reduced with HAF siRNA, whereas no consistent changes were observed in the transcript levels of
TAK1, NEMO
, and
RelA
(Figure
4

http://links.lww.com/HEP/I648
). The transcriptional decreases in

and

TAK1
,

, or

HAF binds its consensus HAF binding site ([HBS]; CCCCRRCCCC) within regulatory regions of
VEGFA
and
OCT-3/4
, which contributes to their hypoxia-induced transcription.
8
We identified 2 potential HBSs flanking the
TRADD
transcriptional start site based on TRADD-201 transcript ENST00000345057: site 1 was 6604 bases upstream, whereas site 2 was 1319 bases downstream of the transcriptional start site. Similarly, we identified a single HBS within the reverse motif 800 base pairs upstream of the transcriptional start site of RIPK1 (based on ENST00000259808) (Figure
4
Fi). To investigate the functionality of these putative HBSs, we generated reporter constructs for each of these binding regions (Figure
4
Fi). The presence of an HBS on the TRADD site 1 construct did not promote an increase in luciferase activity (Supplemental Figure S4E,
http://links.lww.com/HEP/I648
), suggesting that this HBS is not transcriptionally active. However, the presence of the HBS from TRADD site 2 and the RIPK1 site significantly increased luciferase reporter activity, which was significantly increased in HAF overexpressing cells (Figure
4

4

RIPK1
and
TRADD
through the presence of HBSs within the regulatory regions of these genes.
HAF loss promotes spontaneous apoptosis
Consistent with the role of NF-κB in promoting cell survival, ablation of NF-κB pathway components (p65, NEMO, and TAK1) leads to liver damage and HCC in mice due to increased cell death, resulting in inflammation and compensatory proliferation.
34
–
38
To investigate the impact of HAF on cell death, we examined the impact of HAF downregulation on cell viability. HAF knockdown significantly reduced the viability of HCC cells, as well as THLE3 immortalized human hepatocytes (Figure
5
A, Supplemental Figure S5A,
http://links.lww.com/HEP/I649

HAF is suppressed during steatosis but upregulated in MASH/HCC
To investigate potential physiological regulators of HAF, we investigated the impact of hypoxia, increased free fatty acids, or TNF, conditions prevalent during MASH, on HAF levels. Exposure to hypoxia markedly decreased HAF levels (Figure
6
A), which we have previously reported.
7
Exposure of primary hepatocytes isolated from control or
LPC
-S
+/−
mice to hypoxia also resulted in substantial decreases in HAF, which was associated with striking increases in HIF-1α (Figure
6
B), consistent with HAFs previously reported role as an E3 ligase for HIF-1α.
9
In addition, exposure to oleic acid and particularly to stearic acid resulted in marked decreases in HAF protein levels in a time-dependent manner (Supplemental Figure S6A,
http://links.lww.com/HEP/I650
). Conversely, treatment of primary hepatocytes with TNF resulted in significant induction of HAF (Figure
6
C). To determine the involvement of HAF in the progression to HCC, we examined the impact of long-term feeding of the Gubra-Amylin NASH high-fat diet (HFD) on HAF levels. Livers of male C57BL/6 mice fed the Gubra-Amylin NASH HFD for 38 weeks show histological hallmarks of human MASH, including steatosis, inflammation, hepatocyte ballooning degeneration, and fibrosis.
39
Feeding of the HFD for 7 weeks or more resulted in hepatic lipid accumulation and elevated NAS (Figure
6
D, Supplemental Figure S6B,
http://links.lww.com/HEP/I650
). Strikingly, the livers of mice fed the HFD for 7–26 weeks showed profound suppression of HAF, TRADD, and P-p65 compared with mice fed a chow diet, which were then strongly induced after 40 weeks of HFD (Figure
6
E). By contrast, no induction of HAF was seen in the livers of mice fed a chow diet for 48–72 weeks compared with 24 weeks (Supplemental Figure S6C,
http://links.lww.com/HEP/I650
). Similar HAF upregulation was observed in mice with DEN-induced HCC, suggesting a general upregulation of HAF in mouse HCC regardless of etiology (Supplemental Figure S6D,
http://links.lww.com/HEP/I650
). We observed a similar pattern of HAF expression in human livers using TMAs whereby HAF was significantly decreased in steatotic human livers compared with normal human livers but then progressively increased from simple steatosis to cirrhosis through HCC (Figure
6
Fi–iii). These results were obtained using digital quantitation (Figure
6
Fi), and through pathologist-mediated H-scoring of hepatocytes in additional TMAs (Figure
6
Fii) and within large liver sections containing HCC and associated non-HCC tissue (nonmalignant tissue with cirrhosis, fibrosis, and/or steatohepatitis; Figures
6
Fiii, G). The majority of these tissues (>60% of steatohepatitis/HCC tissue and >75% of steatosis tissue) were from patients with etiologies of MASLD/MASH (Supplemental Figure S8,
http://links.lww.com/HEP/I652
). In HCC, we observed HAF expression both within tumor cells and infiltrating immune cells, reminiscent of our previous study.
11
Triple staining of HAF together with anti-CD3 and anti-CD68 suggests that these HAF-positive immune cells were primarily macrophages, although some CD3+ cells also showed HAF expression (Figure
6
H). Nevertheless, although marked HAF upregulation was observed in all 3 cell types in HCC compared with non-HCC tissue, this increase was statistically significant only for tumor cell–specific HAF (Supplemental Figure S6E,
http://links.lww.com/HEP/I650
). Furthermore, we identified highly significant correlations between tumor cell–specific HAF and CD3
+
or CD68
+
cells, suggesting that the expression of HAF in CD3
+
/CD68
+
cells may be driven by factors that also induce HAF in tumor cells (Supplemental Figures S6F, G,
http://links.lww.com/HEP/I650
). Taken together, the data suggest that HAF is suppressed during simple steatosis (possibly due to hypoxia and increased free fatty acids), which exacerbates hepatocyte apoptosis during cellular stress, promoting progression to MASH and HCC. Paradoxically, HAF is strongly induced during MASH/HCC, which may be due to elevated TNF signaling, concomitant with activation of the NF-κB pathway.

DISCUSSION
In this study, we reveal a novel and essential role for HAF in the canonical NF-κB pathway through the transcriptional regulation of
TRADD
and
RIPK1
. The data show that HAF loss in hepatocytes and, to a lesser extent, within myeloid cells is sufficient to induce HCC in mice, whereas the combination of HAF loss in both hepatocytes and myeloid cells possibly conveys an additive effect (Figure
1
E). The data show that HAF is both necessary and sufficient for TNF-mediated NF-κB activation (Figures
3
E, G), and protects against apoptosis both in vitro and in vivo (Figures
5
E, G–J). Thus, loss of HAF within hepatocytes increases apoptosis, driving progression to HCC in mice without any further dietary or other manipulation.
Tumors from
hepS
−/
−
mice showed increases in long-chain ACars and Cers that can promote cell death and increase reactive oxygen species, thus recapitulating the lipotoxicity associated with human MASH (Figures
2
D, E, Supplemental Figure S2,
http://links.lww.com/HEP/I646
).
24
,
25
,
40
Although lipid dysregulation is unlikely to be directly associated with HAF loss (Supplemental Figures S2B–D,
http://links.lww.com/HEP/I646
, Supplemental Figure S5C,
http://links.lww.com/HEP/I649
), it is likely that the combination of increased spontaneous apoptosis combined with downstream lipid dysregulation contributes to the inflammation and liver damage that results in MASH and MASH-HCC in these mice. Although previous studies have implicated HAF in the regulation of the NF-κB pathway, our study presented here reveals for the first time a mechanism for the regulation of NF-κB signaling by HAF.
41
,
42
The transcriptional regulation of
TRADD
and
RIPK1
identified in this study adds to a growing list of validated HAF transcriptional targets (
EPO
,
VEGFA
, and
POU5F1
), which complement HAF’s other functions including as an E3 ubiquitin ligase and as a component of the spliceosome.
6
,
7
,
43
Due to its multifunctional properties, it is possible that HAF deficiency may cause splicing (like in decreasing the ratio of Bcl-X
L
/Bcl-X
S
) and other defects independent of the NF-κB pathway that drive progression to HCC.
The effects of genetic HAF deletion in mice closely resemble that of deletion of key components of the NF-κB pathway (eg, p65, NEMO, and TAK1).
34
–
38
The increased HCC incidence in male HAF-deficient mice reflects the male propensity for HCC incidence in humans and other mouse models, and has been linked to a protective effect of estrogen in blocking the secretion of the proinflammatory cytokine, IL-6.
44
,
45
These studies suggest that deficiency in HAF and/or NF-κB activation promotes hepatocarcinogenesis, at least in part, by enhancing proinflammatory cell death. Intriguingly, the combination of HAF deficiency with 5 months of the Gubra-Amylin NASH HFD did not significantly increase liver pathology (given by NAS, Supplemental Figure S7,
http://links.lww.com/HEP/I651
) similar to that seen in NF-κB1/p50 knockout mice, which could be related to changes in energy expenditure that have previously been reported.
46
However, it is possible that longer exposure to an HFD could result in the escalation of liver pathology and/or progression to MASH-HCC, and this is currently under investigation.
Paradoxically, constitutive activation of the NF-κB pathway is commonly observed in HCC and later stages of tumor development. NF-κB inhibition at this stage does not impact tumor initiation but instead prevents progression to HCC.
47
Similarly, we show that HAF levels are suppressed in livers with simple steatosis but increased during MASH/HCC from both mice and humans which, in mice, parallels increases in P-p65 and TRADD (Figures
6
E–H). This suggests that HAF, similar to NF-κB, can act either as a tumor suppressor or tumor promoter depending on cellular context. The mechanism by which steatosis suppresses HAF levels is currently unclear, although this may be mediated by hypoxia and increased free fatty acids that are typically associated with steatosis.
48
We propose that the hypoxic intensity required for HAF suppression during steatosis may be lower than that required for HIF-1α stabilization and/or transcriptional activation, thus decoupling HAF downregulation from potential HIF-1α activation. This may explain the lack of hepatocyte-specific HIF-1α expression in our HAF-deficient models in the absence of hypoxia (Figure
6
B), and in the previous
SART1
+/−
model.
11
Conversely, HAF is induced by TNF (Figure
6
C), providing a physiological mechanism for the upregulation of HAF during MASH and MASH-HCC.
49
In summary, our findings reveal a novel role of HAF in preventing hepatocyte apoptosis, thus protecting against progression to HCC in the context of MASH, at least in part, through the transcriptional regulation of the NF-κB pathway. HAF levels are suppressed in livers with simple steatosis but increased during HCC in humans, which may indicate a context-specific role of HAF and its potential utility as a biomarker for progression from simple steatosis to MASH and HCC. In addition, the targeting of HAF in advanced MASH/established HCC could be a strategy to fine-tune NF-κB activity (and of HAF’s other pathological targets) to preserve NF-κB’s physiological function while restraining its pathological role.
