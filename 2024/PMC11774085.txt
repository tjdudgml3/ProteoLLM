1
Introduction
With population growth and aging, the prevalence of hypertension continues to rise, and it is estimated that hypertension will affect 1.5 billion people worldwide by 2025 [
1
]. Consequently, hypertension has emerged as one of the most burdensome diseases worldwide [
2
,
3
]. Resistant hypertension (RH), a multifaceted ailment resulting from a combination of genetic and environmental factors, has become a stubborn condition that poses significant challenges to the prevention and management of hypertension [
4
]. According to the 2018 ESC/ESH Guidelines for the Management of Arterial Hypertension [
5
], RH is defined as resistance to treatment when the recommended treatment strategy fails to lower office systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) values to <140 and/or <90 mmHg, respectively, and inadequate control of BP is confirmed by ambulatory or home blood pressure monitoring in patients whose adherence to therapy has been confirmed. The recommended treatment strategy should include appropriate lifestyle measures and treatment with optimal or best‐tolerated doses of three or more drugs, which should include a diuretic, typically an angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitor (ACEI) or an angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB), and a calcium channel blocker (CCB) [
5
]. The prevalence of true RH has been reported to be 10.3% [
6
]. According to the 2023 ESH Guidelines for the Management of Arterial Hypertension [
7
], a reasonable estimate of the RH prevalence is that it might involve about 5% of the overall hypertensive population. It is recommended to manage RH as a high‐risk condition because it is frequently associated with hypertension‐mediated organ damage (HMOD) and increased cardiovascular risk. A prospective cohort study involving 470 386 hypertensive patients at Kaiser South Medical Center established a significant correlation between RH and all‐cause mortality (hazard ratio [HR] 1.06, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.03–1.08), ischemic cardiac events (HR 1.24, 95% CI 1.20–1.28), congestive heart failure (HR 1.46, 95% CI 1.40–1.52), cerebrovascular accident (HR 1.14, 95% CI 1.10–1.19), and end‐stage renal disease (HR 1.32, 95% CI 1.27–1.37) [
8
]. Various factors can affect the precise diagnosis of RH, including erroneous blood pressure measurement techniques, suboptimal treatment adherence, the use of medications that influence blood pressure, unhealthy lifestyle choices, inadequate medications, and secondary hypertension [
5
]. Furthermore, excluding false RH poses a significant challenge. The absence of tailored therapeutic interventions renders combination pharmacotherapies ineffective. Although the implementation of surgical therapies such as renal denervation and carotid baroreceptor stimulation has introduced novel concepts for clinical treatment, further evidence is required to substantiate the efficacy and safety of these methods [
7
]. Consequently, there is an urgent need to enhance the diagnostic capabilities of RH in clinical settings.
Omics analysis technology has proven to be a powerful new tool for revealing complex biological processes and has been successfully applied in the field of medicine, providing different forms and levels of life‐omics data for exploring human health and related diseases. Several single‐omics studies have been conducted on RH. Metabolomics studies showed that changes in RH metabolites are associated with oxidative stress, inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, vasoconstriction, and cell proliferation [
9
]. Metabolomics studies of urine from patients with RH and pseudo‐RH confirmed the predictive ability of metabolites as biomarkers for spironolactone response [
10
]. Proteomics studies found that oxidative stress, chronic inflammatory response, coagulation, complement activation, and focal adhesion regulation were the physiological and pathological mechanisms of RH [
11
]. This study also identified protein changes in urine that predicted response to spironolactone in patients with RH, improving clinical treatment decisions [
11
]. Genomics studies on RH have focused on candidate genes related to BP regulation and monogenic forms of hypertension [
12
,
13
]. Associated genetic variants and their effects on drug response have been explored [
14
]. Studies confirmed that RH and essential hypertension (EH) share the same causal genetic factors [
15
].
However, the cause of disease is a complex process involving many factors, and a single‐omics approach cannot explain the complex cause‐and‐effect relationship. Integration of omics is important for a comprehensive and in‐depth understanding of this disease. Multi‐omics association analysis comprehensively assesses the entire process, from DNA transcription to RNA, RNA translation to protein, and protein regulation of downstream metabolites. This process accurately reflects the characteristics of organism's molecular levels, revealing interaction networks, and providing a dynamic view of the organism. Consequently, it enhances disease diagnosis, prediction, and treatment [
16
]. With the growing popularity of multi‐omics and advancements in bioinformatics, multi‐omics association analysis has gradually become mainstream. Researchers have attempted integrating and analyzing multi‐omics data in the field of disease research to elucidate the potential cause‐and‐effect relationship between specific biochemical molecules and diseases, and to identify decisive biological causes behind these data, thereby accelerating the understanding of disease development, pathogenesis, and potential drug targets. This facilitates the move toward personalized medicine [
17
]. However, the understanding of the pathogenesis of RH and the screening of drug targets are constrained by the paucity of multi‐omics studies on RH. Therefore, this study conducted a multi‐omics analysis of RH, attempting to elucidate the potential relationship between specific biochemical molecules and RH, in order to provide support for the development of more effective treatment plans.
2
Materials and Methods
2.1
Participant Recruitment
In this study, 10 patients with RH, 10 patients with EH, and 10 healthy individuals (N) were included. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee (approval number: 2021) Ethics Review No. (035). All study subjects agreed to participate in this study and signed the informed consent form. Because the blood pressure of patients with RH was more difficult to control, and there was a risk of exacerbating the disease and inducing other diseases if antihypertensive drugs were discontinued, no washout period was set in either the RH or EH groups. The types of antihypertensive drugs were the same in both groups: (i) ACEI or ARB class: benazepril, valsartan, irbesartan, and olmesartan; (ii) CCB class: nifedipine, amlodipine, felodipine; (iii) hydrochlorothiazide; (iv) spironolactone; and (v) metoprolol.
2.1.1
Inclusion Criteria
EH diagnostic criteria: According to the 2018 ESC/ESH Guidelines for the Management of Arterial Hypertension [
5
], hypertension is defined as office SBP ≥ 140 mmHg and/or DBP ≥ 90 mmHg [
5
].
RH diagnostic criteria: The definition of RH in the 2018 ESC/ESH Guidelines for the Management of Arterial Hypertension [
5
] is described in the introduction. Several possible causes of pseudo‐RH should be evaluated and ruled out before concluding that the patient has RH [
5
]: (i) poor adherence to prescribed medicines; (ii) white‐coat phenomenon; (iii) poor office BP measurement technique; (iv) marked brachial artery calcification, especially in older patients with heavily calcified arteries; and (v) clinician inertia, resulting in inadequate doses or irrational combinations of BP‐lowering drug therapies.
2.1.2
Exclusion Criteria
According to the 2018 ESC/ESH Guidelines for the Management of Arterial Hypertension [
5
], secondary Hypertension and advanced HMOD, especially chronic kidney disease or arteriosclerosis were excluded from the study.
2.2
Data Collection
The data of participants were collected, including sex, age, history of smoking, and alcohol consumption, blood pressure, antihypertensive medication, and laboratory indexes, such as alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), glutamyl transferase (GGT), creatinine (Cr), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), glomerular filtration rate (GFR), triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol (TC), low‐density lipoprotein (LDL), high‐density lipoprotein (HDL), and blood glucose (Glu), using Mindray BS‐2000 M automatic biochemical analyzer (Mindray, Shenzhen, China), and the data were standardized.
2.3
Sample Collection and Storage
Blood samples were collected at the same time each day (between 6:00 and 8:00 am) under fasting conditions. Peripheral venous blood (5 mL) was extracted from the participants. Subsequently, 3 mL of Trizol was added to 1 mL of fresh blood. The samples were thoroughly shaken, mixed, and blown several times for complete lysis. The samples were left at room temperature for 5 min to allow complete separation of the protein‐nucleic acid complexes and then labeled and stored in the refrigerator at –80°C. The remaining 4 mL of venous blood was centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was divided into 1.5 mL EP tubes, each EP tube 600 µL, and stored in the refrigerator at –80°C.
2.4
Metabolomics Data Processing and Analysis
The metabolites were extracted from serum [
18
,
19
]. UHPLC‐MS/MS analyses were performed using a Vanquish UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher, Germany) coupled with an Orbitrap Q Exactive HF mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher, Germany). Samples were injected onto a Hypesil Gold column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.9 µm) using a 12‐min linear gradient at a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min. The eluents for the positive polarity mode were eluent A (0.1% FA in Water) and eluent B (Methanol). The eluents for the negative polarity mode were eluent A (5 mM ammonium acetate, pH 9.0) and eluent B (Methanol). Q Exactive HF mass spectrometer was operated in positive/negative polarity mode. The raw data files were processed using Compound Discoverer 3.1 (CD3.1, Thermo Fisher) to obtain accurate qualitative and relative quantitative results of metabolites. The screening criteria for differential metabolites were variable importance in the projection (VIP) > 1,
p
< 0.05, Fold Change (FC) > 1.2, or FC < 0.833. Statistical analyses were performed using the statistical software R (version 3.4.3), Python (version 2.7.6), and CentOS (version 6.6). The detailed data processing procedures are described in Supporting Information
1
.
2.5
Proteomics Data Processing and Analysis
Proteins were extracted from serum and subjected to quality testing and trypsin treatment [
20
,
21
,
22
]. The sample was fractionated using a C18 column (Waters BEH C18, 4.6 × 250 mm, 5 µm) on a Rigol L3000 HPLC system, the column oven was set to 45°C. Proteomics analyses were performed using an EASY‐nLC 1200 UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher) coupled with a Q Exactive HF‐X mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher) in the data‐dependent acquisition (DDA) and data‐independent acquisition (DIA) modes. The raw data of MS detection was named “.Raw.” The database search software Proteome Discoverer (PD version 2.2) was used to search for data in the DDA scanning mode. The identified proteins contained at least one unique peptide. The identified PSMs and proteins were retained and analyzed with an FDR of no more than 1.0%. The results of the search and identification using PD2.2 software were imported into Spectronaut (version 14.0, Biognosys) software to generate a library. The eligible peptides and product ions were selected from the spectrum to generate a Target List. DIA data were imported, and ion‐pair chromatographic peaks were extracted according to the Target List. The ion was matched, and the peak area was calculated for qualitative and quantitative analyses of the peptides. The proteins with significant quantitative differences between the experimental and control groups (
p
< 0.05, FC > 1.5, or FC < 0.67) were defined as differentially expressed proteins (DEPs). The detailed data processing procedures are described in Supporting Information
2
.
2.6
Transcriptomic Data Processing and Analysis
After total RNA was extracted from whole blood, mRNA was purified from total RNA by using poly‐T oligo‐attached magnetic beads and cleaved into small fragments for cDNA synthesis to construct a library. An Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system was used for sequencing. Differential expression analysis between the two groups was performed using the DESeq2 R package (version 1.20.0). The resulting
p
values were adjusted using Benjamini and Hochberg's approach to control for the false discovery rate.
p
< 0.05 and |log
2
(foldchange)| ≥ 0 were set as the threshold for significantly differential expression. The detailed data processing procedures are described in Supporting Information
3
.
2.7
Bioinformatics Analysis
The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) (
https://www.genome.jp/kegg/pathway.html
), Human Metabolome Database (HMDB) (
https://hmdb.ca/metabolites
), and LIPIDMaps (
http://www.lipidmaps.org/
) databases were used to annotate the metabolites. The KEGG database was used for metabolite pathway enrichment analysis. Gene Ontology (GO) and InterPro (IPR) functional analyses were conducted using the InterProscan program against the non‐redundant protein database, and the COG (
http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/COG/
) and KEGG (
https://www.genome.jp/kegg/
) databases were used to analyze the protein family and pathway. GO (
http://www.geneontology.org/
) and KEGG databases were used for protein pathway enrichment analysis. KEGG enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes was implemented by the clusterProfiler R package (version 3.8.1). A weighted correlation network analysis (WGCNA) of multi‐omics was performed using the R package (version 1.7.1). Volcano plots, cluster heat maps, and bubble maps were generated using R (version 3.4.3).
2.8
Validation of Differential Substances
2.8.1
Colorimetry and Enzyme‐Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)
We used colorimetric and ELISA kits to detect the expression levels of differential metabolites and proteins in the serum of participants, further validating the omics results. The test kits include: Vitamin C, Citric Acid, and Reduced Glutathione (GSH) Colorimetric Assay Kits (Elabscience, Wuhan, China); Choline and transferrin receptor (TfR) ELISA Kits (Jianglai Biology, Shanghai, China).
2.8.2
RNA Extraction and Quantitative Real‐Time PCR Assay
We used trizol (Ambion, Texas, America) to extract RNA from the whole blood of participants. SPARKscript II RT Plus Kit (SparkJade, Shandong, China) was used to synthesize cDNA. qRT‐PCR was performed using 2×SYBR Green qPCR Mix (SparkJade, Shandong, China) on LightCycler 480II system (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Relative expression was determined by the 2
−ΔΔCT
method. We used β‐actin as an endogenous control for mRNA. The primer sequences used were as follows: Primers for insulin‐like growth factor 1 receptor (IGF1R): F: 5′‐AGTATGGAGGGGC CAAGCTA‐3′; R: 5′‐CAACCCTCC CACGATCAACA‐3′. Primers for Ring‐Box 1 (RBX1): F: 5′‐TGTCAAGCTAACCAGGCGTC‐3′; R: 5′‐CACCTGTCGTGTTTTG AGCC‐3′. Primers for Egl‐9 family hypoxia‐inducible factor 2(EGLN2): F: 5′‐ATGG AAGAAGCCAAAAGCCA‐3′; R: 5′‐CTCCTTTGGGTCCTGTATGTGT‐3′. Primers for glutathione peroxidase 1 (GPX1): F: 5′‐GAACGCCAAGAACGAAGAGAT‐3′; R: 5′‐CGAAGAGCATGAAGT TGGGC‐3′. Primers for β‐actin: F: 5′‐GACCCAGATCA TGTTTGAGACCTTC‐3′; R: 5′‐CCAGAGGCGTACAGGGATAGC‐3′.
2.9
Statistical Analysis
Clinical data were analyzed using SPSS software (version 26.0). Measures with a normal distribution are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Measures with non‐normal distributions were expressed as quartiles M (Q25, Q75). Categorical variables are expressed as composition ratios. The measures were tested for normal distribution using a chi‐square test. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for comparisons between multiple groups with a normal distribution, the non‐parametric test was used for non‐normally distributed variables, and Fisher's exact test was used for categorical variables. Differences were considered statistically significant at
p
< 0.05.

Impairment of renal function was more severe in the RH group than in the N group. SBP was significantly higher in the RH group than in the EH group, and the difference was statistically significant.

3.2
Identification and Analysis of Differential Metabolites
RH is a specific type of hypertension; therefore, it inevitably has certain pathogenetic features of hypertension but also has the characteristic of being resistant to treatment. In this study, we investigated the specific biochemical molecules of RH as a starting point, focusing on the intersection of two comparative groups: RH versus N and RH versus EH. We used a non‐targeted metabolomic approach to identify 972 metabolites. Partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS‐DA) revealed integrated metabolic changes between the two groups, with a clear separation between the RH and EH groups and between the RH and N groups, indicating different metabolomic profiles among the groups (Figure
1A,B
). Significantly different metabolites were screened according to the screening criteria of VIP > 1.0, FC > 1.2, FC < 0.833, and
p
value < 0.05, as shown in Venn plots, clustered heat maps, and volcano plots (Figure
1C–F
), with a total of 225 for RH versus N and 78 for RH versus EH. There were 50 significantly different metabolites representing RH, which were ranked according to their
p
values. Eleven metabolites were in the top 20 in both comparison groups, as shown in Table
2
(detailed data are in Table
S1
). Compared with EH and N groups, the RH group showed significantly abnormal expression in antioxidant substances, energy metabolism intermediates, and phospholipid metabolisms. We performed a KEGG pathway enrichment analysis to assess the potential role of these differentially expressed metabolites. Three pathways were significantly enriched in the RH versus N group, involving ascorbate and aldehyde metabolism, carbon metabolism, and arginine biosynthesis. Seven pathways were significantly enriched in the RH versus EH group, involving carbon metabolism, glutathione metabolism, hypoxia‐inducible factor‐1 (HIF‐1) signaling pathway, digestion and absorption of vitamins, glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, and citric acid cycle (TCA cycle), as shown in the bubble diagram (Figure
1G,H
, sorted according to
p
value; only the top 20 results are shown).

3.3
Identification and Analysis of Differential Proteins
To track upstream changes in metabolomics, we applied DIA quantitative proteomics identifying 1149 proteins. Significantly different proteins were screened according to the criteria of FC > 1.5, or < 0.67, and
p
< 0.05, as shown in volcano plots and clustering heat maps (Figure
2A–D
). A total of 206 proteins for RH versus N and 93 for RH versus EH were identified. There were 41 significantly DEPs associated with RH. These proteins were ranked according to their
p
values, with 13 proteins appearing in the top 20 in both comparison groups, as shown in Table
3
(detailed data are in Table
S2
). Comparative analysis revealed significant abnormalities in erythropoiesis, thyroid hormone transport, cholesterol transport, immune response, cystatin C metabolism, and cysteine metabolism in the RH group compared to the other two groups. We performed GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses to assess the potential roles of DEPs. Four pathways were significantly enriched in the RH versus N group by GO‐enriched analysis involving bioregulation, protein tyrosine phosphatase activity, protein dephosphorylation, and regulation of biological processes. Five pathways were significantly enriched in the RH versus EH group by GO‐enriched analysis involving heme binding, oxygen binding, cysteine‐type peptidase activity, oxidoreductase activity, and scavenger receptor activity, as shown in the bar graphs (Figure
2E,F
). One pathway was significantly enriched in the RH versus N group by KEGG‐enriched pathways involving autophagy. Six pathways were significantly enriched in the RH versus EH group by KEGG‐enriched pathways involving the hippocampal signaling pathway, malaria, apoptosis, cell cycle, oocyte meiosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma, as shown in the bubble plots (Figure
2G,H
, sorted according to
p
values; only the top 20 results are shown).

Combined Analysis of Multiple Omics
To explore the pathogenesis of RH in‐depth and to construct a network of RH interactions at different levels, we performed a combined multi‐omics analysis. The differential metabolites and proteins screened by single‐omics analysis (the differential proteins were selected from outside the cell membrane) were used as phenotypes for the WGCNA analysis of the differential genes. Gene co‐expression networks were constructed, and the genes were divided into multiple modules. Based on the correlation analysis, we screened gray modules with a strong correlation with differential metabolites and proteins (Figure

Discussion
According to current studies, the risk factors for RH include race, age, BMI, diabetes, and obstructive sleep apnea [
23
,
24
]. However, the pathological mechanism of RH remains poorly understood. The pathophysiology of RH involves interactions between multiple neurohumoral factors such as increased levels of aldosterone, endothelin‐1, vasopressin and increased sympathetic activity. These factors lead to volume and sodium overload, increased peripheral vascular resistance, arterial stiffness, and severe target organ damage [
7
]. First, aldosterone excess has been shown to contribute importantly to the development of RH. Although primary aldosteronism secondary to adrenal hypersecretion is common in patients with RH, studies suggest that adipocytes may also contribute to hyperaldosteronism. Blockade of aldosterone with the use of mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, particularly spironolactone, has been demonstrated to provide particular benefit when added to existing antihypertensive regimens [
25
]. Second, the PRECISION study supports the role of endothelin receptor blockade in the treatment of RH. In addition to the guideline‐recommended triple antihypertensive therapy, the dual endothelin receptor antagonism with aprocitentan is a well‐tolerated and effective treatment for sustained blood pressure reduction [
26
]. Third, vasopressin release by vasopressinergic neurons is chronically stimulated in patients with RH because of a primary central mechanism. Vasopressin has direct vasoconstrictive properties in addition to water and sodium reabsorption in the kidney [
27
]. Fourth, patients with RH have significant sympathetic activation and pressure reflex dysfunction [
28
]. Thoroughly investigating the pathogenesis of RH and identifying targeted and effective treatments carry prime importance. We identified seven differential substances including ascorbic acid, TfR, EGLN2, GSH, GPX1, citric acid, and choline by multi‐omics combined analysis, which may reflect the biological characteristics of RH. We found that differential substances of RH were enriched in the HIF‐1 signaling pathway. HIF is a transcription factor crucial for the cells to sense and adapt to changes in oxygen levels. HIF is extensively involved in various biological processes [
29
]. It regulates immune responses and expression of pro‐inflammatory factors, promotes erythropoiesis and iron metabolism to enhance oxygen delivery, promotes angiogenesis, regulates vascular tone, inhibits tricarboxylic acid cycle, promotes anaerobic metabolism, and regulates proliferation and apoptosis among other functions. The correlation studies between HIF‐1 and tumors have been reported more frequently, while studies examining the correlation between HIF‐1 and RH have been rarely reported. However, this possible association was observed in the present study. Previous studies have found that HIF‐1 can be involved in the regulation of blood pressure through multiple mechanisms, as enumerated here. (a) Smooth muscle cell‐derived HIF‐1α may affect hypertension in patients with obstructive sleep apnea through a renin‐angiotensin system‐dependent mechanism [
30
]. (b) Hypertension is considered a chronic inflammatory disease, and HIF‐1 is involved in the inflammatory response. Hypoxia and HIF/proline hydroxylase (PHD) activity profoundly affect cells of both the innate and adaptive immune systems [
31
]. (c) Chronic intermittent hypoxia‐induced reactive oxygen species (ROS) trigger dysregulation of HIF‐α isoforms through a Ca
2+
‐dependent mechanism. This dysregulation leads to further ROS generation by creating transcriptional imbalances between pro‐oxidant and antioxidant enzyme genes, which is central to the development of hypertension [
32
]. (d) Acute hypoxia rapidly activates the endothelium to release a variety of inflammatory cytokines and activated neutrophils recruit and adhere to the endothelium [
33
], leading to endothelial dysfunction. (e) In hypoxia, HIF‐1 activates the transcription of several genes encoding angiogenic growth factors and cytokines, such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), placental growth factor, platelet‐derived growth factor B angiopoietin 1 and angiopoietin 2. HIF‐1 mediates their biological effects on endothelial cells, vascular smooth muscle cells (SMCs), endothelial progenitor cells, mesenchymal stem cells, and other bone marrow‐derived angiogenesis cells [
34
]. HIF‐1α and VEGF are crucial mediators of the hypoxia‐induced vascular remodeling phenomenon.
Ascorbic acid, also known as vitamin C. We found that the RH group had the lowest levels of ascorbic acid compared to the N and EH groups, suggesting that low levels of ascorbic acid are associated with RH. Sato et al. [
35
] demonstrated that ascorbic acid helps to control blood pressure in elderly patients with RH, which is in accordance with the results of the present study. Inadequate dietary intake of vitamin C is the main reason for its low levels. Previous studies have shown a negative correlation between plasma vitamin C and blood pressure. Vitamin C may be an important component of the effectiveness of fruits and vegetables in lowering blood pressure. Tissue ascorbic acid levels may be important in maintaining low blood pressure [
36
]. Specific mechanisms include the following: ascorbic acid is an antioxidant, improves endothelial function; regulates collagen synthesis and maintains the structural integrity of the vascular wall [
37
]; and regulates the renin‐angiotensin system by inhibiting angiotensin II production [
38
].
Our findings showed that TfR levels were higher in the RH group than in the EH and N groups, suggesting that RH is associated with high levels of TfR. The soluble transferrin receptor (sTfR) is a truncated form of TfR. The Chinese Health and Nutrition Survey [
39
] found that sTfR levels were positively correlated with baseline systolic and diastolic blood pressure, and high sTfR levels independently predicted the development of hypertension. Cross‐sectional studies from the United States [
40
] have shown that sTfR is a biomarker associated with the risk of uncontrolled hypertension compared with controlled hypertension. Studies [
41
] have also found that sTfR is a significant independent risk factor for hypertension in the United States population and that higher sTfR levels are associated with higher odds of developing hypertension. These findings are consistent with ours. Some studies [
41
] have hypothesized that the pathological mechanism may be that reduced iron availability (associated with elevated sTfR) affects NO synthesis, leading to elevated blood pressure.
EGLN2, also known as PHD1. Under normal conditions, HIF‐1α is hydroxylated by PHD, and hydroxylated HIFs are recognized by the protein Von Hippel Lindau (pVHL), ubiquitinated by E3 ligase, and eventually degraded by the proteasome. However, under hypoxic conditions, PHD activity and hydroxylation of HIF‐1α are inhibited. Consequently, HIF‐1α is stably expressed and accumulates due to lack of recognition and ubiquitination degradation [
42
,
43
]. Thus, it is possible to manipulate the hypoxic responses in many disease states, making PHDs attractive therapeutic targets. We observed reduced levels of PHD in the RH group compared to the EH and N groups. PHD is an oxygen‐dependent expression. Is hypertension associated with hypoxia? First, obstructive sleep apnea, as well as exposure to high altitude, can lead to hypoxia and induce hypertension; second, hypertension is an inflammatory disease, and inflammatory environments are inherently prone to combined hypoxia; third, one of the basic functions of the cardiovascular system is oxygen delivery, and thus cardiovascular disease is inherently associated with inadequate tissue oxygenation. Based on the above points, we propose the hypothesis that hypoxia may exist in RH, leading to decreased PHD activity and overexpression of HIF‐1α.
We found significant differences in GSH and GPX1 expression in the RH group compared with the N and EH groups. Previous studies have established that increased oxidative stress is a well‐recognized etiological factor in the development of hypertension and that hypertension is associated with disturbances in glutathione metabolism. This is consistent with our findings. In hypertensive patients reduced levels of GSH and elevated levels of oxidized glutathione lead to increased oxidative stress and ultimately elevated blood pressure [
44
]. The specific mechanism is thought to be that glutathione maintains nitric oxide bioavailability through the formation of nitrosothiols and nitrosohemoglobins, and disturbed glutathione metabolism leads to decreased nitric oxide bioavailability [
45
]. The relationship between GPX1 and clinical pathology is not always straightforward, and GPX appears to be a double‐edged sword. GPX has a protective role in overcoming reactive oxygen species‐mediated oxidative damage and cell death in vivo, but it has also been reported that GPX can potentiate reactive nitrogen species, producing adverse effects [
45
]. While most studies have focused mainly on the protective effects of GPX in hypertension, the present study found the opposite effect.
Citric acid is an intermediate metabolite of the TCA cycle and has antioxidant properties. We found that the expression level of citric acid was significantly lower in the RH group, suggesting that low levels of serum citric acid are associated with RH. In a study of urinary metabolomics in RH patients, the citric acid cycle was found to be the most significantly altered pathway [
10
]. Nakamura et al. found that citric acid acted on the endothelium of the aorta in SHR rats, inducing vasodilatation and a significant reduction in systolic and diastolic blood pressure [
46
]. Therefore, we believe that citric acid reflects the biological characteristics of RH to some extent.
We found that choline expression was significantly higher in the RH group compared with the N and EH groups, suggesting that high choline levels may be associated with RH. Previous studies have shown [
47
,
48
] that plasma choline levels are positively correlated with hypertension and coronary artery damage, and that plasma choline may be a useful biomarker for risk stratification of hypertension and arterial stenosis. This is consistent with our findings. High choline concentrations may lead to elevated synthesis of related metabolites and activation of immune cells, thereby inducing endothelial cell inflammation, which can lead to elevated blood pressure [
48
].
All of these differential substances are associated with the HIF‐1 signaling pathway. PHD hydroxylates HIF‐1α, and ascorbic acid enhances PHD activity. TfR is a downstream target of HIF‐1α. Given the role of GSH as an important antioxidant, it is plausible that the redox state of GSH may regulate HIF‐1 expression induced by hypoxia [
49
]. HIF‐1α inhibits TCA and affects the expression of citric acid. The proximal promoter region of choline kinase α has a conserved hypoxia‐responsive element that binds to HIF‐1α and thus affects choline levels [
50
].
5
Conclusion
This multi‐omics study found that differential substances of RH were enriched in the HIF‐1 signaling pathway, and that differential substances including ascorbic acid, citric acid, choline, GSH, TfR, EGLN2, and GPX1 may reflect the biological characteristics of RH. The mining of RH biological characteristics provides support for identifying effective therapeutic targets and developing more effective treatment plans.
However, conducting combined multi‐omics analysis in RH poses significant challenges. Patients with RH often have multiple diseases, and various confounding factors may interfere with the study of causal relationships between RH and its molecular characteristics. The present study has some limitations, including a small sample size. Therefore, larger clinical samples, standardized study procedures, new analytical methods for integrating data, and cross‐validation of the study findings are required to pave the way for precision medicine in RH. Currently, we are recruiting a validation cohort and plan to conduct a large‐scale cohort analysis in the future to validate the screened differential substances using large samples aiming to enhance the specificity and sensitivity of the differential substances. We believe that our findings will significantly contribute to the development of effective diagnostic tools for RH and their widespread application in clinical diagnosis and treatment.
