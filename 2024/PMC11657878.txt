Background
Infectious bursal disease is an acute and highly contagious immunosuppressive disease in poultry caused by infectious bursal disease virus (IBDV). Clinical signs include listlessness, anorexia, diarrhea, ruffled feathers, and dehydration. Morbidity in flocks is typically 100% and mortality is highly variable. The lesions include edema, atrophy or hemorrhage of bursa, congestion and haemorrhage of the thigh and pectoral muscles [
1
]. The clinical manifestation lasts for 3 to 4 days, followed by rapid recovery in surviving birds. There are two serotypes of IBDV, serotypes 1 and 2. The IBDV strains causing disease in chickens belong to serotype 1, while serotype 2 viruses are non-pathogenic [
2
–
4
]. Since the first infection occurred in the USA in 1957 [
5
], IBDV strains with different virulence emerge worldwide. Classical virulent IBDV (clIBDV) causes inflammatory edema of the bursa with a mortality of less than 40% [
6
]. Variant IBDV (varIBDV), which has a significant antigenic difference from clIBDV, causes rapid atrophy of the bursa but rarely death [
7
,
8
]. Very virulent IBDV (vvIBDV) causes the bursa severe atrophy, hyperaemia and hemorrhage with the mortality more than 50% [
9
,
10
]. Novel variant IBDV (nVarIBDV) can evade immune protection against vvIBDV and induce inflammatory mucous exudation and atrophy of the bursa, but does not cause death [
11
–
17
]. Attenuated IBDV (atIBDV) does not cause clinical morbidity or mortality, however, some strains induce a certain degree of lesion in bursa [
18
,
19
]. IBDV is a double-stranded RNA virus with two genomic segments, A and B. According to the newly proposed IBDV genome classification proposal, clIBDV, varIBDV/nVarIBDV, vvIBDV and atIBDV belong to subgroups A1, A2, A3 and A9 based on segment A [
10
]. Under natural conditions, oral infection appears to be the most common mode of IBDV infection. The virus replicates in gut-associated macrophages and lymphoid cells and enters portal circulation, leading to primary viremia [
20
–
22
]. After reaching the bursa, the virus replicates in the developing B lymphocytes, causing bursal destruction, and then enters the bloodstream, resulting in secondary viremia. The virus spreads to organs and tissues such as kidney and muscle and leads to pathognomonic clinical signs and death [
22
,
23
]. The outcome of IBDV infection depends on the virulence and infective amount of virus, the age and the breed of the chickens, the route of infection, and the level of neutralizing antibodies [
23
–
26
].
Previous studies revealed many details on the tissue damage and immunosuppression caused by IBDV, a comprehensive analysis of the host response to virus infection is helpful to extend our understanding of virus pathogenesis. High-throughput techniques provide powerful tools to identify and quantify changes in gene expression and metabolism. Transcriptomics and proteomics approaches were employed to determine the alterations of transcripts and proteins in chicken embryonic fibroblast (DF-1) cells, chicken B-lymphoid cells, chicken dendritic cells, and chicken intraepithelial lymphocyte natural killer cells, revealing the molecular mechanisms of host cells in response to IBDV infection [
27
–
33
]. Metabonomics analysis was used to determine the additive components suitable for virus proliferation to optimize the process of IBDV production by DF-1 cells [
34
,
35
]. However, serum metabolomics during IBDV infection in chickens has not been fully elucidated.
Serum metabolite profiles can be considered as important indicators of physiological and pathological states and help to understand the mechanism of disease occurrence and progression [
36
]. Serum metabolites were used as biomarkers for early diagnosis, such as early Mycoplasma hyopneumoniae infection in pigs [
37
]. Analysis of the serum metabolome could predict whether COVID-19 disease will progress from mild to severe [
38
]. Metabolites in serum were used to predict the prognosis of hepatitis E virus acute liver failure [
39
]. In recent years, electronic tongue based on serum metabolites has been developed for visual diagnosis of COVID-19 [
40
]. The similarity of perturbed serum metabolic pathways between infected humanized mice and dengue patients helped to establish a small-animal model for dengue virus infection [
41
]. Metabolomics analysis revealed that dopaminergic synapse pathway and guanosine in serum directly affect African swine fever virus replication, which will contribute to the development of targeted drugs [
42
]. These studies highlight the great potential of serum metabolomics in diagnosis, detection, and treatment. The elucidation of serum metabolomics during IBDV infection in chickens will contribute to the improvement of control and treatment strategies.
In the present study, we explored the serum metabolomic profiles of chickens infected with atIBDV and vvIBDV using ultra-high performance liquid chromatography-MS/MS (UHPLC-MS/MS) to identify differential metabolites and perturbed metabolic pathways in the disease process.
Results
Clinical manifestations and mortality
The chickens infected with vvIBDV LX strain showed depression, anorexia, diarrhea, trembling, and dehydration from 2-day post infection (dpi). 11 of 24 birds in LX group died on day 3 to 4 post infection, with the mortality 46%. The surviving chickens did not show any clinical symptoms. During the investigation period, no clinical symptoms and mortality were observed in either atIBDV LM strain infected or mock group.

Discussion
In the present study, we describe the serum metabolic profiles of experimental chickens and explore the fundamental metabolic alterations upon atIBDV and vvIBDV infection. The results demonstrate that a series of metabolites in serum changed after the acute phase of the infected chickens. Of all the perturbed metabolic pathways, tryptophan and glycerophospholipid metabolism pathways were the two most important ones in both infection groups.
Tryptophan metabolism primarily involves the kynurenine (Kyn), serotonin, and indole metabolic pathways [
43
]. As the primary pathway, Kyn pathway generates a range of metabolites and cellular energy [
44
]. The Kyn pathway was correlated with inflammation and oxidative stress. Notably, the accumulation of the Kyn derivatives led to renal damage and loss of function, such as anthranillic acid and xanthurenic acid in the blood were reported to be positively correlated with the degree of renal failure [
45
]. As one of the Kyn derivatives, 2-aminobenzoic acid (2-AA) in serum was served as potential prognostic biomarkers to monitor the progression of renal disease [
46
]. Vanillic acid is one of polyphenol metabolites [
47
,
48
] and exerts nephroprotective effect through its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects [
49
,
50
]. A recent study showed that vanillic acid could reduce the impaired renal function in nephropathic rats by decreasing serum creatinine level [
51
]. However, renal failure led to the retention of phenols and their metabolites in the blood [
52
]. The accumulation of vanillic acid in serum increased with the deterioration of renal function [
43
]. This study showed a significant increase in serum concentration of 2-AA and vanillic acid 7 days after infection, which means that the metabolites of tryptophan and polyphenol are still not excreted in the urine in time after the acute phase, suggesting that kidney damage caused by IBDV infection require a longer recovery.
Serotonin pathway is a minor pathway for tryptophan metabolism. N-acetylserotonin (NAS) is a reaction intermediate in the endogenous synthesis of serotonin to melatonin [
53
]. In a previous report, NAS was identified as a potential biomarker for severity of chronic Chagas cardiomyopath, which is a cardiac illness characterized by immunoinflammatory reactions leading to myocardial fibrosis and remodeling, with elevated plasma level of NAS in patients [
53
]. 5-hydroxyindoleacetate (5-HIAA) is a primary metabolite derived from serotonin [
54
]. One research showed that peripheral blood 5-HIAA level was significantly increased in patients with food protein–induced enterocolitis syndrome [
55
]. In our work, the increase of NAS and 5-HIAA in serum is speculated to be associated with the inflammatory damage to the heart and intestine caused by IBDV infection.
In the indole pathway, tryptophan is directly converted by intestinal microorganisms into several small-molecule metabolites, such as indole-3-lactate (ILA), indole-3-acrylate (IAC), indole-3-propionate (IPA), indole-3-acetate (IAA/I3A), indole-3-aldehyde (IAID), indole-3-acetaldehyde (IAAld/I3AA) [
56
,
57
]. These metabolites play a vital role in maintaining intestinal homeostasis and systematic immunity, and potentially affect the occurrence and development of inflammatory bowel diseases, nervous system disease, cardiovascular diseases, hepatic fibrosis, and infectious diseases [
57
]. IAA is a tryptophan metabolism with protein-binding nature [
58
]. Under normal conditions, most IAA in circulation is bound to proteins and excreted in the urine. However, if renal function declines, IAA or other compounds are retained and accumulate in the serum. Studies showed that IAA activated the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR), which causes endothelial oxidative stress, inflammation, and atherosclerosis, leading to cardiovascular disease [
59
,
60
]. Furthermore, studies found that high circulating IAA concentrations in patients with chronic kidney disease are associated with cardiovascular disease and death [
59
]. In addition, IAA was reported to modulate liver inflammatory responses by reducing the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines in macrophages and attenuating the cytokine-mediated upregulation of lipogenesis in hepatocytes [
61
]. I3AA is a tryptophan metabolite with significant pro-inflammatory properties and is also a substrate for the synthesis of IAA and IAld. Unlike other indole derivatives that activate the intrinsic pathway of AhR to benefit the host immune system, I3AA hinders the activity of regulatory T cells by antagonizing AhR signaling, thereby promoting intestinal inflammation [
62
]. In the present study, the elevated serum concentrations of IAA and I3AA may be associated with renal injury caused by IBDV infection and involved in the inflammatory response in infected chickens.
Glycerophospholipid metabolism is reported to be a major pathway involved in general systemic immune and low-grade inflammatory states. Glycerophospholipids are the main components of cell membrane, including phosphatidylcholine (PC), phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), phosphatidylserine (PS), and other subclasses [
63
,
64
]. PC is the most abundant glycerophospholipids, mainly synthesized and enriched in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER). The synthesize of PC might occur during ER stress, and the increase of PC in return activates ER stress response [
65
–
67
]. PCs showed distinct changes in the progression of many diseases, such as neurodegenerative diseases, cancer, and cardiovascular diseases [
68
–
71
]. PE is the second most abundant glycerophospholipid located on the cell and the mitochondrial membranes [
63
]. PE was involved in membrane fusion, oxidative phosphorylation and mitochondrial biogenesis, and played a key role in proliferative growth and programmed cell death [
72
,
73
]. Metabolic alterations in PEs were associated with neurodegenerative diseases, nonalcoholic liver disease, and the virulence of certain pathogenic organisms [
73
]. PCs and PEs with more polyunsaturated acyl chains have protective effects in the inflammatory process, whereas highly saturated PCs and PEs have adverse effects [
74
]. Our results showed a significant increase in serum PC(20:4(5Z,8Z,11Z,14Z)/18:0), PE(16:0/18:2(9Z,12Z)), PE(16:0/22:6(4Z,7Z,10Z,13Z,16Z,19Z)), PE(18:0/20:4(5Z,8Z,11Z,14Z)), PE(18:0/20:4(8Z,11Z,14Z,17Z)), PE(18:0/22:6(4Z,7Z,10Z,13Z,16Z,19Z)), PE(20:3(8Z,11Z,14Z)/16:0), and PE(22:6(4Z,7Z,10Z,13Z,16Z,19Z)/16:0), and a decrease in PE-NMe(20:5(5Z,8Z,11Z,14Z,17Z)/18:0), reflecting the dysfunction of PC and PE subclasses after IBDV infection. The contents of PCs and PEs in serum are affected by systemic metabolism, whether these identified differential metabolites are involved in the regulation of inflammation in IBDV infected chickens is unknown warranting further investigation.
Lysophosphatidylcholine (LysoPC) is mainly derived from PC cleavage by phospholipase A2 and is converted into PC by lysophosphatidylcholine acyltransferase [
75
,
76
]. LysoPCs were important mediators of inflammatory response and played a vital role in the progression of atherosclerosis, liver damage, renal failure, diabetes, and inflammatory bowel disease [
77
–
81
]. The immunoregulation activity of LysoPC was considered to be largely dependent on the biochemical structure. Saturated and monounsaturated LysoPCs were reported to be inflammatory inducer. LysoPC 16:0 and LysoPC 18:0 activated CD11b/CD18 to promote eosinophil adhesion [
82
]. Lyso16:0 recruited monocyte chemotaxis and induced pro-inflammatory cytokine production in macrophages [
83
]. Intra-peritoneal administration of Lyso 16:0 in mice caused plasma leakage, leukocyte migration to the peritoneum and pro-inflammatory mediators’ formation [
84
]. In contrast, polyunsaturated LysoPC served as anti-inflammatory mediator and inhibited the inflammation induced by saturated LysoPC. LysoPC 20:4 and LysoPC 22:6 were found to prohibit the LysoPC 16:0 triggered vascular dilation and leukocyte trafficking by down-regulating the formation of pro-inflammatory mediators (IL-5, IL-6, NO and so on) and up-regulating the anti-inflammatory mediators (IL-10 and IL-4) [
84
]. We detected significant alterations in serum LysoPCs content, including an increase in LysoPC(14:0/0:0), LysoPC(16:1(9Z)/0:0), LysoPC(18:3(6Z,9Z,12Z)/0:0), and LysoPC(20:5(5Z,8Z,11Z,14Z,17Z)/0:0), and a decrease in LysoPC(22:4(7Z,10Z,13Z,16Z)/0:0), LysoPC(22:5(4Z,7Z,10Z,13Z,16Z)/0:0) (Additional file
3
). Lipid metabolism analysis showed that LysoPC(14:0/0:0) was the primarily up-regulated metabolite after H9N2 virus infection in A549 cells [
85
]. LysoPC(14:0/0:0) pretreatment was found to reduce the high pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α) levels in the plasma and ameliorate LPS- and poly (I: C)-induced lung injury in mice [
86
]. The optimal level of LysoPC in plasma has not been determined and the mechanism underlying LysoPC’s effects is unclear [
87
]. Therefore, we hypothesized that the changes in LysoPCs caused by IBDV infection may be related to inflammatory regulation in chickens.
PS is an anionic glycerophospholipid located on the inner side of cell membrane. PS is considered to play important roles in physiological processes such as apoptosis, hemostasis and coagulation, anti-inflammatory, neurotransmission, and cholesterol metabolism [
88
,
89
]. When apoptosis is triggered, PS is exposed on the outer side of the cell membrane, marking the cell a target for elimination by phagocytes [
90
,
91
]. The exposure of PS provides binding sites for prothrombinase and tenase complexes, which activates the coagulation cascade reaction and facilitates blood clot formation [
90
]. The alterations of PSs were observed in the brain and serum in several central nervous system diseases and cardiovascular diseases [
88
,
89
]. We detected an elevation of serum PS(20:3(5Z,8Z,11Z)/18:2(9Z,12Z)) from the infected chickens, which may be related to inflammatory response and hypercoagulable state caused by IBDV.
Conclusions
Our study confirms the alteration of serum metabolome after atIBDV and vvIBDV infection, the differential metabolites and dysregulated metabolic pathways reflect the host response to the virus. Given that the pathogenicity of IBDV is diverse, serum metabolic alterations upon strains with different virulence may provide insights into fundamental metabolic pathways associated with IBDV and potential novel targets for treatment of metabolic disorders.
Methods
Experimental animals management and serum sample collection
In this study, we used 72 specific-pathogen-free White Leghorn chickens purchased from Beijing Boehringer Ingelheim Viton Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). After adaptation feeding, 49-day-old chickens were randomly assigned into three groups LM, LX, and mock, with 24 birds in each group. Chickens in LM and LX groups were administered with atIBDV strain LM (10
5.8
TCID
50
) and vvIBDV strain LX (10
4.5
ELD
50
) orally, respectively. The mock group was treated with 200 μl phosphate buffer solution in the same way.
All chickens had free access to commercial formula diet provided ad libitum and clean water throughout the whole experimental period. On day 7 after infection, blood samples were collected from 6 chickens in each group through the wing vein by syringe, and serum was harvested by keeping the syringe at room temperature in slanting position. A total of 18 serum samples were stored at -80℃ for further processing.
Metabolite extraction
100 μl of serum was mixed with 400 μl ice-cold mixture of acetonitrile and methanol (1:1, v/v) containing 0.02 mg/ml 2-chloro-L-phenylalanine. The mixed solution was vortexed for 30 s, low-temperature ultrasonicated (5℃, 40 kHz) for 30 min, kept at -20℃ for 30 min to precipitate the proteins, and then centrifuged at 13,000 g for 15 min at 4℃. The supernatant was collected and blown dry under nitrogen, re-solubilized with 120 μl 50% (v/v) acetonitrile solution, vortexed for 30 s, low temperature ultrasonicated (5℃, 40 kHz) for 5 min, and centrifugation at 13, 000 g for 10 min at 4℃. Finally, the supernatant was transferred to sample vials for UHPLC-MS/MS analysis. Quality control (QC) samples were pooled by mixing 20 μl of each sample.
UHPLC-MS/MS analysis for untargeted metabolite profiling
The UHPLC-MS/MS analysis was conducted on a UHPLC-Q Exactive HF-X system (Thermo Fisher, USA). 3 μl sample was injected into an ACQUITY HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm i.d., 1.8 μm; Waters, Milford, USA) at a flow rate of 0.4 ml/min and a column temperature of 40℃. The mobile phases A and B were H
2
O/acetonitrile (95:5, v/v) with 0.1% formic acid (v/v) and acetonitrile/isopropanol/H
2
O (47.5:47.5:5, v/v/v) with 0.1% formic acid (v/v). The solvent gradient elution in the positive ionization mode involved: 0–3 min, 0 − 20% B; 3–5 min, 20 − 100% B; 5–6.3 min, 100% B. Metabolite elution in the negative ionization mode involved: 0–1.2 min, 0–3.3% B; 1.2–5 min 3.3−100% B; 5–6.3 min, 100% B. All samples were kept at 4℃ during the analysis. The mass spectrometric data were collected by using an electrospray ionization source operating in the positive mode and negative mode. The MS conditions involved: a mass range of 70 to 1050 m/z, sheath gas flow rate at 50 arb, aux gas flow rate at 13 arb, source temperature at 425℃, capillary temperature at 325℃, full MS resolution at 60,000, MS
2
resolution at 7500, S-Lens RF Level at 50 V, normalized collision energy at 20-40-60 V, spray voltage at -3500 V and 3500 V in negative and positive mode. Data acquisition was performed with the data dependent acquisition mode. The QC samples were injected at regular intervals to assess repeatability of the data. All procedures were carried out in the laboratory of Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).
Data processing and statistical analysis
Raw data collected from the UHPLC-MS/MS analysis platform was preprocessed by Progenesis QI software (v3.0, Waters Corporation, Milford, USA). The three-dimensional data matrix was exported including the retention time, mass-to-charge ratio, and peak intensities. At the same time, the metabolites were qualitatively identified by alignment with the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB,
http://www.hmdb.ca/
), METLIN (
https://metlin.scripps.edu/
), and Majorbio Database. The data matrix obtained from database was analyzed by Majorbio cloud platform (
https://cloud.majorbio.com
) [
92
]. The data was filtered according to the “80% rule” [
93
] and the missing value was recoded with minimum value. The response intensities of the sample mass spectrum peaks were normalized using the sum normalization method to obtain the normalized data matrix. The variables of QC samples with relative standard deviation greater than 30% were excluded. The data was log transformed for subsequent statistical analysis. Principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal least partial squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) were performed by ropls package (version 1.6.2) in R language, and the stability of the model was evaluated by using 7-cycle interactive validation. Based on the variable importance in projection (VIP) obtained by the OPLS-DA model and the
P
value generated using Student’s t-test, significantly differential metabolites were identified with VIP value > 1 and
P
value < 0.05. Heatmap and Venn diagrams were performed with scipy (version1.0.0) in Python packages and VennDiagram (v1.6.20) in R packages, respectively. The differential metabolites were mapped into biochemical pathways through metabolic enrichment and pathway analysis by scipy based on the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database (
http://www.genome.jp/kegg/
). To further characterize the metabolic changes and obtain the most relevant biological pathways for experimental treatments, topology analysis was performed using MetaboAnalyst 4.0. The metabolic network of the differential metabolites and altered metabolic pathways in KEGG general metabolic pathway was visualized by iPath 3.0 (
http://pathways.embl.de/
).
Potential biomarker identification
To identify potential biomarkers in serum of IBDV infection chickens, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were performed to quantify the predictive performance of differential metabolites. The accuracy (including sensitivity and specificity) of each selected metabolite was evaluated by the area under the ROC curves (AUC), with AUC > 0.8 as the threshold.
