1
Introduction
Cell signalling is an integral part of several physiological processes [
1
,
2
], which are altered in pathological conditions [
3
]. Protein phosphorylation is the major driver of this vital event [
1
], and thus fuelled by advances in mass spectrometry (MS), the analysis of phosphoproteins by liquid chromatography‐tandem MS (LC‐MS/MS) has emerged as a valuable tool for uncovering signalling pathways [
4
,
5
], identifying potential drug targets [
6
] and disease biomarkers [
3
]. However, there are technological challenges which can limit the depth and breadth of their profiling by MS. Phosphoproteins constitute only 30% of the total proteome and several among them have low abundance [
3
,
7
], which can suppress their detection by more abundant proteins. Their poor ionization adds to the difficulty in their detection by MS [
8
,
9
]. To overcome this problem, several groups [
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
] have attempted the enrichment of phosphoproteins before their MS analysis, mainly by different known affinity‐based methods. Among them, in antibody‐based enrichment, specific antibodies that recognize phosphorylated serine, threonine and tyrosine residues in proteins are used either singly or in combination [
15
,
16
]. Antibody‐based enrichment is advantageous for the enrichment of phosphotyrosine‐bearing peptides which are fewer as compared to peptides carrying phosphorylated serine and threonine. However, antibodies are targeted against the phosphoamino acid within the context of its surrounding residues and therefore do not bind with the same efficiency to all phospho‐sites. Antibody‐based enrichment is thus unsuitable for studies covering the whole proteome [
10
]. Alternatively, there are affinity‐based techniques which exploit the specific affinity of certain chemical molecules or entities towards phosphorylated residues [
17
,
18
,
19
], the most commonly employed approaches being immobilized metal ion‐ and metal oxide‐affinity chromatography (IMAC and MOAC) [
20
,
21
,
22
]. In IMAC metal ions such as iron (Fe
3+
), gallium (Ga
3+
), or titanium (Ti
4+
) are immobilized on a solid support [
23
] while MOAC uses metal oxides like TiO
2,
Ge
2
O
3,
and Al
2
O
3
[
24
]. An improvisation in the detection of phosphoproteins using these matrices was reported by Tsai et al. by sequential elution through two different metal affinity columns [
25
]. However, the disadvantage of using either metal ions or metal oxides alone for enrichment is that they exhibit preferential affinity for mono‐ or multi‐phosphorylated proteins, respectively [
26
,
27
]. The study by Thingholm et al. attempted to overcome this lacuna, by sequential application of these two complementary matrices [
28
,
36
]. They enriched the flow‐through of IMAC by MOAC and vice versa. Pooling of the data obtained by both enrichments yielded better returns as compared to the common practice of using them separately. This approach has received attention even in the most recent studies [
29
].
In our study, we used sequential elution of phosphoproteins by IMAC and MOAC of two cell lines. The results led us to attempt the use of these approaches individually rather than sequentially and then pool the data, which improved phosphoprotein coverage.
2
Materials and Methods
2.1
Cell Culture
The wild‐type K562 leukemic cells (referred to as K562/S), and imatinib‐resistant cells (K562/R) derived from them were used for phosphoproteome analysis. The K562/S cells were cultured in a complete medium containing Roswell Park Memorial Institute medium‐ RPMI 1640 (Gibco, 230400‐021), 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, 10270‐106), and 1x antibiotic‐antimycotic (Gibco, 15240‐062) while resistant cell line (K562/R) was maintained in the same medium containing 0.75 µM imatinib (Cell Signaling Technology, 9084‐S) [
30
].
2.2
In‐Solution Trypsin Digestion of Proteins
Approximately 1×10
6
cells were harvested and washed with phosphate‐buffered saline. Cells were lysed in 100 µL of sodium dodecyl sulphate (SDS) buffer (2% SDS, 5% beta‐mercaptoethanol, 10% glycerol, and 62.5 mM Tris pH 6.8) which is routinely used for phosphopeptide enrichment [
31
,
32
], boiled for 10 min, kept on ice for 2 min, and spun at 13000 rpm for 15 min. The supernatant was collected and stored at −20°C till use. In‐solution digestion of proteins was done as previously described [
33
]. Briefly, 1 mL of ice‐cold acetone was added to 100 µL of SDS‐lysate and kept at −20°C overnight for protein precipitation. Thereafter, centrifugation was carried out at 13,000 rpm for 15 min on a refrigerated centrifuge (Plasto Crafts, Rota 4R—V/F
m
). The supernatant was discarded and the pellet was resuspended in 100 µL of urea buffer (6 M urea, 50 mM Tris pH 8). Reduction was done by adding 5 µL of 200 mM dithiothreitol and incubating for 1 hour at room temperature. Protein estimation was done by Bradford assay [
34
]. It was followed by alkylation using 20 µL of 200 mM iodoacetamide for 1 hour in the dark. Urea concentration was adjusted to 0.6 M using 1 mM CaCl
2
. Protein digestion was carried out by adding proteomic grade trypsin (sigma, T6567) prepared in 50 mM acetic acid in the ratio 1:50 trypsin: protein (w/w) and incubated for 16–18 h at 37°C. The sample was dried in a vacuum concentrator (Labconco, speed vac) and dried peptides were further used for phosphopeptide enrichment. In‐solution trypsin digestion was carried out for two biological replicates of each cell line.
2.3
Phosphopeptide Enrichment
Dried tryptic‐digested peptides generated from 300 µg of protein as described in section
2.2
were used for phosphopeptide enrichment. In method 1, peptides were enriched using the Fe‐NTA High–Select Phosphopeptide Enrichment kit (Thermo Scientific, A32992), and in method 2 by using the TiO
2
Phosphopeptide Enrichment kit (Thermo Scientific, A32993) according to the manufacturer's protocol. In both methods, the eluted peptides were further processed for LC‐MS/MS analysis.
In method 3, The Fe‐NTA flow‐through and wash fractions were pooled and used for phosphopeptide enrichment using the TiO
2
Phosphopeptide Enrichment kit. The TiO
2
eluant was further processed for LC‐MS/MS analysis.
In method 4, The TiO
2
flow‐through and wash fractions were pooled and used for phosphopeptide enrichment using the Fe‐NTA Phosphopeptide Enrichment kit. The Fe‐NTA eluant was further processed for LC‐MS/MS analysis.
The eluants were then desalted before subjecting them to LC‐MS/MS analysis using C18 (Ziptip—Pierce, 84850) columns. The eluant was vacuum‐dried, reconstituted in 20 µL of 0.1% formic acid and was spiked with internal standard (1 pmol/µL of digested
Escherichia coli
β galactosidase (β‐gal) peptides, Sciex, USA), vortexed and centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was used for LC‐MS/MS analysis.
2.4
Proteomic Profiling by LC‐MS/MS
Tryptic digests prepared from the cell lysates of two biological replicates of the two cell lines and each enriched by four methods were run in triplicate in LC‐MS/MS. The samples were injected into Eksigent ekspertTM nano‐Liquid Chromatography 400 with cHiPLC R system, with trap column (200 µm × 0.5 mm) and analytical column (75 µm × 15 cm), both packed with 3 µL ChromXp C18 (120 Å). For reverse phase HPLC, 0.1% formic acid in water and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile served as solvents A and B respectively. Gradient elution of 146 min, with an increasing percentage of mobile phase B, was used to elute the peptides at a flow rate of 250 nL/min. Eluate from the column was analysed in a positive ion mode quadrupole‐time of flight (Triple TOF 5600 +, Sciex) mass spectrometer. Each sample was subjected to one information‐dependent acquisition (IDA) run for spectral ion library generation followed by three data‐independent acquisition (DIA)—sequential window acquisition of all theoretical fragment ion spectra (SWATH) runs, which served as technical replicates. IDA mode involved a survey scan over a mass range of 350–1250
m/z
and an MS/MS scan over 200–1800
m/z
with rolling collision energy, 50 mDa mass tolerance, and accumulation time of 250 ms for MS and about 50 ms for MS/MS for the top 30 precursor ions. For DIA‐SWATH acquisition, the instrument was tuned to a looped product ion mode. A sequential isolation window width of 25 m/z (with 1 m/z overlap) covering a mass range of 350‐1250 m/z was set, resulting in 36 overlapping windows. SWATH data analysis, normalization, and quantification were done as previously described [
33
]. Briefly, peak view 1.2 software was used to visualize the total ion chromatogram and to convert the raw spectral generated file from .wiff to a .mgf file. Protein pilot 5.0 software was used for protein identification using a .mgf file with a paragon algorithm. Proteins identified with a 1% false detection rate were included in further analysis.
2.5
Data Analysis
Two biological replicates of lysates prepared from K562/S and K562/R, enriched using four different methods and run thrice (technical replicates) in LC‐MS/MS generated six spectra (three technical replicates of two biological replicates) for each method for a cell line. Data from these six spectra was pooled for each enrichment method for a cell line. Pooling the data from multiple runs is a common practice in LC‐MS/MS analysis of complex protein mixtures to counter inter‐run variation [
33
]. The relative performance of different methods in enriching phosphopeptides is assessed from the percentage increase or decrease in the number of identified phosphoproteins in each method.
3
Results and Discussion
Phosphopeptide enrichment from K562/S and K562/R cells using the reported [
29
,
36
] sequential elution approach with Fe‐NTA and TiO
2
was carried out by passing the FeNTA flow‐through via TiO
2
(method 3) and TiO
2
flow‐through via Fe‐NTA (method 4).
Our observations show that the enrichment of phosphoproteome before mass spectrometric analysis by sequential elution method 3 resulted in the identification of only 29 and 11 phosphoproteins, while method 4 identified 222 and 371 phosphoproteins in K562/S and K562/R cells, respectively (Figure
1
). Even after pooling data from methods 3 and 4 for K562/S and K562/R, only 224 and 375 phosphoproteins were identified in them, respectively (Figure
2A,B
; Table
S1
). Our previous proteomic analysis of K562/S and K562/R cells had identified between 1700 and 2200 proteins [
30
]. Therefore, we expected to identify ∼510–660 phosphoproteins in these cells, since phosphoproteins reportedly constitute 30% of the total proteome [
3
,
7
]. It was possible that during additional steps in sequential elution, there was a loss of peptides. To check this, we decided to use both matrices individually as we acknowledge that a single matrix may not suffice to enrich a complex phosphoproteome of protein mixture like whole cell lysate. We therefore enriched phosphopeptides using Fe‐NTA (method 1) and TiO
2
(method 2) individually and further pooled data from these two affinity enrichments. As shown in Figure
1
, elution fraction from Fe‐NTA enrichment (method 1) identified 512 and 473 phosphoproteins in K562/S and K562/R respectively. While TiO
2
(method 2) identified 22 and 21 phosphoproteins in K562/S and K562/R respectively. We noticed that the number of phosphoproteins identified by pooling the data from both matrices (518 in K562/S and 475 in K562/R; Figure
2C,D
and Table
S1
), were close to the numbers as expected from our theoretical computation. Compared to the identities obtained from pooling data of sequential elution methods (methods 3 and 4), a gain of more than 30% was observed in the identities obtained in K562/S and K562/R (derived from data in Table
S1
). Further, it was striking that Fe‐NTA enriched 98% of the total phosphoproteins in the Fe‐NTA and TiO
2
pooled data. It therefore raised a question on the need to use TiO
2
for enrichment
.
However, the 2% fraction identified exclusively in the TiO
2
‐enrichment were proteins of biological significance (Table
1
), emphasizing the need to use both matrices and then pool the data (raw data of LC‐MS/MS run of all six replicates of each method for both cell lines are uploaded to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE [
35
] partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD050933).

Conclusion
Phosphoproteome enrichment using two affinity materials with complementary chemistries reported by Thingholm et al. [
29
,
36
] achieves enrichment of mono‐ and multi‐phosphorylated proteins. Our study demonstrates that enrichment through the two affinity matrices independently and pooling the data improves phosphopeptide coverage markedly as compared to the sequential elution approach. The enrichment not only allows the identification of the theoretically anticipated number of phosphoproteins based on their known abundance in the cell but also ensures the detection of biologically important phosphoproteins. Thus the incremental modification suggested in this study is effective for phosphoproteome profiling of complex protein mixtures and can potentially contribute to cell signalling research.
