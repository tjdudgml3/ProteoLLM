Introduction
Actinotignum schaalii
is an emerging pathogen that primarily colonizes the urogenital system. It is most often associated with urinary tract infections and bacteremia, particularly in elderly patients, children, and those with underlying urogenital diseases [
1
,
2
].
A. schaalii
has gained increasing attention owing to its ability to cause several invasive infections [
1
–
5
], with rising detection rates in recent years. In China, only one case of inguinal infection caused by
A. schaalii
has been reported so far [
6
]. In this report, we have presented a case of
A. schaalii
-induced recurrent infections at a periumbilical scar after laparoscopy that was complicated by abscess formation. This case has been reviewed in the light of relevant literature to enhance the current clinical understanding and improve the diagnosis and treatment of
A. schaalii
infections.
Medical history
A 50-year-old female presented to the Infectious Disease Outpatient Department of our hospital on April 7, 2023, with a history of recurrent festering at a periumbilical scar after laparoscopy, which had persisted for 9 years, with ruptures occurring over the past 2 years. The patient had previously undergone laparoscopic liver cyst unroofing and drainage in 2015, with no immediate accompanying postoperative complications such as fever or incision pain. The surgical incision healed within approximately 1 week. However, a radial scar with folds and persistent dark-red discoloration developed at the umbilical site. Despite the lack of swelling, pain, itching, fever, or abdominal discomfort, the scar remained prominent. The other two laparoscopic incisions healed completely, with minimal scarring and no signs of redness or folds, thus not raising any clinical concern. The appearance of the umbilical scar remained stable for several years.
In November 2020, the patient began to notice a slight swelling in the affected area following periods of fatigue, although there was no increase in the skin temperature or local pain. Over time, suppuration, rupture, and pus discharge ensued, with the pus appearing light-yellow, slightly viscous, and odorless. Surgical drainage was performed, followed by dressing changes with silver ion dressings (once daily for 10 days) and the local application of mupirocin ointment (thrice daily for 10 days). Her condition improved after approximately 3 weeks, with the skin lesions healing, although the scar retained its folds and persistent dark-red appearance. In December 2022, the symptoms recurred. The patient received the same treatment, which again led to improvement after approximately 3 weeks. However, in late March 2023, the patient experienced another recurrence of infection in the same area. Initially, the affected area appeared as shown in Fig.
1
. This event was immediately followed by suppuration, rupture, and pus discharge, along with mild local discomfort, but no fever or abdominal pain. On April 4, 2023, she returned to the Infectious Disease Outpatient Department for further evaluation.

Medical history
The patient had a 10-year history of Hashimoto’s thyroiditis but had not received any treatment considering that her thyroid function remained normal.
Physical examination: the patient’s vital signs were stable and the patient was in generally good condition. A radial fold scar was observed below the navel, characterized by localized redness and a slight swelling of the skin. Although there was no significant increase in the local skin temperature, tenderness was present. Upon compression, a small amount of pale-yellow, slightly viscous pus was noted at the rupture site, with no discernible odor. The abdomen was free of muscle tension, tenderness, or rebound tenderness. Other physical examinations revealed no abnormalities.
Key auxiliary examinations: A routine blood test showed no abnormalities. The pus was subjected to both aerobic and anaerobic cultures. After 3 days of anaerobic cultivation in blood agar medium, gray-white, non-hemolytic, non-spore-forming, gram-positive, slightly curved rod-shaped bacteria were identified, as shown in Fig.
2
. These bacteria were confirmed as
A. schaalii
through matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) (the score is 9.327, within the confidence interval of the species level). Drug sensitivity testing was performed using the broth microdilution method, and the quality control strain used is
Staphylococcus aureus
ATCC25923, following the CLSI M100 standard (version 33). It is worth noting that the broth dilution method done appeared to show this organism to be sensitive to the below mentioned drugs but requires furthers studies to be brought out by EUCAST or CLSI to define it’s sensitivity pattern. The minimum inhibitory concentrations for ampicillin, piperacillin/tazobactam, amoxicillin/clavulanate potassium, and ceftriaxone were 0.5, 4/4, 1/2, and 4 µg/mL, respectively, indicating sensitivity. However, the minimum inhibitory concentrations for clindamycin and tetracycline were 16 µg/mL and 32 µg/mL, respectively, indicating resistance.

Diagnosis
The patient was diagnosed with
A. schaalii
-induced periumbilical scar infection, accompanied by abscess formation.
Treatment
The patient began with a course of oral amoxicillin capsules (0.5 g, every 8 h) on April 16, 2023, to be continued for 1 month. In addition, surgical debridement and drainage were performed, followed by dressing changes and the application of silver ion dressings (once daily for 10 days). After approximately 3 weeks, the skin lesions fully healed, although the scar folds persisted.
Follow-up
On October 10, 2023, the patient experienced another episode of local redness and slight swelling at the scar site, although it was not accompanied by pain. Shortly thereafter, the scar ruptured, releasing a small amount of odorless, light-yellow, slightly viscous pus. The wound was notably smaller than in previous infections, approximately the size of a mung bean, and was managed with dressing changes. Silver ion dressing was applied locally once daily for 7 days. No pathogen examination was conducted, and systemic antibiotics were not administered. The wound healed within approximately 1 week, as shown in Fig.
3
. Subsequent scar injection therapy with triamcinolone acetonide and lidocaine was administered on November 2, 2023, December 15, 2023, and January 28, 2024, at our hospital. The local conditions after scar treatment are illustrated in Fig.
4
, and the scar was softer than before. The patient was followed up until September 2024, during which time no further local scar infections occurred.

Discussion
A. schaalii
was first reported in 1997 as
Actinobaculum schaalii
, but its nomenclature history remains checkered with taxonomic reclassification [
7
]. Since 2015, the
Actinobaculum
genus has been diversified into the genera
Actinotignum
and
Actinobaculum
[
1
]. The genus
Actinotignum
comprises
A. schaalii
,
Actinotignum massiliense
, and
Actinotignum urinale
[
8
]. Among these,
A. schaalii
is the most commonly reported strain in human infections [
9
].
A. schaalii
is a slow-growing, gram-positive, rod-shaped, non-motile, non-spore-forming, catalase-negative, facultative anaerobe. It requires cultivation on blood agar medium with 5% CO
2
or under anaerobic conditions for 48 h to develop into gray-white small colonies [
3
,
10
]. In this case,
A. schaalii
failed to grow under aerobic culture conditions (containing 7% CO
2
), underscoring the importance of performing anaerobic cultures for pus samples when fastidious bacteria like
A. schaalii
were suspected. Infections caused by
A. schaalii
are typically mixed, often co-cultured with other pathogenic bacteria [
1
,
11
–
14
]. In addition, due to its morphological similarity to normal skin microbiota,
A. schaalii
may be overlooked or misidentified as a contaminant in mixed cultures [
15
]. Its slow growth and specific cultivation requirements make it susceptible to getting dismissed as a contaminant, which has historically contributed to a low clinical detection rate. However, with the widespread adoption of advanced proteomics techniques, such as MALDI-TOF MS and genomics methods like 16 S rRNA gene sequencing, the detection of
A. schaalii
as a pathogenic bacterium has increased [
16
,
17
]. Presently, MALDI-TOF MS is the most accurate and rapid method for identifying
A. schaalii
[
18
]. In this case, the infection caused by
A. schaalii
was confirmed via MALDI-TOF MS.
A. schaalii
is most commonly associated with urinary tract infections, particularly in older adults (particularly those aged > 60 years), younger individuals (particularly those aged 8 months to 16 years), and patients with underlying urogenital conditions [
19
]. However, it can also lead to invasive infections such as endocarditis, intervertebral disc inflammation, arthritis, necrotizing fasciitis, sepsis, and various types of abscesses [
1
–
5
,
14
,
20
]. While
A. schaalii
-induced urinary tract infections are relatively common, skin and soft tissue infections or abscesses caused by this bacterium are less frequent. Maraki et al. [
11
] noted that abscesses caused by
A. schaalii
are often found in younger patients, with an average age of 31.3 years. Reported skin infections or abscesses are predominantly located in the perineum, including the perianal and genital regions [
6
,
12
,
14
,
16
,
19
]. This may be related to
A. schaalii
being a commensal organism of the skin and mucosa of the urogenital tract [
15
].
A. schaalii
has also been isolated from other body areas such as the breast, inguinal region, abdominal wall, and lower limbs [
12
]. Liu et al. described a case involving a skin abscess in the sacrococcygeal region, indicating that
A. schaalii
might also inhabit body folds [
13
]. In this case, the infection occurred at a surgical scar fold, highlighting the need for clinical awareness regarding
A. schaalii
infections in body folds beyond just the urogenital system.
A. schaalii
infections at surgical sites have been documented, including 1 case of maxillary surgery, 1 case of bladder stone removal surgery [
11
], 1 case of sternotomy [
21
], and 6 cases of other unspecified surgeries [
16
]. However, none of these studies specifically addressed the healing of surgical incisions. There is only one reported case of
A. schaalii
infection at the navel [
1
], albeit it is unclear whether this was a postoperative scar. The case patient developed repeated infections at the scar site after laparoscopy, which was accompanied by abscess formation. The lack of an etiological basis for the initial diagnosis and treatment led to repeated infections, underscoring the importance of accurate etiological diagnosis for effective prevention and treatment.
Similar to the present case, recurrent abscesses caused by
A. schaalii
have been documented previously [
11
,
22
]. Maraki et al. [
11
] described a patient with hidradenitis suppurativa who experienced recurrent perineal abscesses due to a mixed infection with
A. schaalii
and
Prevotella intermedius
(melanin-producing). The patient was initially treated with surgical drainage and a combination of oral antibiotics (300 mg of clindamycin every 6 h for 15 days and 100 mg of minocycline every 12 h for 15 days), resulting in improvement but with recurrent infections. The patient was eventually cured with additional antibiotics (not specified) and a tumor necrosis factor inhibitor. Diallo et al. [
22
] reported a case of lower abdominal cellulitis and abscess in a patient with common variable immunodeficiency. This patient was treated with trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (800/160 mg) twice daily. After 4 weeks of treatment, the cellulitis and abscess resolved completely. The patient had a previous abscess in the right breast (2 years before common variable immunodeficiency diagnosis), which demonstrated minimal actinomycete growth in the culture, but the pathogen was not further identified, and specific treatment details were not provided. Both the patients had underlying immune-related conditions, similar to that of the patient in our study who has Hashimoto’s thyroiditis. Thus, infections caused by
A. schaalii
should be considered in patients with immune-related conditions and recurrent abscesses. Mah et al. [
16
] reported that, when compared to urinary tract infections, skin and soft tissue infections, including abscesses and surgical site infections caused by
A. schaalii
, generally have a lower risk of hospitalization. Younger and previously healthy patients can be treated on an outpatient basis with low mortality rates. The patients in our study also received an outpatient treatment and follow-up. Currently, there is no unified clinical guideline for treating
A. schaalii
infections. Management typically involves a multimodal approach, including antibiotics targeting the infection and an invasive procedure to control the source of the infection. Resistance to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole and second-generation quinolones (e.g., norfloxacin and ciprofloxacin) is high among
A. schaalii
strains [
1
,
3
,
8
,
12
,
19
,
23
,
24
], with resistance rates of up to 100% to ciprofloxacin [
19
,
23
] and 67% to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole [
19
]. Resistance to metronidazole and clindamycin is also high [
1
,
12
,
19
], while
A. schaalii
tends to be sensitive to novel fluoroquinolones (e.g., levofloxacin and moxifloxacin) [
3
,
19
,
23
,
25
,
26
]. The bacterium is generally sensitive to all β-lactam antibiotics, except mezlocillin [
3
,
9
,
12
,
19
,
26
,
27
], which has been supported by clinical treatment efficacy [
6
,
19
,
28
–
30
]. Lotte et al. [
9
] found that
A. schaalii
was also sensitive to tetracycline, vancomycin, teicoplanin, linezolid, rifampicin, nitrofurantoin, and gentamicin. Previous studies have reported treatment durations for
A. schaalii
infections ranging from 3 days to 3 months [
1
,
2
,
4
,
6
,
12
,
16
,
19
,
24
,
26
,
28
,
29
,
31
–
33
]. Although there is no standardized treatment course, Lotte et al. recommend β-lactam antibiotics for more than 2 weeks as the first-line therapy [
9
], noting that cases have failed with only 1 week of amoxicillin treatment [
25
]. In this case, despite receiving adequate antibiotic treatment, the patient experienced reinfection during follow-up. However, the subsequent infection was milder and resolved more quickly than previous episodes. Notably, the patient did not receive systemic antibiotics during the last infection, and only local treatment was used, which was effective. After scar treatment, no further infections occurred. The recurrent infections may be attributed to the pathological nature of the scar, which disrupts the normal skin structure, damages sebaceous glands and hair follicles, and becomes prone to secondary infections. In addition, the wrinkled scar was susceptible to rubbing and difficult to clean, which further increased the risk of infection. Pathological scars can also cause long-term burning pain, itching, redness, and swelling, which can significantly affect appearance and function [
34
].
This finding underscores the importance for physicians to adopt a comprehensive approach to treating infections, particularly those secondary to scars. For patients experiencing recurrent scar infections, it is essential to assess the scar stabilization period with input from plastic surgery or cosmetic dermatology. Depending on the specific case, treatments such as medication, laser therapy, or surgical intervention may be necessary to address the scar and reduce the risk of recurrent infections, thereby improving the patient’s quality of life [
34
–
36
].
Conclusions
The unusual aspect of the present case is the long latent period after the likely date of the original acquisition. As an emerging infectious pathogen,
A. schaalii
is not confined to causing urinary tract infections, but can also lead to invasive infections at various sites, thereby significantly impacting patient prognosis.
A. schaalii
infection should be considered in patients with recurrent skin and soft tissue infections or abscesses, particularly in those with immune-related underlying conditions and infections occurring in body folds. To enhance the likelihood of identifying the pathogen, pus samples should be cultured both aerobically and anaerobically. Utilizing advanced diagnostic methods is crucial for accurate pathogen identification, which facilitates effective anti-infection treatment and helps prevent treatment failures and recurrences. For patients with recurrent scar infections, appropriate interventions—such as medication, laser therapy, or surgical procedures—should be used during the scar stabilization period to prevent the recurrence of infection.
