Understanding trafficking defects in rare genetic disorders can provide insights into a broader range of diseases. Hermansky‐Pudlak syndrome (HPS) is a rare, autosomal‐recessive disorder caused by defects in the biogenesis of lysosome‐related organelles complexes (BLOCs) or the adapter protein 3 (AP‐3) complex [
1
,
2
]. HPS encompasses 11 human genetic subtypes, each with a mouse model, and affects approximately 1–9/1 000 000 individuals worldwide. Clinical manifestations of HPS include oculocutaneous albinism, a bleeding diathesis, granulomatous colitis, and pulmonary fibrosis (PF), which develops in patients with HPS‐1, HPS‐2, and HPS‐4 [
3
,
4
,
5
]. Ceroid lipofuscin also accumulates in various tissues [
6
]. The majority of known HPS individuals have HPS‐1. The most common
HPS1
variant is a 16‐bp duplication in exon 15 (c.1472_1487dup16‐bp), which is particularly prevalent in Northwest Puerto Rico, where HPS affects with a frequency of 1 in 1800 individuals [
3
]. The HPS1 and HPS4 proteins interact to form BLOC‐3, a RAB32/38 guanine nucleotide exchange factor that promotes membrane recruitment of RAB32 and RAB38 [
3
,
4
].
Kidney manifestations in HPS‐1 are not well‐characterized histologically due to the risk of bleeding during renal biopsy [
7
]. However, several clinical cases have been reported. Decreased creatinine clearance, indicating impaired kidney function, was found in 28% of HPS‐1 patients homozygous for the 16‐bp duplication [
5
]. Ceroid deposition, interstitial fibrosis, mesangial IgA deposition, crescentic glomerulonephritis, and an interstitial lymphocytic infiltrate were reported in a 40‐year‐old woman with HPS [
7
]. Finally, one child homozygous for the 16 bp founder duplication in
HPS1
presented with chronic kidney disease (CKD), tubular pathology, and focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) [
8
]. These clinical findings are supported by phenotyping of
HPS1
animal models; experimental knockdown of
HPS1
in a zebrafish model resulted in glomerular podocyte effacement, reduction or loss of glomerular endothelial cell permeability, and intracellular debris accumulation [
9
]. Interestingly, the pale ear mouse model, [
10
] a well‐established murine model for HPS1, presented with abnormal kidney lysosomal enzyme accumulation caused by defective secretion into urine, indicating impaired kidney lysosomal function [
11
].
Extracellular vesicles (EVs) comprise a heterogeneous population of membrane vesicles that typically vary between 50 and 500 nm, but they can be even larger (1–10 μm) [
12
]. EVs, categorized based on their origin (cell type), size, morphology and cargo content, are typically grouped into two distinct classes: exosomes and microvesicles [
12
]. Exosomes (40–100 nm) comprise a subset of EVs that form by two membrane inversions, first via inward budding of the plasma membrane to form endocytic vesicles and second as intraluminal vesicles (ILVs) that bud off into the lumen of maturing multivesicular endosomes (MVEs) [
13
]. MVEs then fuse with the plasma membrane to release ILVs as exosomes [
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
]. As a result, exosomes share membrane orientation with the plasma membrane, carry cytosolic components, and contain lipid rafts [
17
,
18
]. Exosomes are enriched in a common set of proteins essential for their biogenesis, structure and trafficking (e.g., Alix, TSG101, CD63, CD81, HSP70), [
12
,
19
,
20
,
21
] as well as distinct subsets of proteins that may be linked to cell‐type specific functions [
12
,
22
]. Exosomes play a crucial role in various cellular processes including non‐classical secretion of proteins, [
22
] lipids, [
23
] and nucleic acids, [
22
,
24
,
25
] immune response regulation, [
26
] signal transduction, [
27
] antigen presentation, [
28
,
29
] transport of infectious cargo (including prions [
30
,
31
] and HIV [
32
,
33
]), and cell–cell interaction/signalling [
34
]. Exosome secretion is an alternative way to alleviate stress when recycling/trafficking pathways of the endo‐lysosomal system are compromised [
35
].
Urinary extracellular vesicles (uEVs) are a heterogeneous group of vesicles that include exosomes as well as other, generally larger, vesicles. uEVs can be used to study protein‐expression changes in renal tubular epithelial cells, podocytes, and transitional epithelia of the urinary collecting system [
36
] via non‐invasive collection of urine samples, which is advantageous for HPS‐1 patients who suffer from a bleeding diathesis. We used a proteomics approach to compare the protein composition of uEVs obtained from 5 HPS‐1 patients with the 16‐bp duplication to that of 5 normal control individuals. Our data reveal distinct protein abundance changes in HPS‐1 patient uEVs, indicating potential abnormalities in protein trafficking and secretion processes within HPS‐1 renal epithelial cells. These results lay the foundation for future investigations into biomarker assessment, pathophysiology, and functional defects in the HPS‐1 kidney disorder; they may also provide outcome measures for therapeutic trials of HPS‐1.

Urine collection and
uEV
isolation
Several methods have been employed to isolate and study uEVs, including ultracentrifugation, [
36
,
41
,
42
] filtration, [
43
] and the use of commercial kits [
17
,
18
,
44
]. However, comparative proteomics experiments using uEV preparations have been hindered by the presence of abundant and variable Tamm–Horsfall protein (THP), an 85 kDa glycoprotein produced by renal tubular cells of the distal loop of Henle [
45
], that can trap EVs, reduce purification yields, and hinder accurate protein quantification using standard methods [
46
]. To overcome this limitation, we used the reducing agent Tris(2‐Carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP) to significantly reduce THP from uEV preparations. This method preserves the integrity of uEVs and enables accurate quantification of uEV proteins.
Specifically, the non‐preservative portion of a 24‐h urine collection was treated with sodium azide and protease inhibitors. Urine was centrifuged at 17 000
g
for 1 h to pellet debris. The resulting supernatant was centrifuged at 200 000
g
for 30 min. Pellets were gently re‐suspended in 160 mg·mL
−1
TCEP in EV isolation solution (EIS) [
36
], then incubated in a 37 °C water bath for 30 min. Pellets were then centrifuged at 200 000
g
and the supernatant was discarded. Pellets were washed in 1XPBS (supplemented with protease inhibitors) to remove remaining sucrose. For proteomics and Western blotting, the pellet was re‐suspended in RIPA buffer (supplemented with protease inhibitors), sonicated 4 times for 5 s using a Misonix probe sonicator (Farmingdale, NY, USA) and frozen at −80 °C until the analysis.
Transmission electron microscopy (
TEM

uEVs
)
TEM was used to examine uEVs. A drop of each preparation was placed on parafilm and covered with a glow‐discharged 200 mesh copper grid. After 5 min of incubation, the grid was washed with PBS and incubated in blocking buffer for 15 min. The grids were incubated with primary antibody against mouse anti‐human CD63, rabbit‐anti‐mouse secondary antibody, and 10 nm protein A‐gold for 30 min. To fix the gold labelling, 2% glutaraldehyde was applied for 5 min, then washed with double distilled water for 10 min. The grids were stained with 2% w/v uranyl acetate solution and analyzed by TEM using a JEM‐1200EX (JEOL USA, Peabody, MA, USA) transmission electron microscope (accelerating voltage 80 keV) equipped with an AMT 6‐megapixel digital camera (Advanced Microscopy Techniques Corp, Danvers, MA, USA). The vesicles were counted using Fiji, a software for biological‐image analysis, [
47
] and the results were visualized using
graphpad prism
version 10.10.2 (341) for Apple, GraphPad Software
https://www.graphpad.com/

In‐gel digestion and
LC
–
MS
/
MS
analysis
Exosome protein lysates in RIPA buffer (40 μg by Bio‐rad DC protein assay) separated by 4–20% Tris‐Glycine gels were solubilized in 2× SDS Protein Gel Solution plus 10× NuPage Sample Reducing Agent and heated at 90 °C for 5 min. Protein lysates separated by 3–8% Tris‐acetate gels or 8% BOLT Bis‐Tris gels were solubilized in 4× LDS Sample buffer plus 10× NuPage Sample Reducing Agent and heated at 70 °C for 10 min. Antioxidant was used in the inner gel chamber and samples were separated at 150 V for approximately 1 h 10 min. Gels were stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue R‐250 Staining Solution and de‐stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue R‐250 De‐staining Solution. Gels were subsequently cut from top to bottom into approximately 1–2 mm bands.
Proteins within the bands were reduced, alkylated, and trypsinized, and the resulting peptides were extracted as described [
48
]. Peptides were separated and analyzed by a fully automated HPLC‐MS system consisting of a Proxeon Easy nLC‐II nano‐flow high‐performance liquid chromatography system connected online to an LTQ NSI‐Ion Trap mass spectrometer (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The reverse phase separation was conducted using mobile phase A: water with 0.1% formic acid, and mobile phase B: acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid. Peptide samples were loaded onto a C18 Easy column (100 μm i.d., 2 cm length, 5 μm particle size) and then eluted and separated on a Picofrit BioBasic C18 column (75 μm i.d., 10 cm length, 5 μm particle size) (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at 300 nL·min
−1
using the following gradient program: 5% B (3 min), a linear gradient of 5–43% B (49 min), 43–100% B (5 min), 100% B (8 min). The LTQ was operated in positive ion mode with dynamic exclusion set to repeat count = 1, repeat duration = 30, exclusion duration = 60, exclusion mass width low = 0.5 amu, exclusion mass width high = 1.0 amu, expiration count = 2. Spectra were acquired in a data‐dependent manner with the top five most intense ions in the MS scan selected for MS/MS. A threshold of 3000 was required to trigger MS/MS.
Database searching
Tandem mass spectra were converted to mzML peak list files using ProteoWizard MSconvert (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and searched against the human subset of the SwissProt database (January 2020, 20 365 entries) using
mascot daemon
(Matrix Science, London, UK; version 2.6.0) on the
NIH HPC Biowulf cluster
(
http://hpc.nih.gov
); charge state deconvolution and deisotoping were not performed. Automated decoy (reversed protein) sequences were searched simultaneously to allow for the calculation of false discovery rates (FDR). The search parameters were as follows: enzyme = trypsin, missed cleavage sites = 1, parent ion mass tolerance = 0.80 Da, fragment ion mass tolerance = 0.80 Da, fixed modification = carbamidomethyl of cysteine, variable modification = oxidation of methionine.
Criteria for protein identification
scaffold
(version Scaffold_5.2.0, Proteome Software Inc., Portland, OR, USA) was used to validate MS/MS‐based peptide and protein identifications. The Scaffold file loading option MUDPIT was used to combine all files from the same biological sample (same gel lane). Peptide identifications were accepted if they could be established at > 95.0% probability by the Percolator posterior error probability calculation [
49
]. Protein identifications were accepted if they could be established at > 99.0% probability and contained at least 3 identified peptides. Protein probabilities were assigned by the Protein Prophet algorithm [
50
]. Proteins that contained similar peptides and could not be differentiated based on MS/MS analysis alone were grouped to satisfy the principles of parsimony. Proteins sharing significant peptide evidence were grouped into clusters. Proteins were annotated with Gene Ontology (GO) terms from NCBI (downloaded Dec 14, 2022) [
51
].

Label‐free protein quantitative analysis was performed using Scaffold's weighted spectral count with a minimum value set to 0.1. Scaffold calculated relative protein fold change differences between HPS‐1 uEV and NL and those values were converted to log
2
(fold change) for consistency. Log
2
(fold change) ≤ −1 and log

P
value < 0.01127). Protein abundance differences between HPS‐1 uEV and NL were considered significant if both
P
value and log
2
(fold change) thresholds were met. Volcano plots and ordinary one‐way ANOVA were performed in
graphpad prism
v.9.4.1 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Functional pathway enrichment analysis was performed using Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) (
www.qiagen.com/ingenuity
) on proteins meeting the log
2
(fold change) and

gRNA
generation
We designed a sgRNA located next to a protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) site in the third coding exon (exon 5) of the
Hps1
gene, to induce a double‐strand DNA break (Fig.
5A
). CRISPR‐Cas9 cutting sites and their respective gRNAs were selected using the CRISPR selection tool of the UCSC Genome Browser. gRNA oligonucleotides were synthesized with overhangs compatible with the BsaI sites of the T7gRNA plasmid vector (Transposagen, San Jose, CA, USA). Oligonucleotides were annealed and phosphorylated using T4 Polynucleotide Kinase (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) in 1× Quick Ligase buffer (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) with incubation at 37 °C for 30 min. pT7gRNA was digested with BsaI (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) and then dephosphorylated using calf intestinal alkaline phosphatase (
CIP
) (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) by addition of 1 μL CIP to above mixture and incubation at 50 °C for 30 min. Digested and dephosphorylated T7gRNA were run out on a 1.5% agarose gel and the ~ 2.14 kb linearized fragment and excised from the gel using the Qiagen Gel Extraction Kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD, USA) as per manufacturer's instructions. Annealed and phosphorylated gRNA oligos were ligated to the digested and dephosphorylated T7gRNA using the Quick Ligation Kit (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) and a 3 : 1 oligo : T7gRNA molar ratio of DNAs, as per manufacturer's instructions. 2 μL of ligation reaction were used to transform 50 μl TOP10 competent cells (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The Rapid Transformation protocol was used per manufacturer's instructions. Colonies were picked and screened by Dideoxy sequencing using M13F primer. One clone was digested with DraI (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) and then run out on a 1.5% agarose gel, with the linearized fragment excised from the gel using the Qiagen Gel Extraction Kit per manufacturer's instructions. Purified gRNA‐encoding DNA were
in vitro
transcribed using the MEGAshortscript™ T7 Transcription Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) per manufacturer's instructions. Input DNA was removed by incubation with 1 μL TurboDNase at 37 °C for 15 min. RNA was purified from the above reaction using the MEGAclear™ Transcription Clean‐Up Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) per manufacturer's instructions, and then quantitated by Nanodrop and assessed by running out on a non‐denaturing 1.5% agarose gel.
Mice injection
Mice injection was performed by transgenic manipulation experts in the NIH. C57BL/6 mice were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA) while 129SVE mice were obtained from Taconic Biosciences (Germantown, NY, USA). The mice were kept and manipulated in an accredited, pathogen‐free mouse facility at the NIH. C57BL/6 female mice were induced to super ovulate using pregnant mare serum gonadotropin and human chorionic gonadotropin, and then mated with 129SVE male mice. The fertilized oocytes were harvested from females displaying vaginal plugs. CRISPR/Cas9 gRNA was diluted to 1 μg·μL
−1
for microinjection. The gRNA was injected into the pronucleus of fertilized oocytes. The injected oocytes were surgically transferred to the oviducts of pseudo pregnant female mice. A total of 204 embryos (B6/129 background) were injected with this sgRNA, and 63 pups were born. Using fluorescence PCR, we detected small insertions and deletions in the directed region in 32 pups. We identified several mice with a discernible phenotype of varying shades of lightly colored coats, tails, and ears, suggesting a defect in melanosome processing possibly similar to HPS‐1 patients [
56
,
57
,
58
]. These mice were further bred to generate homozygous lines. We then chose the lightest lines.

Results
uEVs
are intact and contain known
EV
markers after Tamm–Horsfall protein removal
Normal control uEVs were isolated by differential centrifugation and treated to reduce the levels of contaminating Tamm–Horsfall Protein (THP) (Fig.
1A
). Identification and structural integrity of the treated vesicles were determined by Immuno‐EM using an anti‐human CD63 antibody which confirms the vesicles are intact (Fig.
1B
). Size range of the treated vesicles was measured by Transmission EM (TEM) (Fig.
1C
) and confirmed that the isolated vesicles contain exosomes (30–100 nm). Western blot analysis of NL uEV samples before and after Tris(2‐Carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP) treatment demonstrated significant THP reduction while retaining known exosome markers such as ALIX (ESCRT), TSG101 (ESCRT), HSC70 (chaperone/binding), and CD9 (tetraspanin) [
12
] (Fig.
1D
). Taken together, these results indicate the successful reduction of THP without compromising the content or structure of the vesicles. As a result, we were able to obtain accurate protein concentration amounts for comparative proteomics experiments.

Since HPS1 and HPS4 proteins are part of the BLOC‐3 complex, we determined if BLOC complex proteins were detected in uEV. We did not identify BLOC complex proteins by mass spectrometry (MS), but we were able to measure relative protein abundance of selected BLOC complex proteins with Western blotting (Fig.

In contrast, NL uEV samples contained both HPS1 and HPS4 proteins (BLOC‐3), while HPS‐1 uEV lacked HPS1 and exhibited a notable reduction in HPS4 protein due to the 16‐bp duplication mutation. These findings indicate that BLOC proteins are trafficked in uEVs and that uEV levels of HPS1 and HPS4 proteins correlate to the disease.
RAB proteins play an important role in intracellular membrane trafficking via transport, tethering, and fusion of membrane‐bound organelles and vesicles, as well as in exosome formation and secretion [
18
]. The GTP‐active form of each RAB localizes on a specific organelle (or vesicle) and recruits various effector proteins to facilitate each step of membrane trafficking [
71

72

73

Further experiments are underway to elucidate if the changes in RAB29/7L1 and RAB11a reflect altered protein expression or if they reflect a possible defect involving the slow recycling endosome or trans‐Golgi network due to the BLOC‐3 defect.

To gain a deeper understanding of the extensive MS data set, we employed Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) using the 149 differentially expressed proteins to identify canonical pathways that were perturbed in HPS‐1 uEVs. Eight canonical pathways exhibited a positive IPA

Inhibition was predicted for the following pathways or functions: Liver X Receptor/Retinoid X Receptor (LXR/RXR) activation (which is involved in the regulation of lipid metabolism, inflammation, and reverse cholesterol transport (RCT), inhibition of which may lead to accumulation of lipids/cholesterol), [

,

These results suggest that in the HPS‐1 kidney, there are disruptions in important pathways involving lipid metabolism as well as lysosome and mitochondrial function that can be detected before significant clinical findings/abnormal test results (e.g., urine creatinine clearance) are obvious/apparent.

ApoA1 is one of the 11 LXR/RXR pathway proteins decreased in HPS‐1 uEVs by both MS and Western blotting. Therefore, we evaluated serum ApoA1 as a potential biomarker of early kidney dysfunction in HPS‐1 patients. ApoA1 functions in cholesterol efflux in macrophages and other cells via ABC transporters [
82
]. In kidneys, ApoA1 crosses the glomerular filtration barrier and is taken up by the cubilin‐megalin‐amnionless complex in the proximal tubule. ApoA1 can be filtered, endocytosed, and then degraded or salvaged by the proximal tubule; [
82
] salvaged ApoA1 enters the interstitium, and it is either delivered back to the circulation or excreted in the urine [
83
,
84

82
,
85

Discussion
HPS‐1 is a rare genetic disorder characterized by defects in the BLOC‐3 complex, resulting in a range of clinical manifestations including oculocutaneous albinism, a bleeding diathesis, lysosomal ceroid lipofuscin accumulation, granulomatous colitis, and pulmonary fibrosis [
3
,
4
,
5
]. While the pulmonary manifestations of HPS have been extensively studied, the renal involvement and its significance in HPS‐1 remains poorly understood. This study aimed to shed light on the molecular level changes that occur in HPS‐1 kidneys by investigating the protein composition of uEVs from HPS‐1 16‐bp duplication patients compared with normal individuals.
We overcame the technical hurdle of THP contamination in the 200 000
g
pellets by using TCEP, which can denature the zona pellucida domains in THP [
36
] and render it soluble for removal by high speed centrifugation. A previous study used TCEP to remove THP from a mixed population of uEVs in the low‐speed centrifugation pellet (21 130
g
) [
91
]. Our TEM results indicate that uEVs are intact, while Western blotting and mass spectrometry (MS) showed that they retained known markers of the multi‐vesicular body/endosomal pathways. Thus, our approach enables reproducible isolation of uEVs and subsequent comparative proteomics without the interference from THP, which has confounded research efforts in the past.
Proteomics and Western blotting analysis of uEVs from HPS‐1 patients and normal control individuals identified proteins encompassing a wide range of cellular localizations and functions, confirming the utility and scope of our approach. We identified early cellular level protein changes in HPS‐1 uEVs that support both trafficking and organelle dysfunction in kidney epithelial cells. Specifically, several proteins that function in a calcium‐dependent manner were increased in HPS‐1 uEVs; RAB11a, Villin‐1 and TRPV5. RAB proteins are small guanosine triphosphatases that regulate protein transport, vesicle budding, membrane fusion, and interactions with the cytoskeleton. RAB11 regulates exocytic and recycling processes, directing proteins and membranes towards the cell surface. It is localized to apical recycling endosomes, influences “slow recycling” of transferrin, [
92
] and promotes docking and fusion of multivesicular bodies (MVBs) in a Ca
2+
‐dependent manner [
93
]. Villin is a tissue‐specific calcium‐dependent f‐actin bundling and severing protein in enterocytes and kidney proximal tubule cells that regulates actin dynamics, cell morphology, epithelial‐to‐mesenchymal transition, cell migration and cell survival [
94
]. TRPV5 (transient receptor potential vanilloid protein) is involved in transcellular Ca
2+
transport in the distal convoluted tubule/connecting tubule. It is located on the apical plasma membrane and recycles from the intracellular (recycling) endosomes in a RAB11‐dependent manner [
95
]. It is interesting to consider that if RAB11 recycling/endosomes are affected in HPS1 kidney epithelial cells, that calcium signaling might be affected in HPS1 disease development and progression.
In contrast, several proteins involved in retrograde trafficking, fibrosis, and lipid metabolism and/or lysosomal function were decreased in HPS‐1 uEVs. RAB7L/29 mediates bacterial infection, maintains the integrity of the trans‐Golgi network (TGN) and, along with LRRK2, plays a role in the retrograde trafficking of mannose 6‐phosphate (M6P) receptors from recycling and late endosomes to the TGN [
72
,
96
]. Galectin‐3 is a β‐galactoside–binding lectin that is recruited to damaged lysosomes for membrane repair, removal, and replacement, and mediates the upregulation of TRPV5 cell surface abundance [
97
]. HPS‐1 lung fibroblasts defective in BLOC‐3 had abnormal plasma membrane galectin‐3 expression, reflecting mis‐trafficking of galectin‐3 [
98
]. β‐galactosidase is an apical proximal tubular protein that is trafficked to the lysosome with Cathepsin A (PPCA) and neuraminidase I (NEU1). β‐galactosidase binds Galectin‐3 and is important in trans‐epithelial Ca
2+
transport through modification of the N‐glycan on TRPV5 [
99
]. Interestingly,
Hps1 ep
(pale ear) mice showed lysosomal accumulation but markedly reduced urinary excretion of β‐galactosidase and β‐glucuronidase [
11
]. LIMP2 is a heavily N‐glycosylated transmembrane protein in the CD36 family, whose members are important regulators of lipid metabolism and innate immunity. LIMP2 binds β‐glucoronidase (βGC) and βGC/LIMP2 is transported to the lysosome by an M6PR‐independent pathway [
100
]. LIMP2 also recycles lysosomal low‐density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR)‐derived cholesterol (and possibly other lipids) alongside the NPC1 pathway. LIMP2 depletion leads to upregulation of cholesterol biosynthesis and LDLR expression [
101
]. Napsin‐A is expressed in intra‐alveolar macrophages, the cytoplasm of type II pneumocytes, and kidney proximal and convoluted tubules, where it is believed to function as a lysosomal protease [
102
,
103
,
104
]. Physiologically, Napsin A is involved in the maturation of prosurfactant protein B in type II pneumocytes, potentially in phagocytosis by macrophages, and lysosomal protein catabolism in renal cells [
104
]. Napsin A is regulated by thyroid transcription factor 1 (TTF1), a diagnostic marker in lung cancer and it promotes TGF‐ß induced cell proliferation in lung adenocarcinoma cells [
104
].
IPA analysis of our proteomics data revealed inhibition of several canonical pathways involved in lipid metabolism as well as lysosome and mitochondrial function in HPS‐1 uEVs. To test whether HPS‐1 deficiency might produce mitochondrial abnormalities or lipid/lipofuscin accumulation in HPS‐1 kidney proximal tubule cells, we generated a novel
HPS1
knockout mouse (
Hps1
1bpins‐mal
) and compared ultrastructural and histopathological findings to those of the WT mouse. Most of the findings we observed were in the proximal tubules, which have a significant role in filtration and carry unique characteristics such as high energy, low pH, and increased mitochondrial abundance compared with other segments of the nephron [
105
]. Our novel
Hps1
1bpins‐mal
mice revealed the presence of ceroid‐like deposits (Figs
6
and
7
) which resemble the accumulation ceroid lipofuscin observed in lysosomes of HPS‐1 alveolar macrophages [
4
,
6
] and to certain extent the histopathological findings of Gordillo
et al
. [
8
] in which a child homozygous for the 16 bp founder duplication in
HPS1
demonstrated kidney findings of diffuse tubulopathy with hypertrophic tubular epithelial cells, cytoplasmic vacuolization, and ceroid‐like cytoplasmic accumulations. Accumulation of lipids in kidney tissue has been observed in various animal models of kidney disease, including metabolic syndrome, diabetic nephropathy, aging nephrosclerosis and acute and chronic progressive kidney injury [
82
]. The mitochondrial abnormalities observed in our
Hps1
1bpins‐mal
mice (Fig.
6
) are consistent with previous studies showing mitochondrial dysfunction in HPS‐2 lungs, [
106
]
HPS1
knockout HAP1 cells, [
107
] and HPS‐1 patient‐specific alveolar organoid‐derived epithelial cells, [
108
] as well as the presence of swollen mitochondria with disorganized cristae in IPF lungs [
109
], and mitochondrial fragmentation and perinuclear clustering of mitochondria in MLE15 CRISPR Cas9‐generated
HPS1
knockout cells [
110
].
Serum ApoA1 levels may indicate cellular level dysfunction in polarized renal epithelial cells that is apparent earlier than traditional clinical tests. We observed a moderate, positive Pearson correlation between serum ApoA1 and eGFR in our 40‐patient cohort, which is consistent with other studies linking increased serum ApoA1 to improved eGFR in chronic kidney disease (CKD), [
37
,
111
] and suggests early dysfunction of the glomerular filtration barrier or impaired tubular reabsorption capacity. Whether serum ApoA1 levels are causative of, or responsive to, the HPS‐1 kidney phenotype is still under investigation. In addition, it will also be important to understand the role of ApoA1 in polarized alveolar type II epithelial cells, which are considered the prime driver of pulmonary fibrosis in HPS‐1.
The presence of BLOC complex proteins in uEV samples indicates BLOC proteins are trafficked in uEVs and highlights their potential involvement in EV formation and secretion as well as in HPS‐1‐related renal manifestations. Western blotting demonstrated that normal uEVs express HPS proteins; however, HPS‐1 uEVs lack HPS1 protein and have reduced HPS4 protein, which suggests that uEV proteomics or Western blotting can be used as an outcome measure for interventions that correct the basic defect in HPS‐1. Specifically, the presence of HPS1 protein in uEVs of an HPS‐1 patient or animal after systemic gene therapy would demonstrate efficacy of treatment delivery.
We are aware there are limitations to this study since HPS is a rare disease, with a general prevalence of 1–9/1 000 000 worldwide (the HPS‐1 16‐bp duplication mutation affects approximately 1/1800 individuals in NW Puerto Rico). As such, our uEV proteomics experiments focused initially on a small number of patients [
5
] with the most prevalent HPS‐1 16‐bp duplication mutation to examine specific proteins or pathways affecting endosomal trafficking; including other HPS subtypes in the analysis may have produced confounding results. Although this study does not include functional assays, we have demonstrated that our uEV isolation and proteomics workflow provides insights into the protein landscape associated with HPS‐1‐related renal pathologies; uEVs can be mined for proteins or canonical pathways altered in the endosomal protein trafficking system of HPS‐1 kidneys and they have the potential to inform us about general pathways affected in HPS‐1. Our combined proteomics, ELISA, and mouse data indicate a potential link between the dysregulation of key pathways involved in lipid metabolism as well as lysosome and mitochondrial function in HPS‐1 renal epithelial cells. These findings lay the foundations for future investigations into the pathophysiology and functional implications of the HPS‐1 kidney disorder, as well as the possible use of uEV protein characterization as an outcome measure for therapeutic interventions.
