Recent years have been marked by the development of single-cell sequencing systems for both qualitative and quantitative characterization of cellular composition across tissues.
1
These technologies provide important insights about cellular differentiation
2
and intercellular communication
3
across many different organs and diseases. However, these tools do not take into account the spatial organization of cells, which is crucial to understanding the pathophysiology of living organisms.
In multicellular organisms, integrating the precise physical locations of cells in relation to one another is fundamental to understanding interactive biological networks and deciphering biological phenomena. Three classic examples of the significance of spatial dimensions in biology are embryonic development, tumor pathology, and mechanotransduction. During embryonic development, molecular gradients of morphogens will lead to region-specific transcriptional responses and cell fates.
4
In tumor pathology, the integration of the composition and spatial organization of the tumor microenvironment is crucial to understanding cancer pathophysiology.
5
Finally, in a tissue, each cell or group of cells perceives different mechanical stimuli, depending solely on their spatial location, and will adapt their phenotype (differentiation, proliferation) according to specific molecular pathways, such as the Hippo–Yes-associated protein and transcriptional coactivator with PDZ-binding motif (YAP/TAZ) pathway.
6
,
7
For each individual cell, the spatial molecular network is highly important and dictates the cell's phenotype (eg, cell polarity).
8
The recent arrival of high-resolution spatial omics technologies is generating a veritable revolution in life sciences, enabling biomolecules to be measured in their native spatial context and allowing the integration of morphology and molecular mechanisms of health and diseases states.
9

,

,

Indeed, these technologies have the potential to meet one of the historic challenges of biomedical research, which is to be able to combine, in a single step, a global and unbiased measure of biomolecules together with their direct spatial localization.

Overall, these new methodologic approaches will provide answers to two fundamental questions regarding tissue morphology. First, they will identify the precise spatial cellular composition of each tissue, and second, they will visually decipher in space the molecular mechanisms behind tissue morphology and lesions. The aim of this review is to describe and classify the main ST technologies currently available, and to illustrate their scientific and potential medical interest in the context of kidney disease. Finally, we will discuss the perspectives and challenges of these technologies in the context of biomedical research and their possible use in clinical practice. The global aim of this review is to provide insights into how to understand, identify the benefits of, and perhaps eventually take ownership of these rapidly growing new ST technologies.
Overview and Classification of ST Technologies
A large number of ST technologies are currently available, differing primarily in spatial resolution and gene detection depth. The regular improvement of previous ST technologies, coupled with the frequent arrival of new methods, makes it increasingly difficult to produce an up-to-date and accurate description of all these technologies. In addition, the growing complexity of the molecular methods used and, in some cases, their technological overlap, also makes them increasingly difficult to compare and classify accurately. Many reviews propose a binary classification into two main categories: those based on next-generation sequencing for gene detection and those based on imaging detection. Nevertheless, some technologies combine processes from each of these two categories, using hybridization and microscopy methods specific to imaging-based technologies, together with sequencing procedures specific to sequencing-based technologies. These include the GeoMx digital spatial profiling (DSP) technology

Extraction-Based Technologies
As their name suggests, EBTs are based on the extraction of RNA from tissues, which is both their strength and their limitation. On the one hand, they enable high-throughput next-generation sequencing to reach the scale of the entire transcriptome, and to address certain biological questions in an unbiased manner. Moreover, these technologies are potentially able to give insights on genomic variation and splicing variants (of particular importance in cancer research) as they provide the direct sequence of tissue RNA. On the other hand, these technologies have more limited spatial resolution than NEBTs. In addition, this approach requires relatively intact mRNA and, therefore, their usefulness may be limited for samples with degraded RNA or formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissues. Finally, because of the RNA capture process, these technologies may alter the sample, complicating efforts to perform complementary omics studies targeting molecules other than RNA, such as proteins or metabolites, on the same tissue slice.
EBTs can then be divided into three groups: i)- macroscopic- or microscopic-guided region of interest (ROI) selection, followed by bulk or single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNAseq); ii)- spatial barcoding technologies; and iii)- computational-based technologies, at the crossroads of the two major ST categories (EBTs and NEBTs).
ROI- and Image Guiding–Based Technologies
This first group corresponds to the early days of EBTs, with, for example, the possibility of sequencing serial adjacent sections of the same sample and reconstructing RNA expression maps on a longitudinal axis (tomo-seq).

Spatial Barcoding-Based Technologies
Spatial barcoding technologies rely on the construction of spatially indexed particles, in which each particle contains a specific barcoded DNA that marks a unique location. Tissue RNAs are then locally extracted by each unique particle and sequenced, allowing researchers to map a specific localization of all the RNAs from a single particle. Mostly, primers with poly(T) sequences are used to capture mRNAs from tissues on each single particle (
Figure 2
). RT-based Visium,
32
Slide-seq,
33
,
34
Stereo-seq,
35
Seq-Scope,
36
and DBiTseq
37
are the main technologies of this spatial barcoding group. All these technologies differ principally in the type and size of these particles or spots, which in turn determine the spatial resolution and sequencing depth of each technology. The RT-based Visium technology has been adapted from the pioneering method in this category described by Stảhl et al
32
in 2016. It enables unbiased analysis of tissues at the whole transcriptomic level scale by spatial capture of polyadenylated RNAs on a spatially barcoded microarray slide, followed by sequencing in which each captured RNA could be mapped back to its original spot. The resolution of RT-based Visium is limited to the spot size: 55 μm of diameter with 100 μm center-to-center distance, leading to the absence of a single-cell resolution. Vickovic et al
38
recently improved their barcoding technology with high-definition spatial transcriptomics, recovering several hundred thousand transcript coupled spatial barcodes at 2-μm resolution. Slide-Seq, similarly to RT-based Visium, uses barcoded microbeads, allowing a near cellular resolution (10 μm) with high sensitivity (>1000 transcripts per beads).
33
,
34
Stereo-seq combines DNA nanoball patterned arrays and tissue RNA capture, allowing researchers to reach cellular resolution on a large field of view.
35
Seq-Scope barcoding is based on a solid-phase amplification of randomly barcoded single-molecule oligonucleotides using an Illumina (San Diego, CA) flow cell, achieving submicron spatial resolution.
36
Finally, DBiTseq barcoding is based on a microfluidic method to deliver barcodes to the surface of a tissue slide, allowing spatial RNA detection with 10-μm resolution.
37

Computational-Based Technologies
This group is heterogeneous and lies at the boundary between the two main technologies (EBTs and NEBTs). These methods use artificial intelligence and computational analysis to compensate for the lack of spatial resolution of certain sequencing-based technologies, while retaining the advantage of great sequencing depth. Early techniques involved combining scRNAseq data with reference RNA atlases corresponding to spatial expression mapping of certain target RNAs based on
in situ
hybridization detections.
39
,
40
NovoSpaRc has helped to improve these technologies by spatially reconstructing tissues based on the hypothesis that cells in physical proximity share similar gene expression profiles.
41
This method constructs the spatial positioning
de novo
without the reliance on a reference RNA atlas. More recently, a computational framework based on deep data fusion allowed researchers to integrate spatial gene expression data with a high-resolution histologic image from the same tissue section to predict spatial gene expression and infer higher-resolution expression maps.
42
,
43
These new computational analyses illustrate both the importance of generating high-quality sharable databases and the extent to which artificial intelligence enables certain technologies to be combined to increase their respective capacity.
Non–Extraction-Based Technologies
Alongside EBTs, a host of NEBTs have been developed for ST. The NEBT category is based on the absence of direct RNA capture and often provides a high spatial resolution, albeit with an often more limited number of RNA targets. In addition, the absence of an RNA capture step allows their use on samples with potentially degraded RNA and often on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissues. The maintenance of sample structure and composition may also enable its re-use for further downstream omics studies. The vast majority of these technologies involve an initial hybridization step with a specific probe set, which provides comprehensive and specific coverage of the known transcriptome. However, these methods often fail to provide information on the actual sequence of tissue RNAs and limit the use of a probe set to a single species in particular. Finally, the main theoretical limitation of
in situ
analysis is the spatial molecular crowding that might potentially limit their performance mainly in terms of sequencing depth.
NEBTs could also be divided into two major groups:
in situ
–based technologies, also referred as imaging-based technologies; and
ex situ
–based technologies.
In Situ
– or Imaging-Based Technologies
In this major group, RNAs are labeled directly within the tissue with fluorescent probes, which are then imaged using fluorescent microscopy.

Another inherent limitation of these imaging-based technologies is optical crowding, which can lead to failure to detect weakly expressed RNA in certain regions of high RNA density.

Ex Situ
–Based Technologies
This group lies at the frontier between sequencing- and imaging-based technologies. Like imaging techniques, these ST methods use a first step of targeted
in situ
hybridization; and like sequencing-based methods, they also use next-generation sequencing for probe or RNA identification. The main advantage of these technologies is that they eliminate the need to capture RNA from tissues, enabling them to be used on samples with degraded RNA and on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissues. These ST methods can be divided into two categories: first, those based on a predefined tissue-area ROI; and second, those based on the entire sample (without predefined ROI), which make secondary use of spatial barcoding-based EBTs.
This first group, based on a predefined ROI, includes GeoMx DSP
23
,
24
and Light-Seq
25
technologies. GeoMx DSP platform allows user-defined ROI of 20 to 300 cells with transcriptome profiling using photocleavable barcoded probes.
23
,
24
After an initial
in situ
targeted hybridization step, these photocleavable barcoded probes are then selectively removed from the sample and sequenced, enabling RNA identification and location within the predetermined ROI. More recently, Light-Seq, by combining technologies from the
in situ
sequencing methods, allows a higher spatial resolution and provides information on the actual sequence of the targeted RNA. Light-Seq uses light-directed spatial barcoding, with photocross-linking of DNA barcodes onto cDNAs in a selected ROI combined with transcript sequencing.
25
The second group (without a predefined ROI) uses a principle of probe-based capture thanks to spatial barcoding-based EBTs. This technology uses ligated probes that bind to the mRNA target site, which are then captured using the same Poly-dT tail on spatial barcode oligos and prepared into a library for sequencing. Unlike spatial barcoding-based EBTs, these methods provide no information on RNA sequence, and require the design of a specific probe panel that can only be used on samples of the same species. This group is mainly composed of the probe-based capture Visium technology using the same methods as the RT-based Visium technology
32
and the Visum HD technology using high-definition spatial transcriptomics technology.

Computational Analysis of ST Data
A major challenges facing ST is the computational analysis required for data interpretation and visualization. In fact, as a result of the technological development of ST methods, a large number of computational pipelines are currently available and are constantly being updated.
58
These tools are often difficult to evaluate and apply, because of the often high complexity of the underlying statistical and mathematical algorithms together with their user interface, which requires knowledge of computer coding in languages such as R, Python, or Julia. The aim of this review is not to go into the comparative details of the current computational analysis methods, but to give a brief overview of the analysis main steps, citing some of the available tools. First, groups such as 10x Genomics or Nanostring provide user-friendly software for basic data analysis and visualization, without the need for computer coding skills. However, it is often necessary to use coding-based software to customize and deepen data analysis and visualization. The main integrated software packages include the following: Seurat
59
and Giotto
60
(R language) or Scanpy
61
and Squidpy
62
(Python language). These integrated tools cover most of the major stages for the analysis and visualization of ST data sets. Recently, our group developed a new open-source computational pipeline written in Julia, named CellScopes, for the rapid analysis, multi-omic integration, and visualization of spatially resolved transcriptomic data sets.
63
Generally speaking, ST analysis can be divided into eight main steps, as follows.
i)
Preprocessing: This step involves gene and/or cell/spot filtering based on a specific threshold for the data set, to optimize the overall quality of the analysis by limiting noise. Filtering can be used, for example, to eliminate cells with a low count or a high proportion of mitochondrial RNA, or to filter out certain genes whose expression is limited to a small number of cells.
58
ii)
Cellular segmentation: This step is crucial for imaging-based technologies, as it involves determining the cellular boundary that enables each RNA to be attributed to a particular cell. There are several existing algorithms for cellular segmentation, like Cellpose
64
or Baysor.
65
Cellpose, like other imaged-based algorithms, globally approximates cell positions and boundary only using cell morphology, mostly on nuclei stains, whereas Baysor optimizes cell boundaries by taking into account not only cell morphology but also the joint probability of transcriptional composition.
65
Other technologies use multimodal cell segmentation based on the combination of different morphologic markers: boundary staining, interior staining, and nuclear expansion, such as 10x Genomics' multimodal cell segmentation.
66
Overall, cell segmentation is essential to produce an accurate cell-feature matrix by assigning each transcript to a specific cell. This is a challenging step in its realization and qualitative evaluation, and constitutes one of the main limitations of imaging-based methods because misassigned transcripts can lead to incorrect clustering, cell identification, and downstream analysis.
iii)
Gene expression normalization and integration: The aim of gene normalization is to eliminate undesirable technical artifacts that may induce spot-to-spot variations and high dropout rates, which can impact downstream analyses. Several normalization strategies have been generated, mostly inspired by bulk and scRNAseq studies.
58
Similarly, integration is also an essential step when dealing with multiple samples and experiments, to limit batch effect likely to induce nonbiological variations in high-throughput experiments.
67
There are several existing algorithms for data integration, like Harmony,
68
scVI,
69
or GraphST.
70
iv)
Clustering and dimensional reduction: Clustering and dimensional reduction for cluster visualization are critical for obtaining accurate cell type identification. Agglomerative clustering methods, such as hierarchical agglomerative clustering, are the most popular for ST data. These methods iteratively aggregate data points into clusters without assumptions on cluster size or shape. Dimensionality reduction methods can then allow researchers to visualize and project the identified clusters in a two-dimensional expression space. Uniform manifold approximation and projection and t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding are two nonlinear reduction algorithms widely used in single-cell and ST analysis.
58
v)
Cell type identification, spatial libraires, and deconvolution: This step provides biological information for each cluster, assigning it to a specific cell subtype. It is based on the identification of cell type–specific genes or marker genes, which are then compared with reference data to assign each cluster to a particular cell subtype. Cell mapping algorithms in ST are thus mainly derived from those used in scRNAseq. This is also where integration with scRNAseq data comes in, acting as a reference atlas of tissue cell subtypes.
71
This step also allows spot deconvolution when the ST measurement technology has a multicellular resolution, such as Visium technology. Again, spot deconvolution tools are mostly derived by analogy with the deconvolution allowed by scRNAseq data on bulk RNAseq data. SPOTlight,
72
especially designed to deconvolute Visium ST, has notably been applied to kidney study.
73
In addition, SpatialDecon
74
is also a recent and flexible tool for spot deconvolution and mapping cell types in ST studies. Finaly, integration of ST data with scRNAseq (especially for imaging-based technologies with a predefined and limited number of target genes) also allows tissue to be spatially resolved at single-cell resolution with whole-transcriptome coverage.
71
There are, however, two main issues to raise. The first concerns NEBTs, particularly imaging-based technologies, which requires a predefined limited number of target genes, which might not be enough to confidentially separate all cell types. In addition, these manually predefined genes may induce biases in cell type taxonomy within the tissue. The second main limitation is inherent to deconvolution algorithms assuming that all cell types from the scRNAseq data are present in the spatial data. This assumption is questionable in the field of ST as samples usually correspond to focal anatomic sections of an organ, potentially inducing sampling bias, unlike scRNAseq or bulk RNAseq analysis, where the amount of tissue is often much higher. Furthermore, the more limited sequencing depth of ST technologies compared to with scRNAseq or bulk RNAseq and the use of a restricted probe panel for imaging-based technologies often do not allow the identification of certain rare cell subtypes.
22
,
58
,
71
Of interest, by using a deep generative model that incorporates archetypal analysis and any known cell type mark, a recent computational toolbox, named Starfysh, was developed to characterize known or new tissue-specific cell states without a single-cell reference.
75
Moreover, Starfysh integrates histology, which improves cell density estimation and neighborhood characterization in complex tissues.
vi)
Spatial visualization of genes, cells, and domains: This step corresponds to the spatial visualization of gene expression, the different cell subtypes previously identified, or a particular spatial domain/tissue region. It is at this stage that ST really comes into its own, enabling detection of early transcriptional variations before morphologic changes in tissues, or identification of the molecular mechanisms of a lesion observed on histology. At the cellular level, this step enables high-precision identification and mapping of all the cell subtypes responsible for tissue morphology, and the generation of reference atlases for each tissue. Of interest, this analysis step also allows researchers to decipher which genes and/or cells have an organized, non-stochastic spatial distribution, further enabling them to determine potential new spatial domains even beyond morphologic changes.
58
Most of the aforementioned integrated software packages (Seurat,
59
Giotto,
60
Scanpy,
61
Squidpy
62
) enable these visualizations. Recently, the BSP (big-small patch) model allows researchers to identify spatially variable genes from two- or three-dimensional ST data by calculating the variances of the gene expression across all ST spots under different scales.
76
At the cellular level, single-cell spatial position associated coembeddings (scSpace) is an integrative method that uses ST data as a spatial reference to reconstruct the pseudospace of scRNAseq data. As such, this method could be likened to the reverse of ST deconvolution, where scRNAseq data sets are used to optimize cell subtype identification in an ST spot.
77
vii)
Cell interaction and communication analysis: This step enables the determination of the spatial organization of different cells and the nature of their communication at the molecular level. First, some software packages can be used to build a spatial neighborhood network to identify cell types in close spatial proximity.
58
Second, these cell communication analyses are largely inherited from scRNAseq's own algorithms based on ligand-receptor analyses.
3
There are also several tools available for this type of ligand-receptor analysis, such as CellPhone DB,
78
SpaOTSc,
79
or Giotto.
60
However, cells can interact in different ways, and most of these models ignore the spatial proximity of cells to define spatially restricted groups of cells (niche). New methods, such as the node-centric expression models based on graph neural networks, help to uncover niche composition and intercellular communication by capturing statistical dependencies between cells and gene expression profiles in space.
80
In addition, a new tool particularly suited to ST data has been developed, named COMMOT.
81
This analysis method not only takes into account the competition between different ligand and receptor species but also the spatial distances between cells. Furthermore, this tool allows researchers to infer spatial signaling directionality and genes regulated by signaling using machine learning models.
81
viii)
Pathway, transcription factor, and lineage analysis: Finally, depending on the sequencing depth, functional molecular analyses derived from bulk RNAseq and scRNAseq studies may be performed, such as pathway enrichment analysis, transcription factor analysis, or lineage differentiation analysis.
2
,

Importantly, the kidney is an organ that lends itself particularly well to the use of ST technologies, as it possesses an important morphologic heterogeneity in both physiological and pathologic conditions. In fact, because of its multiple functions, the kidney is made up of various anatomic segments with highly different structural, cellular, and molecular properties. In addition, as many kidney diseases preferentially affect a limited segment of the kidney (glomerulus, tubule, vessels, or renal interstitium), this morphologic variation is particularly present in pathologic conditions. Thus, to determine the cellular and molecular mechanisms responsible for kidney injury, it is essential to have precise information on the topography of biological events; otherwise, these biological events may be diluted or confused. Another aspect of heterogeneity is the temporal dimension, with the presence of time-dependent lesions, which in the kidney in particular may give insight on the chronology of cellular and molecular mechanisms involved in lesion development.

In nephrology, ST has enabled advances in various fields, such as acute kidney injury (AKI), AKI–to–chronic kidney disease (CKD) transition, CKD, diabetic nephropathy, specific glomerulopathies, lithiasis, kidney development, or kidney transplantation (
Table 2
). These technologies have also enabled researchers to build cellular and molecular atlases, which can serve as benchmarks for numerous studies.

Regarding AKI, Janosevic et al
87
performed a spatial and temporal analysis of murine endotoxemia to decipher the orchestrated cellular and molecular responses of the kidney. By combining scRNAseq and ST, Ferreira et al
83
uncovered mechanisms driving immune cell infiltration in a specific anatomic region of two murine AKI models: ischemia/reperfusion (IR) injury and cecal ligation puncture. Dixon et al
73
published a spatial and temporal atlas of AKI using bilateral IR injury in a female mouse model. In particular, Dixon et al
73
were among the first to develop an interactive online tool for gene exploration, which is critical for omics data and promotes access to these new technologies. More recently, Chu et al
84
showed that OTU deubiquitinase 5 (OTUD5) autophagy destabilizes glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), promoting the development of ferroptosis-dependent renal lesions in mouse models of IR injury.
Regarding AKI-to-CKD transition, Cheung et al
88
used scRNAseq and ST (Visium) to identify seven major subpopulations of kidney-resident macrophages, with distinct specialized functions and spatial organization. Following ischemic injury, these macrophage subpopulations exhibited an altered transcriptomic program and spatial distribution 28 days after injury, possibly reflecting chronic immune dysregulation favoring the transition from AKI to CKD.
88
By combining ST and single-cell combinatorial indexing RNA sequencing on IR injury and unilateral ureteral obstruction mouse models, Li et al
89
were able to identify diverse injury states of proximal tubular cells, including one with a transiently activated lipid metabolism at early phase of kidney injury. Polonski et al
90
also examined IR injury in the mouse kidney and uncovered spatially dependent gene expression patterns in distinct cellular microenvironments, including one resembling early tertiary lymphoid structures. Regarding CKD pathophysiology, Kayhan et al
91
recently deciphered the role of transforming growth factor-β signaling on mitochondrial homeostasis in the proximal tubule during CKD. In particular, they revealed that mice carrying proximal tubule deletion of transforming growth factor beta receptor 2 (Tgfbr2) have increased mitochondrial injury and exacerbated type 1 helper T cell immune response in the aristolochic acid model of CKD.
91
Regarding diabetic nephropathy and other glomerular diseases, Marshall et al
96
were able to detect changes in the cellular organization of the kidney filter and blood flow–regulating apparatus in a mouse model of diabetic kidney disease. By using the GeoMx DSP technology, Smith et al
99
profiled spatial gene expression in glomeruli with collapsing lesions isolated from HIV- or severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2)–infected patients. Similarly, Clair et al
98
have also used GeoMx technology to better characterize the transcriptional signatures of human glomeruli in three other glomerular diseases: Alport syndrome, focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, and membranous nephropathy. Richoz et al
101
applied ST technologies to a mouse model of lupus nephritis and identified distinct pathogenic roles for resident and monocyte-derived macrophages, with resident macrophages orchestrating leukocyte recruitment, whereas monocyte-derived ones were taking up and presenting immune complex antigens. More recently, by applying the CosMx technology on kidney samples from eight children with lupus nephritis, Danaher et al
103
generated a spatially resolved, single-cell resolution atlas of childhood-onset lupus nephritis.
Regarding stone disease, Canela et al
114
were able to describe the spatial molecular pathways characterized by immune activation, oxidative stress, and extracellular matrix remodeling, contributing to stone-mediated injury in the human kidney. Regarding kidney development, Sanchez-Ferras et al
106
identified transcription factor AP-2a/b (Tfap2a/b) and GATA-binding factor 3 (Gata3) transcription factors as key coordinators of progenitor cell progression at the origin of the renal collecting duct and associated urinary tract development.
Finally, regarding kidney transplantation Salem et al
108
were able to detect variability in transcriptional signatures across different areas of a T-cell–mediated rejection in a human biopsy. Furthermore Loupy et al
109
recently studied the spatial immune molecular response after pig-to-human kidney xenotransplantation, providing insights to improve xenotransplantation results in patients.
In addition to the study of specific kidney diseases, ST has also enabled the generation of a comprehensive atlas of healthy and injured human kidney.
14
Through the Kidney Precision Medicine Project, Lake et al
14
combined multiple single-cell and two ST technologies (Visium and SLIDE-Seq2) to study 45 healthy control and 48 diseased kidneys. This large-scale study allowed the identification of 51 main kidney cell types, including novel cell populations, and 28 kidney cellular states representing injury or disease. Notably, the ST technologies used in this study allowed researchers to precisely localize these cell populations and establish niches of altered cell states together with their neighboring immune cell components.
14
More recently, Abedini et al
97
also generated high-quality data sets for 81 human kidneys (healthy, diabetic, and hypertensive diseased), including scRNAseq and single nucleus RNAseq, single nucleus assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing (snATACseq) spot level (Visium), and single-cell resolution (CosMx) spatial-RNA expression. This study provides a detailed and comprehensive spatial and cellular characterization of the fibrotic microenvironment in diseased kidneys. More importantly, this work demonstrates that the fibrotic microenvironment spatial gene signature offers an improved prognosis prediction compared with traditional histopathologic analysis, paving the way for the clinical utility of spatial transcriptomics.

Perspectives and Challenges of ST
ST as Part of Multi-Omics Integration
The first challenge is of integrating ST with other omics technologies with a spatial component to provide broader and more comprehensive molecular information on tissue characterization.
116
Indeed, in-depth understanding of biological events cannot be restricted to gene expression, but must involve exploration from gene to metabolite, via protein expression and signaling. Some spatial technologies offer multi-omics exploration by combining different molecular detection modalities, such as RNA and proteins.
116
Alternatively, serial sections of a sample can be taken to independently perform detailed omics explorations. NEBTs may be particularly attractive as most of them preserve the sample in its entirety, potentially enabling successive omics explorations to be performed on the same slide.
Starting with genomic exploration, slide-DNA–sequencing technology enables the spatial mapping of genomic sequences together with gene expression profiles, allowing the molecular characterization of different tumor clones within a tissue.
117
In addition to RNA detection, gene expression is also explored through two major components: the detection of transcriptional variants resulting from alternative splicing and epigenetic characterization. First, spatial detection of splicing variants can be achieved by targeting exon-exon junctions or by using long-read sequencing technologies with, for instance, the spatial isoform transcriptomics technology.
118
Second, spatial epigenetic information can be achieved through two main approaches: spatially resolved chromatin accessibility profiling or spatially resolved epigenomic profiling of tissue sections. By combining
in situ
transposon 5 transposase (Tn5) transposition chemistry and microfluidic deterministic barcoding, spatial-ATAC–sequencing technology can provide spatially resolved chromatin accessibility profiling.
119
In addition, Epigenomic MERFISH enables spatially resolved single-cell epigenomic profiling by using
in situ
tagmentation and transcription, followed by multiplexed imaging.
120
After genetic and transcriptional explorations come spatially resolved protein and metabolite studies. These approaches can be broadly separated into two main methods: the first approach is based on a list of predefined targets named targeted based, and the second approach is unbiased and global, named untargeted based.
121
Targeted-based technologies mostly rely on
in situ
antibody-mediated protein detection. The technologies available differ principally in the methods used to reveal the different antibodies, which can be based on the following: multiple or iterative detection of antibodies coupled to fluorescent dyes (Opal multiplex technology),
122
sequential immunofluorescence with oligo-barcoded reporter antibodies (CODEX technology),
123
metal particle mass spectrometry–based technology (Hyperion technology),
124
or probe sequencing–based technology (DBiTseq).
37
Importantly, several ST technologies, such as GeoMx DSP, CoMsx, Merfish, seqFISH or DBiTseq, also offer spatial protein exploration based on these principles of targeted approaches. On the other hand, untargeted-based technologies for protein detection mostly rely on mass spectrometry–based methods, such as matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization imaging mass spectrometry.
125
These mass spectrometry–based technologies enable not only a broad-spectrum study of the spatial expression of tissue proteins, but also provide comprehensive spatial distribution maps of different classes of biomolecules, such as lipids, metabolites, or even drugs.
126
The future of these spatial protein detection technologies may lie in the development of new methods based on
in situ
protein sequencing, providing both protein sequence information together with their spatial detection.
Technical and Practical Challenges of ST
We can distinguish two main groups of challenges for spatial omics technologies: the first is technical and based on the improvement of detection and analysis methods; the second is practical and based on the access and use of these technologies in research and potentially in clinical practice.
This first challenge has already been discussed in the first section of the article by describing the advantages and limitations of each of the different ST modalities, particularly in terms of spatial resolution, sequencing methods, and depth
13
(
Table 1
). Other technological challenges in this category include the following: faster throughputs, shorter workflow time line, better versatility of assayed sample, larger tissue area analysis, three-dimensional reconstruction, better multiplexity with other spatial omics technologies, or greater tissue preservation for other uses. Another technical challenge is what might be called four-dimensional RNA analysis by inferring temporal information on spatial RNA detection, allowing better information about the dynamics of gene expression in tissues.
13
These RNA temporal analysis tools have recently been developed for scRNAseq with Timestamp's technology, a method for inferring the age of individual RNAs in RNAseq data by exploiting RNA editing.
127
However, its spatial integration remains to be developed. Imaging-based technologies with high spatial resolution may provide insights to RNA velocity by using the balance of nuclear versus cytoplasmic RNA counts.
128
Finally, the last technological challenge is the crucial one of improving and diversifying the computer analysis tools required for data visualization and interpretation. In particular, we can mention the improvement of tools for standardizing and integrating data from different experimental batches or omics technologies.
67
The second main challenge facing ST technologies is their practical application. This major challenge can itself be broken down into two main limits: the first concerns access to ST technologies, and the second concerns downstream computational analysis. As far as accessibility is concerned, the main obstacle is financial, as these technologies are highly expensive. The other limitation to access is what we might call the embarrassment of choice of these ST methods, because of the crucial lack of studies comprehensively comparing the use and application of these different ST technologies.
51
,
52
Finally, the challenge of computational analysis is also an important obstacle to the use of ST technologies. As previously mentioned, the computational methods used to interpret ST data are often difficult to evaluate and apply, because of the often-high complexity of the underlying statistical and mathematical algorithms, together with their user interface, often requiring knowledge of computer coding. In addition, there is a lack of comparative studies allowing researchers to propose a standardized and practical analysis of ST data. Finally, it seems important to improve and develop software with a user-friendly interface and interactive visualization of analysis results, as proposed by platforms such as 10x Genomics or Nanostring.
58
Whether ST technologies may ultimately be applied to clinical practice is still an open question. The overall aim will be to determine whether these new technologies can potentially improve the diagnosis and management of patients.
129
One of the main areas of potential clinical use for these ST technologies appears to be cancer pathology, where the spatial-temporal understanding of biological events is fundamental.
129
This includes the tumor microenvironment and the complex relationships between immune cells and cancer, which have led to new therapeutic approaches.
130
In practice, ST has already been applied to the study of numerous cancers, including the most common: breast cancer,
131
lung cancer,
132
colorectal cancer,
133
and prostate cancer.
134
By providing a comprehensive transcriptional atlas of the cellular architecture of each cancer, these studies will certainly help in identifying new biomarkers or new therapeutic strategies for patients.
Aside from cancer pathology, ST will certainly provide pathologists with significant assistance in the analysis of a wide range of tissues and diseases. Indeed, one of the major challenges in this field is to be able to go beyond morphologic analysis under the microscope, and therefore beyond the visual detection of a lesion. In this way, ST can help detect early molecular changes before the visual detection of tissue injury, or identify different cell subtypes despite their similar morphology under the microscope. In addition, with a focus on pathophysiology, ST could also potentially contribute to the identification of the molecular mechanisms responsible for a specific tissue lesion or identify the cell of origin of a lesion or tumor. However, as previously mentioned, not all these ST technologies are applicable for formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded clinical samples.
129
Conclusion
Spatially resolved transcriptomic approaches are new technologies with continuous and rapid development, allowing for increased sensitivity and resolution of spatial gene expression. ST technologies offer the potential of improving the understanding of tissue biology and disease at the molecular level and may also provide meaningful clinical insights. However, the lack of comparative studies of ST technologies, their accessibility, their applicability to certain tissues or diseases, and the complex computerized interpretation of ST data are all limiting factors that need to be improved, particularly with a view to their future use in clinical practice.
