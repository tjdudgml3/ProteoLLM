Carbohydrates are one of the four fundamental organic biomolecules (
1
,
2
). They serve as important energy sources and structural components and play crucial roles in numerous biological processes, including protein folding, cell recognition, and immune responses (
1
–
8
). Carbohydrates in the form of oligosaccharides or polysaccharides, which we will collectively refer to as glycans for simplicity, can be covalently attached to proteins or lipids, forming structures known as glycoconjugates. The remarkable complexity in the type and chemical structure of monosaccharides, branching patterns, and chain lengths result in the structural and functional diversity of glycoconjugates and meanwhile posing substantial challenges to structural determination. The lack of structural information on glycans has in turn hindered a comprehensive understanding toward the function and mechanism of glycans (
9
–
14
).
Recent advancements in single-molecule imaging have enabled direct elucidation of glycan linkages and structures (
15
–
17
). Nevertheless, these methods usually do not capture glycoconjugates in their native states. While structural methods such as cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) can sometimes help resolve glycan structures, they typically only provide moderate-resolution details for glycans near the modification sites or stabilized by sugar-binding proteins such as lectins or specific antibodies (
18
–
21
). Although AI-based structure prediction has revolutionized structural biology of proteins (
22
–
24
), the lack of glycan structural data hampers accurate prediction of glycan structures (
25
,
26
).
In a recent study, we identified numerous arabinoglycans within the intact structure of native mastigonemes from
Chlamydomonas reinhardtii
(
27
). These well-structured glycans are linked to hydroxyproline (Hyp) residues and play a significant role in mastigoneme assembly. This study underscores the importance of employing appropriate methodologies and selecting suitable research targets for accurate structural determination of glycans. Aligned with this line of thinking, we have employed cryo-EM as a discovery tool and proposed a research strategy termed CryoSeek (
28
).
By applying CryoSeek to water samples collected from the Tsinghua Lotus Pond, numerous fibrils with varying lengths and widths were observed in the raw electron micrographs. Despite the heterogeneity and limited number of particles, we successfully determined the structures of two related fibrils, named TLP-1a and TLP-1b (Tsinghua Lotus Pond). Through automatic model building with CryoNet and bioinformatic analysis (
29
), we afforded insights into the assembly mechanism and potential origins of TLP-1a/b. CryoSeek exemplifies the emerging paradigm of structure-guided discovery of uncharacterized bioentities, which may also facilitate in identifying systems suitable for glycan structure analysis. Building upon our success with TLP-1a/b, we continued our efforts to determine the structures of other fibrils.
In this study, we report the cryo-EM analysis of a glycofibril at 3.3 Å resolution. Unexpectedly, the cryo-EM reconstruction reveals that the only protein moiety of this 8-nm wide fibril is a linear peptide chain in the center, made of tetrapeptides. Therefore, we name it TLP-4. The linear chain of tetrapeptide repeats is covered with a thick and well-assembled glycoshell. In each repeat, three glycans are identified, two O-linked to a conserved dihydroxyproline (diHyp) and one to a Ser or Thr. The tightly packed glycans define the overall structure of the fibril. Our present study demonstrates the enormous potential of CryoSeek in identifying suitable model systems for characterizing glycan structures.
Results
Discovery of TLP-4 from the Tsinghua Lotus Pond.
As a continuation of our recent report on CryoSeek (
28

Briefly, water samples were collected from the Tsinghua Lotus Pond, followed by simple filtration and concentration. The concentrated samples were directly subjected to plunge-freezing and data acquisition for cryo-EM imaging (

As there are a variety of fibrils, segmented units of the fibrils, which are regarded as particles for data processing, were automatically picked using the Filament Tracer in cryoSPARC on several distinctive classes of fibrils, each with a similar diameter (
31

The fibril has a diameter of approximately 80 Å, with a helical rise of 12.4 Å between adjacent asymmetric units and a helical twist of 134° (

SI Appendix
, Fig. S1

). At first glance, the 3D reconstruction of this fibril displayed unusual EM densities, with extensive branching patterns that could not be attributed to amino acids. Despite our success in automatic model building for TLP-1a/b using the AI-enabled tool CryoNet (
28
,
29
), the same method failed to generate any structural model for this 3D cryo-EM reconstruction, whose resolution is similar to that of TLP-1a/b. Through manual check of the EM map, we found that the fibril studied here is a simple linear polypeptide coated with dense glycans. As will be elaborated below, the linear peptide chain is made of tetrapeptide repeats. We have thereby named the fibril TLP-4.
The TLP-4 Fibril Is a Glycan-Coated Linear Chain of Tetrapeptide Repeats.
In the TLP-4 fibril, the helical rise of one asymmetric unit is 12.4 Å, roughly corresponding to a linear peptide containing four residues (
Fig. 1
C
). The thin axis and the thick outer shell remind us of our recently reported structure of the
Chlamydomonas
mastigoneme, which features a densely glycosylated type II polyproline (PPII) helix in its shell protein Mst1 and the Mstax-specific Hyp Repeats (MHRs) in the axis protein Mstax (
27
). However, TLP-4 distinguishes itself from the PPII helix or MHRs in at least two aspects.
First and foremost, both the PPII helix and the MHRs are constituents of a large, high-order assembly and the sugar moieties are involved in interactions with proteins. In contrast, TLP-4 exists as an integral and complete entity, having no binding partners. As the protein moiety is just a linear peptide, the structure of the fibril is completely defined and supported by glycans. Second, the glycans attached to the PPII helix and the MHRs are oligosaccharides with the fixed number of sugar residues clearly determined at similar resolutions. In TLP-4, however, the thickness of the resolved glycoshell is already approximately 3 to 4 nm, suggesting the glycans to be polysaccharides. In addition, the resolution of the glycans drops from the center to the periphery, with an even larger amount of sugar moieties unresolved (
Fig. 1
D
). While glycans account for approximately 20 to 25% of the molecular mass of the resolved mastigoneme, those in TLP-4 exceeds 90%. Therefore, TLP-4 affords a unique opportunity to study the structure of glycans.
Two Polysaccharides Simultaneously O-Linked to a 3,4-Dihydroxyproline.
TLP-4 displays the unique feature that two glycan branches, respectively, named as glycan 1 and glycan 2 for the larger and smaller ones, linked to a single residue (
Fig. 2
A
and
B
). N-glycosylation was immediately ruled out for all three glycan densities, as the side chain of Asn would create a larger distance between the sugar and amino acid residues (
32
). O-glycosylation typically occurs on Thr, Ser, Hyp (hydroxyproline), and Hyl (hydroxylysine) (
33
). Among these, 3,4-dihydroxyproline, which was originally identified in diatom species, emerged as the best candidate for di-glycosylation (
34
,
35
). It is noted that the chirality of the map cannot be determined. Therefore, (3S,4S)-3,4-dihydroxyproline and (3R,4R)-3,4-dihydroxyproline, both are naturally occurring (
34
–
37
) and can fit well into the mirrored maps, respectively. Without additional knowledge, we cannot determine the stereoisomer of the dihydroxyproline residue or the directionality of the glycofibril. We will use diHyp to refer to the residue. In either case, its 4-OH and 3-OH groups are bonded to glycans 1 and 2, respectively (
Fig. 2
B
). A third patch of density, named glycan 3, is bonded to the next residue, which could be Thr or Ser based on the relatively small side-chain densities at this locus (
Fig. 2
B
).

As variations in the sequences of tetrapeptide repeats would be averaged out during iterative EM data processing, the relatively well-resolved diHyp, the neighboring Thr/Ser, and the three glycans must be highly conserved across most of the repeats. For the same reason, the identity of the remaining two residues, which may be less conserved, could not be determined owing to the lack of distinctive features. In the subsequent model building, these two residues were roughly assigned based on the averaged densities.
While there is no means to accurately determine the identities of the sugar residues in the three glycans of each tetrapeptide repeat at our current stage of research, we tentatively modeled glycosyl moieties to the better-resolved regions of the local maps in each repeat to gain an idea of how each glycan branches and orients (
Fig. 2
C
). A total of 52 glycosyl moieties were modeled, with 29, 7, 16 units for the three glycans (
Fig. 2
D
).

We started with liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS) analysis of the sample concentrated from 2.5 L pond water. However, only a few proteins were detected, and none of them matched TLP-4. There can be two potential explanations for this outcome. First, the abundance of TLP-4 may fall below the sensitivity threshold of LC–MS. Second, the dense glycoshell of TLP-4 may block protease access to the peptide core during sample preparation, impeding the generation of fragmented sequences that can be detected by LC–MS.
We then attempted to characterize the microorganisms in the Tsinghua Lotus Pond to see whether any of them may contain TLP-4-like proteins. As most of the microorganisms would remain on the filter membrane, which has the pore size of 0.22 μm, during water filtration, we sent the membrane to metagenomic sequencing. In the returned dataset for assembled nonredundant proteins, we identified 38 tetrapeptide repeats with the patterns of PT/Sxx or T/SPxx, with sequence lengths ranging from 20 to 340 residues (
SI Appendix
, Fig. S2
A
). Nevertheless, a BLAST search of these sequence against the NCBI Nonredundant (NR) database resulted in no exact matches (
38
,
39
). The above analysis suggested that the pond organisms that possess the TLP-4-like tetrapeptide repeats might not have been characterized or sequenced. Therefore, the exact protein sequence and the origins of TLP-4 and its analogs remain unclear.
TLP-4 Tetrapeptide-Like Repeats Exist in Many Organisms.
Since the sequences retrieved from the metagenomic analysis did not yield any identical match, we conducted a bioinformatic analysis to identify proteins with TLP-4-like tetrapeptide repeat patterns in the NCBI NR database. A BLAST search of the NR database using 50 tandem repeats of PTAA, PSAA, TPAA, or SPAA (with Ala substituted for the last two residues of the tetrapeptide repeat for simplicity) identified 251 candidates from both eukaryotic and prokaryotic organisms. After deduplication, there remained 187 sequences from 71 organisms or strains (
SI Appendix
, Fig. S2
and
Table S2
). Among these, 50 entries from animals, including insects, crustaceans, and fishes, were largely mucins or mucin-like proteins, which are known for their abundant O-glycosylation (
40
). Given the lack of evidence for the existence of diHyp in animals, we decided to focus on the 137 sequences from 47 microorganisms.
Nearly half of the species in clade Ochrophyta are diatoms.
Nitzschia inconspicua
, an elite commercial diatom species, has the largest number of PT/Sxx-containing proteins (

KAG7367681.1

Structural Role of Glycans.
The glycofibril TLP-4 affords an invaluable opportunity for structural and functional characterizations of glycoproteins, especially because its glycan organization is different from that of mastigonemes (
27

Fig. 4

Discussion
In this study, we extended our CryoSeek strategy to investigate a heavily glycosylated fibril, TLP-4, from water samples collected from the Tsinghua Lotus Pond. Structural analysis revealed that TLP-4 consists of tetrapeptide repeats, with diHyP and Ser/Thr residues serving as sites for O-linked glycosylation. The glycans attached to these residues constitute the majority of the fibril’s biomass and play essential roles in the structural assembly of TLP-4.
Although the exact identity of the TLP-4 fibril may not be able to be precisely revealed due to the species heterogeneity in the pond and the limited sequence information of the tetrapeptides, the structural and bioinformatic analysis reported here establishes TLP-4 as a prototype for structural investigation of glycoconjugates. Similar tetrapeptide repeats in numerous proteins across various species have been identified. Many, if not all of them, may be glycofibrils, with the structure determined and supported through glycans (
Fig. 3
C
). Therefore, the structural information derived from TLP-4 may shed light on the structural prediction, functional investigation, and mechanistic understanding of the large number of related proteins. For example, many benthic diatoms secrete extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) in their adhesive trails (
43
–
45
), which are typically rich in PTSA/G repeats and acidic polysaccharides (
45
). Notably, diHyp has also been identified in the adhesive trails of some diatom species (
44
–
46
). Given that each tetrapeptide repeat in TLP-4 is 12.4 Å long, the 667 repeats in the aforementioned diatom protein (GenBank ID:
KAG7367681.1
) would span approximately 800 nm. The length of these repeats and the thick glycan shell make such fibrils ideal for adhesion or tethering. The dense glycoshell may protect TLP-4 from protease-mediated degradation of the peptide core, thereby allowing TLP-4 to stably exist in the native environment. On the other hand, the mass ratio of sugar and amino acid residues in such glycofibrils is larger than 9:1, providing an alternative route for organic carbon storage outside the cell body.
AI-facilitated structure prediction and model building tools have significantly advanced scientific research (
22
–
24
). However, in our study, these tools performed well only for the previously resolved TLP-1a/b, where the EM densities are predominantly protein-based. For TLP-4, autobuilding tools failed to produce any plausible models. Additionally, the structural prediction for the long, repetitive peptides of TLP-4 remained flexible and disordered. This highlights the need for more glycan structures datasets to train AI-based tools for accurate glycan structure prediction and model building.
Our understanding of glycans lags behind that of proteins and nucleic acids. Glycans are highly diverse biomolecules with complex compositions, configurations, and linkages. Most available glycan structures are relatively simple, typically involving a small number of glycosyl units attached to the extracellular loops of membrane or secreted proteins. Prior to this study, mastigoneme was the only prototypical bioassembly used to study the well-ordered glycan structures. Our CryoSeek strategy and cryo-EM analysis of TLP-4 open a different avenue toward the study of glycan structures by isolating glycofibrils from natural environment.
Materials and Methods
Pretreatment of Water from the Tsinghua Lotus Pond.
Water samples were treated as previously described (
28
). In brief, about 2.5 L water was collected from the Tsinghua Lotus Pond and filtered using a 0.22 μm filter membrane (ShangHaiXinYa). The flowthrough was then collected and concentrated to approximately 50 μL using 100-kD cutoff Centricons (Millipore) for further analysis.
Cryo-EM Samples Preparation and Data Acquisition.
Cryosamples were prepared as previously described (
28
). EM data were acquired using a Titan Krios electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with a Gatan K3 detector, a GIF Quantum energy filter, and a CS corrector. A total of 18,037 movie stacks were collected using AutoEMation (
47
), at a nominal magnification of 64,000× in super resolution. The pixel size is 0.54895 Å. Each movie stack, consisting of 32 frames, was exposed for 2.56 s at a total dose rate of 50 e
−
/Å
2
. The stacks were subsequently motion-corrected with MotionCor2 and binned twofold (
48
), resulting in a pixel size of 1.0979 Å. The range of preset defocus values for data collection varied from −1.8 to −1.3 μm.
Data Processing.
The data processing procedures were similar to those previously described (
28
). A total of 18,037 motion-corrected micrographs were imported into cryoSPARC and processed with patch CTF estimation (
31
). A total of 9,740,128 particles were picked using the Filament Tracer, with a preset filament diameter of 7 nm. After manual inspection, 8,930,454 particles remained and were extracted with a rescaled box size of 80 pixels from the original 320 pixels. Multiple rounds of 2D classification were conducted to distinguish among different classes. For class averages possessing the feature of TLP-4, an additional round of deep 2D classification was performed to eliminate junk particles.
A total of 63,798 particles were selected for ab initio 3D map reconstruction, followed by re-extraction with a box size of 320 pixels for Helical Refinement without predefined helical symmetry parameters. The resulting map was used for helical symmetry search, which identifies the helical rise and twist of 12.42 Å and 133.90°, respectively. The parameters were then applied for another round of Helical Refinement, generating a 3.6 Å EM map of TLP-4. To achieve higher resolution, we re-extracted particles with a box size of 256 pixels and generated several bad references focusing on the solvent region surrounding the TLP-4. The re-extracted particles and bad references were then subjected to Heterogeneous Refinement, resulting in 32,240 good particles. After another round of Helical Refinement, a map with an overall resolution of 3.3 Å was obtained. The final helical rise and twist were calculated to be 12.41 Å and 134.02°, respectively.
The reported resolution for TLP-4 was calculated based on the gold-standard Fourier shell correlation (FSC) 0.143 criterion using cryoSPARC (
31
,
49
).
Model Building and Refinement.
Both CryoNet and ModelAngelos were applied for automatic de novo model building of TLP-4 (
29
,
50
), but they failed. Through integrated analysis of the helical information and the density map, we found that TLP-4 is a glycofibril composed of tetrapeptide repeats. The two neighboring glycosylated residues were identified as 3,4-dihydroxyproline (diHyp) and Ser/Thr, respectively. The other two residues within a tetrapeptide repeat were imprecisely assigned based on the averaged densities. Since the actual identities of the glycosyl moieties could not be determined, we therefore constructed hexoses or pentoses based on the EM map. Finally, we manually built the atomic model of TLP-4 in COOT (
51
).
The final model of TLP-4 was refined using PHENIX with secondary structure and geometry restraints in real space (
52
). For refinement of glycans linked, the ligand restraint files for refinement were generated using PHENIX, and the restraint files for covalent linkages of glycans were prepared manually. The structure was validated through examination of the Clash scores, MolProbity scores, and statistics of the Ramachandran plots in PHENIX (
52
,
53
). Statistics of the data collection, refinement, and validation can be found in
SI Appendix
, Table S1
.
Sequence Alignment of TLP-4.
De novo model building identified four residues in each repeat of TLP-4, in which the 3,4-dihydroxyproline (diHyp) and the neighboring Thr or Ser are heavily glycosylated. However, owing to the lack of orientation information for the fibril, the four residues in each repeat can be either PT/Sxx or T/SPxx. We thereby conducted a search for sequences containing the tetrapeptide repeats of PTAA, TPAA, PSAA, or SPAA repeats using BLAST against the NCBI NR database to identify proteins that may fit into the EM map of TLP-4 (
38
,
39
). Considering the length of TLP-4, we performed the BLAST search using sequences of 50 tandem repeats. A total of 251 protein sequences were retrieved. After deduplication and exclusion of candidates lacking the TLP-4 tetrapeptide features, 187 proteins from 71 species were retained (
SI Appendix
, Table S2
).
Species Classification Enrichment Analysis.
To analyze the species annotation of proteins with TLP-4 repeats, we downloaded the species taxonomy information from NCBI Taxonomy. For all species with TLP-4 proteins, we analyzed the species enrichment in phyla by Fisher’s exact test.
Mass Spectrometry (MS) and Metagenomic Analysis of the Lotus Pond Water.
Proteins in the concentrated Lotus Pond water were digested and identified by MS. In addition, the filter membranes used for pretreatment of the Lotus Pond water were employed for metagenomic sequencing. The experimental and analytical procedures for MS and metagenomics sequencing were nearly identical to those previously described (
28
).
In sum, the candidate sequences of TLP-4, exemplified as “PTxx,” “TPxx,” “PSxx,” or “SPxx” (where x stands for any residue), were applied to BLAST analysis against the nonredundant protein datasets derived from MS and metagenomic sequencing. No significantly matched sequences were found in the dataset derived from MS. Thirty-eight TLP-4-like tetrapeptide repeats with sequence lengths ranging from 20 to 340 residues were identified in metagenomics sequencing. BLAST was used to search the identified sequences against NCBI NR database for protein-coding gene annotation (
38
,
39
). However, no identical sequences were found.
