Introduction
RNA polymerase II (RNAPII) transcribes all protein-coding and numerous non-coding nuclear RNAs in eukaryotic cells (
1
). Eukaryotic RNAPII is remarkably complex: the core machinery alone consists of between 10 and 12 subunits that together form a 0.5 megadalton complex. This core complex is subject to several post-translational modifications during the RNAPII transcription cycle of initiation, promoter-proximal-pause, elongation and termination. In particular, the phosphorylation pattern of the heptapeptide repeats on the C-terminal domain (CTD) of the largest RNAPII subunit dynamically changes during the transcription cycle and reflects its functional state (
2
). Moreover, RNAPII interacts with a plethora of transcription co-factors that specifically associate with the RNAPII core complex at different stages of the transcription cycle and often form intricate multi-protein complexes themselves. This outstanding complexity of eukaryotic RNAPII is even more evident when compared to simpler RNA polymerases, such as those found in cell organelles or encoded by bacteriophages, which often consist of only a single polypeptide chain (
3
). Since the complex RNAPII interactome is not necessary to polymerize nucleotides and transcribe genes, it is widely accepted that it accounts for the increased requirement for transcriptional regulation in compartmentalized cells (
4
).
The regulation asserted by the phosphorylation changes of RNAPII and by the interacting co-factors is best understood during the early stages of the transcription cycle, in particular at the initiation of transcription, promoter proximal pause and subsequent release into productive elongation (
5
). One reason why the function of initiation and pause factors involved is so well understood is that their inhibition characteristically alters the phosphorylation pattern of the CTD (
6
), which can be easily studied using phospho-specific antibodies. For example, inhibition of the kinase activities of the pause release complex P-TEFb (CDK9) by 5,6-Dichlorobenzimidazole 1-β-D-ribofuranoside (DRB) is accompanied by an almost complete block of phosphorylation of the second serine in the CTD-heptapeptides (pS2) (
7
,
8
). S2-phosphorylated RNAPII is often considered an indicator of RNAPII pause-release and its transition to elongation (
9
,
10
).
Recent studies also culminate in a mounting pile of evidence for the regulation of the elongation step of transcription. A variety of core-associated factors influence the speed and processivity of RNAPII during productive elongation, such as RECQL5, SPT5, SPT6 and PAF1 (
11–20
). While elongating, RNAPII is prone to transient pausing and backtracking, which is mitigated by the association of TFIIS (
21–23
). Upon recognition of the polyA-signal, termination factors are recruited (
24
). This triggers cleavage and release of the pre-messenger RNA (pre-mRNA), and the slowing down of the RNAPII traveling beyond the polyA-site and dissociation from the DNA by the exonuclease factor XRN2 (
25
,
26
). Intriguingly, polyA-signals are also present within the gene body, and the association of RNAPII core-associated factors such as SCAF4 and SCAF8 (
27
) prevent the utilization of genic polyA sites, and premature transcription termination. Lastly, the ultimate product of transcription, a complete mature RNA, is also dependent on co-transcriptional processes such as 5′ capping, pre-mRNA splicing and polyadenylation. Several reports have shown that factors involved in such processes, for example, the spliceosomal component U1 small nuclear ribonucleoprotein (
28–30
) and the mRNA export complex TREX (
31
), also affect RNAPII passage on genes. Such coupling proteins provide a means of feedback and layer on yet another mode of regulation (
32
). Elongation factors, therefore, can encompass proteins that affect parameters such as speed and processivity, as well as those that affect the quality of RNAPII passage on genes. While several such factors have been shown to influence cellular transcriptional output, we are far from a comprehensive functional understanding of RNAPII core-associated factors, particularly when regulating RNAPII activity independently of S2-phosphorylation.
We therefore aimed to utilize the RNAPII-interactome as a starting point for the systematic identification of new regulators of RNAPII transcription. We first purified transcriptionally engaged RNAPII complexes from human U2OS osteosarcoma cells to identify associated proteins by mass spectrometry. We then used an siRNA-based screening approach to investigate the role of 95 RNAPII-associated proteins on transcription rates by an 5′-ethynyl uridine (EU) incorporation assay, and identified several proteins that activate or repress cellular RNA synthesis. We further investigated SCAF1 (serine/arginine rich(SR)-related CTD-associated factor 1), a candidate which, when depleted, decreased RNAPII transcription while increasing pS2-RNAPII levels. The acute depletion of SCAF1 arrests pS2-RNAPII complexes at GC-rich regions, and subsequently blocks the recruitment and initiation of further transcription complexes at the affected genes. The stalled RNAPII complex showed increased association with components of the ubiquitin-proteasome (UPS) system. Cells lacking SCAF1 survive with compromised levels of transcription, but become strictly dependent on the ubiquitylation machinery. Overall, this work systematically estimates the role of RNAPII-associated proteins on transcription activity, as a result of which we identified SCAF1 as a global activator of RNAPII transcription.
Materials and methods
Cell culture
Human U2OS (female) and Human HEK293 (female) were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (Thermo Fisher Scientific) enriched with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum(FBS) (Capricorn Scientific), 100 μg/ml streptomycin and 100 U/ml penicillin (Sigma–Aldrich) at 37°C with 5% CO
2
. Routine polymerase chain reaction (PCR) screening for mycoplasma contamination was performed, and the results were negative.
SCAF1-AID cell line with V5-RPB3
For V5-RPB3 expression, U2OS
SCAF1-AID
cells were transduced with pRRL-puro-RPB3-V5 and selected with 2 μg/ml puromycin (InvivoGen).
U2OS cell line with HA-RPB3
For HA-RPB3 expression, U2OS wild-type cells were transduced with pRRL-puro-RPB3-HA and selected with 2 μg/ml puromycin (InvivoGen).
Treatments with auxin and inhibitors
For experiments involving auxin-inducible degradation of SCAF1, U2OS
SCAF1-AID
cells were treated with 1 μM auxin (5-phenyl-1H-indole-3-acetic acid, BioAcademia) in the culture medium for indicated durations. RNAPI- and RNAPII-inhibition was carried out by incubating cells with media with actinomycin D (0.05 μg/ml) and/or DRB (100 μM) for a total duration of 3 h before fixation. For proximity ligation assays (PLA), cells were treated with media containing final concentrations of MG132 (20 μM) or cisplatin (50 μM) for 3 h.
siRNA treatment
For RNA interference, an siRNA SMARTpool (Horizon Discovery) targeting CDK9 or against non-targeting control, were transfected using RNAiMAX transfection reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Total cell lysate was used for protein (for immunoblotting) or cells were fixed (for immunofluorescence) 48 h post-transfection.
EU assay and quantification
Cells were pulsed with 1 mM final concentration of EU (Thermo Fisher Scientific) 1.5 h prior to end point. In cases of inhibitor treatment, the EU and inhibitor containing media was added. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100. Linkage of incorporated EU to the fluorophore was performed using click-chemistry. The click-chemistry reaction mix [100 mM Tris (pH 8.5), 4 mM CuSO
4
, 10 μM Alexa-flour 488 azide and 10 mM ascorbic acid] was added to cells and incubated in 30 min protected from light. Cells were then washed with 100 mM Tris (pH 7.5) and nuclei were stained using Hoechst 33343 solution (1:1000, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 15 min. Nuclear staining solution was washed off by washing thrice with PBS. Imaging of fluorescence signals was done with the Operetta High Content Imaging System (Revvity, formerly Perkin Elmer) across multiple fields per well. Background (fluorescence signal around the nucleus) subtracted mean nuclear fluorescence was reported per well.
Immunofluorescence and quantification
After performing the EU protocol or after fixation and permeabilization, cells were were blocked in blocking solution [2% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 5% sucrose in PBS] and then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies diluted in blocking solution. Cells were then washed in Tris-buffered saline supplemented with 0.2% Tween-20 (TBST) and incubated for 45 min with fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies at room temperature (RT). Following 3× wash with TBST, nuclear staining was performed with Hoechst. Imaging of fluorescence signals was done with the Operetta High Content Imaging System (Revvity, formerly Perkin Elmer) across multiple fields per well. Background (fluorescence signal around the nucleus) subtracted mean nuclear fluorescence was reported per well.
PLA and quantification
Cells were seeded to achieve a density of 40 000 per 10 mm² glass coverslip placed in a 24-well plate. Cells were treated with or without auxin for 6 h and then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. They were then permeabilized using 0.3% Triton X-100, treated with a blocking solution for 1 h and subsequently incubated in the blocking solution containing a primary antibody against pS2-RNAPII (1:1000, Biolegend 920204) and another primary antibody against K48-ubiquitin (1:1000, Abcam ab140601) or VCP/p97 (1:500, Thermo Fisher Scientific A300-589A).
In-situ
PLA were performed using Duolink kits (Sigma–Aldrich). Briefly, cells were incubated for 1 h at 37°C with PLA probe-labeled anti-rabbit and anti-mouse IgG. Following this, a ligase solution was added and the cells were incubated for an additional 30 min at 37°C.
In-situ
PCR amplification was then conducted using far-red-conjugated oligonucleotides for 1.5 h at 37°C. Cells were counterstained with Hoechst 33342 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Alexa 568-conjugated phalloidin (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Coverslips were mounted on microscope slides using Fluoromount (Sigma–Aldrich) and imaged using a confocal microscope (Leica SP8) with a 63× objective under glycerol immersion, to gather atleast 90 (VCP/p97 with pS2-RNAPII) or 262 (K48-ubiquitin with pS2-RNAPII) cells per condition. Images were processed using Cell Profiler (
33
) pipeline to identify and quantify PLA dots within the cell nucleus.
Immunoblotting
For total cell lysis, Radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer [50 mM 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)piperazine-1-ethanesulfonic acid (HEPES) (pH 7.9), 140 mM NaCl, 1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), 0.1% sodium deoxycholate] was used supplemented with phosphatase and protease inhibitor cocktails (Sigma–Aldrich). Scraped cells were incubated at 4°C, head-over-tail for 20 min. After centrifugation at 17 000 ×
g
for 10 min at 4°C, the supernatant was collected. Protein concentration was determined with the Bicinchoninic assay (BCA), and equal amounts of protein were then separated by Bis-Tris-PAGE. The separated proteins were transferred to Polyvinylidene (PVDF) membranes (Merck Millipore) and blocked with 5% (
w/v

v/v

For band visualization,horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labeled secondary antibodies were used, and detection was performed using chemiluminescent HRP substrate (Merck Millipore) in the FUSION FX (Vilber). The signal was quantified with Image Studio Lite (LI-COR Biosciences, v5.2.5).

siRNA-screen for transcription regulators
U2OS cells were seeded on 96 well-plates 18–24 h before siRNA transfection was carried out. Prior to transfection, cells were washed twice with PBS and incubated in DMEM containing 2% FBS. For the siRNA transfection, Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific) reagent was used. A total of 2.5 μl of 1 μM siRNA was mixed with 7.5 μl of OptiMEM calculated per well and incubated for 5 min. In parallel, 0.2 μl of RNAiMAX was mixed with 9.8 μl OptiMEM per well and also incubated for 5 min. siRNA mixture was then added dropwise to the RNAi mixture, mixed by pipetting and then incubated for 20 min at RT. A total of 80 μl of transfection media per well was then added to siRNA-RNAiMAX mix. The cell media was replaced with 100 μl siRNA-RNAi containing transfection media per well. Spent transfection media was aspirated 18 h post-transcfection, cells were washed with PBS to remove traces of RNAi and full DMEM was added. EU treatment to label new RNA was 1.5 h prior to fixation. Cells were fixed 48 h after transfection. Fluorescence staining for EU incorporation was carried out as described above, and followed by immunofluorescence for assessing pS2-RNAPII levels.
siRNA-screen was performed with and without actinomycin D treatment. Multiple wells of negative (siCTR) and positive controls (siCDK9) were included on the 96-well plate format, to account for variation within the EU assay. Inhibitor based positive control of DRB was included in both, with and without actinomycin D conditions, as measure of variability across different plates/days of measurement. A total of 40 fields of images were taken per well, and background subtracted intensities were reported for EU and pS2-RNAPII. In order to be able to compare between multiple replicates, background subtracted intensities of every well were normalized to the average of siCTR and DRB-treated wells on the plate. This was done such the calculated normalized (EU/pS2-RNAPII) intensity value for the average of siCTR wells were set 0 and that of DRB to −1. Mean normalized EU incorporation or mean normalized pS2-RNAPII could therefore be calculated from assays performed in biological triplicates on separate plates, across a period of time.

Mass spectrometry for RNAPII complexes
Extraction of chromatin fraction: To isolate RNAPII-associated proteins, the chromatin associated fraction was prepared from cells following as described (
38
). V5-tagged RPB3-expressing U2OS
SCAF1-AID
or HA-tagged RPB3 expressing U2OS wild-type cells were used. SCAF1 degradation in V5-RPB3-expressing U2OS
SCAF1-AID
was induced by adding auxin to the medium for 6 h prior to harvesting. For each condition, 200 million cells were washed twice with sterile ice-cold PBS and then harvested in sterile ice-cold PBS containing phosphatase and protease inhibitor cocktails (Sigma), followed by centrifugation at 300 ×
g
for 15 min at 4°C to collect cells. To obtain the cytoplasmic fraction, cell pellets were resuspended in Extraction Buffer I [10 mM HEPES (pH 7.9), 0.34 M sucrose, 3 mM CaCl
2
, 2 mM magnesium acetate, 0.1 mM EDTA, 0.5% nonyl phenoxypolyethoxylethanol (NP-40)] with freshly added phosphatase and protease inhibitor cocktails (Sigma) and incubated on a rotating wheel for 20 min at 4°C. Nuclei were isolated by centrifugation at 3900 ×
g
for 20 min at 4°C and the supernatants were collected as the cytoplasmic fraction. The collected nuclei were washed once with Extraction Buffer I without NP-40. Nuclear fraction extraction was performed using Extraction Buffer II [20 mM HEPES (pH 7.9), 3 mM EDTA, 10% glycerol, 150 mM potassium acetate, 1.5 mM MgCl
2
], followed by homogenization (10 strokes) and a 20-min incubation at 4°C with rotation. After centrifugation at 13 000 rpm for 20 min at 4°C, the supernatants were collected as the nucleoplasmic fraction and the chromatin was pelleted. Chromatin pellets were treated with nuclease incubation buffer [150 mM HEPES (pH 7.9), 1.5 mM MgCl
2
, 150 mM potassium acetate], homogenized with 30 strokes and sheared by sonication (four 10-s pulses with 45-s pauses at 20% output). Benzonase (100 units/ml; Novagen) was added and samples were incubated for 40 min at 16°C in a thermoshaker (1400 rpm). Samples were centrifuged at 18 000 rpm for 30 min at 4°C. The supernatants, representing the chromatin fraction, were collected and used as input for immunoprecipitation (IP). The insolubilized chromatin pellet was dissolved in 1× Lämmli buffer [15 mM Tris (pH 6.8), 3% SDS, 0.015% bromophenol blue, 10% glycerol, 1.5 mM 1,4-dithiothreitol (DTT)] and used to check the efficiency of fractionation.
IP: To enrich for RNAPII-associated proteins, IP of the soluble chromatin fraction was performed by incubating the fraction with 80 μl of V5-coupled magnetic beads (Biozol) or HA-coupled magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific), using V5-/HA- RPB3 as bait, and 100 units of benzonase at 4°C for 3 h with rotation. The beads were then washed three times with IP washing buffer [20 mM HEPES (pH 7.9), 150 mM KCl, 0.5 mM EDTA, 10% glycerol] containing 0.1% Triton X-100, followed by two additional washes without Triton X-100. Protein complexes bound to the beads were eluted in 100 μl of 1× LDS Sample Buffer (NuPAGE, Thermo Fisher Scientific) by incubating for 30 min at 37°C on a thermoshaker set to 400 rpm. To the eluates, 50 mM of DTT was added and the samples were heated at 95°C for 5 min.
Single-pot, solid-phase-enhanced sample preparation (SP3): Samples were processed using an adapted SP3 protocol (
39
) Briefly, reconstitution solution was added to the sample up to 250 μl resulting in a DTT concentration of 20 mM. Alkylation was performed with 50 mM iodoacetamide. A total of 5 mM additional DTT was used for quenching. For V5-RPB3: Equal volumes of two types of Sera-Mag Speed Beads (Cytiva, #45152101010250 and #65152105050250) were combined, washed with water and 10 μl of the bead mix were added to each sample. A total of 280 μl 100% ethanol was added and samples were incubated for 5 min at 24°C, 1000 rpm. Beads were captured on a magnetic rack for 2 min, and the supernatant was removed. Beads were washed twice with 200 μl 80% ethanol (Chromasolv, Sigma) and then once with 1000 μl 80% ethanol. For HA-RPB3: Proteins were acetone precipitated (four volumes) over night at -20°C. Protein pellet (16 000 ×
g
, 20 min, 4 C) was washed three times with acetone. Digest was performed on beads with 0.25μg Trypsin (Gold, Mass Spectrometry Grade, Promega) and 0.25 μg Lys-C (Wako) in 100 μl 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate at 37°C overnight. Peptides were desalted using C-18 Stage Tips (
40
). Each Stage Tip was prepared with three discs of C-18 Empore SPE Discs (3M) in a 200 μl pipette tip. For HA-RPB3: Digest was done with LysC protease in 0.5% sodium deoxycholate for 2 h at 30 C followed by the addition of trypsin at 37 C overnight (enzyme/protein 1/200). Sodium deoxycholate (SDC) extraction was done using ethyl acetate and 0.5% trifluoroacetic acid and peptides dried under vacuum to remove remaining ethyl acetate. Peptides were eluted with 60% acetonitrile in 0.3% for V5-RPB3 (0.1% for HA-RPB3) formic acid, dried in a vacuum concentrator (Eppendorf) and stored at −20°C. Peptides were dissolved in 2% acetonitrile/0.1% formic acid prior to Nanoscale liquid chromatography coupled to tandem mass spectrometry (nanoLC-MS/MS) analysis.
NanoLC-MS/MS analysis
NanoLC-MS/MS analyses were performed on an Orbitrap Fusion (Thermo Scientific) equipped with a PicoView Ion Source (New Objective) and coupled to an EASY-nLC 1000 (Thermo Scientific). Peptides were loaded on a trapping column (2 cm × 150 μm ID, PepSep) and separated on a capillary column (30 cm × 150 μm ID, PepSep) both packed with 1.9 μm C18 ReproSil and separated with a 120-min linear gradient from 3% to 30% acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid and a flow rate of 500 nl/min. Both MS and MS/MS scans were acquired in the Orbitrap analyzer with a resolution of 60 000 for MS scans and 30 000 for MS/MS scans. For HA-RPB3: Orbitrap analyzer at a resolution of 60 000 and 15 000 for MS/MS scans. Higher energy collisional dissociation (HCD) fragmentation with 35% normalized collision energy was applied. A top speed data-dependent MS/MS method with a fixed cycle time of 3 s was used. Dynamic exclusion was applied with a repeat count of 1 and an exclusion duration of 90 s (20 s for HA-RPB3); singly charged precursors were excluded from selection. Minimum signal threshold for precursor selection was set to 50 000. Predictive automatic gain control (AGC) was used with a AGC target value of 4 × 10
5
for MS scans (5 × 10
5
for HA-RPB3) and 5 × 10
4
for MS/MS scans. EASY-IC was used for internal calibration.
Mass spectrometry data analysis
Raw MS data files were analyzed with MaxQuant version 1.6.2.2 (
41
) Database search was performed with Andromeda, which is integrated in the utilized version of MaxQuant. The search was performed against the UniProt Human Reference Proteome database (Release2023_03, UP000005640, 104451 entries). Additionally, a database containing common contaminants was used. The search was performed with tryptic cleavage specificity with three allowed miscleavages. Protein identification was under control of the false-discovery rate [(FDR; <1% FDR on protein and peptide spectrum match (PSM) level]. In addition to MaxQuant default settings, the search was performed against following variable modifications: Protein N-terminal acetylation, Gln to pyro-Glu formation (N-term. Gln) and oxidation (Met). Carbamidomethyl (Cys) was set as fixed modification. Further data analysis was performed using R scripts developed in-house. Label-free quantification (LFQ) intensities were used for protein quantitation (
42
). Proteins with less than two razor/unique peptides were removed. Missing LFQ intensities were imputed with values close to the baseline. Data imputation was performed with values from a standard normal distribution with a mean of the 5% quantile of the combined log
10
transformed LFQ intensities and a standard deviation of 0.1.

Cleavage under targets and release using nuclease sequencing
Cleavage under targets and release using nuclease (CUT&RUN) followed by sequencing was carried out as previously described (
44
). Briefly, cells were detached from the plate using Accutase (Sigma). The cell suspension was centrifuged, the medium aspirated and 1 ml of Wash buffer [20 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM Spermidine] was added. This washing step was repeated three times. Cells were then coupled to Concanavalin A-coated magnetic beads (Polysciences Europe) by incubating cells suspended in 1 ml Wash buffer with 10 μl bead slurry per sample [pre-washed with 1 ml binding buffer: 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 10 mM KCl, 1 mM CaCl
2
, 1 mM MnCl
2
] for 10 min at RT with rotation. Using magnetic separation, the supernatant was removed and 150 μl Dig-wash buffer (Wash buffer supplemented with 0.05% Digitonin and containing 2 mM EDTA) was added to permeabilize the cells. Each sample was then incubated overnight at 4°C with antibodies specific to HA-tag (cell signaling) with shaking (800 rpm). The following day, cells were washed twice with 1 ml Dig-Wash buffer and then incubated with Protein A/G-MNase fusion protein (1 μg/ml in 150 μl Dig-Wash buffer) for 1 h at 4°C with shaking (800 rpm). Two additional washes, with 1 ml Dig-Wash buffer and a wash with 1 ml Low salt rinse buffer [20 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 0.05% Digitonin, 0.5 mM Spermidine] were performed. MNase was then activated by adding 200 μl ice-cold Incubation buffer [3.5 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 10 mM CaCl
2
, 0.05% Digitonin] and incubating for 30 min at 0°C. The reaction was stopped by adding 200 μl of STOP buffer (170 mM NaCl, 20 mM EGTA, 0.05% Digitonin, 50 μg/ml RNAse A, 25 μg/ml Glycogen) and incubating at 37°C for 30 min to release the MNase-cleaved DNA fragments from the insoluble nuclear chromatin. Proteins were digested by adding SDS and proteinase K to final concentrations of 0.1% and 250 μg/ml, respectively, and incubating at 50°C for 1 h. DNA was isolated via phenol-chloroform extraction and quantified by assessing the concentration of small fragments (7–50 bp) using the Fragment Analyzer (Agilent). The input material was equalized and libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina Sequencing(New England Biolabs) with PCR amplification of 16–18 cycles.

The sequencing quality of the obtained FASTQ files was checked using FastQC. Reads were mapped using Bowtie2 2.3.5.1 (
45

Heatmaps were calculated for the average of three replicates using plotHeatmap function.

4sU-seq analysis
The 4sU-seq data were processed via the GRAND-SLAM (
53
) pipeline. Adapter sequences were trimmed using Cutadapt (version 3.4) with the parameters ‘-a AGATCGGAAGAGC -A AGATCGGAAGAGC’. Subsequently, Bowtie2 (version 2.3.0) was employed to align reads against databases for rRNA (mouse
NR_046233.2
, human
U13369.1
) and Mycoplasma, using the default settings. The remaining reads were aligned to a combined human + mouse genome using STAR (version 2.5.3a) with the parameters ‘–outFilterMismatchNmax 20 –outFilterScoreMinOverLread 0.4 –outFilterMatchNminOverLread 0.4 –alignEndsType Extend5pOfReads12 –outSAMattributes nM MD NH’. The genomic sequences for both species were downloaded from the Ensembl database (version 90). The mouse sequences were prefixed with ‘mus_’ to distinguish them from human sequences. A combined annotation file was created by concatenating modified (mus_ prefixed) mouse GTF files with human genome GTFs, and a genome index was generated using STAR. The reads mapped to the mouse genome were counted for each library for normalization and then discarded. Labeled human mapped reads were extracted from the BAM files by sorting the files by read id (‘samtools sort -n -o’) and subsequently iterating through each pair of reads. Read pairs mapping inside of a gene body on the plus or minus strand were determined using the Ensembl (v90) annotation, and reads having T > C mismatches in the forward read or A > G mismatches in the reverse read were then saved into a separate ‘TC-BAM’ file (taking into account that mismatch types need to be determined with respect to the orientation of the gene). Mouse reads were used for spike normalization, with a average scaling factor calculated for all three replicates. Bedgraphs were made using Bedtools v2.27.1, using spike-normalization factors, and ‘-bga, -split’ options. The following calculations were done using Deeptools v3.5.2 (
48
). BAM files were converted to BigWig format using the operation ‘bamCoverage’ with scale factors set to corresponding spike-normalization factors, binSize 10 and a black list file for hg38 (
49
). Replicates were averaged using ‘bigwigAverage’ for metagene analysis with ‘computeMatrix’ and ‘computeMatrixOperations’ was used to isolate sense-transcription information. Metagene plots were created with ‘plotProfile’ operation to represent the mean and standard error of mean across all expressed 14 966 genes.
Differential gene expression

Results and discussion
Identification of RNAPII interactors and establishment of an RNAPII activity assay
In preparation for identifying novel regulators of RNAPII transcription, we first aimed to analyze the protein composition of transcriptionally engaged RNAPII complexes in human osteosarcoma (U2OS) cells. We isolated chromatin-associated RNAPII by subcellular fractionation of U2OS cells expressing an HA-tagged subunit of RNAPII (RPB3) as described previously (
12
,
38
). After removal of cytoplasmic and soluble nucleoplasmic protein fractions, chromatin-associated proteins were solubilized by benzonase and RNAPII-associated proteins were enriched by IP with HA antibodies (Figure
1A
). RNAPII-bound proteins were then identified by label-free quantitative mass spectrometry by comparing protein abundance with HA-immunoprecipitated proteins from cells not expressing HA-tagged RPB3.

In total, we identified 184 proteins associated with transcriptionally engaged RNAPII (log
2
fold change > 1,
P
-value < 0.05) (Figure
1B
and
Supplementary Table S2
), including 9 of the 12 RNAPII subunits. Comparison with the published RNAPII interactome in mouse T-lymphoma cells (
12
) shows that protein enrichment is tightly correlated in both datasets (r = 0.75,
P
= 1.9 × 10
–118
), indicating a conserved RNAPII protein composition in mammalian cancer cells (

). Based on these proteomics data, we selected 95 RNAPII-associated proteins as candidates for an siRNA-based phenotypic screen, focusing on proteins with unknown direct functions in transcription (Figure
1B
and
C
).
We then assessed a EU incorporation assay as a readout for the planned siRNA screen in U2OS cells. EU is a uridine analog used to metabolically label newly synthesized RNA (
57
). We supplemented cell media with 1 mM EU for 1.5 h. After fixation and permeabilization, newly transcribed RNA labeled with EU was conjugated with alexa-fluorazide-488 via a click chemistry reaction (
58
). The amount of incorporated EU per cell was estimated by fluorescence microscopy. Visual inspection of images revealed EU signals in nuclei with hot spots of bright intensities indicative of nucleolar transcription (Figure
1D
).
To analyze how much of the fluorescence signal in U2OS cells is due to EU incorporation into newly synthesized RNAs, we pulsed cells with EU in presence of specific inhibitors for RNA polymerase I (RNAPI; 0.05μg/ml of actinomycin D ‘ACTD’) (
59
) or RNAPII (100 μM DRB) (
7
,
8
). Actinomycin D-mediated inhibition of RNAPI resulted in an 61% reduction in total fluorescence signal due to loss of fluorescent hot spots, confirming that these structures represent nucleoli (Figure
1D
and
E
). In contrast, uniform nucleoplasmic staining was not reduced, indicating that these concentrations of actinomycin D do not reduce RNAPII activity. In line, DRB-mediated inhibition of RNAPII resulted in an overall 67% reduction in fluorescence signal, which is the result of an almost complete loss of nucleopasmic labeling, while the nucleolar EU hot spots largely remained. Combined treatment with both inhibitors reduced the EU signal by 91%, similar to a control condition where cells were stained with alexa-fluorazide-488 without any prior EU incubation (reduction of 95%, Figure
1D
and
E
).
Phosphorylation at the second serine (pS2) of the 52 heptapeptides in the RNAPII-CTD is considered to be an indicator of its activity (
60
). Therefore, we combined the described EU incorporation procedure with immunostaining using pS2-RNAPII specific antibodies. As expected, DRB, but not low concentrations of actinomycin D, reduced the pS2-RNAPII signal to background levels (
Supplementary Figure S1B
and
C
).
We next aimed to establish a siRNA-based positive control for the siRNA screen. For this we assessed an siRNA pool against CDK9, a kinase known to mediate pause release of RNAPII (
61
) and transcription elongation. Immunoblot analysis showed that CDK9 levels decreased by 65% 48 h after siRNA transfection (
Supplementary Figure S1D
and
E
). At this time point, EU incorporation was reduced by 38% (Figure
1F
and
G
), indicating that candidates that decrease RNAPII activity can be identified. We concluded that the EU incorporation assay can be combined with the immunostaining of pS2-RNAPII and allows estimation of transcriptional activity in U2OS cells. Furthermore, utilization of actinomycin D as an inhibitor for RNAPI creates a condition where the EU signal is almost exclusively due to RNAPII activity.

We then aimed to identify novel regulators of RNAPII activity by transfecting siRNAs (pool of four for each target,

A total of 24 wells were transfected with non-targeting siRNA pool (siCTR) as negative control, and 7 wells with an siRNA pool targeting CDK9 as positive control.

Quite striking was the identification of splicing-associated proteins as activators of global transcription. The knock-down of the spliceosomal components IK (

), BCAS2 (

), CRNKL1 (

The latter seems plausible since we focused on proteins interacting with RNAPII (and by far not all spliceosomal proteins are enriched in our proteomic analyses) and since two splicing factors, BUD31 (
79

80
), did not reduce EU incorporation.
siRNA-mediated depletion of RNAPII-associated factors in some cases also increased EU incorporation. One example is the zinc finger protein VEZF1. Repression of global transcription by VEZF1 is well explained, since we and others have associated this protein with slow RNAPII elongation rates (

We next compared the results of the EU incorporation screen in untreated cells to the EU incorporation in cells where RNAPI was inhibited by actinomycin D. As anticipated, the effects of siRNA depletion are very similar upon treatment with actinomycin D (r = 0.88,

We then compared the changes in EU incorporation to changes in pS2-RNAPII levels (Figure
2E
,
Supplementary Figure S2A
and
C
, and
Supplementary Table S4
). The siRNA-mediated depletion of RPB1 induced the greatest reduction in in pS2-RNAPII levels. This effect is expected since this subunit contains the CTD and thus the recognition motifs of the phospho-antibody used.
Interestingly, depletion of many of the screening candidates that increased EU incorporation, such as VEZF1 or CCAR1, also increased pS2-RNAPII levels, suggesting that transcriptional repression is normally associated with reduced levels of pS2-RNAPII. Notably, however, it is unclear whether these candidates act by directly reducing the amount of pS2, or whether reduced pS2 is an indirect consequence of transcriptional repression. In contrast, depletion of most screening candidates that reduced EU incorporation did not reduce pS2-RNAPII levels to the same extent. In fact, depletion of many candidates, such as SCAF1, RPRD2, INIP, INTS5, INTS8 and PLK1, actually increased pS2-RNAPII levels while decreasing EU incorporation (Figure

). This observation is particularly intriguing, as mechanisms on how elevated pS2-RNAPII levels are associated with decreased transcriptional output have been less frequently reported. Among the candidates that showed increased pS2-RNAPII levels, we observed strongest effect on EU incorporation (in presence and absence of actinomycin D), for SCAF1. Additionally, in contrast to the other candidates, the function of SCAF1 in transcription was completely unknown.Therefore, we selected SCAF1 for the validation of the screen and a more detailed characterization.

Acute depletion of SCAF1 globally reduces RNAPII transcriptional activity

Acute depletion of SCAF1 increases S2-phosphorylated RNAPII at GC-rich genic regions
We found it intriguing that siRNA-mediated depletion of SCAF1 caused one of the strongest reductions in EU incorporation, while pS2-RNAPII levels rather increased (Figure

Notably, visual inspection of individual genes indicated that the increase in pS2-RNAPII does not appear to be uniform. Instead, we observed local variations of read densities in many genes, resulting in peak-like enrichments of ∼250 bp in length (Figure
5D
). To analyze these sites of local pS2-RNAPII enrichment, we systematically identified peaks in the auxin condition compared to the control condition using the MACS2 peak calling software (
56
) In total, we identified 147 298 peaks, of which 92 643 are in gene bodies in the auxin condition, while much fewer peaks were detected in the control condition (22 767 in total, 14 229 in gene bodies). We then used the HOMER algorithm to identify enriched sequence motifs in the pS2-peaks envoked by auxin-mediated depletion of SCAF1. While we did not identify highly conserved short sequence motifs characteristic of transcription factor binding sites, we did find motifs with high GC content strongly enriched in the peak summits (Figure
5E
).
We then looked at changes in pS2-RNAPII density with respect to GC content at individual genes. This revealed that there was a striking correlation of pS2-RNAPII accumulation with the local increases of GC content within the gene upon auxin treatment (Figure
5D
and
F
). We analyzed the GC density of all pS2-RNAPII peaks in the gene body and observed a global enrichment at the peak summits (Figure
5G
).
We wondered whether and how the local enrichments of pS2-RNAPII influences transcription activity and therefore analyzed the 4sU-read density at these peaks. Interestingly, within the peaks, the relative 4sU-signal locally decreased upon auxin-mediated depletion of SCAF1 indicative of less active pS2-RNAPII complexes at these sites compared to control cells (Figure
5H
). We then ranked expressed genes by their pS2-RNAPII peak density and observed on heatmap and scatter-plot analysis that genes with the highest pS2-RNAPII peak density showed the strongest reduction in 4sU-seq-read density (r = −0.5,
P
= 3.27 × 10
–154
) and thus transcription activity upon auxin-mediated depletion of SCAF1 (Figure
5I
and Suplementary Figure S5D). This suggests that the increased pS2-RNAPII signal is not caused by more productive RNAPIIs, but rather results from lower elongation rates or even stalled pS2-RNAPII. In line, short and highly transcribed genes, as well as genes with the highest SCAF1 occupancy, appear to have the highest pS2-RNAPII peak density when SCAF1 is depleted (
Supplementary Figure S5A–C
). Interestingly, normalization of the pS2-RNAPII signals to total-RNAPII ChIP-seq (see below) showed a reduced but still detectable enrichment at these sites (
Supplementary Figure S5E
), indicating that elongation of pS2-RNAPII is affected disproportionately compared to total RNAPII. This suggests a possibility that SCAF1 depletion may lead to an increase in S2-phosphorylation of intragenic RNAPII or that the pS2-RNAPII could be less prone to degradation as compared to total RNAPII (
88
). Intriguingly, elevated levels of pS2-RNAPII may also indicate premature, unproductive termination, since CDK12-mediated RNAPII S2-phosphorylation has been linked to RNAPII pausing and termination (
89
). Overall, we conclude that auxin-mediated depletion of SCAF1 leads to local enrichment of pS2-RNAPII at GC-rich regions accompanied with transcriptional repression of these genes.
Notably, auxin-mediated depletion of SCAF1 not only reduces 4sU-seq reads at GC-rich regions, but uniformly decreases the overall genic 4sU-seq signal from transcription start to end sites (Figure
4C
), reminiscent of reduced recruitment, initiation or pause release of RNAPII at promoters. To further explore the role of SCAF1 in the transcriptional cycle of RNAPII, we estimated the total genic occupancy of RNAPII (regardless of its phosphorylation status) by spiked ChIP-sequencing with antibodies recognizing the N-terminal domain of RPB1. Metagene plots at the transcription start site of all expressed genes showed a substantial reduction in RNAPII occupancy, indicating a global decrease in RNAPII recruitment to promoters (Figure
5J
). The decrease in RNAPII promoter binding was most pronounced at genes with most intragenic pS2-RNAPII peaks (Figure
5I
), with a slight but significant overall correlation between pS2-peak density and RNAPII promoter loss (r = −0.1,
P
= 1.42 × 10
–17
). We conclude that SCAF1 depletion globally reduces RNAPII recruitment to promoters and that genes with the strongest effect have a rather high density of intragenic pS2-RNAPII peaks, which have a preference for GC-rich regions. We speculate that the connection between the observed elongation defects and RNAPII promoter recruitment may be causal, as several reports suggest that DNA damage-dependent RNAPII stalling blocks transcription elongation, but also reduces levels of promoter-bound RNAPII, thereby limiting RNAPII initiation and transcriptional stress (
90
,
91
). Interestingly, short genes may exhibit more pS2-RNAPII peaks and thus a greater reduction in transcription upon SCAF1 depletion, as their gene span contains a higher exon (GC-rich) to intron (GC-poor) ratio as compared to that of long genes (
28
,
92
).
SCAF1 depletion renders cells dependent on ubiquitin-mediated clearance of RNAPII
Since SCAF1 depletion has such a drastic effect on transcription, we wondered if it would also alter the interaction of RNAPII with its associated proteins. To gain further mechanistic insight into SCAF1 function, we repeated the isolation and mass spectrometric analysis of transcriptionally engaged RNAPII complexes described above, this time with U2OS
SCAF1-AID
cells expressing a V5-tagged RNAPII subunit (RPB3). We compared the RNAPII interactome of cells upon auxin-mediated SCAF1 depletion with that of control cells. Volcano plot analysis based on triplicate experiments identified several RNAPII initiation and elongation factors that show decreased abundance in the RNAPII interactome upon SCAF1 depletion (Figure
6A
and
Supplementary Table S5
) including SPT6 (
13
,
14
), GTF2H3/TFIIH (
93
), PAF1 complex subunits (
14
) and RECQL5 (
11
). This observation reinforces the notion that the increase in genic pS2-RNAPII upon SCAF1 depletion does not reflect more productive RNAPII complexes, but rather is associated with slow/terminal RNAPII that has already lost association with several elongation factors. Consistent with this conclusion, we observed an increased association of RNAPII with CPSF1 and CPSF7 upon auxin-mediated depletion of SCAF1, reflecting the activation of precocious but unproductive termination (Figure
6A
). Strikingly, among proteins with increased abundance upon SCAF1 depletion are also proteins with a function in ubiquitylation and proteasomal degradation, including proteasomal subunits PSMD1, PSMD12, PSMC6, ubiquitin ligases CUL4A/B and the AAA ATPase VCP/p97 (Figure
6A
). This may be relevant since RNAPII can be removed from its template after terminal stalling by ubiquitylation and proteasomal degradation. This ‘last resort’ mechanism is most evident in cells exposed to DNA damage (
94
), but also occurs in unstressed cells (
95–98
). In line, we also observed decreased stability of RNAPII complex subunits upon auxin treatment (Figure
6A
). Interestingly, we also observed increased interaction of DNA-damage repair proteins with RNAPII upon SCAF1 depletion, such as Ku70, Ku80 (
99
) and DNA-damage sensor RPA3 (
100
) (Figure
6A
).

We therefore speculated that SCAF1 depletion increases the amount of stalled RNAPII at GC-rich sites and that RNAPII is then removed from chromatin not only by conventional termination but also by last-resort mechanisms, including its ubiquitylation and degradation. To test this hypothesis, we analyzed the proximity of RNAPII and ubiquitin in U2OS
SCAF1-AID
cells by PLA. We observed a striking increase in the PLA signal upon auxin-mediated depletion of SCAF1, to an even greater extent than upon proteasomal inhibition by MG132 or upon the introduction of DNA damage by cisplatin (Figure
6B
and
C
). The unfoldase VCP/p97 has been implicated RNAPII degradation in cells with impaired transcription elongation (
101–103
). We therefore also performed a PLA assay between pS2-RNAPII and VCP/p97. In confirmation with the mass spectrometry results (Figure
6A
), we observed a significant increase in proximity between VCP/p97 and pS2-RNAPII in SCAF1 depleted cells as compared to control cells (Figure
6D
and
E
). We concluded that ubiquitylation of RNAPII itself or RNAPII-associated proteins is increased in the absence of SCAF1.
Next, we aimed to evaluate the cellular relevance of SCAF1-mediated changes in transcription. Surprisingly, the effect of SCAF1 depletion on the growth of U2OS cells is marginal (Figure
6F
). To determine conditions under which SCAF1 is important for cell growth, we performed a screen using a targeted inhibitor library consisting of compounds targeting cellular stress pathways and transcription factors (Figure
6G
). Overall, we treated U2OS
SCAF1-AID
cells with 67 inhibitors (two concentrations each) in the presence and absence of auxin and measured cell viability after 7 days with the alamar blue assay. Analyses of triplicate experiments identified 20 compounds that affected auxin-treated cells more drastically than control cells, with DNA damage inducing agents (cisplatin, hydroxyurea), inhibitors of DNA-damage response pathway (MRN complex inhibitor, Mirin and DNA-PK inhibitor, NU20726) and inhibitors of the UPS system (such as HUWE1 inhibitors, BI8626, BI8622, and proteasomal inhibitor RA-190) scoring highest (Figure
6H
and
Supplementary Table S6
). SCAF1 has been identified as a tumor suppressor in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma via promoting DNA-damage response (
104
). In alignment with this study, we observed an increased sensitivity of osteosarcoma cells to PARP inhibitor, olaparib, upon SCAF1 depletion (Figure
6H
). Interestingly, the usage of intronic polyadenylation sites and subsequent premature transcription termination observed in CDK12 mutated cells, leads to a strong down-regulation of DNA damage response genes, explaining the observed synergism of CDK12 and PARP inhibition (
105
,
106
). We therefore evaluated if downregulation of the DNA damage response could be the culprit for Olaparib sensitivity observed in SCAF1-depleted cells. Indeed we observed that DNA damage response genes were downregulated in SCAF1 depleted cells, however the degree of downregulation does not exceed the overall downregulation of all expressed genes [average log
2
fold change of DDR gene set (−0.487), average log
2
fold change of all genes (−0.495)].
The UPS machinery plays a central role in the DNA-damage response in cells (
107
). This, along with the observation that treatment with DNA-damage eliciting agents, as well as inhibitors against multiple DNA-damage response pathways induced sensitivity in SCAF1-depleted cells, led us to hypothesize that the UPS machinery could be the common denominator in allievating transcription stress under SCAF1 depletion. Furthermore, it has been observed that while, depending on the context, distinct components of the ubiquitination machinery take part in the removal of RNAPII from the template, the involvement of VCP/p97 has been common among these different contexts (
90
,
94
,
108–110
). We therefore validated the effect of the VCP/p97 inhibitor, NMS-873, at various concentrations. While single treatment with NMS-873 only induces a growth arrest at 2 μM, already 250 nM showed a robust inhibition of cell growth when combined with auxin-mediated depletion of SCAF1 (
Supplementary Figure S6A
and
B
). We concluded that transcriptional deregulation upon SCAF1 depletion can be tolerated by unstressed cells, but becomes lethal when combined with drugs that inhibit the cellular UPS system or that activate DNA-damage-response.
Taken together, data suggest a model in which SCAF1 prevents RNAPII stalling at GC-rich sites (Figure
7
). Consequently, SCAF1 prevents elongation blockade and thereby promotes RNAPII re-initiation, allowing for high global transcription rates. Since short genes are particularly GC-rich, SCAF1 is particularly important for the efficient transcription of short genes. SCAF1 depletion promotes the proximity of RNAPII and ubiquitin, induces the association of RNAPII with proteins that mediate degradation and renders cells dependent on the last-resort mechanism of ubiquitin-mediated RNAPII eviction.
Figure 7.
Model of SCAF1 as a RNAPII transcription activator SCAF1 was identified in a focused siRNA library screen of RNAPII interacting proteins as a top candidate that affected RNAPII activity in U2OS cells. In our study, the data points to a model that in unstressed cells, SCAF1 aids RNAPII passage through genes and ensures optimal gene transcription. Upon SCAF1 depletion, pS2-RNAPII are found to accumulate at GC-rich regions in the gene body, and initiation of further transcription is reduced. pS2-RNAPII show an increased association with proteasomal machinery components, which mediate the clearance of stalled RNAPII complexes. Therefore, under SCAF1 depletion condition, cells depend on the UPS machinery, and cannot sustain further DNA-damage inducing stress.
Limitations and outlook
Our study systematically evaluates the functional relevance of RNAPII-associated factors and identifies a key function for SCAF1 in maintaining global transcription rates. While our data clearly show that SCAF1 associates with the transcribing RNAPII and prevents the accumulation of stalled RNAPII complexes at intragenic GC-rich regions, it remained unclear how SCAF1 mediates this effect at the molecular level. RNAPII elongation rate has been shown to fluctuate based on the nucleotide content of the transcribed region (
28
,
82
,
111
). It is also possible that a certain fraction of RNAPII stalls at these sites regardless of whether SCAF1 is present or absent, and that SCAF1 promotes their termination and subsequent eviction from chromatin, either directly or via the recruitment of other proteins. The absence of SCAF1 would then interfere with RNAPII termination and thus induce the observed pS2-RNAPII peaks and activation of UPS machinery. The auxin-degron model established here is ideal to further elucidate the molecular function of SCAF1, e.g. by studying the chromatin association of elongation and termination factors upon auxin-mediated depletion of SCAF1 or by complementation studies with SCAF1 mutants.
