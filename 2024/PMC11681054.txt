Introduction
Preeclampsia (PE) is a hypertensive pregnancy disorder, which can lead to severe maternal and fetal morbidity and mortality
1
–
6
. The disorder is characterized by new onset hypertension in combination with proteinuria
7
or other signs of organ dysfunction including new onset thrombocytopenia, renal insufficiency, impaired liver function, pulmonary edema or persistent cerebral or visual symptoms
8
. However, the syndrome may also progress free from symptoms or arise unexpectedly
9
. Although the clinical manifestations of PE have been recognized for over a century, the pathophysiology remains poorly understood
6
. Several clinical characteristics such as high BMI, extremes of maternal age, and nulliparity are associated with PE
10
,
11
, but molecular data such as blood proteomic profiles of PE patients are still scarce
12
.
Reduced levels of the pro-angiogenic placental growth factor (PlGF) and elevated levels of the anti-angiogenetic soluble fms-like tyrosine kinase 1 (sFlt-1) and soluble Endoglin (sEng) are associated with PE at the time of and prior to diagnosis, especially in early on-set PE
13
–
23
. However, the predictive performance of these angiogenic and anti-angiogenic proteins has been debated, hence they are not yet universally adopted in clinical practice as biomarkers
13
,
24
–
26
. The predictive performance of sFlt-1 and PlGF increase with gestation
17
–
19
,
24
, and have limited predictive value early in pregnancy. Combination of these proteins with other biomarkers is therefore needed to improve the risk assessment of PE at an early gestation, well before the onset of clinical symptoms
13
,
18
. Furthermore, women with term preeclampsia and pre-existing hypertension and obesity have a lower prevalence of angiogenic imbalance
27
, hence additional biomarkers are needed to improve prediction of PE for this group of women.
New developments in proteomics allow for quantification of thousands of proteins simultaneously, and several novel large-scale proteomic studies have attempted to predict PE using multi-protein models
28
,
29
. A challenge for clinical implementation is that the proteins included in the prediction models, as well as the predictive power of these models, vary across cohorts
30
. Studies based on a large number of patients from heterogeneous populations are therefore required to identify biomarkers which are accurate regardless of demographics and pathophysiology
11
,
12
,
31
. The aim of this study was to identify biomarkers of late-onset PE (LOPE) by combining three independent pregnancy cohorts. We utilized quantitative expression patterns of 1006 proteins across gestation using the aptamer-based protein assay from SomaLogic Inc in all three cohorts
32
–
36
. By using robust data transformation and predictive analytics we demonstrate that it is feasible to make inferences across cohorts and identify shared protein dysregulation that could provide the basis for subsequent validation studies.
Results
Three study cohorts were included in this meta-analysis, referred to as the Detroit, Stanford, and Oslo cohorts. The three cohorts differed significantly regarding maternal age, BMI, ethnicity and parity (Supplementary table
S1
). Participants in the Detroit cohort were younger and had higher BMI than participants in the Oslo and Stanford cohorts. The Detroit cohort was dominated by African Americans, the Stanford cohort by Caucasians, and the Oslo cohort consisted of Caucasians only. There were no nulliparous women in the Stanford cohort, while the Detroit and Oslo cohorts consisted of both nulliparous and multiparous women.
Participants with LOPE and healthy controls differed significantly in age and BMI in the Oslo cohort, and in BMI in the Stanford and Detroit cohorts. In the combined multi-cohort, there were significant differences in BMI and nulliparity between LOPE participants and healthy controls (Table
1
).

The timing of blood sampling varied among the three original study cohorts. Samples were grouped into three new time intervals, to achieve a relatively even distribution of the number of samples within each interval: T1 (12–19 weeks), T2 (19–27 weeks) and T3 (27–34 weeks) (Fig.
1
).

Sample clustering by principal component analysis
Different versions of the SomaScan proteomic platforms were used to quantify 1125, 1310 and 4979 unique SOMAmers, in the Detroit, Stanford and Oslo cohorts respectively. After protein quality filtering, 1006 common proteins were retained for downstream analyses.
Principal component analysis showed that winsorized and log2 transformed data clustered according to cohort (Fig.

B,C).

Differential abundance analysis per time interval
Sixty-two proteins were differentially abundant (q-value < 0.1) in LOPE as compared to healthy controls, in at least one of the three time intervals, including 40 increased and 22 decreased proteins. No proteins were differentially abundant at time interval T1, 31 proteins at time interval T2 and 48 at time interval T3 (Fig.
3
and Supplementary table
S2
). Seventeen proteins (MMP7, PPID, METAP1, ITGA2B ITGB3, CDK8 CCNC, RAN, C4A C4B, SAA1, KLKB1, TEC, PDPK1, CD86, NID1, FYN, DNAJB1, PDE11A, PRKCQ) were differentially abundant at both time intervals T2 and T3. Although none of these proteins were significantly differential between LOPE and controls at time interval T1, most of them had consistent dysregulation patterns (increased or decreased) throughout gestation (Fig.
4
). Plotting the abundance of these 62 proteins over time (supplementary figure
S1
-
S3
) showed that the overall differential abundance between LOPE and controls was mostly consistent among cohorts, and not driven by a strong signal in only one cohort, as exemplified by a selection of proteins showed in Fig.
5
. Performing moderated t-tests on the cohorts separately also showed that the fold changes of the 62 significant proteins at specific time intervals were correlated among cohorts, indicating similar expression change patterns with LOPE across cohorts (Fig.
6
and Supplementary figure
S4
).

Prediction of LOPE
Two supervised machine learning algorithms were considered to predict LOPE based on plasma proteomic profiles at each time interval; the linear regression-based elastic net (EN), and the decision-tree based random forest (RF). Predictive performance was evaluated by area under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (AUC-ROC) and inspection of predicted risk scores vs true outcomes (Fig.
7
and Table
4
). In general, prediction performance increased with gestational age at data collection (Table
4
). For models trained with all proteins, the cross-validated AUC statistics for elastic net models were slightly higher than for the random forests models at time interval T1 (AUC = 0.74 (0.68–0.81) and AUC = 0.70 (0.64–0.77) respectively), while the random forest cross-validated AUCs were slightly higher than the elastic net models at time interval T3 (AUC = 0.79 (0.74–0.84) and AUC = 0.76 (0.70–0.81), respectively). The two algorithms trained with all proteins performed equally well at time interval T2. Limiting models to include only up to 50 proteins (selected by univariate analysis) slightly enhanced performance of the random forest models at time intervals T1 (AUC = 0.72 (0.65–0.78)) and T3 (AUC = 0.80 (0.75–0.85)) compared to models including all proteins. For the elastic net models on the other hand, reducing the models to 50 proteins slightly decreased the predictive performance at time intervals T2 (AUC = 0.72 (0.66–0.77)) and T3 (AUC = 0.73 (0.67–0.79)), and rather substantially at time interval T1 (AUC = 0.67 (0.60–0.74)). Including maternal age, BMI and nulliparity as predictors slightly increased the prediction performance, although not significantly (Table
4
). ROC curves obtained for the three cohorts separately showed generally similar performance for the Detroit and Stanford cohorts, while the performance for the Oslo cohort was lower (Supplementary figure
S5
A-D). All models, except elastic net with 50 proteins at time interval and 3, had significantly better performance than PlGF alone (Supplementary figure
S6
and Supplementary table
S3
).

Discussion
By combining data for 1006 proteins in maternal plasma from three independent longitudinal pregnancy cohorts, this work demonstrated significant prediction of LOPE by plasma proteomics data collected prior to diagnosis. We identified 62 proteins with differential abundance between LOPE and healthy controls during the second half of pregnancy including proteins related to cellular signaling, immune and stress response. Furthermore, random forest prediction models with up to 50 proteins showed moderate to good prediction performance at all three time intervals (12–19, 19–27 and 27–34 gestational weeks). Including clinical characteristics as predictors did not increase the prediction performance significantly. These results confirm that the disease is reflected in the plasma proteome prior to diagnosis of LOPE, and that protein biomarkers could aid in prediction, risk stratification and triage of women with suspected LOPE in the third trimester of pregnancy.
Matrilysin (MMP7), and Peptidyl-prolyl cis–trans isomerase D (PPID) were the two most important proteins in the random forest prediction models (ranked by mean decrease in accuracy) at time interval T1 and T2 (Supplementary figure
S8
). At time interval T3, PPID was the most important, followed by GTP-binding nuclear protein Ran (RAN) and MMP7. In univariate differential analysis, MMP7 and PPID were also the top three proteins together with Sialic acid-binding Ig-like lectin 6 (SIGLEC6), (smallest q-values). MMP7, PPID and Serum amyloid A-1 protein (SAA1) were the top three proteins with highest fold-change between LOPE and controls.
PPID belongs to the group of cyclophilins, which is a subgroup in the peptidyl-prolyl cis–trans isomerase (PPIase) family. This group is known for their functions in protein folding, chaperoning and repair in the extra cellular matrix (ECM), and plays crucial roles in processes such as mitochondrial apoptosis, RNA splicing and adaptive immunity
38
,
39
. PPIases also affects ECM networks and structures
38
, and maladaptive EMC expression and organization has been linked to PE and other serious pregnancy disorders and outcomes
40
. Thus, further research on PE and PPID and other PPIases is warranted. Elevated levels of PPID in LOPE between 8 and 28 weeks of gestation has previously been published on data from the Detroit cohort, however, non-significant differences were reported between weeks 28 and 36
28
. Non-significant elevated levels in LOPE has also previously been reported for the Oslo cohort
41
. In the current analysis, PPID had higher abundance in LOPE samples compared to controls throughout pregnancy (although not significant at time interval T1) and across all three cohorts, making it a potential new biomarker candidate for LOPE across demographics.
RAN, also known as RanGTPase and TC4, belongs to the Ras superfamily of small GTPases, and is involved in transport of RNAs and proteins over the nuclear membrane, mitotic spindle assembly and post-mitotic nuclear envelope assembly
42
,
43
. Several studies have identified RAN proteins in both the nuclei and mitochondria of the human placenta
44
,
45
, but a link between RAN in maternal plasma and PE has yet to be established. In the current study, RAN was upregulated in LOPE at time interval T2 and T3, making it a potential new biomarker candidate for LOPE after week 19.
SAA1 is part of the SAA family, and their expression increases drastically during the acute phase response
46
. SAA also promotes trophoblast invasion through binding to the Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)
47
, and hence might be of importance for proper placentation. This is in line with the reduced levels of SAA1 in LOPE in the current study. In addition, SAA has been linked to angiogenesis and production of immunoregulatory mediators
48
, which could play a role in PE. Although the fold change of SAA1 was among the highest in our data, it must be noted that the differences in SAA1 levels between LOPE and controls were not significant in any of the individual cohorts, emphasizing the value of combining data across cohorts in the discovery of new biomarker candidates. The large variability in SAA1 abundance must therefore be considered in future studies and possible clinical implementation. It must also be noted that higher levels of SAA1 has previously been reported in PE contradicting the findings in the current study
49
. However, these samples were gathered at admission for delivery, and even after delivery, and protein quantification was performed with mass spectrometry.
SIGLEC6 is a part of the SIGLEC receptor family, which is a group of immunomodulatory transmembrane receptor proteins with their active site on the extracellular surface. Increased levels of SIGLEC6 has previously been linked to PE
50
,
51
, which is in accordance with our findings of increased levels of SIGLEC6 after week 27 in LOPE pregnancies compared to controls. SIGLEC6 is expressed by trophoblast cells and is believed to reduce trophoblast invasion
52
, further leading to reduced vascularization, hypoxia and PE. Nevertheless, the mechanisms of SIGLEC6 signaling in PE and the difference in SIGLEC6 expression in late-onset PE compared to early-onset PE need further investigation.
MMP7 is a part of the matrix metalloproteinase family involved in a variety of biological processes such as angiogenesis, cell apoptosis and tissue repair
53
. During development of preeclampsia, it has been postulated that elevated levels of MMP7 result in vascular remodeling/stiffening, increased vascular resistance and hypertension as a response to uterine hypoxia and oxidative stress
54
. Previous claims of elevated maternal levels of MMP7 early in LOPE pregnancies have suggested MMP7 as a potential biomarker for early detection of LOPE
28
,
55
, although findings were not successfully replicated
30
,
56
. In the current study, we confirmed elevated levels of MMP7 in LOPE from gestational week 19. However, the promising results of MMP7 were mainly driven by the Detroit cohort, as MMP7 levels were not significantly elevated in LOPE when analyzing the Stanford or Oslo cohorts separately (Supplementary figure
S4
).
Together with serum soluble fms-like tyrosine kinase-1 (sFlt-1), Placenta growth factor (PlGF) is currently considered the most promising biomarkers of PE
13
,
16
,
17
,
57
–
59
. As expected
60
, our analyses found significantly lower levels of PlGF in LOPE compared to controls at gestational weeks 27–34 (time interval T3). PlGF was also among the top ten most important proteins in RF prediction models at time interval T3, and among the top 30 and 20 most important proteins in time interval T1 and T2 respectively. As a predictor alone, PlGF had significantly lower performance than the multi-protein models, illustrating the importance of combining PlGF with other biomarkers. Unfortunately, sFLT1 was not measured in the Stanford and Detroit cohorts, and hence not included in this study. Other previously suggested biomarkers are C-reactive protein (CRP)
61
,
62
and endoglin (ENG)
63
,
64
which have been reported to be increased in women with PE, and Pappalysin-1 (PAPP-A) which has been reported to have lower first trimester abundance in women destined to develop PE
65
–
67
. Neither of these proteins showed significant differences between LOPE and controls in univariate tests in the current multicohort analysis, nor when testing the cohorts separately. Nevertheless, it must be mentioned that conflicting results on PAPPA-A have been shown by multiple studies
68
–
70
. It is therefore debated whether lower levels of PAPP-A predict early onset PE (EOPE) and not LOPE
68
,
70
, emphasizing the importance of separating EOPE and LOPE into distinct PE groups and study participants.
LOPE is the most prevalent form of PE, constituting about 90% of PE cases
71
. Predicting LOPE would allow healthcare providers to provide more focused and targeted antenatal care for women at risk. Predicting PE in early gestation is highly welcomed, as treatment with low-dose aspirin preferably before week 16 of gestation is recommended to prevent or delay development of PE
72
,
73
. However, while low-dose aspirin treatment have shown great results for women with EOPE, little or no effect has been found for women developing LOPE
74
. Substantial resources are also spent on follow-up of women with suspected PE in the third trimester of pregnancy. Biomarkers that can predict PE in later stages of pregnancy are therefore warranted as they could aid in clinical triage of high-risk individuals, while low risk individuals could be allocated back to standard antenatal care. This would allow for appropriate resource allocation in hospitals and clinics, secure right level of care for women, and to aid clinicians to perform timely interventions including induction of labor. Correct triage of high-risk women could also prevent complications and improve maternal and perinatal outcomes. The current study shows that the maternal plasma proteome can be used to predict LOPE, with best performance after week 19. We created predictive models with 50 proteins that performed equally well, or in some cases better than models of > 1000 proteins. Currently, measuring 50 proteins simultaneously in a clinical setting is resource demanding and sparse protein models are therefore desired for clinical implementation in the near future. However, if screening of tens or hundreds of proteins would be cost- and labor-efficient in the future, having a large protein model as a first screening could be beneficial as it could span over a range of demographics and clinical factors.
This is, to our knowledge, the most comprehensive study of the maternal plasma proteome in LOPE when considering the demographic diversity, the number of participants (124 LOPE and 178 controls) and the depth of the proteome coverage. Unlike in a previous report considering cross-cohort prediction
30
, the prediction task herein was limited to LOPE and models were fit based on data collected in narrow intervals of gestation, hence tackling disease heterogeneity and variation in the time from sampling to disease onset.
Nevertheless, the study also has some limitations. There were unbalanced numbers of samples between the cohorts, and the Stanford cohort contributed with fewer participants (13 LOPE and 18 controls) than the Oslo and Detroit cohorts (35 and 75 LOPE, and 70 and 90 controls, respectively). One could therefore expect that the results were mainly driven by the Oslo and Detroit cohorts, and the Detroit cohort in particular. We therefore analyzed the detailed longitudinal patterns of our top candidate proteins both by combining the cohorts and isolated per cohort. Most proteins showed the same patterns of higher or lower abundance in LOPE relative to the controls within all three cohorts (Fig.
5
and Supplementary figure
S1
-
3
). Hence, the proposed biomarker candidates seemed to be able to separate women with healthy pregnancies from women with PE across nationalities, ethnicities and sociodemographic differences, although these findings need to be confirmed in new studies with larger external cohorts.
Protein quantification was performed at different time points, and the Oslo cohort utilized a newer SomaScan platform. We can therefore not exclude some differences in aptamer sensitivity and specificity between the utilized platforms due to changes/development over novel aptamers with the more recent SomaScan platform. However, when performing differential abundance analysis on the cohorts separately, the resulting fold changes of the 62 significant proteins correlated significantly well between all three cohorts (Fig.
6
and Supplementary figure S4). This does not only reflect biological similarities between the cohorts, but also similarities of aptamer sensitivity and specificity across the platform version.
Ethnicity varied between the cohorts, as the Detroit cohort was dominated by participants of African American origin, while the Stanford and Oslo cohorts had mainly Caucasian participants. Women of African ancestry have a higher incidence of PE than other ethnicities
75
. However, whether the reason is due to differences in physiology, socio-demographics or other structural differences in society is unclear and under-examined
76
, although socio-demographics is known to contribute to higher prevalence of severe maternal morbidity among non-Hispanic Black women in the US
77
,
78
. We performed sensitivity analyses with and without adjusting for ethnicity, and the results did not change significantly (data not shown). Another explanation for cohort specific differences is that key maternal plasma proteins, such as angiogenic and anti-angiogenic factors also reflect the burden of placental lesions of maternal vascular malperfusion
79
,
80
. The prevalence of such lesions was not documented in the Stanford and Oslo cohorts and hence not accounted for in the current study. The definition of LOPE also differed between the cohorts. The Detroit cohort defined LOPE as PE diagnosed ≥ 34 weeks of gestation, while the Stanford and Oslo cohorts defined LOPE as PE patients giving birth ≥ 34 weeks of gestation. In theory, some Oslo and Stanford LOPE participants could have been classified as early-onset PE based on the definition used in Detroit.
Conclusions
In the current study, we successfully integraded quantitative proteomic data from three longitudinal cohorts into a larger multi-cohort. We identified 62 differentially abundant proteins between LOPE participants and controls across cohorts, and developed proteomic models able to predict LOPE. The performance of the proposed prediction models increased throughout gestation, and hence with proximity to onset of PE. Among proteins with the most promising biomarker potential were PPID, RAN, SAA1, MMP7, and SIGLEC6, which deserve further investigation. Our study confirms that proteomics hold promise for the prediction of LOPE, and the findings may lead to the development of non-invasive and reliable tests for early detection and risk stratification.
Methods
Study populations and design
The Stanford cohort was a nested case–control study of 31 women, including 13 healthy and 18 LOPE pregnancies, recruited between 2014 and 2015 at Stanford University
30
,
81
. Inclusion criteria were women of at least 18 years of age, in first trimester and with singleton pregnancies. Plasma was collected prospectively at three visit intervals: 7–14, 15–20, and 24–33 weeks of gestation. Each participant donated plasma at two or three visits. Upon sampling, blood specimens were collected to EDTA tubes and immediately placed on ice. Plasma was separated by centrifugation at 1500
g
for 30 min at 4 ֯C within 60 s after collection. The supernatant was further transferred to fresh 2 mL microfuge tubes and centrifuged again at 12,100 g for one minute. The samples were stored at -80 ֯C until proteomic analysis. The original study was approved by the International Review Board of Stanford School of Medicine where all participants signed an informed written consent. All methods were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.
The Detroit cohort was a nested case–control study of 166 women, including 90 healthy and 76 LOPE pregnancies. Participants were recruited at the Detroit Medical Center and Wayne State University, between 2007 and 2013 under the protocol “Biological Markers of Disease in the Prediction of Preterm Delivery, Preeclampsia and Intra-Uterine Growth Restriction: A Longitudinal Study”, approved by the International Review Board of Wayne State University, and signed an informed written consent
28
,
29
. Exclusion criteria were < 18 years of age, multiple pregnancies (twins or more), severe or acute maternal morbidity, fetal chromosomal abnormalities, and congenital anomalies. Plasma samples were collected prospectively at six visit intervals: 8- < 16, 16- < 24, 24- < 28, 28- < 32, 32- < 37 and 37–41 weeks of gestation, and each participant donated plasma between two and six times. Upon sampling, blood was collected EDTA tubes and plasma was separated by centrifugation at 1300
g
for 10 min. Plasma samples were stores at -80 ֯C until proteomic analysis. All methods were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.
The Oslo cohort was a nested case–control study of 105 women including 70 healthy and 35 LOPE pregnancies recruited at the Oslo University Hospital between 2001 and 2008
82
. Inclusion criteria were healthy women of at least 18 years of age, Scandinavian heritage with singleton pregnancies, and exclusion criteria were known pre-gestational diabetes and any severe chronic disease. Plasma samples were collected prospectively at three visit intervals: 12–19, 20–26 and 28–34 weeks of gestation. Two participants donated plasma at visit one and two, two participants donated plasma at visit two only, and the remaining 102 participants donated plasma at all three visits. Blood specimen were sampled into EDTA tubes, centrifuged at 3000
g
for 25 min at 4֯ C, and stored at -80 ֯C until proteomic analysis. All participants signed an informed written consent approved by the Regional Committee for Medical Research Ethics, Southern Norway, Oslo, Norway (reference number S-2014/224-0119a). All methods were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.
In all three cohorts, PE was defined as hypertension (≥ 140/ ≥ 90mmHg) and proteinuria (≥ 300 mg in a 24-h urine collection, two dipsticks taken 4 h to 1 week apart demonstrating ≥ 1 + protein, or one dipstick demonstrating ≥ 2 + protein)
83
. In the Detroit cohort, LOPE was defined as PE diagnosed ≥ 34 weeks of gestation, while in the Stanford and Oslo cohorts, LOPE was defined as PE patients giving birth ≥ 34 weeks of gestation.
Protein abundance measurement
Protein abundances were measured using the microarray-based SomaScan assay from Somalogic, Inc (Boulder, CO)
32
–
36
. This platform offers a wide dynamic range for accurate quantification of both high-, and low-abundant proteins, by using three serial dilutions of the sample (20%, 0.5% and 0.005%)
84
,
85
. The least concentrated dilution is designed to detect the most abundant proteins (~ μM concentration in 100% sample), and the most concentrated solution is designed to detect the least abundant proteins (fM to pM concentration in 100% sample). The assay is based on capture of specific proteins by hybridization to unique DNA aptamers (SOMAmers). The hybridization is followed by several washing and elution steps, leaving only SOMAmers that hybridized with proteins in the sample. These SOMAmers were then quantified in relative fluorescent units (RFU), which is directly proportional to the protein concentration in the original sample. Protein quantification of the Detroit cohort was performed in December 2014, the Stanford cohort in 2019 and the Oslo cohort in July 2020. The SomaScan has been used in several high impact studies
86
–
88
, and is continuously under development, with the most recent version able to quantify 11 000 proteins.
Selection of time intervals and data imputation
The data were divided into three time intervals according to the gestational age, measured in weeks; 12–19, 19–27 and 27–34 weeks of gestation (see Table
1
and Fig.
1
). Missing BMI variables (one in the Stanford cohort, and one in the Oslo cohort) were imputed by the mean BMI of the respective cohort. Missing gestational age (10 samples at time interval T1, one sample at time interval T2 and one samples at time interval T3 in the Oslo cohort) were imputed by the mean gestational age of the respective time interval. Missing ethnicity (one in the Stanford cohort) were imputed by the majority ethnicity in the respective cohort (Caucasian).
Statistical analysis of clinical variables
Clinical variables were reported using the mean values and standard deviation for continuous variables, and number and corresponding percentage for categorical variables. Differences between LOPE and controls within each cohort were compared by Welch’s two sample t-test for continuous, and Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables. Differences between LOPE and controls within the new “multi-cohort” were compared using linear regression while adjusting for cohort for continuous variables, and logistic regression while adjusting for cohort for categorical variables. Statistical significance was inferred by two-sided p-values < 0.05.

Enrichment analysis for functional annotation of differentially abundant proteins and gene sets
Overrepresentation analysis (ORA) of biological process Gene Ontologies (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), Reactome Pathways (RA), Disease Ontologies, and Wiki Pathways (WP) was performed on differentially abundant proteins between LOPE participants and healthy controls using the
clusterProfiler

Prediction of late-onset PE
In search of biomarkers able to predict LOPE, we utilized the supervised machine learning algorithms random forest (RF) (
randomForest
(v. 4.7–1.1) R-package
97
) and elastic net (EN) (
glmnet
(v. 4.1–6 R-package
98
) to predict LOPE. We used tenfold cross validation to train and evaluate models by dividing the data into ten subsets (folds) and trained the model on nine folds (training set), while predicting on the fold left out (test set). This process was repeated until each fold was used as test set once. Within each training session, EN hyperparameters were tuned using an internal cross-validation procedure. The predictions across the 10 folds were concatenated to construct a full receiver operating characteristics (ROC) curve and area under the ROC (AUC of ROC curves) was calculated using the pROC (v. 1.18.0) package in R
99
. We ensured that the folds were balanced in terms of group and cohorts by using the
Caret
(v. 6.0–93) R-package
100
. We trained models using only proteins as input, and also models including the clinical characteristics age, BMI and nulliparity in addition to proteins. To determine whether parsimonious models could capture most, or all, of the predictive power in the 1006 available proteins, we added an additional variable selection step by using the top 50 proteins (ranked on p-value) based on Wilcoxon test. All steps in model development, including variable selection, were part of the cross-validation scheme to avoid introducing biases in accuracy estimates.
To investigate predictive ability across cohorts, a leave-one-cohort out cross-validation was also used and implemented. With this procedure, models were fit as described above, but on data from two cohorts at a time and models were tested on the cohort left out.
