Introduction
Human cytomegalovirus (HCMV), also known as human herpesvirus 5 (HHV‐5), is a ubiquitous virus that affects a substantial portion of the global population, with seroprevalence rates varying greatly across different regions and reaching over 90 % in some developing countries.[
1
,
2
] HCMV is a member of the
Herpesviridae
family, a group of viruses known for their ability to establish lifelong latent infections within their hosts.
[3]
This family includes several other well‐known viruses that have a significant impact on human health. These include herpes simplex viruses type 1 and 2 (HSV‐1 and HSV‐2), which are primarily responsible for oral and genital herpes;
[4]
varicella‐zoster virus (VZV), the causative agent of chickenpox and shingles;
[5]
Epstein‐Barr virus (EBV), which is associated with mononucleosis and certain types of cancer;
[6]
human herpesviruses 6 and 7 (HHV‐6 and HHV‐7), which are most commonly associated with a childhood illness called roseola;
[7]
and Kaposi's sarcoma‐associated herpesvirus, also known as human herpesvirus 8 (HHV‐8), which is linked to Kaposi's sarcoma, a type of cancer that causes patches of abnormal tissue to grow under the skin, in the lining of the mouth, nose, throat, or in other organs.
[8]
Infections caused by most members of the
Herpesviridae
family are typically mild or asymptomatic and are quickly resolved in individuals with a healthy immune system.[
9
,
10
] However, these viruses can cause severe and life‐threatening disease in immunocompromised and immunonaive individuals (neonates).
[11]
For both the immunocompromised individuals and neonates, an infection with or reactivation of a herpesvirus, like HCMV, can lead to serious complications.[
12
,
13
]
At present, ganciclovir (GCV) and its prodrug, valganciclovir (Figure
1
) are the standard of care for treating HCMV infection. However, GCV has severe side effects, including hematological toxicity, neurotoxicity, and fetal toxicity.[
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
] Animal studies have also demonstrated that GCV potentially causes cancer.
[18]
A recent analysis showed the probability of infections caused by drug resistant HCMV in bone marrow or solid‐organ transplant (SOT) recipients has increased over the last decades.[
19
,
20
] When GCV is ineffective, the second line therapies, cidofovir and foscarnet (Figure
1
), are utilized. However, these come with their own more severe challenges, including very high rates of nephrotoxicity among other serious side effects.
[21]
Cidofovir, like GCV, has also been shown to cause cancer in animal studies.
[22]
Letermovir (Figure
1
), a new addition to the arsenal of HCMV antivirals, has been approved for prophylactic use in hematopoietic stem cell transplant recipients. However, its application is limited and it is not recommended for therapeutic treatment in patients with a high viral load as breakthrough and resistance is rapidly developed.[
23
,
24
] Due to the desperate need for salvage therapies recently the US FDA approved the use of maribavir for refractory with or without resistance HCMV infection in transplant patients, despite modest improvement in outcomes and high resistance rates for the target (pUL97 kinase).
[25]
This approval of a marginal therapeutic improvement demonstrates the serious unmet medical need for treatments of GCV‐resistant HCMV infections.[
26
,
27
]
Figure 1
Drugs approved for therapeutic or prophylactic treatment for HCMV.
During our examination of HCMV related literature, we found multiple series of non‐nucleoside inhibitors that potentially bind to a different site of the HCMV DNA polymerase. One of the inhibitors, PNU‐183792 (Figure
2
), was reported to be active against herpesviruses including HCMV, HSV and VZV,[
28
,
29
,
30
,
31
] and its binding site was later confirmed by X‐ray crystallography.[
32
,
33
] Another structurally similar inhibitor PNU‐243672 (
2
) entered clinical trials in 2000,
[34]
but since then there was no additional disclosure of the progress from the company. Our initial examination of the representative non‐nucleoside inhibitors from different series revealed that many of them suffered from the off‐target activity against human ether‐ à ‐go‐go (hERG) potassium ion channel.[
35
,
36
] The hERG ion channel is critical for proper cardiac rhythm and blocking its function increases the risk of arrhythmia and even sudden death.[
36
,
37
] Thus, it is desirable to screen compounds for hERG activity and eliminate compounds with potential cardiotoxicity in the early stage of drug discovery.[
38
,
39
,
40
] In our prior work with a pyrroloquinoline core we identified a compound (
3
) that exhibited HCMV antiviral activity comparable to PNU‐243672 but with significantly reduced hERG activity.
[41]
This demonstrated that achieving a balance between strong antiviral effects and low hERG activity is possible through adjustments to the substituent groups.
Figure 2
Examples of non‐nucleoside polymerase inhibitors.
Meanwhile, we expanded our research to a different series which contains a 1,8‐naphthyridinone core reported in the literature.
[42]
Compound
4
, a representative example of this series, demonstrated a promising HCMV antiviral activity albeit with micromolar hERG inhibitory activity. We saw a potential for refinement by introducing changes to the naphthyridinone core, as well as the substituents on it. Lowering the lipophilicity is one of the methods that can help reduce the hERG activity.
[40]
Thus we replaced a carbon atom in the naphthyridinone ring with a nitrogen, to create a novel compound
5
with a less lipophilic pyrido[2,3‐
b
]pyrazine core. Incorporation of fluorine into the molecule in place of a methyl or chlorine is a commonly used approach in medicinal chemistry to reduce lipophilicity.
[43]
Compound
6
, in which a methyl group on the azetidine ring was replaced with fluorine, was designed and synthesized based on this concept. Furthermore, overlaying PNU‐183792 and compound
5
with the polymerase crystal structure revealed an opportunity for substitution at 2‐position of the pyrido[2,3‐
b
]pyrazine ring (see Figure S1A in supporting information), which is in a similar trajectory of the substituents of
3
(Figure S1B). We then designed and synthesized a series of compounds with different substitution groups on the 2‐postion of the pyrido[2,3‐
b
]pyrazine ring. This paper describes the results of these novel compounds in the study of their pan‐herpesvirus antiviral activity and hERG inhibitory activity.

The literature compound
4
(Table
1
) demonstrated a 5‐fold improvement in potency over
2
in HCMV antiviral activity, while it also showed a single‐digit micromolar hERG inhibitory activity (IC
50
=5.2 μM). Hence, a therapeutic index (TI), defined as hERG IC
50
/antiviral EC
50
, of smaller than 10 was observed. When the carbon atom on the 5‐position of the 1,8‐naphthyridinone was replaced by a nitrogen to form the novel compound
5
, a 3‐fold reduction of HCMV antiviral activity was observed, which closely correlated with the loss in HCMV DNA polymerase inhibition. The compound
6
, which was derived from compound
5
by replacing one of its methyl groups with a fluorine atom, showed a further reduced antiviral activity. The hERG inhibition of
6
was not affected by these modifications, resulting in a low TI value for both compounds
5
and
6
. Substituting the proton at the 2‐position in compound
6
with a methoxy group (compound
19
), resulted in a 10‐fold enhancement in antiviral activity, but due to the single digit micromolar hERG IC
50
value, the TI value was still less than 10.
Four derivatives corresponding to different 5‐membered heterocyclic ring at the 2‐position of the pyrido[2,3‐
b
]pyrazine ring, namely 2‐oxopyrrolidinyl (
20
), 2‐oxo‐1,3‐oxazolidinyl (
21
), 2‐oxoimidazolidinyl (
22
) and 3‐methyl‐2‐oxoimidazolidinyl (
23

20
showed significantly reduced affinity towards hERG (IC
50
>40 μM), yet demonstrated cytotoxicity (CC
50
=16 μM). Despite
20
and
21
being structurally closely related, the carbamate
21
showed greater hERG inhibition with an IC
50
value of 5.6 μM, but exhibited decreased cytotoxicity, indicated by a CC
50
of 33 μM. The 2‐imidazolidinone analog
22
and its
N
‐methyl substituted derivative
23
demonstrated good polymerase inhibition without cytotoxicity (CC
50
>40 μM). More importantly, these substitutions significantly decreased their hERG inhibition activity, elevating the IC
50
values to over 30 μM and thereby resulting in a TI exceeding 150. Homologation of the heterocycle from the 5‐membered to a 6‐membered ring (compound
24
) negatively impacted antiviral activity of the inhibitor and led to 13‐fold decrease of the HCMV polymerase activity, indicating a strong preference for the 5‐membered ring.
Based on the improved therapeutic index of 2‐imidazolinone analog
22
and its
N
‐methyl derivative
23
, we designed and synthesized a series of
N
‐substituted analogues containing polar groups. Compound
25
, with a carboxylic acid group as the substituent, demonstrated a similar IC
50
value against HCMV polymerase compared to
23
. However, it showed a loss in antiviral activity, which could be attributed to decreased permeability or increased protein binding, resulting in a reduced free fraction. Literature indicates that incorporating a molecule with a negative charge in a series of analogs typically results in reduced binding affinity to the hERG channel, which is logical since it is a potassium ion channel.[
40
,
45
] Based on this, we anticipated that the inhibition of the hERG channel would be minimal for carboxylic acid
25
. Contrary to expectations, however,
25
showed an even lower hERG IC
50
value (24 μM) compared to
23
, and, along with a higher antiviral EC
50
value, resulted in a TI of less than 20.
When compared to
25
, the amide derivative
26
exhibited similar HCMV polymerase inhibition and slightly improved EC
50
value of 0.83 μM. The aminoethyl‐substituted derivative
27

50

25
. Both of the compounds showed no sign of cytotoxicity. Notably, the hERG IC
50
values of
26
and
27

27

28
; however, this compound showed cytotoxicity (CC
50
=23 μM). The
N

29

27
. Intriguingly, hERG inhibition reemerged upon the introduction of the acetamide group (IC

=13 μM).

First, the selected compounds were submitted to a HCMV plaque reduction assay (PRA), which measures the ability of an antiviral substance to inhibit viral infection by quantifying the reduction in the number of viral plaques formed in cell culture. Compounds
22
and
27
exhibited comparable antiviral activity in the plaque reduction assay to the GFP assay in Table
1
, both outperforming ganciclovir. The antiviral activity against the HCMV Davis strain was tested in the HCMV cytopathic effect assay (CPE), which measures the ability of a virus to cause visible damage to a monolayer of host cells. In this assay, compounds
22
,
23
, and
27
exhibit strong antiviral activity against this specific strain. Compared to ganciclovir, these compounds demonstrate a 30–50‐fold higher antiviral potency. The results from these different antiviral assays, PRA and CPE versus GFP, confirm that the observed antiviral activity is attributable to the compounds and not assay interference.
Compared to the positive control acyclovir, which is used in the treatment of HSV‐1/HSV‐2 infection and has some activity against EBV,[
46
,
47
] compound
22
demonstrated marginally improved antiviral activity against HSV‐1 but a 4.5‐fold reduction against HSV‐2. Compound
23
displayed a 3‐fold more potent HSV‐1 antiviral activity and similar HSV‐2 antiviral EC
50
. Compound
27
is the only compound showed reduced antiviral activity in both HSV‐1 and HSV‐2 antiviral assays. This is possibly due to reduced permeability caused by the extra hydrogen bond donor from the amino group of
27
.
Compounds
22
,
23
, and
27
exhibited minor cytotoxicity, with CC
50
values above 40 μM in MRC‐5, HFF‐1, and Vero cell lines. A puzzling and unexpected outlier was the EBV‐producing marmoset B‐cell line (B95‐8) where all three derivatives showed single digit micromolar CC
50
values. Despite this, all three compounds displayed exceptional antiviral potency in the EBV quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) assay, achieving double‐digit nanomolar EC
50
values—a more than 100‐fold improvement compared to acyclovir. Additionally, the selectivity index (SI, CC
50
/EC
50
) for each derivative remained above 60, with compound 23 achieving an index greater than 200.
Conclusions
Treating HCMV infections, especially those refractory to current standards of care, is still a serious unmet medical need. A novel series of non‐nucleoside HCMV DNA polymerase inhibitors based on the pyrido[2,3‐
b
]pyrazine core with different substituents at 2‐position was synthesized. Compounds with substituted 2‐imidazolinone ring (
23
,
27
and
28)
demonstrated superior therapeutic index number (>120). Among these, compound
27
exhibited potent HCMV antiviral activity (EC
50
=0.33 μM), low cytotoxicity, and minimal hERG‐related cardiotoxicity, making it a promising lead for future investigations. For 2‐substituted pyrido[2,3‐
b
]pyrazine derivatives, reducing lipophilicity generally resulted in decreased hERG inhibition, whereas non‐2‐substituted derivatives did not exhibit this expected trend. Additionally, compounds
23
,
27
, and
28
showed potent antiviral activity against other members of the
Herpesviridae
family, including HSV‐1, HSV‐2, and EBV, demonstrating their broad spectrum antiherpetic potential. An improved solubility was observed for the compounds
25
and
27
which have polar groups on the 2‐imidazolinone ring. These results provide a foundation for advancing the pyrido[2,3‐
b
]pyrazine‐based or related templates in the development of next generation of antiherpetic non‐nucleoside polymerase inhibitors suitable for clinical evaluation.
