1
BACKGROUND
Dementias, such as Alzheimer's disease (AD) or Lewy body dementias, which include dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB) and Parkinson's disease dementia (PDD), are a growing public health concern with limited disease‐modifying therapies and are an increasing health and financial burden for individuals and society worldwide. Seventy‐five million individuals are predicted to have AD and 10 million patients are estimated to suffer from Parkinson's disease (PD; out of which approximately half also are experiencing dementia) by 2030. Additionally, another 10 million patients are estimated to suffer from DLB worldwide.
1
,
2
,
3
Recent trials with lecanemab and donanemab showed potential in slowing cognitive decline in some AD patients but were not universally effective in all subtypes and were linked to significant side effects like ARIAs (amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities), headaches, and falls.
4
Thus, refining patient selection or considering additional targets will be essential to maximize benefits and limit adverse effects.
5
Importantly, co‐pathologies like vascular pathology or Lewy bodies, characteristic of DLB and PDD, may have influenced clinical trial outcomes, particularly as diagnosing DLB in the early or preclinical stages of dementia is challenging. Misdiagnosis often occurs with AD, PDD, or other atypical parkinsonism, which share similar cognitive and motor features.
6
,
7
Additionally, co‐pathologies collectively impact cognitive decline and disease progression.
8
,
9
AD and Lewy body dementias are multifaceted diseases, likely caused by a combination of alterations in key pathways that are further driving disease progression such as immunity, vascular dysfunction, mitochondrial respiration, and synaptic alterations beyond the amyloid cascade, Lewy bodies, or neurofibrillary tangles.
7
,
10
,
11
,
12
Exploring the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms of these dementias, their biological diversity, and individual prognosis are a critical research focus to identify novel treatment targets to enhance both current symptomatic and disease‐modifying therapies.
Recent developments in mass spectrometry‐based proteomics offer enhanced proteome coverage, facilitating a reliable comparison of molecular phenotypes between patient groups and comprehensive quantitative analysis.
13
The emergence of cost‐effective proteomic techniques has enabled large‐scale studies to identify disease‐specific pathways and biomarkers.
14
,
15
,
16
Moreover, the widespread adoption of big data analysis tools such as weighted gene co‐expression network analysis (WGCNA) and the co‐expression modules identification tool (CEMiTool) has facilitated the identification of co‐expression modules in various dementia types.
14
,
16
,
17
For instance, comparative proteome profiling indicated a more generalized synaptic loss across various brain regions in AD compared to age‐matched controls without dementia, while translation dysregulation was predominant in the entorhinal, parahippocampal, and frontal cortices of AD patients.
18
Moreover, the spatial and temporal dynamics of pathology across multiple brain regions add complexity to dementias, a factor often overlooked in previous studies.
19
,
20
While quantitative proteome profiling of AD cohorts in various brain regions is increasing, comprehensive comparisons with Lewy body dementias, including PDD and DLB, in highly affected brain regions are currently lacking.
In this study, we aimed to overcome this limitation by applying a data‐driven approach of prospectively followed patients diagnosed with AD, DLB, and PDD, compared to age‐matched controls without dementia. We explored the biological processes underlying core features of these neurodegenerative disorders through an unbiased quantitative
post mortem
proteomic analysis of 33 individuals across the prefrontal, anterior cingulate, and parietal cortices. Using hierarchical clustering, we identified protein modules and correlated their eigengene values with clinical data specific to each dementia type and brain region. Comparing patient groups to those without dementia and the different dementia types to one another, the analysis of differential protein expression revealed both shared traits as well as distinct signatures. This set‐up allowed us to identify alterations in the proteome across brain regions among the most common dementia diagnoses, establishing a framework for understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying spatial differences across dementias.

METHODS
2.1
Brain tissue
Post mortem
human brain tissues from the prefrontal cortex (Brodmann area 9, BA9), anterior cingulate gyrus (BA24), and parietal cortex (BA40) of eight PDD patients (age: 72–88 years), eight DLB patients (age: 79–87 years), nine AD patients (age: 80–88 years), and eight elderly non‐neurological controls (age: 79–89 years) were provided by the Brains for Dementia Research network.
21
BA9 was chosen based on its role in executive function and global cognition,
22
,
23
which decline in all three diseases. BA24 was selected because it exhibits early changes in PDD and DLB.
24
BA40 was selected due to its involvement in AD as well as DLB and PDD.
23
,
25
A detailed description of the diagnostic criteria for AD, DLB, and PDD has been previously published.
26
Patient diagnoses were based on clinico‐pathological consensus criteria. Non‐demented individuals without significant neurological or psychiatric diseases and who only presented mild age‐associated neuropathological changes (e.g., neurofibrillary tangle Braak stage I–II) were included. Experienced neuropathologists, blinded to clinical diagnosis, conducted semiquantitative assessments of senile amyloid beta (Aβ) plaques, phosphorylated tau, and α‐synuclein pathology using a four‐tiered scale (0: none, 1: sparse, 2: moderate, 3: severe/frequent) to score sections from each brain region, following established protocols.
26
AD patients with low α‐synuclein pathology were selected to ensure clear differentiation between AD and DLB or PDD patients. The Lewy body dementia cases that were selected were in a pathologically “diffuse neocortical” stage, incorporating the 1‐year rule to differentiate between DLB and PDD.
6
,
26
The majority of patients had cognitive data, which included the last Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores, obtained in most cases within 1 to 2 years before death,
27
whereas MMSE decline was calculated over a period of 8 to 10 years. All participants gave informed consent for the use of their tissue in research.
2.2
Sample preparation
Protein extraction and preparation of 97 brain homogenates comprising 32 prefrontal, 32 anterior cingulate, and 32 parietal samples was carried out according to a previously described method, with each brain region being analyzed independently.
28
Lysis of the tissues was performed in sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS)‐lysis buffer (4% [w/v] SDS, 25  mM HEPES pH 7.6, 1  mM dithiothreitol). The lysates were then heated at 95°C for 5 minutes and were sonicated to fragment DNA. After centrifugation at 14000 × g  to pellet cell debris, the supernatant containing the proteins was recovered for protein concentration estimation. Subsequently, 250 µg of total protein from each sample underwent processing according to the FASP (Filter Aided Sample Preparation) protocol,
29
with samples digested on the filter with Lys‐C for 3 hours before trypsin digestion. After determining peptide concentration, 100  µg peptides from each sample were labeled using the respective TMT10plex reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer's instructions. Each tandem mass tag (TMT) set contained eight channels with randomized samples and two channels with the internal reference sample (made by pooling aliquots from all samples). Each TMT set was fractionated into 72 fractions by high‐resolution isoelectric focusing (HiRIEF)
30
with the broad range immobilized pH gradient (IPG) 3 to 10 strip before liquid chromatography mass spectrometry (LC‐MS) analysis.
RESEARCH IN CONTEXT
Systematic review
: We reviewed existing literature using PubMed. Although there is an increase in the quantitative proteome profiling of Alzheimer's disease (AD) cohorts, thorough comparisons to Lewy body dementias (LBD) in particularly affected brain regions are lacking.
Interpretation
: Our findings highlight distinct protein profile alterations in LBD and AD by the reduced levels of the autophagy protein p62, whereas altered levels of TRIM33 and the cysteine/glutamate transporter across brain areas differentiated dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB) from both AD and Parkinson's disease dementia (PDD). Interestingly, increased levels of mitochondrial and synaptic proteins were associated with improved cognition, whereas enrichment in the extracellular matrix, complement system, and autophagy proteins was linked to greater cognitive impairment.
Future directions
: Longitudinal studies with larger sample sizes—ideally including plasma and cerebrospinal fluid samples—along with brain imaging modalities are required to disentangle these molecular mechanisms and validate possible treatment targets.
2.3
HiRIEF separation
Briefly, each TMT set was cleaned using strong cation exchange solid phase extraction (strata‐X‐C‐cartridges, Phenomenex, P/N 8B‐S029‐TAK‐TN) after its samples were pooled. This was followed by a drying step in a SpeedVac with refrigerated vapor trap. The dried peptides (equivalent to 400 µg of each sample) were dissolved in 250  µl of 8 M  urea, 1% pharmalyte (broad range pH 3–10, GE Healthcare, P/N 17‐0456‐01), and this solution was used to rehydrate the IPG drystrip overnight (pH 3–10, 24 cm, GE Healthcare, P/N 17‐6002‐44). The focusing was carried out on an Ettan IPGphor 3 system (GE Healthcare) by gradually increasing the voltage to 500 V in 1 hour, then to 2000 V in 2 more hours, and finally to 8000 V in 6 more hours. The voltage was then maintained at 8000 V for an additional 20 hours or until 150 kVh was reached. After completing the focusing the strips were quickly dipped into deionized water to remove the oil, the plastic backside wiped for excess water, and placed in specified positions on a ceramic tray of the liquid‐handling robotics (Etan digester from GE Healthcare Bio‐Sciences AB, which is a modified Gilson liquid handler 215). A well‐former with 72 wells was placed on each strip, and the peptides were extracted by adding 50 µL of solvent in three rounds ([1] milliQ water, [2] 35% acetonitrile, and [3] 35% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid). After 30  minutes of incubation in each round, the supernatant solution of each well was collected to a microtiter plate (Greiner, 96 well, PP, V‐bottom, sterile, P/N 651261), which was then dried in a SpeedVac.
2.4
LC‐MS analysis
LC‐MS analysis was performed as previously described.
28
Briefly, for each HiRIEF fraction in each LC‐MS run, the auto sampler (Ultimate 3000 RSLC nanoUPLC system, Thermo Scientific Dionex) dispensed 15  µL of mobile phase A into the corresponding well of the microtiter plate, mixed by aspirating/dispensing 10  µL 10 times, and finally injected 7  µL into a C18 guard desalting column (Acclaim pepmap 100, 75  µm x 2  cm, nanoViper, Thermo). After 5 minutes of flow at 5 µL/minute, the 10‐port valve switched to analysis mode, allowing the NC pump to provide a flow of 250 nL/minutes through the guard column. Then the curved gradient (curve 6 in the Chromeleon software) progressed from 3% mobile phase B to 45% B in 50 minutes followed by wash at 99% B and re‐equilibration. The total LC‐MS run time was 74 minutes. Online LC‐MS was conducted using a hybrid Q‐Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific). Fourier transform mass analyzer master scans, set at 70,000 resolution and covering a mass range of 300 to 1600 m/z, were succeeded by data‐dependent MS/MS (35,000 resolution) on the top five ions using higher energy collision dissociation (HCD) at 30% normalized collision energy. Precursors were isolated with a 2 m/z window. Automatic gain control (AGC) targets were 1e6 for MS1 and 1e5 for MS2, with maximum injection times of 100 ms for MS1 and 150 ms for MS2. The entire duty cycle lasted ≈ 1.5 seconds. Dynamic exclusion was applied for 60 seconds, excluding precursors with unassigned charge state or charge state 1. An underfill ratio of 1% was used.
2.5
Proteomics database search
Orbitrap raw MS/MS files were converted to mzML format using msConvert from the ProteoWizard.
31
For all subsequent steps we used the ddamsproteomics pipeline (v.2.3) in nextflow (v.20.01.0). Spectra were searched using MSGF+ (v2020.03.14)
13
and Percolator (v3.04.0),
32
in which search results from all fractions were grouped for Percolator target/decoy analysis. All searches were done against the Swissprot human protein subset (42,367 canonical and isoform protein entries, downloaded from uniprot.org on 2020_04) using target/decoy concatenation. MSGF+ settings included precursor mass tolerance of 10 ppm, fully tryptic peptides, isotope error –1, 2, peptide length 7 to 50 amino acids, and precursor charge states 2 to 6. Fixed modifications were TMT‐10plex on lysines and peptide N‐termini, and carbamidomethylation on cysteine residues; a variable modification was used for oxidation on methionine residues. Quantification of TMT‐10plex reporter ions was done using OpenMS (v.2.5.0) project's IsobaricAnalyzer.
33
,
34
Relative quantification for each protein was calculated by taking the median TMT ratio from the set of peptide spectrum matches (PSMs) unique to that protein. Protein TMT ratios were then normalized by column median centering. False discovery rate (FDR) was estimated by target/decoy competition. PSMs and peptides that were found at 1% FDR were used to infer gene identities. Protein FDRs were calculated using the picked‐FDR method
34
using gene symbols as protein groups and limited to 1% FDR. Hardklor (v.2.3.0) was used for peptide feature identification and isotope deconvolution. Peptide areas were then estimated by Kronik (v.2.20) and integrated to the data tables using msstitch (v.3.5). Protein areas were calculated by averaging the areas of the top 3 peptides mapping to the protein. The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE partner repository with dataset identifiers PXD006122 (BA9 dataset) and PXD047324 (BA24 and BA40 datasets, as well as an updated search of BA9 dataset).
2.6
Weighted correlation network analysis
WGCNA was used to cluster co‐expression protein modules. An unsigned co‐expression network with proteins that had < 25% missing values was created using the R program WGCNA for each brain region and dementia group. First, we performed sample hierarchical clustering to detect outliers. Second, a soft threshold power
β
value was chosen for the different networks using the WGCNA::pickSoftThreshold() function to satisfy a scale‐free topology index of 0.8. Then we used WGCNA::blockwiseModules() function, which automatically constructs networks and detects modules in a block‐by‐block fashion on large expression datasets. The following parameters were used: deepSplit = 4, minimum module size = 50, merge cut height of 0.25, and reassignment threshold of
P
< 0.05. The correlation type was set to pairwise biweight mid‐correlation (bicor), network type was kept as unsigned, and the corresponding soft threshold power for each network (
β
= 13 for AD,
β
= 7 for PDD,
β
= 12 for DLB,
β
= 9 for C,
β
= 7 for BA9,
β
= 13 for BA24,
β
= 4 for BA40). Thus, an unsigned adjacency matrix was calculated using pairwise bicors between each protein pair (a reliable correlation metric), which was later transformed into a topological overlap matrix (TOM) from which we can assess the connectivity of genes within the network. Gene connectivity was calculated as the sum of each gene's adjacency to each other gene in the network. According to TOM, a greater value (toward 1) denotes that the group of genes are highly interconnected, and as a result, the strong interconnectedness will result in a significant co‐expression correlation, whereas a lower value (toward 0) means that there are no connections between genes and thus there is no similarity of protein expression patterns among samples in the cohort. Using 1‐TOM, hierarchical clustering analysis of protein correlations was performed, and initial module assignments were determined using dynamic tree cutting. The resulting modules or groups of co‐expressed genes were used to calculate module eigengenes (MEs; or the first principal component of the module). MEs were correlated with different biological traits indicating certain dementia‐specific modules. Eigengene expression was analyzed for each sample and module.
Hub genes in each module were defined as the 25% most interconnected genes using WGCNA:intramodularConnectivity(). Finally, we examined the modules’ hubs to find genes that differed significantly among the dementia groups.
2.7
Statistical analysis
The Kruskal–Wallis rank‐sum test from R Studio (v.4.0.3) was used to evaluate differences in demographic, clinical, and genetic variables among groups, considering the non‐normal distribution of the data. Additionally, to assess the statistical significance of differences in module eigengene values between brain regions in the AD, DLB, and PDD comparisons, we used pairwise Kruskal–Wallis tests. FDR corrections
35
were applied across the modules in each pairwise comparison to control for multiple comparisons (
q
<  0.05). Moreover,
t
tests were used to compare the sample groups based on log2‐transformed ratios, aiming to identify proteins with differential accumulation (requirements:
P
< 0.05, which is based on the 95% confidence interval of the variance between the two replicate internal pooled standard TMT channels).
Finally, we performed separate correlations between the last MMSE before death, MMSE decline, apolipoprotein E (
APOE
) ε4 allele, and different neuropathological measures (Braak stage, plaque, tangle, and synuclein pathology) with all genes within each brain region, using the “rcorr” function from the R package “Hmisc.” Genes with a correlation
P
value < 0.05 were retained for further analysis.
2.8
Gene Ontology and pathway enrichment analyses
Over‐representation analysis (ORA) was performed using functions defined in the CEMiTool package. We used CEMiTool's pathways from curated databases (gmt file) to determine whether genes within each module were enriched for known pathways or functions (
P
< 0.05).
Then, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of the hubs of each module to discover enriched categories after FDR (
P
value = 0.01,
q
value = 0.01) using ClusterProfiler. The GO enrichment result was plotted using DOSE R library functions
dotplot
and
cnetplot
(Gene‐Concept Network). We performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) on differentially expressed proteins (DEPs), using the fgsea R package (
https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/fgsea.html
) with 10,000 permutations. All proteins were ranked by log2 fold change and the ones that survived FDR corrections (
q
< 0.05) were considered significant. The cell type specificity of differentially regulated genes between groups was assessed by overlapping these genes with established markers for neurons, astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, and endothelial cells, as defined in the CellMarker 2.0 database (
http://bio‐bigdata.hrbmu.edu.cn/CellMarker/
). The Expression Weighted Cell Type Enrichment (ECWE) package
36
was used for this analysis. The significance of the overlap was determined after applying FDR corrections, with a threshold of
q
< 0.05.
Ultimately, we conducted ORA analysis along with ClusterProfiler GO enrichment analysis to investigate genes positively and negatively associated with the last MMSE scores, MMSE decline,
APOE
ε4 allele, and the different neuropathological measures (Braak stage, plaque, tangle, and synuclein pathology) within each brain region.

3.5
Enrichment analysis of dysregulated pathways in AD, DLB, and PDD
We conducted GSEA to identify pathways with predicted involvement in neurodegeneration, revealing significant regional differences in enriched GO terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathways. Gene sets related to respiratory chain complexes, oxidative phosphorylation, or GABA‐ergic synapses were primarily negatively enriched in parietal and cingulate cortices and positively enriched in the prefrontal cortex (Figure
5D,E
, Supplementary
Information S7
in supporting information) across most dementias. Conversely, inflammatory response, innate immune response, and the complement and coagulation cascade were positively enriched in AD and Lewy body dementias across most brain regions compared to healthy individuals (Figure
5D,E
, Supplementary
Information S7
). The comparison of the different dementias to one another provided additional refinements via their enrichment profiles that might help distinguish among the three dementias (Figure
6G, H
). More precisely, RNA splicing, synaptic vesicles, glutamatergic synaptic and metabolic processes in the prefrontal cortex showed a similar pattern of alterations in DLB and PDD compared to AD, while oxidative phosphorylation and mitochondrial respiration appeared to differ significantly in DLB compared to either AD or PDD (Figure
6G, H
).
Neuroinflammation and innate immunity play pivotal roles in signaling, potentially contributing to the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative diseases.
39
Unlike autoimmune conditions in which T and B lymphocytes invade the central nervous system, microglia serve as the primary immune component in AD and Lewy body dementias. Our study identified alterations in several innate immune response factors, including upregulation of complement C4B and amyloid precursor protein (APP) in all dementia types in the parietal cortex, along with overexpression of C4B, CFI, and C6 specifically in AD. Interestingly, receptors such as TREM2, RAGE, CLEC7A, and toll‐like receptors, known for modulating microglial cytokine production in response to Aβ, remained unaltered in the late stages of these dementias.
We found region‐specific changes in the brain's energy metabolic pathways, absorption of glucose, and mitochondrial respiration. These changes contribute to cognitive decline by being involved in both normal aging and a variety of dementias.
40
,
41
,
42
Age‐related deficits in brain bioenergetics have been particularly pronounced in the frontal cortex, although they have also been observed in the parietal and temporal cortices. Both AD and Lewy body dementias were associated with higher levels of the glucose transporter GLUT1 (encoded by SLC2A1) compared to non‐demented healthy individuals, accompanied by a further increase in GLUT6 (encoded by SLC2A6) in DLB, which may indicate impaired glucose absorption in both astrocytes and neurons. Lactate is an alternate energy source that is necessary for neuronal energy provision and synapse function under conditions of increased energy demand or glucose deprivation. It is interesting to note that MCT2, which is encoded by the
SLC16A7
gene and is accountable for neuronal lactate influx, was downregulated in the parietal cortex of PDD patients in contrast to controls and all other dementia types.
The enrichment analysis indicated widespread synaptic changes that have been previously documented in AD and, more recently, in Lewy body dementias.
43
,
44
,
45

Additionally, VGLUT1 and STXBP1 were more specifically altered in PDD compared to DLB. In PDD, the prefrontal and parietal cortices were predominantly affected by changes in PPP1R1B, GNB5, and GSK3B levels (Figure
7A–E
) while GABAergic (GABRB1, GABRB2, GABRB3, GABRA5) synaptic alterations were primarily observed in AD. Of note, changes in synaptic vesicle cycle proteins were equally affected in AD and DLB (17 DEPs in PDD, 30 in AD, and 33 in DLB, Figure

Specifically, unique alterations in synaptic vesicle proteins were observed in DLB, including CPLX1, CPLX2, CPLX3, dynamin1, and isoform 2 of SNAP25 while AMPA receptor GLUA4, APP, DNAJC6, SYT2, and SGTA alterations were shared across Lewy body dementias. Additionally, it is noteworthy that active zone proteins (8 in PDD, 13 in AD, and 15 in DLB) appear to be more dysregulated in AD and DLB compared to non‐demented individuals. Synaptic proteins such as MAP4, PLCB1, and SYNJ2BP were altered across the three explored brain regions in AD (Figure

Comparable numbers of modified pre‐ and postsynaptic proteins were found in AD and PDD, while in DLB, there was a higher number of affected postsynaptic proteins compared to presynaptic proteins (68 post‐ vs. 55 presynaptic in AD, 101 post‐ vs. 69 presynaptic in DLB, and 53 post vs. 41 presynaptic in PDD, Figure

Information S8
in supporting information). More specifically, postsynaptic cytosolic (e.g., HOMER1, PLCB1, PLCB3, PIN1, and MTOR), endosomal (e.g., RAB11A, VPS35, and SH3GL3), and postsynaptic membrane proteins were more affected in DLB than in PDD. 3.6
Association of cognitive measures,
APOE ε
genotype, and neuropathological hallmarks with protein expression
Across the three brain regions and various dementia types, cognitive assessments such as MMSE decline and the last MMSE before death showed positive correlations with the expression levels of 647 proteins and 486 proteins, respectively. Conversely, MMSE decline was negatively correlated with 167 proteins, whereas the last MMSE exhibited negative correlations with 915 proteins (Supplementary
Information S9
in supporting information). Mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation proteins such as TOMM40L, NDUFV1, and COXA2, and synaptic proteins such as GABRB3, GLUA3, GLUA4, SNAP47, or dynamin1 have shown a strong negative correlation with MMSE decline or positive correlation with last MMSE scores, highlighting the general alteration of these functions. Proteins associated with the extracellular matrix, the complement system, and lysosomal/autophagy functions—such as decorin, biglycan, C4A, C4B, and p62—showed positive correlations with MMSE decline. Additionally, metabolic pathways and autophagy‐related proteins—including GSK3A, ATG3, ATG101, and MTOR—exhibited some of the strongest negative correlations with the last MMSE scores.
The presence of the
APOE
ε4 allele positively correlated with 301 proteins with a role in protein binding or translation (e.g., EIF3E, ZNF24), while it was negatively associated with 260 proteins, such as SYNJ2 or GRIK3, involved in synaptic transmission across the three brain regions (Supplementary
Information S9
).
Finally, neuropathological measures, including Braak stage, plaque, tangle, and synuclein pathology, displayed notable correlations with changes in protein expression (Supplementary
Information S9
). Proteins exhibiting positive correlations with neuropathological assessments were predominantly associated with RNA and protein binding or metabolic pathways. Conversely, proteins negatively linked to Braak stages, amyloid plaques, neurofibrillary tangles, or alpha‐synuclein plaques were primarily involved in synaptic or mitochondrial translation and oxidative phosphorylation. Interestingly there were 65 proteins such as the autophagy‐related p62, the immune response‐related SNX18 or STAT3, or the astrocyte‐specific GFAP protein that consistently positively correlated with plaque, tangle, and Braak stages, while 28 principally synaptic proteins (GABRA5, GluN2B, KCNA3, etc.) were significantly negatively associated to these neuropathological hallmarks.

DISCUSSION
In this study, we applied a unique comprehensive approach to characterize the proteomic profiles of AD, DLB, PDD, and age matched non‐demented individuals across three brain regions. Specifically, our novel bioinformatic models used holistic methods, co‐expression, and enrichment analysis. This allowed us to take a significant step forward in deciphering the complexity of the wide array of factors underlying these dementia types, as well as their divergences across brain regions. A notable finding is the protein modules, hub proteins, as well as individual proteins involved in synaptic transmission, mitochondrial respiration, and metabolic processes we found through modular co‐expression network analyses. Several of these protein modules correlated with cognitive impairment and dementia severity as well as with disease‐specific neuropathological changes and the presence of
APOE
ε4 allele. We observed high preservation of these modular networks across diseases as well as across brain regions, suggesting shared molecular mechanisms across dementias. These observations are consistent with previous studies performed on community‐based
post mortem
brain or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) cohorts, highlighting the robustness of these findings.
14
,
16
,
17
The transdiagnostic overlapping pathways and modules likely result from shared protein aggregation pathology, or genetic or other common risk factors, highlighting the potential for common therapeutic targets.
Another significant discovery is the specific alteration in the levels of p62, known for its role in defective protein clearance via the ubiquitin‐proteasome system and autophagy, in Lewy body dementias compared to AD throughout various brain regions. Autophagy has been shown previously to be disrupted in AD as shown by the accumulation of autophagosomes and p62 accumulation.
46
,
47
Consistently, elevated levels of p62 were observed in both parietal and prefrontal cortices of AD patients indicating impaired autophagy. Remarkably, p62 was significantly downregulated in both Lewy body dementias compared to AD indicating enhanced autophagy‐mediated degradation in Lewy body dementias. This downregulation is strongly correlated with MMSE decline and disease pathology. Notably, p62 might offer a prospective therapeutic target in neurodegenerative disorders.
48
,
49
Additionally, the innate immunity modulator TRIM33 and the cysteine/glutamate transporter protein contributed to distinguishing DLB from both AD and PDD in at least two brain regions.
Studies suggest the involvement of p62 in tau and α‐synuclein accumulation, potentially impeding autophagic flux and contributing to protein aggregate buildup in both AD and Lewy body dementias.
50
,
51
,
52
,
53
Interestingly, autophagy‐related proteins (GSK3A, ATG3, ATG101, MTOR, etc.) were among the most negatively correlated proteins with the last MMSE scores before death. The marked differences observed between AD and Lewy body dementias might stress a distinct role of autophagy in Lewy body dementias. Of note, alterations in autophagy likely contribute to altered mitochondrial functions, as mitochondria are key targets of autophagic degradation in the brain of AD patients. We have reliably identified distinct alterations in key mitochondrial, metabolic, and synaptic pathways across the dementias, with distinct regional differences possibly partially due to their selective regional vulnerability and underlying differences in their energy metabolism. Prefrontal cortical neurons transiently maintain information participating in working memory and are known for altered energy metabolism, while the largest energy hypometabolism was previously observed in the posterior cingulate cortex.
54
Interestingly, we have recently described early mitochondrial hypermetabolism in AD knock‐in mouse models preceding synaptic and autophagy alterations, which in later disease stages shift to a state of hypometabolism with reduced abundancy of mitochondria in presynaptic terminals.
41
Notably, the activity of mitochondrial complex I has been previously shown to be reduced in the prefrontal cortex in patients with PDD compared to controls.
55
Recently Wingo et al. have also reported that altered mitochondrial and synaptic proteins were more abundant in participants with high cognitive stability,
14
a finding consistent in our cohort as well, as lower last MMSE scores correlated negatively with proteins involved in synaptic transmission and mitochondrial respiration.
Synapses, crucial for interneuronal and glial communication, are impacted in various dementias, and their specific disruption contributes to cognitive decline. Synaptic dysfunction is thought to be an early event in the AD continuum with mild synaptic changes occurring already in patients with subjective cognitive decline. Synaptic dysfunction is strongly associated with cognitive impairment in AD.
28
,
56
,
57
Furthermore, cortical reduction of synaptic density in a cohort of DLB/PDD subjects correlated with cognitive functions.
58
Alterations are region specific but share universal patterns across diseases; interestingly, regional synaptic loss has also been suggested to underlie clinical differences between PD and PDD or DLB.
18
,
59
Proteins involved in glutamatergic, GABA‐ergic, or dopaminergic neurotransmission and synaptic vesicle cycle were notably affected. We, and others, have previously identified associations of several key synaptic proteins with cognitive measures in dementia.
28
,
43
,
60
,
61
Key mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation proteins (e.g., COX7A2, TOMM40L, NDUFV1), and synaptic proteins (e.g., GABRB3, GABRB2, GLUA3, GLUA4, SNAP47, dynamin1) were more abundant in preserved cognitive states, while extracellular matrix proteins and members of the complement system (decorin, biglycan, C4A, C4B) showed strong positive correlation with MMSE decline. Remarkably a handful of synaptic proteins such as SNAP47, GABBR1, or CACNG3 reliably showed a high correlation with both cognitive functions and neuropathological lesions, underscoring the importance of synaptic integrity in dementia progression. In various dementias, alterations in the levels of synaptic proteins represent a critical predictive molecular fingerprint, with a potential target for early disease intervention.
14
,
28
,
62
Understanding these associations could inform therapeutic strategies aimed at preserving or restoring synaptic function in AD and Lewy body dementias.
We have observed high synergy across the prefrontal and parietal cortices across all types of dementia, while the cingulate cortex showed fewer differences between healthy controls and dementias. While many proteins within a given dementia were exclusively dysregulated in specific brain areas, possibly driven by the high degree of functional specialization among the brain regions,
18
most altered pathways across dementias were conserved across brain regions. Alterations in functional connectivity among the various brain regions driven by synaptic dysfunction and Aβ plaque accumulation have been hypothesized to represent an early AD marker,
63
with loss of synaptic proteins predicting functional connectivity alterations. Moreover, in AD, there is a reduction in functional connectivity in the parietal cortex even at early stages, while connectivity increases in the anterior cingulate cortex.
64
Conversely, in PDD, regional efficiency declines in the frontal and parietal regions among individuals with mild cognitive impairment.
65
It is important to acknowledge the limitations of the current study. Although our proteome study yielded ≈ 11,000 proteins, we cannot exclude that some possibly important changes in protein levels may have been overlooked. Biological samples exhibit considerable variability between individuals, and combined with a relatively small sample size, this variability might not be fully captured. Although this pilot study serves primarily as a methodological proof of principle and is underpowered for robust disease characterization, the results are promising and suggest that this approach could be effectively applied in larger scale studies of human tissue, likely leading to more refined identification of protein networks. Given the exploratory nature of the study and the relatively small sample size, correlations with cognitive impairment,
APOE ε
genotype, and neuropathological hallmarks are presented without adjustments for multiple comparisons. These findings should be carefully interpreted, although previous research has already linked a number of these proteins to cognitive trajectories. Additionally, because patients had an advanced disease stage, the proteome profile at an earlier disease stage might differ. Despite these limitations, our comprehensive comparative human brain proteomic study will be an important resource for understanding the molecular profiles and connectivity within brain regions highlighting the synergies and divergencies in the pathologic mechanisms between Lewy body dementias and AD.
Alterations in the protein profile across the studied brain regions in Lewy body dementias and AD reflect the complex interplay of neurodegenerative processes, mitochondrial functions, neuroinflammation, and synaptic dysfunction underlying cognitive impairment. Our finding that the autophagy flux marker p62 and the innate immunity marker TRIM33 can distinguish between Lewy body dementia and AD patients is an exciting development. This finding opens new possibilities for understanding the role of autophagy flux and protein clearance in various dementia. Overall, the disparity in the expression of proteins observed across the brain regions within a dementia diagnosis reflects the complex organization and specialization of the brain, essential to enable the execution of a wide range of functions. Further research with larger sample sizes, ideally including plasma and CSF samples along brain imaging modalities, is needed to fully elucidate the molecular mechanisms driving these alterations and to validate potential therapeutic targets.
