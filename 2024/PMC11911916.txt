INTRODUCTION
Timely cell-state transitions during differentiation are essential for normal development, and defects in these transitions can lead to birth disorders and death.
1
–
6
Gene products that are no longer required or define a different cell state are often cleared from the cell through mechanisms that act at the transcriptional and post-transcriptional levels to allow new deterministic gene products to function in a timely manner. However, it has not been determined how the transcript degradation is precisely timed and executed during cell-state transitions.
The cell type hierarchy of neuroblast lineages in the fly larval brain has been characterized at both the functional and molecular levels.
7
–
9
A wild-type larval brain lobe contains approximately 100 neuroblasts, each of which divides asymmetrically every 60– 90 min to regenerate itself and produce a sibling progeny that commits to differentiation. Eight of these neuroblasts are type II and characterized by the generation of an immature intermediate neural progenitor (INP) in every asymmetric division. Notch signaling must be robustly and rapidly terminated at all levels in newborn immature INPs to permit commitment to an INP identity, which occurs approximately 90 min after neuroblast division.
10
–
14
The brain tumor (Brat) RNA-binding protein (RBP) localizes to the basal cortex of mitotic type II neuroblasts and segregates into newborn immature INPs, where Brat promotes the decay of Notch downstream effector gene transcripts.

–

Numb, a conserved antagonist of Notch signaling, also asymmetrically segregates to newborn immature INPs.

Thus, the Brat-regulated cell-state transition in newborn immature INPs provides a highly sensitive platform for investigating, at single-cell resolution, the assembly and activation of RBP-based complexes that act to destabilize transcripts that would otherwise disrupt cell differentiation.
Brat is a member of the conserved TRI-partite motif-NHL (TRIM-NHL) family of RBPs and is characterized by two N-terminal B boxes, a central coiled-coil domain, and a C-terminal NHL domain.
1
Brat functions through the B boxes to recruit proteins that are essential for Brat-mediated mRNA decay in newborn immature INPs while binding the Brat-responsive element (BRE; WUGUUD) in the 3′ UTRs of target transcripts via the NHL domain.
23
,
29
–
31
Tis11 (the fly homolog of vertebrate tristetraprolin) interacts with Brat via the B boxes and functions together with Brat to degrade Notch downstream effector gene transcripts in newborn immature INPs.
14
Tis11 recognizes the AUUUA pentamer sequence (AU-rich element [ARE]) in the 3′ UTRs of target transcripts and promotes their decay by recruiting the CCR4-NOT deadenylase complex.
32
–
34
Although high-affinity BREs are frequently located in tandem with AREs, overexpressing RNA-binding-defective Tis11 can partially substitute wild-type Tis11 in Brat-mediated repression of Notch downstream effector gene transcripts in newborn immature INPs.
14
Thus, Tis11 likely promotes robust Brat-mediated mRNA decay.
Immunoprecipitation of Brat followed by mass spectrometry (IP-MS) identified ubiquitin-specific protease 5 (Usp5) as an RNA-independent Brat binding partner. Usp5 is required for timely clearance of Brat target mRNAs in early embryos as well as in newborn immature INPs in larval brains. Usp5 promotes Brat-mediated localization and segregation of scaffolding components of the CCR4-Not1 and Pan2-Pan3 deadenylase complexes in the basal cortex of mitotic neuroblasts. The adopter protein Miranda (Mira) binds the coiled-coil and NHL domains of Brat and asymmetrically localizes Brat in mitotic neuroblasts.
18
,
23
Displacement of Mira from the cortex of newborn immature INPs releases its cargo proteins into the cytoplasm.
35
,
36
Transgenic Mira that cannot be displaced retains Brat in the cortex of newborn immature INPs, leading to aberrant expression of Brat target transcripts driving their reversion to supernumerary neuroblasts. Thus, the cortical release of Mira promotes activation of the Brat-deadenylase complex in newborn immature INPs. We propose that assembling an enzymatically inactive and RNA-binding-deficient pre-complex poises the mRNA decay machineries for rapid activation during cell-state transitions and will likely be relevant in most, if not all, similar biological processes throughout metazoans.

Mira binding regulates cell-type-specific activation of the Brat-Usp5 complex
Precise spatiotemporal control of target mRNA acquisition by RBP-deadenylase complexes and activation of their proteolytic activity to induce transcript degradation is essential for timely cell-state transitions, but the mechanisms regulating these key steps are poorly defined. Mira binds its cargo proteins, including Brat, and asymmetrically segregates them into the cortex of neuroblast progeny destined to differentiate. Mira releases its cargo proteins into the cytoplasm of neuroblast progeny when cortically displaced. Brat functions through arginine 875, phenylalanine 916, asparagine 933, and asparagine 976 on the top surface of the NHL domain to bind target mRNAs and through glycine 774 and tyrosine 829 to interact with Mira.

DISCUSSION
Rapid and robust clearance of mRNAs is a critical component of the multilayered gene-regulatory mechanism that drives timely cell-state transitions to ensure normal development.
2
,
5
,
6
The role of RBPs and their downstream effectors in cell-state transitions is well documented, but spatiotemporal control of the assembly and activation of RBP-effector complexes is not completely understood. Our data suggest that the formation of the RBP-deadenylase pre-complex poises the mRNA decay machinery for rapid activation to clear obsolete transcripts, thus allowing for timely cell-state transitions.
Pre-complex assembly allows for precise spatiotemporal control of the mRNA decay machinery
Our study raised important mechanistic questions about the assembly of RBP-effector pre-complexes and the spatiotemporal control of their activity during cell-state transitions in other developmental contexts. Brat is required for clearing subsets of maternally deposited transcripts during the MZT (
Figures 1X
and
S1G
–
S1I
). Previously published sequencing datasets suggested that the degradation of distinct Brat target transcripts (
hb
vs.
GC1
mRNAs) varies by 1–2 NCs (
Figure 7
).
30
,
53
Loss of
usp5
function delayed the clearance of both Brat target transcripts, suggesting that Usp5 is a core component of the Brat-deadenylase complex (
Figure 7
). A top priority for the immediate future is to verify the formation of the Brat-deadenylase pre-complex prior to NC11. Our model predicts that Usp5 and scaffolding components of the deadenylase complexes should be in the complex, whereas enzymatic components should not. Another key experiment is to determine whether an inhibitory protein similar to Mira might exist prior to the MZT. Insights into the spatiotemporal control of Brat-deadenylase complex assembly and activation should provide generalizable knowledge of how other RBP-deadenylase complexes are regulated during developmental transitions.
A conserved role for Usp5 as a scaffolding protein
Hydrolysis of ubiquitin from ubiquitylated proteins or unanchored polyubiquitin chains into free monoubiquitin is the most well-recognized mechanism by which Usp5 regulates biological processes.
41
,
44
,
45
,
54
The ubiquitin-specific processing protease domain of Usp5 exerts deubiquitinase activity, but other domains directly or indirectly influence its catalytic activity. Structural analysis of full-length Usp5 led to the discovery of the cryptic nZn-UBP that tightly interacts with the enzymatic domain and is indispensable for its catalytic activity despite lacking enzymatic activity.
46
Ubiquitin-associated domains (UBAs) in the C terminus indirectly contribute to the deubiquitinase activity of Usp5 by binding ubiquitin.
55
,
56
Studies of the vertebrate immune response suggest that Usp5 can function as a scaffold to promote the assembly of multi-subunit protein complexes independent of its enzymatic activity.
57
,
58
Our data suggest that Usp5 functions as a scaffolding protein independent of its enzymatic activity to promote Brat mRNA decay pre-complex assembly during asymmetric neuroblast division. Identical to the

Structural analyses suggest that the cZn-UBP domain contains a deep binding pocket, while the linkers on either side are disordered and lengthy, allowing for considerable flexibility to accommodate substrates with a variety of structural ensembles within the vicinity of the ubiquitin-specific processing protease domain.
46
,
47
The C-terminal UBA interacts extensively with the ubiquitin-specific processing protease domain, and its loosely tethered nature allows for considerable conformational mobility relative to the ubiquitin-specific processing protease domain, allowing for its binding to a variety of proteins. Thus, we propose that Usp5 functions through the cZn-UBP domain and ubiquitin-specific processing protease domain to promote the assembly of the Brat pre-complex binding to Brat.
Limitations of the study
Our biochemical, functional and genetic analyses suggest that Usp5 interacts with Brat to promote the formation of the Brat-deadenylase pre-complex. We attempted to confirm a direct Brat-Usp5 interaction using larval brain extract enriched with type II neuroblast proteins but were unable to co-immunoprecipitate Brat and Usp5 from protein lysates extracted from type II neuroblasts. One possible interpretation is that an intermediary protein is required to facilitate Brat’s interaction with Usp5. Alternatively, Usp5 might exist in a separate sub-complex that depends on Brat for asymmetric localization and segregation in mitotic neuroblasts. We will test other candidate Brat-interacting proteins identified from IP-MS to better define the core components of the Brat-deadenylase complex.

Immunoprecipitation and mass spectrometry
Methods were as described in Laver et al.
30
0–3-h old embryos were collected, dechorionated and crushed in a minimal volume of lysis buffer (150 mM KCl, 20 mM HEPES-KOH pH 7.4, 1 mM MgCl2, 0.1% Triton X-100, supplemented with protease inhibitors and 1 mM DTT), cleared by centrifugation for 15 min at 4°C and 20,000 g, and stored at −80°C. Immediately prior to immunoprecipitation (IP), sample was diluted to 15 mg/mL protein concentration with lysis buffer. For IP, 800 μL of diluted lysate was mixed with or without 350 μg/μL RNase A, 40 μL of anti-Flag M2 beads (Sigma) that were pre-loaded with 20 μg of either anti-BratA1 Fab antibody or control C1 Fab antibody. IPs were incubated for 3-h at 4°C with end-over-end rotation. Beads were washed four times with lysis buffer, twice with lysis buffer lacking Triton X-100, then transferred to new tubes and washed twice more with lysis buffer lacking Triton X-100 to remove all traces of detergent. Bound proteins were eluted by tryptic digest: beads were resuspended in 200 μL of 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate pH 8, supplemented with 2 μg of trypsin, and incubated overnight at room temperature with end-over-end rotation. The following day, the digested supernatant was recovered, and beads were washed once with an additional 200 μL of 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate to collect any residual eluted material. The two supernatants were pooled and dried by speed-vac. Liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) was performed using a Thermo Q-Exactive HF quadrupole-Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) following previously described
methods
.
71
–
73
Three biological replicates of Brat IPs and control IPs were performed, in both the presence and absence of RNase A.
To identify Brat-interacting proteins, we used the ProHits software package
74
to perform Significance Analysis of INTeractome (SAINT), comparing RNase-treated Brat IP versus control IP samples, and non-RNase-treated Brat IP versus control IP samples. Specifically, SAINT input files were generated using the “TPP iProphet” search engine option in ProHits, filtering for iProphet probability ≥ 0.9 and number of unique peptides <2. SAINTexpress (exp3.3) was run using the ProHits interface, including only detected Drosophila proteins, with the following settings: number of compressed controls = 3; burn-in period, nburn = 2000; iterations, niter = 5000; lowMode = 1; minFold = 1; normalize = 1; nCompressBaits = 2. Identified proteins were defined as RNA-independent or RNA-dependent Brat-interacting proteins, if, in the analyses of the respective samples, they achieved a SAINT score R R 0.9 and a Bayesian false discovery rate (BFDR) < 0.05.
