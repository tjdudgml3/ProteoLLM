INTRODUCTION
Photoaffinity labeling (PAL), also called affinity photocrosslinking or affinity photolabeling is a well‐established method to study noncovalent interactions between biomolecules that was introduced in the early 70's by Singer and Doolittle (
1966
). It is a simple and direct way to stabilize noncovalent interactions by creating a covalent bond under UV irradiation of the sample, thus enabling simplified downstream sample handling and analysis. It can be implemented in vitro on model systems, or in a native context, and gives the possibility to identify close atomic contacts even in a complex environment. This approach, however, is not always easy to implement and many difficulties can be encountered. These difficulties are related to (i) the incorporation of the photoreactive label in the molecular structure of one of the interacting partners, (ii) positioning and reactivity of the photoreactive label to avoid disrupting/modifying the naturally occurring interactions, (iii) photoadduct isolation, and (iv) sample analysis. The use of photoreactive molecules to probe molecular interactions has recently re‐attracted researchers' attention. Coupling PAL to highly sensitive analytical methods, that is, subpicomolar, such as mass spectrometry (MS) and proteomics greatly enhances the performance of the approach, knowing that photolabeling yields are usually low regardless of the chosen photoreactive label. The emergence of efficient biorthogonal reactions such as click‐chemistry allows sample post‐functionalization and significantly improves issues related to sample recovery and enrichment that are essential to get reliable information.
In this review, we will focus on the identification of peptide partners using PAL‐MS in different contexts such as in vitro with a purified potential partner, in model systems such as model membranes, and with live cells as summarized in Figure
1
. Different biological partners will be described, among which glycoconjugates, oligonucleotides, peptides, proteins, and lipids, with the photoreactive label inserted either on the peptide of interest or on the potential partner.

To identify and characterize the unknown interaction partner, or to characterize the site of interaction of the photoreactive partner on the peptide of interest, formed photoadducts are classically chemically cleaved or enzymatically digested, purified, and the resulting cross‐linked products are analyzed by MS or LC‐MS and MS/MS in a so‐called bottom‐up strategy, involving global or targeted proteomic approaches. A purification step involving an affinity tag such as biotin can be performed before or after sample chemical or enzymatic digestion. These final treatment steps can be skipped, allowing the identification of the intact photoadduct, which can be relevant in some applications.
PAL relies on the singular photochemical properties of three main types of photoreactive labels: aryl azides, diazirines, and benzophenone (Bzp). Under irradiation at the appropriate wavelength, these stable precursors generate highly reactive species such as carbenes and/or radicals, which in turn react extremely quickly with molecules in their immediate environment, typically binding partners. An ideal photoreactive label should meet different prerequisites. (i) It should be relatively small compared to the native probe, not to induce steric hindrance and alter the interaction. (ii) Its activation wavelength should not damage the biomolecules of interest. (iii) The life span of the photoactivated label should be short enough to allow formation of a covalent bond with the partner before dissociation, and long enough to form the photoadduct in sufficient quantity to be detected. (iv) It should photocrosslink any partner in its immediate surrounding, regardless of its chemical nature, with the same efficiency and without a specificity biais. The specificity and accuracy of the photolabeling process may be considerably impaired if the photoprobe crosslinks distant more reactive functions instead of less reactive or even inert functions located in its immediate proximity. (v) Finally, the photoreactivity of the photolabel should be well known and lead to a unique photoproduct, as far as possible, to avoid dilution of photolabeled species and ambiguous partner identification.
After presenting the reactivities of aryl azide, diazirine, and Bzp labels, we will present various examples of global and targeted proteomic studies involving PAL‐MS applied to the identification of peptides interacting partners. For the global proteomics approaches, we will focus on the critical design strategy of the photoprobe with the choice of the nature and position of the photoreactive label and the affinity tag in the sequence of the modified peptide, lipid, sugar or oligonucleotide partner, without giving too many details on the addressed biological question. As for targeted proteomics examples, relevant secondary reactions will be presented. Tandem MS fragmentation of photoadducts will be discussed as a function of the activation energy and some unexpected peaks observed in the MS/MS spectra corresponding to secondary rearrangements will be presented.
This review should help researchers who are interested in building PAL‐MS strategies, starting at the design of the photoprobe to the validation of the photolabeling data, to get a fully integrated understanding of biological interaction networks from the molecular to the cellular level.

PHOTOREACTIVITY OF THREE PHOTOLABELS CLASSICALLY USED IN PAL‐MS STUDIES
Design and interpretation of PAL‐MS experiments can turn out highly challenging without a good molecular understanding of the reactivity of the photolabels in the presence of biomolecules and/or in a biological environment. Therefore, we found necessary to get back to the basis and emphasize less known photoreactivities, limitations, advantages and drawbacks of the three classically used photolabels: aryl azides, diazirines, and Bzp, applied to peptide interactions with various partners such as proteins, lipids, glycoconjugates, or oligonucleotides. Many reviews concerning the reactivities of these three photolabels are available (Dormán et al.,

2.1
Azides
Aryl azides were first reported as potential reagents for photolabeling of biological receptors in 1969 (Fleet et al.,
1969

Therefore, intramolecular rearrangements do not compete effectively with intermolecular abstraction and insertion and aryl azides lead to reasonable yields of efficient crosslinking. Both aryl and alkyl azides are reported in the literature as efficient PAL reagents. Aryl azides will be described first.

2.1.5
Advantages and drawbacks
A major drawback of the azido groups is their maximum absorption wavelength below 300 nm. When irradiation is performed at 350 nm, which is compatible with biological systems, crosslinking yields are relatively low (<30%). Hence, popularity of azides in PAL studies is more based on the relative ease of preparation and incorporation into natural products rather than on their photochemical properties and performance as a label.

2.2.5
Advantages and drawbacks
Major advantages of the diazirine, especially TFMD, compared to azides are their absorption at a wavelength of 350–360 nm which does not damage biological systems and their relative stability towards a wide variety of environments, including strongly acidic, strongly basic, oxidizing and reducing conditions. Drawbacks of diazirines are the rapid quenching of the carbenes by water and the formation the diazo species after photolysis, even if this reactivity is minimized with TFMD compared to alkyl diazirine. However, TFMD is not always the best choice due to its bulky size and hydrophobicity.

3
PAL‐MS STUDIES TO DECIPHER MOLECULAR INTERACTIONS INVOLVING PEPTIDES
Peptides are short amino acid polymers, linear, cyclic or with more complex branched structures, with usually less than 50 residues. There are numerous families of peptides classified according to their source and function. They can be synthesized through ribosomal or nonribosomal pathways. They frequently have posttranslational modifications (PTMs) and play various and important roles in cells signaling, by interacting and regulating different biological systems. Among this large family, we can cite for example neuropeptides; peptide hormones; toxins; opioid peptides; membrane‐active peptides, such as antimicrobian peptides (AMPs), cell penetrating peptides (CPPs), amyloid peptides, short trans‐membrane segments and fusion peptides. In addition, naturally occurring peptides can be modified by other families of biomolecules, such as lipid‐peptides conjugates (lipopeptides) or carbohydrate‐peptide conjugates (glycopeptides, peptidoglycan…).
Aside those natural peptides, for which the understanding of the mechanisms of action and interaction with their biological partners is crucial, for instance in the context of further therapeutic developments, short synthetic peptides are more and more used as biological tools, especially to mimic protein–protein interactions (PPIs), protein–glycoconjugates or protein–nucleic acids interactions. Peptides represent the simplest functional protein mimetic, but when removed from their full protein sequence context, peptides usually fail to adopt the proper bioactive conformation, affecting their affinity and/or activity. Methods allowing more stable 3D organization of relevant chemical functions found on peptide side‐chains have been developed over the last century to compensate. Peptide‐based interaction proteomics recently emerged as a powerful technology to study PPIs (Meyer & Selbach,
2020
). However, the reversible and transient nature of PPIs, and more generally of any peptide/partner interactions, makes them difficult to study. Therefore, PAL is a well‐suited approach to overcome these issues and study peptide interactions in biological environments. Combining PAL with high‐throughput sequencing and MS‐based “omics” significantly increases the depth of information that can be obtained regarding the identification of the partners, the stoichiometry, as well as the description of the molecular mechanisms and/or interactions at the atomic level. However, mapping the interacting regions remains a great challenge, mainly because of the difficulties in the enrichment step and the low yield of photocrosslinking. Finding the ideal photoaffinity probe, containing a photolabel and an affinity tag (e.g., biotin) or bioorthogonal handles (e.g., alkyne), which can be cleavable or not, can reveal fastidious, mainly due to the negative impact of these modifications on the biological activity/interaction.
Photolabels can be introduced either on the peptide during peptide synthesis or in the potential partner of the peptide of interest. For example, in the context of membrane‐active peptides (see Section
3.2.5
), when looking for lipid partners, photoreactive lipids can be introduced in the membrane. Both strategies are complementary and do not provide the same information. Photoreactive peptides will give information on the site of interaction of the peptide within the membrane, whereas photoreactive lipids will inform on the peptide region or residue that are directly in interaction with the photoreactive lipid. In all cases, checking the affinity and/or activity of the modified peptide probe or the modified partner is a prerequisite before starting any PAL experiment.
MS identification and characterization of the photoadducts, usually require
prior
purification to get enough material, and chemical or enzymatic cleavage(s) to allow efficient detection and fragmentation. The relatively high mass of the photoadducts and, thus, the large number of degrees of freedom is not in favor of an effective fragmentation through low energy collision induced dissociation. On the other hand, enzymatic digestion can be impaired when the covalent bond is located on the residue that is recognized/cleaved by the enzyme or by the presence of a bulky photolabel close to this residue (Jahn et al.,
2002
,
2004
). In addition, fragmentation spectra obtained for a precursor ion where two partners are covalently linked are complex and contain fragmentation patterns of both species as well as possible internal fragmentations. This requires dedicated software taking into account special features of cross‐linked entities such as their fragmentation pathways (Tinnefeld et al.,
2014
). Specific computer programs have been developed to deal with chemical crosslinking, StavroX is dedicated to data interpretation of the fragmentation of peptide–peptide adducts (Götze et al.,
2012
), MeroX (Götze et al.,
2015
), or Mango (Mohr et al.,
2018
) have been designed to specifically deal with MS/MS‐cleavable cross‐linkers. PAL experiments require more complex data processing, analysis and validation since the site of the covalent bond is unknown. In the litterature, global proteomics experiments mainly compair identified proteins obtained with and without PAL. Most of the widely applied searching programs, are not developed and thus are not suitable, for analyzing fragmentation spectra from photocrosslinked peptides. In the case of more targeted proteomic studies, when at least one partner is known, characterization of the interaction site can be envisaged, even if challenging, by setting the modification as variable on any of the residues. A manual validation, by calculating the possible crosslinked fragments with the GPMAW program or PeptideMass (Expasy), for example, is always needed. Automation of the data treatment is required to extend this strategy to unknown interacting partners.
Examples detailed in this review will cover the cases of global proteomics in Section
3.1
and more targeted studies in Section
3.2
. For ease of reading, we chose a color code and all the figures throughout the manuscript will be organized in the same way, with the photolabels colored in red, the affinity purification or tags or reactive handles in purple, cleavable linkers in green, and partners in blue.
3.1
Global proteomics studies to identify peptide partners
Intentionally, we will not detail the workflows of the proteomic experiments, unless it is of particular interest, nor comment on the identified biological partners or their biological relevance. Instead, we decided to focus on the critical design of the photoprobe with the choice of the nature and position of the photolabel and the affinity tag in the structure of the photoprobes or an original synthetic route. The following examples will describe different PAL‐MS strategies involving global proteomics to understand peptides mode of action, to identify PTMs protein readers, to map PPIs or to identify peptide partners at the cell membrane.

The first example is a study from Zhai (

PAL‐MS using photoreactive structured peptides or new crosslinking reagents to map PPIs

3.2
Targeted studies to identify peptide partners, peptide structures, and modes of action
When the biological question is not anymore the identification of all potential partners of a specific peptide in a complex cellular environment, but the characterization of known or suspected interacting partners, biochemical approaches such as co‐immunoprecipitation or pull‐down assays, chemical crosslinking, phage display, tandem affinity purification, or biophysical techniques such as Fluorescence Resonance Energy Transfer (FRET), Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR), Isothermal Titration Calorimetry (ITC), NMR… are available. When the initial question is the fine characterization of the interaction site or region between known interacting partners, PAL coupled to targeted proteomic studies is a method of choice.
The next examples will deal with targeted PAL‐MS studies. Despite the a priori lower complexity of the samples compared to global proteomic studies, these experiments generally require more material to be able to perform MS/MS characterization, to obtain a deeper and finer level of information compared to global proteomics approaches. Therefore, enrichment of the photoadducts, via affinity purification is still necessary. MS/MS data interpretation usually require manual assignments in addition to conventional data interpretation software. In the following examples, PAL‐MS will be used to study PTMs of proteins, enzymes catalytic sites, neuropeptides interaction with their receptors, peptides structures, membrane‐active peptides interaction with membranes. In these studies, unexpected photoreactivities or MS/MS fragmentation have been observed and will be discussed. Photolabeling to study glycopeptides interactions with membranes will also be presented through various examples. A final study with the use of Ru
II
‐TAP complexes as a crosslinking tool will open new perspectives to probe protein‐protein but also protein–DNA interactions.

Lasso peptides structure—The case of lariatin
: lasso peptides represent an emerging class of bacterial peptides. They are functionally and structurally different from the known peptide antibiotics. Their known antibacterial targets include RNA polymerase, lipid II, and the Clp protease involved in protein homeostasis (Tan et al.,
2019
). They are ribosomally synthesized and post‐translationally modified (RiPPs). They have a specific topology and their biosynthesis requires the presence of at least three partners A, B, C, the corresponding genes of which are located in a biosynthesis gene cluster. The precursor lasso peptide A, contains an
N
‐terminal “leader” region and a
C
‐terminal “core” region. A specific motif of the leader region is recognized by a RiPP recognition element which allows the recruitment of the biosynthetic enzymes which are required for the structure. A site‐specific protease B removes the leader region. In some actinobacteria and firmicutes, protease B comprises two proteins, protein B1, involved in the recognition of the precursor peptide A, and protein B2 which hydrolyzes the precursor and eliminates the leader peptide. Then, the enzyme C or lasso cyclase probably catalyzes the adenylation of an acidic residue (Asp or Glu) of the core peptide while inducing a lasso prestructure. The
N
‐terminus of the released core peptide then attacks the adenylated residue to form a macro‐lactam ring of 7 to 9 residues while the
C
‐terminus of the peptide is inserted into the lactam ring. This very compact lasso structure is thermostable and resistant to proteases. Only the cyclic peptide with the linear
C
‐terminal part can be obtained by chemical synthesis. When studied, lasso peptides are usually produced by heterologous expression.
The soil bacterium
Rhodococcus jostii
, produces the antimicrobial lasso peptide lariatin whose 3‐D structure requires both B1 and B2 enzymes. In the study from Cheung et al. (
2016

The precursor lasso peptide was produced as a fusion with the Maltose Binding Protein (MBP) that can be cleaved with the Factor Xa which recognizes the IEGR motif between the peptide sequence and the MBP. To introduce the photolabel, authors first replaced Phe21 from the precursor lasso peptide A sequence with the photoreactive aminoacid Phe(N

This was performed by introducing an amber codon at the position of the Phe21 and using an engineered aminoacyl‐tRNA synthetase/tRNA pair for the site‐specific introduction of the photoreactive residue. After incubation of the photoreactive lasso precursor A with the B1 enzyme, they irradiated the complex at 365 nm and the free B1 enzyme was eliminated using a 30 kDa cutoff filter. MBP was cleaved off by the Factor Xa and the resulting mixture was purified by IMAC chromatography to isolate the photoadducts containing the His‐tag. The photoadducts were then digested by trypsin and analyzed by MALDI‐TOF. Based on the

As previously stated, MS/MS fragmentation of photoadducts is not always straightforward and data interpretation often challenging. The knowledge of the cross‐linking site and residue remains difficult to extract from the list of branched peptide fragments even with known interacting partners in vitro. A general workflow to characterize interacting systems using PAL‐MS and different activation technics such as CID, ETD, and HCD was then developed (Clavier et al.,

Bovine serum albumin (BSA) is a negatively charged protein known to interact with cationic carrier peptides via electrostatic interactions to form peptide/protein complexes that can be delivered into cells. Arginine‐rich CPPs are known to interact with serum proteins like albumin, modifying their ability to internalize in cells (Kosuge et al.,

CONCLUSION AND PERSPECTIVES
Affinity photocrosslinking was first introduced in the 1960s but 60 years after, it is still widely used, especially when coupled to sensitive and performant analytical methods like MS, MS/MS, and proteomics. PAL combines the efficiency of crosslinking methods such as chemical crosslinking, with the high control provided by activation upon irradiation. Exploiting photochemical properties of photoreactive molecules is a strategy that is widely used for applications in biochemistry and biology, beyond photocrosslinking, for example for photo‐uncaging or photo‐isomerization.
Herein, we proposed a comprehensive review of the existing literature on PAL‐based studies coupled to MS aimed at deciphering peptides mechanism of action. These studies allowed identification and characterization of binding partners of different nature: peptides and proteins, lipids, glycoconjugates and nucleic acids. The scale of applications ranges from identification of a large number of proteins to the characterization of binding sites at the atomic level. PAL strategies rely on the careful design of photoprobes and the unique reactivity of essentially three families of photolabels: azides, diazirines, and Bzp. Secondary reactions can be a source of difficulties, but can also be advantageously exploited, as illustrated by some examples in this review.
