1
Introduction
The analysis of biomolecules is crucial across various applications, including disease diagnostics [
1
], pharmaceutical development [
2
], the study of biomolecular interactions [
3
], and biomarker discovery [
4
]. Biomolecular research typically focuses on compounds associated with biological functions, such as proteins, peptides, oligonucleotides, and other metabolites (e.g., lipids, carbohydrates, among others) [
5
]. In the case of proteins, which are essential components of organisms involved in numerous critical life functions, their analysis is vital for proteomics and therapeutic research, as discussed later.
Therefore, there is an urgent need for fast and reliable analytical techniques and methodologies to afford the structure analysis and quantitation of major and minor components for early diagnosis through the study of interactions and understanding of biomolecular functions. Additionally, these techniques and methodologies can be extremely useful for developing more specific drugs that can help to raise patient outcomes. For this purpose, several advanced analytical techniques have been employed, including liquid chromatography (LC) coupled with ultraviolet (UV)–visible, fluorescence, and/or mass spectrometry (MS) detectors [
6
,
7
,
8
], enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) [
9
], spectrophotometry and fluorescence [
10
], electrochemical sensing [
11
], polymerase chain reaction (PCR) [
12
], vibration spectroscopy [
5
,
13
], as well as point‐of‐care testing platforms for biomolecule analysis [
14
]. Nevertheless, the potential of capillary electrophoresis (CE) has often been overlooked. CE, especially when combined with MS (CE–MS), offers significant advantages, such as low consumption of sample, solvents, and reagents (in contrast to conventional LC), reduced ion suppression and better sensitivity than direct MS analysis, good separation efficiency and recovery, short analysis time, instrumental simplicity, and full automation. These features are particularly beneficial for analyzing biomolecules such as proteins [
15
], peptides [
16
], and oligonucleotides [
17
]. Nevertheless, lipid analysis using CE–MS has been less explored, with most of the studies focused on their use as additives to the background electrolyte (BGE) [
18
] instead of their determination and characterization. Regarding carbohydrates, non‐MS detectors, such as UV (direct and indirect), capacitively coupled contactless conductivity (C4D), and laser‐induced fluorescence (LIF), have been more utilized [
19
]. Nevertheless, CE–MS proves to be an intriguing alternative for analyzing other biomolecules, including amines, phosphoderivatives, drugs, and metabolites from contaminants.
CE is a highly adaptable technique with numerous modes, each differing in attributes and offering multiple approaches to obtain the desired results. [
20
,
21
]. Capillary zone electrophoresis (CZE) stands out as the most commonly used mode, accompanied by others, such as microchipCZE (mCZE), affinity CE (ACE), multisegment injection (MSI)‐CE, capillary isotachophoresis (CITP), and non‐aqueous CE (NACE), each finding applications in diverse fields. CZE and mCZE are often employed for quantitative biomolecular analysis and/or rapid diagnostics. ACE proves valuable for studying biomolecular interactions, such as protein–ligand binding or enzyme–substrate interactions. MSI‐CE is ideal for high‐throughput analysis and screening of multiple samples, whereas CITP allows effective preconcentration, especially when dealing with complex mixtures. In addition, CITP can also be used to separate proteins based on their isoelectric point (p
I
). It is important to note that, although the aforementioned modes are suitable for analyzing hydrophilic biomolecules, the NACE mode is more appropriate for the analysis of hydrophobic biomolecules, such as lipids.
In any case, the selection of the CE mode is closely linked to the BGE, which serves as the buffer transporting the analytes to the detector inside the CE capillary. Therefore, optimizing the BGE is crucial for achieving high efficiency and effective separation of the target analytes. Another intriguing aspect is the selection of the appropriate capillary material. In general, non‐coated fused silica (FS) capillaries are most prevalent. FS capillaries offer numerous advantages, including cost‐effectiveness, ease of surface modification, small internal diameter, excellent thermal stability, and high mechanical strength and flexibility. Nevertheless, in order to avoid the adsorption of high molecular mass (
M
r
) molecules, particularly proteins, into the inner capillary walls, coating is often mandatory [
22
]. Coatings typically involve cationic or neutral polymers such as polyethyleneimine (PEI) or polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), respectively, among others. Although materials other than FS, such as polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE), can be used [
23
], the use of FS remains predominant among CE capillary materials. It is important to remark that in the case of mCZE, non‐FS capillaries are employed. Instead, etched glass or polymer microchips provide the channels for the CE separation [
24
].
CE instruments can be coupled with various detectors, including UV–visible, C4D, LIF, and MS. However, despite the versatility, MS offers distinct advantages over other detectors. For instance, MS provides high sensitivity and selectivity, low limits of detection (LODs), and the unique ability to identify thousands of biomolecules in a single run. Additionally, the hyphenation of CE with MS has shown excellent performance [
25
] due to the minimal sample volume requirement, uniform separation environment, analyte concentration capability, and rapid analysis. Moreover, CE modes are well suited for integration with miniaturized microfluidic systems and can be readily interfaced with MS.
In general, electrospray ionization (ESI) is the most commonly employed ionization source for biomolecular analysis in CE–MS [
26
], although atmospheric pressure chemical ionization (APCI) [
27
] has also been explored. The integration of CE with ESI–MS presents several challenges that necessitate careful optimization of the interface to achieve a balance among robustness, sensitivity, and reproducibility. Two primary types of interfaces are typically utilized: sheathflow microESI and sheathless nanoESI. Sheathflow interfaces are the most widely used due to their ability to provide stable spray formation that is independent of the electro‐osmotic flow (EOF) and their greater flexibility in selecting the BGE [
28
]. This combination of stability and adaptability makes them highly reliable across diverse applications. However, a significant drawback of these interfaces is the dilution of the sample caused by the sheath liquid, which can diminish separation efficiency and alter the molecular conformation of biomolecules, depending on the composition of the sheath liquid [
29
]. In contrast, sheathless interfaces offer superior sensitivity by eliminating the sheath liquid, thereby preserving the original sample concentration and enabling undiluted nanoflow for more efficient ionization. A notable example is the high‐sensitivity porous sprayer (HSPS) sheathless interface, commercially available from AB Sciex and based on Moini's design. This interface has garnered considerable attention for its ability to increase sensitivity by 10‐ to 100‐fold [
30
]. Its design involves etching the capillary terminus to create a porous wall that facilitates electrical conductivity when in contact with a conductive solution. Despite this significant increase in sensitivity, sheathless interfaces face limitations related to reduced stability and the technical challenges of establishing a reliable electrical contact at the capillary tip. These issues, combined with the higher costs associated with commercially available setups, have hindered their broader application in routine biomolecular analysis [
28
,
30
,
31
]. Nevertheless, there is substantial potential for sheathless CE–MS to enhance the analysis of biomolecules in complex samples, and further research is essential to fully exploit this capability.
As commented above, CE–MS offers several advantages for advanced biomolecular research due to the synergistic benefits derived from their combination. However, effectively harnessing these advantages to achieve desired results remains a challenge. Therefore, here we present a comprehensive overview of the most relevant CE–MS applications published in the literature between 2020 and 2024 for the analysis of intact proteins, peptides, oligonucleotides, and other metabolites within the biomolecular field. Additionally, we summarize their advantages, disadvantages, and future perspectives, aiming to provide insights into the current state and potential advancements in biomolecular analysis.

M
r

1
refer to the development of methodologies with standard solutions or drug formulations. The utilization of coated capillaries was essential in these studies, aiming to prevent protein adsorption on the inner capillary walls [
25
,

,
33
]. To tackle this challenge, the adoption of permanent coatings, including cationic and neutral types, was imperative. Cationic coatings like PEI were utilized to suppress ionic interactions, whereas neutral coatings, such as polyethylene oxide, linear carbohydrate polymer, and PVA, among others, were used to effectively cover the silanol groups of the inner capillary surface [
32
]. Regarding BGEs, acidic solutions containing acetic acid (HAc, pH around 2) were employed to weaken the protein adsorption by reducing the silica net charge close to zero [
32
]. However, to prevent protein unfolding, aggregation, and precipitation under highly acidic conditions, NH
4

54
]. Some applications also evaluated the addition of organic solvents, such as 2‐propanol (2‐PrOH), methanol (MeOH), or dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO), to the BGE, as they can mitigate hydrophobic interactions and protein conformational changes involved in the adsorption process [
55
]. Detailed information of these applications can be found in Table
1
.
Besides the challenge of protein adsorption on the inner walls of FS capillaries, another factor that could limit the use of CE–MS in biomolecular research is the reduced sample loading capacity of CE, as seen in other microseparation techniques (i.e., injection volumes of nL of sample) [
32
,
33
]. This constraint leads to low‐concentration sensitivity, even when coupled to MS, and it is particularly detrimental for the analysis of low‐abundance protein biomarkers and biopharmaceuticals [
56
]. To overcome this issue, off‐line sample clean‐up and preconcentration methods have been explored, including filtration with molecular mass cut‐off (MWCO) centrifugal filters, as shown in some of the applications detailed in Table
1
. However, chromatographic preconcentration techniques based on online solid‐phase extraction (SPE)–CE offer a more practical solution to reduce the LODs while minimizing sample handling [
56
]. In the valve‐free SPE–CE–MS setup, a microcartridge with a sorbent that selectively retains the target analyte is integrated near the inlet of the separation capillary, operating in a unidirectional configuration [
29
,
44
]. This enables loading a large sample volume (typically ∼50–100 µL) for selective clean‐up and preconcentration, followed by injection of a small eluent volume (typically ∼25–50 nL) for subsequent electrophoretic separation and selective detection by MS [
56
]. SPE–CE–MS with different sorbents (e.g., aptamer or antibody‐functionalized particles and monoliths) has demonstrated utility for analyzing intact protein biomarkers across various biomolecular applications [
51
,
53
,
56
,
57
]. For instance, Pero‐Gascon et al. [
51
] introduced aptamer affinity SPE–CE–MS (AA‐SPE–CE–MS) for the purification, preconcentration, separation, and characterization of α‐synuclein (α‐syn), a key protein in Parkinson's disease (PD). Under the optimized conditions, the method showed good repeatability and microcartridge lifetime (>20 analyses), with an LOD of 0.2 µg mL
−1
, 100 times lower than by CE–MS (20 µg mL
−1
) (Figure
1A
) [
51
]. Finally, the method was applied to the analysis of α‐syn from red blood cell lysates (RBCs) of healthy controls and PD patients after a simple thermoenrichment clean‐up pretreatment, indicating that
N
‐acetylated α‐syn is the main proteoform of α‐syn in blood (Figure
1B
) [
51
].

2.2
Peptides
Peptides play crucial roles in both normal physiological and pathophysiological processes, making them valuable clinical biomarkers. Due to their ability to transfer across endothelial barriers by different mechanisms (passive diffusion, receptor‐mediated transport, or endocytosis), they can be analyzed in various biological fluids, including urine, blood, and cerebrospinal fluid [
58
]. Similar to protein biomarkers, the presence or abnormal concentration of certain peptides can indicate the presence, stage, or progression of disorders, such as cancer [
59
,
60
], liver‐related diseases [
61
], or neurodegenerative diseases [
62
,
63
,
64
,
65
], among others.
CE–MS has proven indispensable for analyzing peptide biomarkers, with the bottom‐up analysis approach being extensively utilized in CE–MS‐based proteomics [
66
]. In this approach, samples undergo enzymatic digestion, typically using trypsin as the gold standard, to convert proteins into peptides, which are subsequently separated, detected, and identified by CE–MS. LC–MS has also been widely applied for this purpose [
62
,
67
,
68
]. However, CE–MS offers several advantages, including shorter analysis times, reduced solvent consumption, and lower instrumentation costs. For example, in clinical diagnosis, one of the most widespread applications of CE–MS is the study of urinary peptide biomarkers for renal and urological diseases, as well as kidney and cardiovascular diseases [
69
]. This is because urine is a rich source of biomarkers and offers several benefits over other biological fluids, including high stability and easy, non‐invasive accessibility [
70
]. Moreover, peptides present in urine can provide valuable insights into specific diseases, and PTMs, especially glycosylation, can serve as relevant biomarkers for neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer's disease [
71
]. Table
2
provides an overview of the most relevant CE–MS applications published from 2020 to 2024 for the analysis of peptide biomarkers within the biomolecular field.

Oligonucleotides
The characterization of oligonucleotides is rapidly advancing within the field of biomolecular research. Oligonucleotides primarily function as small RNA molecules, notably microRNAs (miRNAs), which regulate gene expression or act as degradation intermediates resulting from the breakdown of larger nucleic acid molecules [
79
,
80
,
81
]. MiRNAs, typically comprising from 19 to 23 nucleotides, play a pivotal role in governing various physiological and pathological processes. Given their observed dysregulation in diseases, such as cancer, neurodegenerative disorders, and immune‐related conditions, miRNAs exhibit considerable potential as biomarkers for diagnosing, monitoring, and predicting disease progression and hold promise for advancing therapeutic interventions [
79
]. A wide array of bioanalytical methods has been developed for measuring oligonucleotides. Although PCR remains the gold standard [
82
], LC–MS stands out as one of the most commonly used techniques [
83
]. However, LC–MS analysis often requires lengthy sample run times to achieve separation of multiple oligonucleotide species. Additionally, LC methods frequently involve the use of mobile phase additives such as triethylamine (TEA) and 1,1,1,3,3,3‐hexafluoro‐2‐propanol (HFIP), which can lead to ion suppression and contamination of the MS instrument [
79
].
As an alternative, CE–MS is emerging as a promising technique, although the literature on this subject is limited (see Table
3
). PP CZE modes have been utilized for the analysis of phosphodiester and phosphorothioate oligonucleotides [
84
] and miRNAs [
85
], employing uncoated FS capillaries and BGEs composed of NH
4
Ac solutions with a pH range of 6–8. Additionally, sheathflow microESI interfaces have been employed, operating in positive ESI mode with sheath liquids containing hydroorganic solutions in the absence or presence of low concentrations of ammonium acetate [
84
,
85
]. As an example, Vásconez et al. [
85
] were the first to compare the performance of zwitterionic–hydrophilic interaction capillary LC–MS (capZIC‐HILIC‐MS) and CE–MS for the direct and multiplex analysis of three cancer‐related miRNAs. Although capZIC‐HILIC‐MS achieved approximately five times lower LODs compared to CE–MS (1 vs. 5 µM), CE–MS demonstrated superior repeatability with spiked serum samples, along with reduced costs, longer capillary column lifespan, and shorter conditioning times. However, despite these encouraging results, the sensitivity was limited, underscoring the necessity for improved interfaces and platforms (e.g., sheathless nanoESI interfaces, ZipChip platforms) to enhance ionization efficiency. A recent research of Lippens et al. [
86
] has delved into this area, exploring the effectiveness of mCZE‐MS via a ZipChip interface for the rapid analysis of short oligonucleotides (ranging from 18 to 25 nucleotides). The results unveiled promising applications for this integrated microchip CE–MS platform, offering significant advantages such as minimal sample requirements and reduced solvent needs, in line with the principles of green chemistry, as well as reduced LODs (50–250 nM, depending on the chip used) [
86
].

Some researchers have also delved into the analysis of other intriguing molecules, such as amines. Two interesting works have been published for the analysis of polyamines [
88

89

In the first case, it is noteworthy that the methodology, employing an FS capillary and an acidic BGE (1 M HFor), allowed the analysis of up to 40 discrete samples of polyamines in saliva samples within one single run using the MSI mode. This amount of samples allows the screening of polyamines at a rhythm of 1 sample per min. Additionally, the proposed methodology was able to identify a promising biomarker for colorectal cancer (N1‐acetylspermine). In the second case, the optimized method allowed the detection of 12 biogenic amines in human urine after a simple sample pretreatment (dilution), providing the possibility to use them as biomarkers of inflammatory bowel diseases. MSI–CE, in combination with a sheathflow microESI interface, was also used to detect 1‐hydroxypyrene glucuronide in urine samples, a metabolite of pyrene [
90
]. In this case, MSI allowed the analysis of up to 13 discrete samples in one single run using an FS capillary and a BGE consisting of a 50 mM NH
4
Ac solution. However, prior to CE separation, a liquid extraction step using methyl‐
tert
‐butyl ether was required, adding complexity to the methodology.

4

3
Conclusions
CE–MS stands out as a flexible and powerful analytical technique in biomolecular research, offering distinct advantages for the separation and analysis of a wide range of biomolecules, including proteins, peptides, oligonucleotides, and other metabolites. CE–MS effectively combines fast separation, low reagent and sample consumption, high selectivity, and reduced ion suppression, providing superior sensitivity compared to direct MS analysis. Modes such as CZE and mCZE are commonly employed for the quantitative analysis of biomolecules. ACE is valuable for studying biomolecular interactions, whereas NACE is preferred for the analysis of hydrophobic biomolecules, such as lipids.

The hyphenation with ESI–MS facilitates high‐throughput and reliable analyses, significantly enhancing sensitivity and selectivity. In this context, sheathless nanoESI interfaces are gaining attention due to their ability to eliminate sheath liquid and enable undiluted nanoflow, which improves ionization efficiency. However, their application in biomolecular analysis is limited, likely due to concerns regarding robustness and the higher costs associated with these interfaces compared to sheathflow microESI systems. Despite the promising capabilities of CE–MS, most studies still rely on small sample sizes and often lack comprehensive validation steps. This limitation creates a gap between discovery and practical implementation. To advance the field, larger scale studies and standardization of methodologies are imperative. Future developments should prioritize improving interface designs, enhancing automation, integrating chromatographic preconcentration techniques based on SPE–CE (as suggested in the review), and expanding the range of detectable analytes. Hence, exploration of CE–MS in emerging areas, such as high‐throughput screening and personalized medicine, can broaden its application scope.
In conclusion, CE–MS represents a significant advancement in biomolecular research, offering potential for diverse applications. Concretely, CE–MS excels in handling intricate molecular profiles and plays a pivotal role in advancing diagnostic methods and facilitating biomarker discovery. However, achieving its full potential requires addressing current challenges through technological and methodological improvements.
