Introduction
Substance identification
and characterization are prerequisites
for producing robust and reliable results when biological or biochemical
activity is assessed in any type of bioassay screening, including
high-throughput screening (HTS) assays. The objectives of HTS range
from identifying potential target actives in drug discovery
1
to identifying actives or inactives across a
diverse set of assays with the potential to inform chemical safety
assessments.
2
The success of the HTS endeavor,
in turn, will be influenced to different degrees by the quality of
individual chemical samples, with large (>100K) drug discovery
libraries
that provide good coverage of local chemical domains possibly less
impacted by individual chemical sample quality variations and sample
loss compared with smaller (<15K), structurally diverse, small-molecule
libraries. The latter describes the Tox21 toxicity screening program,
where not only broad patterns of activity are of interest, but the
activity (or inactivity) of individual chemical samples takes on heightened
importance when used to inform individual chemical safety assessments.
Furthermore, in toxicology screening, the priority is on sensitivity,
whereas in pharmacology, the emphasis is more typically on efficacy
and/or specificity.
Certificates of Analysis (CoAs) are often
available for commercially
procured chemicals, reporting the purity and identity of neat (powder
or pure liquid) samples, along with the analytical method used and
date of analysis. All Tox21 compounds, to the extent possible, were
procured as analytical grade quality (i.e., >90% purity). Neat
chemicals
can also be analyzed after procurement, prior to solubilization and
plating, to ensure sample quality (see, e.g.,
https://cebs.niehs.nih.gov/datasets/search/tox21
). However, once chemicals are solubilized, frozen, thawed, plated,
and subjected to atmospheric test conditions, quality issues can arise
that can adversely affect the HTS outcome. Additionally, analytical
quality control (QC) methods and analysis techniques are often tailored
to the chemicals of interest, taking into consideration the nature
of the solvent, volatility, polarity, and presence of metals. Hence,
the cost and practical challenges of analyzing large HTS libraries,
comprising thousands of solubilized, microplated chemical samples,
can be prohibitive. As a result, QC analyses of HTS libraries are
seldom undertaken, and results are rarely published.
The Tox21
“10K” chemical sample library, developed
as part of the Toxicology in the 21
st
Century partnership
to advance
in vitro
methods for toxicity assessment,
consists of over 8900 unique compounds spanning a structurally diverse
landscape of industrial, commercial, environmental, and pharmaceutical
chemicals.
3
Given the novel and transformative
nature of the Tox21 screening effort
4
,
5
and anticipating
the potentially broad range of applications of Tox21 HTS results,
the initial project design included funding and plans to perform analytical
QC for the full Tox21 chemical sample library. Construction of the
library was completed in 2012 and HTS testing commenced soon thereafter.
As described in earlier publications,
3
,
6
ten copies
of the full Tox21 library were created from stock solution plates
provided by the three initial Tox21 partners—the National Center
for Advancing Translational Sciences (NCATS, formerly known as the
National Institutes of Health (NIH) Chemical Genomics Center, or NCGC),
the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), and the National Institute
of Environmental Health Sciences (NIEHS) Division of Translational
Toxicology (DTT), formerly known as the NIH National Toxicology Program
(NTP). (
Note: Due to the very recent organizational name change,
we will continue to use “NTP” to refer to the historical
Tox21 affiliation in this study.)
One of these ten plate
sets was designated and set aside for analytical QC, whereas the remaining
nine plate sets have supplied Tox21 HTS efforts to the present day.
By design, each of the ten Tox21 library plate sets contained multiple
copies of a common set of 88 compounds drawn from the same stock solution
(i.e., same Tox21 ID) to serve as library replicates. In addition,
thousands of compound replicates, mostly sourced from different suppliers
(i.e., different Tox21 IDs), were included in each full plate set
by virtue of Tox21 partner library overlaps and chemical sample replenishments.
A primary objective of the analytical QC of Tox21 samples was to
confirm the identity of the compounds being tested to support the
interpretation of HTS assay results. However, since problems can arise
from impurities, poor solubility, volatility, hydrolysis, or degradation,
additional efforts were to be made to assess the purity, concentration,
and stability of chemical samples under storage conditions that mirrored
test plates at two different time points: (1) when the Tox21 plates
were freshly taken out of the −80 °C storage and thawed
at room temperature (T0); and (2) at the end of a 4-month period stored
at room temperature (T4) and under atmospheric conditions, mirroring
the experience of a companion plate set undergoing testing on the
Tox21 robotics system. Multiple analytical methods and approaches
were expected to be required, given the structural diversity of the
Tox21 library compounds. Liquid chromatography mass spectroscopy (LC-MS)
was proposed as the initial screen of the full library due to the
method’s relatively low cost, speed, and broad applicability,
with a small number of metal-containing compounds to be subjected
to flow-injection analysis (FIA). A subset of T0 chemicals deemed
unamenable to LC-MS or that did not yield usable results, including
many nonpolar and lower molecular weight compounds, were to be submitted
for follow-up gas chromatography mass spectroscopy (GC-MS). Finally,
nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy would be employed to
analyze ungraded samples at T0 and T4, pending the availability of
sufficient remaining chemical sample and/or contract funds.
To date, the Tox21 library has been screened against more than
80 cell-based assays for potential biological pathway disruptions
that may result in downstream toxicity effects.
7
−
9
In addition,
a significant fraction of the Tox21 library’s unique chemical
sample content contributed by EPA (∼4000 unique compounds)
has undergone additional HTS in association with EPA’s ToxCast
program.
10
Factors that affect chemical
sample quality can introduce uncertainty in the interpretation of
chemical-assay results, leading to false positives if the activity
is caused by an impurity or degradation product or false negatives
if the active chemical of interest is missing or undetected, e.g.,
due to sample loss, degradation, or analytical method amenability
issues. Hence, chemical sample quality and stability information impacted
by storage and testing protocols has broad applicability and importance
for interpreting and assessing confidence in assay results from both
the Tox21 and ToxCast programs. To date, more than 13000 unique Tox21
chemical samples (i.e., unique Tox21 ID stock solutions), comprising
the full Tox21 testing library, have been analyzed by one or more
analytical methods, at one or both T0 and T4 time points. These 13000
unique chemical samples correspond to more than 8900 unique chemical
substances (i.e., compounds with unique CAS RN), of which over 8500
are assigned to unique defined structures (i.e., InChIs), with the
remainder mixtures and ill-defined substances.
Henceforth,
“chemical sample” will be shortened to “sample”
to denote unique Tox21 ID chemical stock solutions, i.e., the level
at which analytical QC data are reported herein. The term “compound”
will refer to the CAS RN substance level, corresponding to multiple
Tox21 IDs when differently sourced samples or different stock solutions
were included in the full Tox21 QC library.

This previously published set of QC results constitutes what we will

To aid in discerning patterns in this large, multifactorial (
QC grade x time x method)
data set, the 13 possible QC grades
are reduced to a smaller number of numeric quality scores. These quality
scores are color-coded to generate a visual overview of the data set
that is intended to highlight overarching patterns of quality assessment,
stability, and analytical method successes/failures, as well as to
identify chemical domains with missing, and potentially questionable
or less reliable, QC outcomes. A result of this analysis is the improvement
of QC grades for more than 700 Tox21 ID samples, and an updated “v1”
version of the Tox21 QC data set. This section concludes by briefly
considering possible sources of error in the sample-level analytical
QC data and estimating the reproducibility of Tox21 sample-level QC
grades.
Section

(Tox21
Compound-Level QC Results) considers ways in which replicate compound-level

quality bin at T0 or T4 to aid in structure-based analyses of the
QC results. Compound-level QC bins are designed and prioritized to
facilitate structure-based modeling objectives with preliminary results
provided herein. A previously developed chemotype (CT)-enrichment
approach

features significantly enriched in compounds labeled as “unstable”

those labeled high-quality versus low-quality at T0. We also consider
how well computed vapor pressure values correlate with QC indicators
of low-concentration initially (T0) or over time under ambient conditions
(T4) relative to the target plated concentration.
Lastly, in

, we briefly report

of the v0 QC data set was motivated by concerns about conflicting

to reevaluate original QC spectra to support regulatory use of Tox21

in more nuanced spectral interpretations and, in some cases, revision

effort is still underway, it has already generated new insights and
will lead to some revised Tox21 sample-level QC grades.
A hallmark
of both Tox21 and ToxCast testing programs has been
the public release of both raw and processed summary HTS data. In
keeping with that objective, the v0 Tox21 ID, sample-level QC grades
and supporting pdf spectral summaries previously were published in
searchable form on the NCATS Tox21 online browser for the majority
of samples for which usable QC results could be obtained.
11
A complete table of the v0 Tox21 sample-level
analytical QC grades, with each grade corresponding to a single analytical
method and time point (T0 or T4), is provided herein along with the
modified v1 QC grades, scores, and quality bins resulting from the
present study. These v1 Tox21 sample-level and compound-level QC results
point to how the Tox21 analytical QC data set is evolving and can
be mined for further patterns and associations to support HTS assay
results interpretation, as well as serve as a large computational
chemistry and analytical chemistry resource. The soon-to-be public
EPA analytical QC data browser, created to support the QC expert re-evaluation
effort, will provide complete access to pdf spectral summaries and
will facilitate more thorough and detailed investigations of the legacy
QC data moving forward, as well as support ongoing revisions and addition
of expert annotations as these data are reevaluated.
The present
study lays the groundwork for future structure-based
analyses of compound-level properties revealed by the analytical QC
results, such as analytical method amenability, as well as the quality
and stability of DMSO-solubilized compounds, the latter including
sample loss, degradation, or reactivity due to the hydroscopic nature
of DMSO. Limitations of the Tox21 analytical QC effort described herein
include incomplete coverage of the data set by method and time point,
as well as the inherent challenges and technical constraints of applying
multiple analytical methods in a semi-high-throughput manner, involving
a limited range of instrument or column settings, to the analysis
of the large HTS library. Hence, no single analytical method provided
full library coverage at either the T0 or T4 time point, and no Tox21
QC quality grade could be generated for subsets of T0 and T4 samples.
Despite these limitations, the structurally and analytically diverse
Tox21 QC data set, with the accompanying summary spectral data, provides
one of the largest publicly available QC data sets associated with
diverse chemistry and assay data. It also provides an invaluable chemistry
resource and quality lens for data mining. An area ripe for improved
understanding, but relegated to future study, is investigation of
patterns in the analytical QC results related to sample loss, low
purity, etc., in relation to the corresponding Tox21 and ToxCast HTS
assay profiles. The present work is intended to provide the background
and foundation for future versioning and uses of the Tox21 QC analytical
data set.

Materials and Methods
2.1
Quality Control Procedure
To prepare
compounds for analysis or screening, they were first dissolved in
anhydrous DMSO (≥99.9%, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) to a
target concentration of 20 mM and dispensed into 384-well plates (made
from polypropylene). Initially, ten copies of the full Tox21 chemical
sample library were created as 20 mM DMSO 384-well stock solution
plates, with nine copies designated for HTS and one copy designated
for analytical QC.
3
(
Note: A small
number of Tox21 chemical samples were plated at lower concentrations
(5–10 mM) due to DMSO insolubility, and a handful of highly
potent estrogenic compounds were plated at 0.2 mM
). HTS plates
were subsequently diluted to multiple concentrations in 1536-well
screening plates and both these HTS plates and the undiluted analytical
QC plates were stored in the freezer at −80 °C when not
being screened or analyzed.
6
During screening
in various HTS assays, the chemical plates were maintained on the
robotics system under atmospheric, humidity-controlled conditions
at room temperature (22 °C, 50% relative humidity) for up to,
but not exceeding, 4 months. Analytical QC was conducted on sample
solutions drawn from the designated 384-well Tox21 20 mM stock solution
plates to assess the compound identity and purity at two time points:
T0, when compound plates were freshly taken out of the freezer (−80
°C) and thawed; and T4, after compound plates had been exposed
to ambient conditions for 4 months. To prepare the T4 samples, compound
plates were removed from the freezer, allowed to thaw overnight at
room temperature, and briefly centrifuged, and the seal (composed
of three layers: polyester on top, aluminum in the middle, and polyethylene
on the bottom (facing the solvent)) removed/discarded. A heavy metal
gasketed compound lid was placed on top, and the plates were placed
in the Tox21 Kalypsys compound carousel (22 °C, 50% relative
humidity) to mirror the test sample plate treatment. After 4 months,
the lid was removed, the plates were centrifuged briefly, heat sealed
with an Agilent brand DMSO-resistant heat seal on an Agilent PlateLoc,
and refrozen at −80 °C until time of analysis. Aliquots
of 20 mM stock solution from each of the full library T0 and T4 384-well
plate sets were replated and shipped overnight to contracted analytical
laboratories and packed on dry ice. Prior to analytical QC analysis,
DMSO samples were diluted further to a target concentration of 3 mM.
All LC-MS (and related FIA) and NMR analyses were performed by
OpAns (OpAns LLC, Durham, NC) under Contract No. HHSN271201500023I
issued by the National Toxicology Program. The GC-MS analyses were
performed under Interagency Agreements, Nos. ATR12001-0000 and ATR12001-001,
between NCATS and the National Institutes of Standards & Technology
(NIST). Samples were provided to OpAns and NIST in approximately 27
μL aliquots as a 20 mM DMSO solution in 384-well plates. Further
details of analytical methods are provided in the
subsections
2.1.1
–

2.1.1
High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC)
Mass Spectrometry (MS) Instrumentation
LC-MS analysis was
performed on an Agilent 1100/1200 HPLC system (Agilent Technologies,
Santa Clara, CA, USA) equipped with an Agilent diode array detector
(DAD), binary pump, column thermostat, autosampler and Agilent 6140/6150
single quadrupole MS. Additionally, an evaporative light scattering
detector (ELSD) (SofTA Corporation, Westminster, Colorado, USA) and
an Antek 8060 chemiluminescent nitrogen detector (CLND) (Antek Instruments,
Houston, TX, USA) were coupled to the HPLC-MS system for compound
quantitation analysis. The mobile phases used for the separation were
deionized water (>18 MΩ/cm in house purification) with 0.1%
acetic acid (Fisher Scientific) (solvent A) and HPLC grade methanol
(Fisher Scientific) with 0.1% acetic acid (solvent B). Gradient elution
was performed at a flow rate of 1.5 mL/min, and the mobile phase gradient
was 5–100% B in 3 min and maintained at 100% B for 0.3 min.
A high surface area (>350 m
2
/g), high carbon load (>18%),
and efficient packing material were requirements for the HPLC column
to provide the specified resolving power (fwhm <0.025 min in UV
detector for all standards). Initially, a Varian Pursuit UPS C18 1.9
μm 50 × 3.0 mm column was used, but that column was discontinued
by the manufacturer. A Fortis C18 2.5 μm, 50 × 3.0 mm column
was used for most of the analyses and yielded similar retention times
and resolving power as the Varian column.
Separations were performed
at a column temperature of 50 °C. One microliter of sample dissolved
in DMSO in the 384-well plate was injected onto the column for each
experimental run. All effluent from the HPLC column went through a
2 μL flow cell in the diode array and was then split with approximately
200 μL/min going to the MS, 300 μL/min going to the CLND,
and 1000 μL/min going to the ELSD. The MS was equipped with
a AJS ESI source operating in alternating positive/negative ion mode,
and spectra were acquired from
m
/
z
50 to 1350 in both modes. The electrospray ionization (ESI) source
parameters were used as follows: gas temp, 300 °C; drying gas
flow, 12 L/min; nebulizer pressure, 50 psi; Vcap (both positive and
negative), 3000 V; fragmentor, 70 V. The CLND nebulizer oxygen flow
was 320 mL/min with a nitrogen flow of 50 mL/min. The furnace was
at 1050 °C and the PMT voltage at −700 V. The ELSD used
a low dispersion nebulizer with the Spray Chamber at 25 °C and
the Drift Tube held at 60 °C.
2.1.2
Preparation of LC-MS Calibration and Quality
Control (QC) Standards
A mixture composed of ethyl adenine-9-acetate
(10.0 mM), verapamil (5.0 mM), terfenadine (2.0 mM), and propranolol
(0.4 mM) in DMSO (Fisher Scientific) was prepared as a standard to
determine system suitability and perform calibration. A 10.0 mM solution
of diclofenac in DMSO was used as a QC standard to verify the performance
of the quantitative detectors. Compounds were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.
Both the calibration mix and QC sample were injected at the beginning
of each analysis batch, after every 96 samples, and at the end of
the batch. Sample results must be bracketed by passing calibration
for QC samples to be accepted (see
Supporting Information Methods
,
Table S1
).
2.1.3
LC-MS Sample Preparation
Samples
were received in 384-well plates on dry ice. The samples were transferred
to a −80 °C freezer until analysis. When preparing for
analysis, a plate was removed from the freezer and placed on the laboratory
bench for approximately 1 h to thaw without assistance. The plate
was “floated” in an ultrasonic water bath for 45 s then
centrifuged for 1 min at 32
g
and placed in the LC-MS
autosampler for analysis. The seal applied at NCATS was not removed.
2.1.4
CLND
Quantification
Peak areas for each compound of interest (COI) in the calibration
mixture were plotted against the corresponding nitrogen concentration.
A linear calibration curve was fit to the data.
2.1.5
ELSD Quantification
Peak areas
for each COI in the calibration mixture were plotted against their
corresponding mg/mL concentration. A linear calibration curve was
fit to the data. If a peak was found corresponding to the COI, then
the observed mg/mL concentration was converted to mM based on the
molecular weight of the COI. Quantification of COI that eluted in
the solvent front required the peak from the blank (DMSO only) to
be small relative to the peak from study samples. In general, the
peak from the COI eluting in the solvent front must be at least 5
times the solvent blank to be quantified.
2.1.6
LC-MS Data Analysis
Retention times
of the detectors were adjusted to align the standard peaks, a process
known as “peak grouping”. This was accomplished by holding
the MS time constant, while the time was added to the UV detector
(0.01 min) and subtracted from the ELSD and CLND (0.05 min). Once
the chromatograms were aligned, peaks could be grouped across all
detectors, which was essential for calculating the results. Results
are reported based on peak number, which was the same for all detectors
even though not all peaks were observed by each detector. The average
MS was determined for each peak group by averaging the spectra for
all points with an intensity greater than half the height of the peak
for the narrowest peak in the peak group. For each peak group in the
chromatogram, the average MS value was interpreted. The peak was considered
consistent with the COI if the dominant ions could be assigned to
adducts of the COI (e.g., [M + H]
+
, [M + Na]
+
, [2M + H]
+
, [2M + Na]
+
, [M + H + DMSO]
+
, etc.) and the response in the other detectors was consistent
with the COI (e.g., peak observed in CLND for nitrogen containing
COI).
2.1.7
Flow Injection Analysis (FIA)
FIA
was performed on the same system as LC-MS with the only change being
the column was removed. The HPLC pump was set to an isocratic 95%
B at 1.4 mL/min and the data acquisition time ended at 0.8 min. ELSD
and CLND quantifications were performed using a single point calibration
line fit to the data with the intercept set to 0.
2.1.8
Gas Chromatography–Mass Spectrometry
(GC-MS) Instrumentation
An Agilent 7890 gas chromatograph
(Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, USA) equipped with an Agilent
programmable temperature vaporizer (PTV), an Agilent capillary flow
purged splitter, an Agilent 5975 mass selective detector, a flame
ionization detector (FID), and a Gerstel MPS2 autosampler was used
to perform simultaneous GC-MS and GC-FID analysis. The ionization
energy was 70 eV, the ion source temperature was 230 °C, and
the transfer line temperature was 270 °C. The vendor’s
standard tune method was used to tune the instrument. A Varian FactorFour
VF-5 ms column (Agilent Technologies, Sana Clara, CA, USA), 15 m ×
0.25 mm × 0.25 μm, was operated at a constant flow of 1.0
mL/min with helium as the carrier gas. The GC parameters were as follows:
inlet temperature program: 60 °C, hold for 1 min, then 200 °C/min
up to 270 °C; split ratio 30:1; oven temperature program 50 to
70 °C at 5 °C/min then up to 280 °C at 10 °C/min.
The oven and PTV programs were started at the same time. The conditions
for the injection into the PTV were 1.5 μL of a 1:1 mixture
of derivatization reagent and dichloromethane anhydrous, ≥99.8%,
(Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO), followed a minute later by a sandwich
injection of 0.5 μL of derivatization reagent, a 0.2 μL
air gap, a 1.0 μL sample, and a 0.2 μL air gap for a total
injection volume of 3.4 μL. The splitter, located at the end
of the column, was operated with a helium make up gas flow such that
the split was approximately 1:1 at an oven temperature of 50 °C.
The GC split conditions were such that the holdup times (retention
times) were the same for both detectors within ±0.002 min. Retention
indices (RI) were determined by using a mixture of the straight chain
hydrocarbons from C7 to C30 as external standards. All data were acquired
with Agilent MassHunter (Versions: B.05.00, B.06.00, and B.07.00)
software.
2.1.9
GC-MS Sample Derivatization
Derivatization
was accomplished by coinjecting (sandwich injection) an aliquot of
the sample with N,O-bis(trimethylsilyl)trifluoroacetamide containing
1% trimethylsilyl chloride (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO).
2.1.10
GC-MS Sample Analysis
Identity
confirmation for the expected compounds and tentative identifications
of other observed materials were achieved by matching spectra extracted
with the Automated Mass Spectrometry Deconvolution and Identification
System (AMDIS) with spectra from the National Institute of Standards
and Technology (NIST) with either the NIST11 or NIST14 Mass Spectral
libraries using the Windows MS Search Program (v2.2) [
https://chemdata.nist.gov
],
as well as by expert evaluation. For compounds with a spectrum in
the NIST11 or NIST14 libraries, a high match factor and consistency
with the expected structure constituted positive identification.
For compounds without a spectrum in NIST Mass Spectral libraries,
the compound was considered identified if the observed spectrum was
judged to be consistent with an expected structure. Identifications
were attempted for all compounds having concentrations estimated to
be above 1%. Samples were considered as a “QC fail”
or “identity not confirmed” when the expected compound
was not observed.
2.1.11
Quantitation by Flame Ionization Detector
(FID)
Approximate concentrations were calculated from only
the flame ionization detector (FID) response. A mixture of the trimethylsilyl
derivatives of five alcohols (1-heptanol, 1-decanol, 1-hexadecanol,
1-nonadecanol, and 1-docosanol) at alcohol concentrations of 0.1 0.3,
0.7, and 0.9 mg/mL in DMSO were used as external standards for semiquantitative
analysis. In some cases, the derivatization of the samples gave a
mixture of underivatized or derivatives with different numbers of
trimethylsilyl groups. Estimated purities and concentrations were
calculated using the sum of the under- and derivatized components.
The program used to calculate peak areas for the FID peaks sometimes
failed to properly separate adjacent peaks. In those cases, peak areas
from AMDIS were used to calculate the purity.

Instrumentation

2.1.14
NMR Reference Standard Preparation
A reference standard of 3,4-methylenedioxymethamphetamine (MDMA)
was run on the NMR instrument at the beginning and end of each acquisition
block of samples to monitor the instrument performance and stability.
The standard was prepared by accurate weighing of the solid material
and subsequent dilution with DMSO
-d
6
to
give a concentration comparable to the test samples. The concentration
of the reference standard was independently verified with a validated
quantitative LC-MS/MS instrument. NMR data were acquired in the same
manner as the test samples, and the integral values were used to calibrate
the sample data.
2.1.15
NMR Data Analysis
Processing of
the NMR sample data was performed with ACD/Laboratories Spectrus and
ACD/HNMR prediction software (Advanced Chemistry Development, Inc.,
Toronto, ON, Canada). For each sample, a report was generated containing
the Fourier transformed spectrum as well as a predicted spectrum based
on the expected chemical structure. For substances with multiple potential
isomers or components, the structure used for prediction was specified
as only one particular isomer or component when the structure provided
included such information. For tautomers, the most stable form predicted
by the ACD/HNMR software was used. In both cases, if there was interpretable
evidence of multiple isomers/tautomers, the presence of such was noted,
and the integrals were added to produce the estimate of quantification.
Identity confirmation for the expected compounds was accomplished
by matching the experimental and predicted spectra as well as by expert
evaluation. A high match level and consistency with the expected structure
constituted a positive identification.
1
Samples with missing peaks in the spectrum due to overlap with DMSO
and H
2
O or because they are correlated to exchangeable
protons (e.g., OH, COOH) were considered a positive identification
(e.g., Tox21_300032, Tox21_300066, Tox21_300137). An approximate purity
was assigned to each sample based on the presence of extraneous peaks
in the spectrum. A sample was considered failed when the spectral
data did not match predicted data, was evaluated as being inconsistent
with the expected chemical structure (e.g., Tox21_300128), or no peaks
for the desired chemical were observed (e.g., Tox21_300049). For samples
where the expected product was identified, the quantitated concentration
was calculated relative to the integration of the external MDMA reference
sample of known concentration.

A previously developed Chemotype Enrichment Workflow
(CTEW)
12
was used to identify ToxPrint
chemical features
(or chemotypes) found to be significantly enriched in a QC “activity”
category. In the present application, categories consist of binarized
QC grade bins or stability bins, as described in
Section
4
. For the enrichment analysis,
a positive (POS) hit indicates the presence of the chemotype (CT)
in the target QC category and a negative (NEG) hit indicates the absence
of the CT in the target category. The CTEW generates a standard confusion
matrix for each ToxPrint CT in association with the corresponding
set of binary hit-calls (1 = POS; 0 = NEG), where the following definitions
apply: True positives (TP) are compounds that contain the CT and have
an associated POS hit-call; true negatives (TN) have neither the CT
nor a POS hit-call; false positives (FP) contain the CT but have a
NEG hit-call; and false negatives (FN) do not contain the CT but have
a POS hit-call. Based on the confusion matrix, the CTEW applies default
statistical thresholds for establishing enrichment that are intended
to filter out insignificant results while retaining both weak and
strong enrichments for follow-up analysis. The default thresholds
include: number of True Positives ≥3, Fisher’s exact
p
-value one-tailed ≤ 0.05, and odds ratio (OR) ≥
3, where OR = (TP × TN)/(FN × FP).
For analysis of
volatility in association with compound-level QC
results indicating sample loss (i.e., low concentration relative to
expected target concentration), we used DSSTox molecular weight (MW)
values and OPERA-predicted VP values exported from the EPA’s
CompTox Chemicals Dashboard. We constructed box plots and computed
point-biserial correlation statistics using the CORREL function within
the MS 365 Office version of Excel.

Early on, much discussion across the multiagency Tox21 leadership
and workgroups took place regarding communications that were to accompany
QC grades presented in association with HTS sample results. At one
extreme and for some types of HTS studies, an analytical chemist might
label only grade “A” chemicals as PASS, and discard
HTS results for all other chemicals. However, given that many environmental
and industrial chemicals might produce only weak target activity in
HTS assays, grades “A–C” and “I”
were initially labeled “PASS” for purposes of assay
results interpretation. Since analytical QC method amenability issues
can mistakenly produce poor QC grades (i.e., the analysis method may
not be suited to detection of the chemical), any grade lower than
those in the PASS group was considered less reliable and potentially
suspect. Hence, all sample grades outside the PASS bin were accompanied
by a CAUTION flag and a corresponding note. Due to the greater uncertainty
attributed to the CAUTION-labeled samples, the Tox21 leadership supported
full publication of all HTS data associated with Tox21 samples, including
those in the lowest quality bins, giving users the opportunity to
carefully consider both the QC analysis and the HTS results. For example,
a low concentration QC grade (Ac, Bc, or Cc) associated with an inactive
HTS assay profile might reasonably be considered inconclusive, whereas
the same sample manifesting high assay target activity might be of
keen interest for a follow-up study. And, finally, there was the possibility
of human error in testing or review of the raw QC data. (
Note:
The relatively small number of “C” graded samples (75–50%

better delineate the highest quality A,B,I graded samples.)

3.6
Tox21 ID Sample-Level Grade Reproducibility
and Method Amenability
There are several possible sources
of error and uncertainty applied to the Tox21 ID sample-level analytical
QC grades. These include plating errors (resulting in different or
contaminated plate-well level samples) or analytical method limitations
and associated analytical chemist review challenges when spectral
results are unclear or confounded, e.g., when NMR resonances are buried
under solvent signals in the spectrum, or when the chemical elutes
too late to be observed during the method observation time. The latter
are more likely to lead to conflicting grades either at two time points
(such as in
Figure
6
, bin b), or in results obtained by different analytical methods.
Errors in supplier-provided information or incorrect structure assignments
to the Tox21 ID, on the other hand, would lead to a failed identity
grade assignment (F). In general, method amenability and higher confidence
in results can be inferred when a high-quality grade is assigned,
particularly at both time points since this requires clear positive
evidence of identity and concentration. A low-quality grade assignment,
on the other hand, is associated with greater uncertainty, as it can
be confounded by method limitations or difficulty interpreting spectra.
3.6.1
Tox21 ID 88 Replicate Set
In the
present data set, LC-MS was applied initially to analyzing the entire
Tox21 T0 sample library. Referring to
Figure
2
, all v0 analytical QC grades are reported
at the unique Tox21 ID sample level, which also corresponds to the
plate-well level, except in the case of the Tox21 IDs corresponding
to the 88-replicate sample set. The full 88 replicate set of stock
solutions (denoted “Tox21_4#####” in
Table S1
) was a subset of compounds provided by the EPA, primarily
pesticides with reliably measured bioactivity in early ToxCast studies
that were chosen to serve as replicates across the entire Tox21 library.
Two sets of the 88 Tox21 ID samples were plated randomly on each of
the three 1536-well plate sets of chemicals submitted by each of the
three Tox21 federal partner’s collections. Hence, each of these
88 samples were analyzed by LC-MS at least 18 times at T0. During
NCATS analytical chemist review, these T0 sample results were aggregated
to a single Tox21 ID grade by majority rule (data not shown). Of the
67 samples for which an LC-MS grade could be assigned, 61 were graded
A consistently, with the majority having no grade conflicts, 2 were
consistently graded D, and only 4 samples (6%) received multiple grades
across the sample set. Hence, although a relatively small data set,
this 88 replicate Tox21 ID sample set indicates a high degree of analytical
grade reproducibility, with the conflicting grades for 6% of samples
most likely an indication of analytical method challenges. Final v0
grades for the 88 samples are associated with Tox21_4##### IDs in
Supporting Information Table S5
.
3.6.2
Inferring T0–T4 QC Score Reproducibility
In

6

8

at the 2 time points. An approximate, indirect means of inferring

considering the total number of samples for which T0 and T4 scores

Combining the 6176 total from Bin a in

only of positive sample identity confirmation but also of method amenability.
If we remove the 1130 samples in Bin d, where degradation from T0
to T4 is indicated and plausible, from the total graded at T0 and
T4, the percentage of consistent T0–T4 scores (high-quality
to high-quality or low-quality to low-quality) increases to 97%. Note
that these numbers include same-method T0 and T4 score pairings (only
for LC-MS and NMR), as well as different method pairings.
Alternatively,
in
Figure
6

we encountered the most clear-cut cases indicating possible method
or reviewer error in assigning sample grades, where a low-quality

previously, a high-quality result is more reliable than a low-quality
result since the former requires positive confirmation of identity,
purity, and concentration, whereas the latter may be due to a variety
of factors, including method amenability, misinterpretation of spectra,
etc. If all T0 and T4 method pairings are considered, then only 225

data set has a high rate of PASS/CAUTION label consistency across
T0 and T4 samples.

4.2
Structure Enrichments in Compound-Level Quality
Subsets
The analytical QC results and discussion presented
to this point are intended to provide the reader with a deeper appreciation
not only for the richness and complexity of the multidimensional QC
results data set at the sample-level but to point toward ways in which
the analytical QC data can be represented, mined, and modeled at the
compound-level. A thorough investigation of the compound-level data,
exploring structure–property correlations to compound-level
QC properties, is beyond the scope of this manuscript. However, a
few preliminary analyses and results are provided to illustrate approaches
that could be taken and to encourage others to continue these types
of investigations.

Re-evaluation of Tox21 QC Results
Since
the Tox21 v0 QC data were made publicly available from 2016–2019,
EPA researchers have frequently consulted the QC results to aid in
the interpretation of Tox21 and ToxCast HTS bioassay data for individual
chemicals, as well as for larger data sets for use in modeling QC-related
compound-level properties, such as LC-MS amenability
20
,
21
or a particular bioassay target activity.
22
−
24
Particularly
when questions arose in the case of missing or low-quality QC data,
the original pdf files and spectra were accessed from source files
obtained directly from OpAns and NIST and re-evaluated, which often
led to additional insights and, occasionally, reinterpretations resulting
in QC grade changes. For example, Sulfluramid (Tox21_300377) was originally
labeled as Ungraded (U) at both T0 and T4 based on LC-MS spectra.
This is a highly fluorinated, lipophilic chemical that eluted late
on the column and could only be detected in the negative ionization
mode, with no signal in the detectors to allow for quantitation, and
therefore was assigned Z grade (i.e., mass confirmed but no purity).
An NMR spectrum was located in the source OpAns data files, and based
on this, the sample was judged to be of good quality but with some
degradation and, thus, assigned a QC grade of “Bc” (identity
confirmed, 75–90% purity but low concentration). In other cases,
closer reexamination of source spectra has revealed additional chemically
relevant details, such as isomers (or tautomers) not indicated in
the Tox21 grade or labeled as impurities, known mixtures labeled as
failing when component signals can clearly be seen, etc.
In
the interest of simplicity and promoting usability of the QC
data for informing a single Tox21 ID bioassay result or profile, the
current v0 sample-level QC data set intentionally provided a single
Tox21 ID sample-level grade attributed to a single method for each
T0 and T4 sample successfully analyzed. At the source data level,
however, there were often multiple spectra generated, either at different
LC-MS detection settings or for different methods, that can potentially
provide additional insights. Presenting an informed user of the analytical
QC data with multiple grades and notations for different spectra provided
for a single Tox21 ID sample can provide a more nuanced view of the
analytical challenges posed by particular chemicals or classes of
chemicals as well as point to the most appropriate method to apply.
Hence, the assignment of a single Tox21 QC grade can be an oversimplification.
Another oversimplified aspect of the Tox21 v0 QC graded database is
the assignment of a single “U” label when a sample is
ungraded. This label was originally defined on the NCATS Tox21 sample
browser as “Unknown/Inconclusive”, but we have found
many cases where the result should have been labeled “Not Determined”
(ND), e.g., no source spectra file exists at T0 or T4. The original
U label was misleading; hence, its definition has been revised as
“Ungraded” in the present article as well as on the
NCATS Tox21 sample browser to be more encompassing of all possibilities,
including ND.
Motivated by the above observations and past experiences,
a re-evaluation
of the OpAns and NIST source-level data was undertaken to re-examine
and curate source-level data and add analytical and reviewer notations
for every chemical in the Tox21 sample collection. This effort began
two years ago and is ongoing, having covered thousands of records
thus far. Given the large scope of this effort, the re-evaluation
of analytical spectra and source notes necessitated construction of
a database and web-based application to standardize and facilitate
curation review as well as to capture relevant source and reviewer
notes. In addition, predicted physicochemical properties (such as
vapor pressure and log
K
ow
) were generated
to inform interpretation of the data, with all supporting data stored
in the database records. Finally, the data are being fully integrated
within the EPA’s CompTox Chemicals Dashboard, also allowing
for continued and independent user review and updating, as needed.
This substantially expanded Tox21 analytical QC database and web-based
application are slated for public release by early 2025 and a manuscript
is in progress that will describe this re-evaluation effort in greater
detail and relate it to the present work.
The result of this
re-evaluation and research effort will be a
newly versioned, enriched Tox21 sample QC data set with many observations
that have already influenced analytical methods being applied to new
measurements within EPA projects. Additionally, patterns in the data
now inform the selection process of new chemicals to be analyzed in
EPA’s bioactivity screening programs. Occasional re-evaluation
of individual sample QC results continues based on questions from
researchers reviewing the HTS bioactivity data. It should be emphasized
that the entirety of this re-evaluation effort would have been impossible
without the investment in generating the analytical QC results and
Tox21 v0 QC data set reported in this work and represent continued
valuation of these data. Revised QC grades resulting from this re-evaluation
were not included in the present manuscript because we felt it important
to document and report on the initial Tox21 v0 QC effort underlying
all subsequent efforts. However, it is hoped that the detailed presentation
of QC results at both the sample and compound levels in this manuscript,
as well as the insights gained from the CT-enrichment analyses, have
shone a light on subsets of the QC data where re-evaluation might
be warranted and informative.
6
Conclusions
We have attempted in this
review to provide an overview of the
Tox21 analytical QC methods and original (v0) published results for
more than 13,000 unique samples mapped to ∼8900 unique compounds,
analyzed by various analytical methods at two possible time points,
mirroring ambient testing protocols. Both the public Tox21 analytical
QC undertaking and its companion Tox21 HTS effort are unprecedented
in size and scope and, thus, not likely to be soon repeated. Equally
unprecedented are the efforts to make all analytical QC and HTS testing
methods and data fully transparent and publicly available to the research
and regulatory communities. The full set of Tox21 ID sample-level
analytical QC results has been searchable and available for download
through the Tox21 NCATS sample browser since 2019. To date, however,
these results have primarily been consulted for individual or small
sets of Tox21 samples or compounds in cases where the corresponding
HTS bioassay data (Tox21 or ToxCast) were of sufficient interest to
warrant assessment of sample quality. In the present study, we provided
global views of the sample-level QC data to aid in understanding and
identifying QC subsets of high and low quality and confidence, as
well as a subset with QC grade inconsistencies that resulted in elevating
hundreds of low quality or missing T0 QC grades based on high-quality
T4 grades. Throughout our analyses, we were guided by the assumption
that high-quality QC grades, by virtue of the need for definitive
and clear positive confirmation in interpreting analytical spectra,
imbue those results with higher confidence over low-quality QC grades,
which can result from a variety of chemistry- and analytical-method-related
challenges, in addition to inherent sample quality issues.
Various
assumptions were made throughout the course of grading,
binning, and collapsing the sample-level QC data to the compound level.
Each of these assumptions could be examined and potentially modified
to provide QC results that are better fit for different needs. For
instance, changing the thresholds for PASS/CAUTION bins, STABLE/UNSTABLE,
or excluding certain grades from inclusion (e.g., M or Z) could be
examined for their impact on modeling results. To underscore this
point, we have demonstrated with the CT-enrichment analyses in
Section
4
that assumptions
underlying the grading, binning, and collapse of sample-level QC data
to the compound level as well as the resolution of sample subsets
chosen for modeling are important to achieving modeling success. Equally
important to the scientific enterprise is the clear articulation of
assumptions underlying the preparation of modeling inputs, including
the method by which the sample-level QC results are collapsed to the
compound level. The degree to which analytical method limitations
impact the reported low-quality QC results is also an area that could
be better understood and articulated with expert reexamination of
the original spectral results.
A major goal of this manuscript
has been to document and present
the Tox21 (v0) analytical QC data in ways that aid understanding and
promote deeper appreciation of both the limitations and potential
uses of the data, with the goal to better inform ToxCast and Tox21
HTS results as well as provide inputs for structure-based modeling.
Anticipating the use of the published Tox21 analytical QC results
to aid in the interpretation of both Tox21 and ToxCast HTS results
moving forward, inferences pertaining to sample loss and degradation
derived from the T0 to T4 sample comparisons, presented herein, warrant
qualification. The QC analysis of T4 sample plates occurred at a single
time point, i.e., after 4 months of ambient storage mirroring the
companion Tox21 plate testing conditions, whereas Tox21 screening
took place along the continuum of 0–4 months. Hence, the age
of samples at the time of bioassay data collection and the effect
of possible sample loss or degradation inferred from T4 QC results
on these bioassay results are unknowns. Similarly, whereas the majority
of ToxCast samples were drawn from the same stock solution (Tox21
ID) or neat samples as were included in EPA’s Tox21 library,
ToxCast bioassay testing conditions were not as extreme as those indicated
for Tox21 plates (the latter 4-month usage more typical of pharmaceutical
HTS screening) and hewed more closely to T0 storage conditions. Where
Tox21 and ToxCast assays are most similar, this might provide an interesting
angle for a comparison of bioassay results comparison. In any case,
compound-level inferences indicating the potential for DMSO-solubilized
compounds to potentially volatilize, hydrolyze, or degrade should
be considered when interpreting Tox21 and ToxCast bioassay data.
Some compounds associated with a CAUTION QC grade that produce a strong
bioactivity result should undergo further scrutiny, i.e., was the
activity due to testing artifacts, high potency counteracting low
concentration, or activity of an impurity or secondary aqueous degradant
due to the hydroscopic nature of DMSO? Similarly, if a compound is
broadly negative across the full bioassay screen, could this be explained
by sample loss or degradation indicated by the QC results and associated
physical chemical properties, i.e., is the solvated compound outside
the domain of the testing conditions?
The re-evaluation effort
undertaken by EPA researchers, accompanied
by a structure-indexed database and web-based application, will allow
for continued annotation, revision, and use of the Tox21 QC data as
well as encourage broader scientific engagement with these data by
the analytical and environmental chemistry communities. Nothing comparable
to the Tox21 QC database is available publicly in association with
a large and diverse chemical landscape, increasing its value beyond
its initial focus in serving the Tox21 HTS program. Ultimately, however,
the primary motivation and objective for QC evaluation of the Tox21
sample library was to provide assurances that the chemicals undergoing
Tox21 HTS testing were of sufficient quality to lend confidence to
the HTS bioassay outcomes. The large percentages of T0 and T4 high
quality subsets of QC results are reassuring in that regard (
Figure
9
). For the remainder
of chemicals for which varying degrees of “CAUTION”
are indicated from the QC results, there are many possible underlying
reasons for such findings, some of which can be explained and countered,
leading to grade improvements. The total number of such grade reversals
ultimately is expected to be small relative to the total number of
Tox21 samples screened, i.e., hundreds rather than thousands. We argue,
however, that each of these individual samples and associated compounds,
by virtue of being associated with a Tox21 HTS bioactivity profile,
can find themselves the subject of focused inquiry with respect to
a research or regulatory concern. Hence, each time a CAUTION QC result
can be better understood, it can potentially “rescue”
a chemical and associated HTS profile that could be of particular
interest or inform future testing efforts.
The application of
large-scale HTS technologies and approaches
to both the Tox21 HTS and the QC evaluation effort has, by necessity,
imposed practical limitations on each enterprise. At the same time,
the assay results and supporting QC results of both programs are of
intense interest to the toxicology and regulatory communities for
informing assessments of individual or small groups of chemicals.
Reconciling these two objectives has been a challenge for the Tox21
program from the start. Our hope that the present review has provided
a path and some guideposts for making greater utilization of the Tox21
analytical QC results in association with the Tox21 and ToxCast HTS
results.
