Introduction
Primary liver cancer is an important public health issue worldwide, standing at the sixth position in terms of cancer prevalence and being the fourth major cause of cancer deaths globally(Sung et al.
2021
; Yang and Heimbach
2020
). Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) serves as the leading histological form of primary liver neoplasm, accounting for roughly 70—80% of these malignant diseases (Llovet et al.
2021
). Despite the developments in clinical interventions including surgery, transplantation, chemotherapy, radiotherapy and immunotherapy for liver cancer the management of advanced HCC remains challenging with 5-year survival rates below 20% (Kulik and El-Serag
2019
). Therefore, there is a strong need to uncover new molecular markers and create efficient ways to treat individuals with HCC.
The protein LIMA1 (LIM domain and actin binding 1), alternatively called EPLIN (epithelial protein lost in neoplasm), which was originally discovered as an actin-binding protein by Maul and Chang et al. in 1999, shows preferential expression in human epithelia (Chang et al.
1998
). The LIMA1 gene, located on chromosome 12q13.12, consists of two isoforms: a longer EPLIN-β and a shorter EPLIN-α isoform (Wang et al.
2023a
). Previous studies have consistently demonstrated downregulation or loss of LIMA1 expression in various malignancies, including breast cancer (Jiang et al.
2008
), prostatic carcinoma (Zhang et al.
2011
) and esophageal cancer (Ohashi et al.
2017
). However, recent evidence has revealed a contrasting pattern, with high expression of LIMA1 observed in HNSCC (Ma et al.
2022
) and colorectal cancer (Li et al.
2023a
), as well as other malignancies, suggesting a more complex role for LIMA1.
The Wnt and Hippo pathways are two signaling pathways that hold significant importance in the onset and progression of HCC. The Wnt/β-catenin signaling cascade, known for its evolutionary conservation, has been implicated in enhancing the proliferative, invasive, and stem cell-like properties of hepatocellular carcinoma (He and Tang
2020
). The start of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway is brought about by the interaction of Wnt ligands with their appropriate receptors on the cell membrane The pathway culminates in the activation of β-catenin, which acts as its terminal mediator (Aberle et al.
1997
; Valenta et al.
2012
). Upon receipt of the upstream activation signal, intracellular accumulation of β-catenin occurs rapidly. Thereafter, β-catenin relocates to the cell nucleus and associates with TCF/LEF transcriptional factors. This triggers the expression of genes within the Wnt/β-catenin pathway that govern cellular processes like survival, differentiation, proliferation, and migration (Cruciat and Niehrs
2013
).
The Hippo pathway, inhibits the growth, migration, and angiogenesis mainly by inhibiting the transcriptional function of YAP and TAZ, while also being associated with the differentiation and stem cell properties of HCC (Liu et al.
2020
). The Wnt pathway and the Hippo pathway can influence and regulate each other through direct or indirect ways, and these relationships may involve multiple molecules and mechanisms (Li et al.
2019
); Research on this can help to uncover the pathological mechanism of HCC and also aid in finding new therapeutic targets and approaches. However, it remains unclear whether LIMA1 can exert its tumor-promoting effects by influencing the WNT/β-catenin and Hippo pathways.
M6A Methylation, is the most prevalent internal chemical modification in eukaryotic mRNA(Lan et al.
2019a
; Dominissini et al.
2012
).It influences mRNA stability, localization, and alternative splicing by modifying coding sequences (CDS) and untranslated regions (UTR) (Lan et al.
2019b
). This procedure is regulated by a variety of enzymes, among which are m6A methyltransferases, readers, and demethylases(He et al.
2019
). The m6A methyltransferase complex comprises multiple enzymes including METTL3, WTAP,METTL14, and RBM15B, as the core enzyme, has a crucial catalytic function in enhancing mRNA m6A modification(Vu et al.
2017
). Recent research has indicated that METTL3-induced m6A modification is strongly connected with the occurrence and advancement of cancer(Wang et al.
2021
). In clinical tissues of HCC, the expression of METTL3 is upregulated. Its level is distinctly linked to an unfavorable prognosis for those with hepatocellular carcinoma(Chen et al.
2018
). Research shows that METTL3 is associated with the proliferation, invasion, and metastasis of HCC cells, and thus drives the oncogenesis and progression of HCC(Pan et al.
2021
). However, the mechanism by which METTL3 mediates the m6A modification of LIMA1affecting HCC progression has not been reported.
This research demonstrated that LIMA1 is remarkably highly expressed in HCC and has a crucial part to play in the self-renewal, tumor-forming capacity, growth, and spread of liver cancer cells. Mechanistically, we discovered that LIMA1 interacts with β-catenin, promoting its nuclear translocation and activation of downstream signaling pathways. Additionally, our research revealed that METTL3 regulates the stability of LIMA1 mRNA through m6A methylation in HCC. These findings show that LIMA1 not only has an important function in the emergence and advancement of HCC but also might act as a likely therapeutic target by virtue of its interaction with the Wnt/Hippo signaling pathways.
Materials and methods
Clinical samples, cell lines, animals
The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) provided data on gene expression levels (measured as FPKM) and clinical details, which were collected. This dataset encompasses 371 hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) samples and 160 samples of normal hepatic tissue.
All human HCC tissue samples were obtained from Shanxi Bethune Hospital, and no patient had any additional cancer diagnosis or had received any anticancer therapy prior to surgical resection. All participants are presented fully informed consent and the Shanxi Bethune Research Ethics Committee gave authorization for this study (No.YXLL-2022–005).
The cell lines (HepG2 and HepG2 2.15) were procured from the Chinese Academy of Sciences' Shanghai Cell Bank. These cell strains (Hep3B2.1–7, SUN-387) were purchased from Suzhou Starfish Biotechnology Co., Ltd.The liver cancer cell lines, including THLE-3, MHCC-97L, MHCC-97H, and Huh 7, were acquired from Fudan University’s Zhongshan Hospital Liver Cancer Research Institute in Shanghai, China. These cell lines underwent comprehensive characterization through a series of assessments: cellular morphology, viability post-thaw, isoenzyme profiles, DNA fingerprinting, and screenings for mycoplasma, bacterial, and fungal contaminants. The HCC cell lines were maintained in DMEM enriched with 10% FBS and a blend of 100 international units per milliliter (IU/ml) of penicillin and 100 µg per milliliter (μg/ml) of streptomycin. These cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humid environment with 5% CO2.
Male nude mice of 4-to 6-week age were obtained from Sperford, Beijing, China, and were kept in specific pathogen-free circumstances in the Shanxi Bethune Center for Experimental Animals. The experimental procedures involving animals received the green light from the ethics panel of Shanxi Bethune Hospital( SBQQL-2022–038).
TMT proteomic sequencing
Proteins were isolated from various samples, quantified for concentration, and uniformly digested with trypsin to yield peptides from the respective samples.Distinct TMT tags were used to label the peptides individually, which were then combined in equal proportions into a single sample.This combined sample was subsequently fractionated and purified through either ion exchange chromatography or high-performance liquid chromatography technique. Liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) was utilized to examine the purified samples. APTBIO conducted the sequencing and data analysis (Shanghai, China).

https://www.gpsadb.com/

http://rm2target.canceromics.org/#/home

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)
Tumor specimens were first preserved using 4% paraformaldehyde, then encased in paraffin before being sectioned into slices of 5 µm in thickness. These slices were incubated with 10% goat serum to prevent non-specific binding and then subjected to incubation with a mouse-derived anti-EPLIN antibody(1:25,sc-136399, Santa).
Immunofluorescence
The cultured cells on coverslips were subjected to fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde and subsequent permeabilization using 0.5% Triton X—100 within PBS. After being blocked by a solution that contains 5% bovine serum albumin in PBS, the cells were then treated with a Mouse anti-Beta-Catenin antibody.(80,488–1-RR,Proteintech), and Rabbit anti-EPLIN (16,639–1-AP,Proteintech). The secondary antibodies were FITC Conjugated AffiniPure Goat Anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (BA1101,Boster), and CY3 Conjugated AffiniPure Goat Anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (BA1032,Boster). The coverslips were counterstained with DAPI. Laser scanning confocal microscopy (FV3000,Olympus) or fluorescence microscope (Ts2RFL Nikon) was used to observe the cells.

RNA-Sequencing (RNA-Seq)
APTBIO (Shanghai, China)was responsible for carrying out the RNA-Seq experiment, performing high-throughput sequencing, and analyzing the resulting data.
Transwell assay
As stated earlier, transwell plates having an 8 μm pore size and being 24-well in format from Corning (NY, USA) were utilized for evaluating the mobility of HCC cells. 24 h after cells were seeded, the chambers were collected for crystal violet staining. Three random views were captured under 100 × magnification by a microscope (Ts2RFL Nikon) for each chamber. The quantity of stained cells was determined by Image-Pro Plus v6.0 software.
Cell counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay
A CCK-8 assay kit from Boster (Wuhan, China) was employed to assess the viableness of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cells. Initially, the cells were sown at a concentration of (2 × 10
3
) cells per 100 μl in a 96-well plate and maintained in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere at 37 °C. The stock solution was diluted by fresh DMEM in a ratio of one to nine to obtain the CCK-8 solution. After removing the original medium from the wells, 100 μl of the diluted CCK-8 solution was poured into every well,subsequently, the plate was maintained at 37 °C for an extra two hours. After incubation, a microplate detector from Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc. in Germany was employed to record the optical density at 450 nm.
Colony formation
In 6-well plates, HCC cell colonies were cultivated, and every well contained 1000 cells along with 2 mL of medium. The cells were kept at 37 °C in an environment with 5% CO₂ for 14 days, receiving fresh medium bi-daily. Post-incubation, the colonies were preserved using 70% ethanol and subsequently dyed with 0.5% crystal violet solution. colonies were conserved using 70% ethanol and later colored by 0.5% crystal violet solution. The program ImageJ from NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA was utilized to tally colonies consisting of at least 50 cells.
RNA extraction and quantitative Real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)
Cellular and tissue RNA was obtained by using Trizol reagent from Invitrogen, which is located in Carlsbad, CA, USA. The M5 Sprint qPCR RT kit of Mei5bio, Beijing, China, enabled the transformation of mRNA into cDNA by reverse transcription. In the qRT-PCR process, SYBR premix from Takara, Tokyo, Japan was employed, and the process was performed on the 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System of Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA. For mRNA normalization, ATCB served as the reference gene.
Extraction of nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions
A kit for separating subcellular structures was obtained from Boster in Wuhan, China was engaged to effect the partition of cytoplasmic and nuclear elements in HCC cells. The cells were broken open in a buffer having protease inhibitors and then left to chill on ice for 10 min. After a double wash with PBS, a protein lysis reagent was applied. Post-centrifugation at a centrifugal force of 12,000 g for 10 min, the upper layer was used to collect the nuclear fraction.

Cell cycle assay
Hepatocellular carcinoma cells underwent transfection with either siRNA or plasmids for a duration of 48 h. Close to a million cells were then collected and subjected to fixation using 75% ethanol at a temperature of 20 °C throughout the night. Following this, the cells were treated with a Propidium Iodide staining solution (sourced from Solarbio) and maintained under a condition of 37 °C for a duration of 30 min. Post-staining, the distribution of the cell cycle was determined using a FACSCalibur flow cytometer.
Cancer cells stemness test
The assessment of stemness in HCC tumors is conducted through the evaluation of spheroid formation rates. For this purpose, HCC cells were grown in a DMEM/F12 medium without serum to which penicillin (100 IU/ml) was added streptomycin (100 μg/ml), human recombinant epidermal growth factor (20 ng/ml), recombinant basic fibroblast growth factor (20 ng/ml), 1% non-essential amino acids, 1% GlutaMax, 2% B27 supplement, and 1% methylcellulose, to facilitate spheroid culture. The cells were then incubated in petri dishes designed for ultra-low attachment. The efficiency of spheroid formation (SFE) was quantified as the ratio of spheroids exceeding 75 μm in diameter to the total count of cells initially seeded in every well. The Limiting Dilution Neurosphere Formation Assays were executed in accordance with the methodologies outlined in references (Li et al.
2023b
; Larionova et al.
2022
). In brief, glioma stem cells (GSCs) derived from separated neurospheres were allocated into 96-well plates at varying densities of 20, 40, 80, and 100 cells per well and were cultured over a span of 7 days. The acquired data were processed using the software for Extreme Limiting Dilution Analysis accessible at the provided web link(
https://bioinf.wehi.edu.au/software/elda/index.html
).
Coimmunoprecipitation (co-IP)
Cell lysates were introduced to culture plates to achieve complete lysis at a temperature of 4 °C. When subjected to centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for ten minutes, the transparent supernatant was delicately removed. Some of the lysate was set aside for ancillary experiments, while the remainder received an addition of the specified antibody (10 μg) and was left to incubate at 4 °C through the night. The PierceTM Protein A/G Agarose Beads (Proteintech Group PR40023) underwent multiple washes with the lysis buffer to ensure thorough preparation. Subsequently, these beads were placed in the cell lysate and left to incubate throughout the night at 4 °C. Subsequently, a brief spin at 2,500 rpm for three minutes was carried out at the same temperature. After discarding the supernatant, the beads were subjected to three successive washes using 1 ml of the lysis buffer each time. Subsequently, 100 μl of 2 × SDS loading buffer was put into the beads. After that, the combination was heated to 95 °C for five minutes before conducting Western Blot analysis.
MeRIP-qPCR
MeRIPRT-qPCR-A-P-9028-KIT was purchased from IVDSHOW. Total RNA was first extracted, followed by RNA fragmentation.The magnetic A/G beads were combined with the m6A-specific antibody and then introduced to the fragmented RNA for incubation. This step facilitated the enrichment and subsequent purification of RNA fragments that bore the m6A mark. Following this, both the enriched RNA and the initial input were subjected to reverse transcription to form cDNA. Subsequently, quantitative PCR (qPCR) was employed to assess the relative expression levels of genes. The m6A modification’s relative presence within the RNA of the target gene was quantified and presented as a percentage of the input%.

Tumor xenograft model
In order to develop a xenograft model of human hepatocellular carcinoma in athymic nude mice, a total of 5 million cells, which had been stably transfected with sh-Ctrl and sh-LIMA1 (obtained from Genepharma in Shanghai, China), were suspended in 200 µl and then injected into the flank zones of male nude mice at four weeks of age.Tumor dimensions were systematically documented tri-weekly utilizing this calculation:Volume (mm3) = Length (mm) × Width2 (mm2) / 2. At the end of 24 days,the mice were put to death, and then the tumor weights were determined.

METTL3-mediated M6A modification of LIMA1 mRNA in HCC
The mechanisms leading to aberrant LIMA1 expression are not fully understood. It is commonly known that DNA methylation and m6A methylation play essential roles in the advancement of cancer. Therefore, we explored whether the elevated expression of LIMA1 in hepatocellular carcinoma is associated with epigenetic alterations. Our analysis of the methylation and expression data of LIMA1 indicated that there was a negative correlation between DNA methylation levels and mRNA expression (Fig.
8
A). Given that DNA demethylation may be responsible for the upregulation of LIMA1 expression, we treated cells with low MHCC97H and LIMA1 expression with the DNA methyltransferase inhibitor 5-Azacytidine. However, we failed to notice any significant variation in detecting LIMA1 expression after a period of four days (Figure
S6
A).

We used the SRAMP database to predict the presence of M6A sites (Fig.

To further investigate the relationship between m6A regulation and LIMA1, we constructed the METTL3 knockdown sequence and found that METTL3 was downregulated in MHCC-97L and Hep3B2.1–7 cells, as was LIMA1 expression. Concurrently, the half-life of LIMA1 mRNA in HCC cells with METTL3 knockdown after being treated with the RNA synthesis inhibitor actinomycin D was shorter than in the corresponding control cells (Fig.

8

The total m6A methylation modification abundance of LIMA1 was examined in both control and MHCC-97H cells overexpressing METTL3. Initially, mRNA was extracted from the total RNA of the cells and then fragmented into smaller RNA fragments. Three sets of corresponding qPCR primers were designed for detection based on the possible m6A methylation modification sites on LIMA1 mRNA. The MeRIP analysis revealed a significant increase in m6A modification in LIMA1 mRNA when the expression level of METTL3 was increased, particularly in regions 1–2 of LIMA1(Fig.
8

Discussion
The significance of LIMA1 in numerous cancers has been widely investigated: Ma et al. (Ma et al.
2022
) reported that in vitro silencing of LIMA1 inhibited EMT and related pathways in HNSCs; Another research indicated that EPLIN-β was abundantly expressed in colorectal cancer cells and had a close relationship with prognosis (Li et al.
2023a
). However, it has also been shown to play a tumour suppressor role in some cancers. In cholangiocarcinoma, LIMA1 leads to increased stability of the cytoplasmic insulin-like growth factor 1 receptor (IGF1R) through ubiquitination degradation, which activates the PI3K/AKT pathway and promotes proliferation, migration and invasion of CCA cells (Jiang et al.
2023
). LIMA1 may be a favourable factor in gastric cancer and may also be associated with the sensitivity of patients to neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) (Gong et al.
2021
). In a study by Qi Y et al.(Qi et al.
2022
), the expression level of LIMA1 in HCC and adjacent tissues was determined by qRT-PCR. The outcomes demonstrated that the expression of LIMA1 was markedly decreased in HCC and was connected with increased tumor volume and decreased overall survival (OS). However, in this study, clinical samples were analysed using the TCGA database, proteomics sequencing, and through IHC and Western blot analysis, it was found that the mRNA and protein levels of LIMA1 were significantly elevated in HCC samples compared to normal liver tissue.In addition, LIMA1 expression was significantly associated with alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) positivity, hepatitis B virus (HBV) positivity and tumour size in HCC patients, and it was notably related to overall survival (OS) and disease-specific survival (DSS) in hepatitis B patients. We speculate that this discrepancy may be due to assay error or tumour heterogeneity. Our study also showed that LIMA1 was able to enhance the proliferation and invasiveness of HCC cells that exhibited a malignant phenotype. At the same time, inhibition of LIMA1 expression demonstrated the potential to reduce the tumourigenicity of HCC cells. These observations indicate that LIMA1 could be a diagnostic marker, a prognostic element and a therapeutic objective for HCC.
Increasingly, there is proof that cancer stem cells (CSCs) are found in the majority of solid tumors, including HCC(Cao et al.
2019
). Cancer stem cells (CSCs) make up a tiny fraction of tumour cells and have the capacity for self-renewal and differentiation, as well as the ability to induce tumour progression, metastasis and chemoresistance(Huo et al.
2017
). LCSCs(Liver cancer stem cells) are enriched for certain established markers such as CD90, CD133, SOX2, CD44 and others (Liu et al.
2020
; Ma et al.
2020
). This study has revealed a significant role of LIMA1 in the qualities of LCSCs.. The alteration of LIMA1 (either knockdown or upregulation) significantly affected the proliferation and self-renewal ability of LCSCs. Similarly, after knocking down LIMA1, the expression of stem cell markers and stem factors decreased. Our results provide crucial insights into the relationship between LIMA1 and LCSCs.
The Wnt-β-catenin signalling pathway is involved in cancer progression and is activated in several tumours, including HCC (Yu et al.
2021
). The Hippo signaling pathway performs an essential function in controlling organ size and keeping tissue homeostasis, and the dysregulation of YAP1/TAZ is associated with tumour cell proliferation, stem cell self-renewal and tumourigenesis (Liu et al.
2023
). In this research, we discovered that the Wnt-β-catenin and Hippo signaling pathways are implicated in LIMA1-mediated HCC progression Our data suggest that inhibition of the Wnt pathway attenuates the proliferation, migration, invasion and stemness enhancing effects of LIMA1 on HCC. Our study further demonstrates that LIMA1 can interact with β-catenin to promote its translocation into the nucleus and activate its downstream transcription factors (CyclinD1 and MYC) to drive HCC progression.
In this research, we discovered that the Wnt-β-catenin and Hippo signaling pathways are implicated in LIMA1-mediated HCC progression (Pan, et al.
2021
; Wang et al.
2023b
). In our study, we found that METTL3 could affect LIMA1 expression and its mRNA stability, and then further found by MeRIP that overexpression of METTL3 could increase the abundance of M6A modification sites of LIMA1. This suggests that METTL3 can directly modify the M6A level of LIMA1 downstream, thereby affecting LIMA1 expression.
Past studies have shown that the function of M6A modification sites is dependent on binding to M6A methylated reading proteins. In particular, the family of IGF2BPs (IGF2BP1, IGF2BP2, and IGF2BP3), as key M6A-reading proteins, plays a crucial role in mRNA stability (Lu et al.
2021
). We observed that the level of LIMA1 expression was in positive relation to IGF2BP1, IGF2BP2 and IGF2BP3, and the prediction results of RM2Target supported that IGF2BP1 and IGF2BP2 were able to bind to the M6A site of LIMA1. This suggests that IGF2BPs may regulate LIMA1 through the M6A site, but the specific details of this mechanism still need to be elucidated by further studies.
However, there are some limitations in our study. First of all, a greater sample size is needed to further confirm the prognostic importance of LIMA1 in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma. Second, regarding the upstream modifications, it is necessary to explore whether the methylation of LIMA1by METTL3 can alter the progression of HCC. Finally, the specific mechanism of how LIMA1affects the Hippo pathway remains to be further investigated.
In conclusion, we found that LIMA1is a novel tumor promoter in HCC, which interacts with the Wnt-β-catenin and Hippo signaling pathways, promoting tumor cell proliferation, invasion and stem cell properties. We also revealed that LIMA1expression is regulated by METTL3-mediated m6A modification, providing a possible explanation for the abnormally high expression of LIMA1in HCC. This study by us provides a potentially promising target for the diagnosis and therapy of HCC.
