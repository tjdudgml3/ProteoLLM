During healthy development, the fertilized egg (zygote) must divide to establish complex cell heterogeneity to pattern the vertebrate body plan in space and time. Knowledge of the proteome, the full set of proteins within a cell, is critical to understanding this process and controlling it on demand. Xenopus laevis

They shrink rapidly in size and proteome content upon each cell division, usually every ∼15 min. They also migrate over long distances in three dimensions during development. In
X. laevis

At present, smart data acquisition is the focus of active research to deepen the detectable portion of the single-cell proteome. Early on, capillary electrophoresis (CE) electrospray ionization (ESI) was tailored to data-dependent acquisition, identifying up to ∼400 proteins from ∼10 ng of proteome from giant
X. laevis

X. laevis

Here, we introduce the concept of Eco-driven IMS (Eco-IMS) to profile the proteomic state of single blastomeres in the animal cap, a tissue of pluripotent cells, in the
X
.
laevis
embryo. This report follows the original disclosure of the technology at the 2023 annual meeting of the American Society for Mass Spectrometry (
38
). With an ability to impart little internal energy to molecules during ionization (
40
,
41
), ESI is broadly accepted for probing the composition of the condensed phase by analyzing that of the generated ions in the gas phase. We reasoned that CE and IMS separate based on similar physical principles: they both move charged species under the effect of an external electric field. Therefore, we proposed that Eco-sorting based on charge (z), mass (m), and mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) during CE-ESI (
2
,
38
) would be translated from the liquid into the gas phase, essentially sorting peptide ions based on similar metrics and their resulting ion mobility. To test this hypothesis, we set out to correlate the detected migration time (MT), m/z, μ
ef
, and K
0
. Next, we benchmark the sensitivity and reproducibility of Eco-IMS for proteome identification and quantification on single-cell equivalent (∼200 pg) amounts of HeLa proteome digests. As a proof of principle, we deploy Eco-IMS to profile blastomeres that form the animal cap in the
X. laevis
blastula. Using unsupervised multivariate and statistical data analysis tools, we systematically quantify the proteomic state of nine cells in pursuit of cell heterogeneity in this otherwise classically viewed as a pluripotent population of cells critical to vertebrate development.
Experimental Procedures
Materials
The following solvents and chemicals were HPLC-grade and from Thermo Fisher Scientific: acetic acid (AcOH), acetonitrile (ACN), formic acid (FA), and methanol (MeOH). Ammonium bicarbonate (AmBic) was obtained from Avantor. Thermo Fisher Scientific supplied the HeLa protein digest standard (part no. 88329, Pierce) and N-dodecyl-β-D-maltoside (DDM, part no. 89903). The ESI emitter in the CE-ESI setup was fabricated from a pulled borosilicate glass capillary (0.75/1.00 mm inner/outer diameter, part no. B100-75-10, Sutter Instrument). The fused silica capillaries for CE were obtained from Polymicro Technologies (40/105 μm inner/outer diameter, part no. 1068150596) and used without further modification. MS-grade Trypsin Platinum (part no. VA900A, Promega) was used for proteome digestion. All protein and peptide samples were processed as a microarray of droplets on a fluorosilane-coated plate (termed as “microplate” hereon, Cytonix LLC).
Solutions
The
background electrolyte
(BGE) for CE was composed of 1 M FA and 25% (v/v) ACN (resulting pH 2.3). The
CE-ESI sheath solution
contained 10% (v/v) MeOH and 0.5% (v/v) AcOH. The
sample solvent
was prepared of 50% (v/v) ACN and 0.05% (v/v) FA. For dissociating the embryo to a cell suspension, the Newport buffer was prepared as instructed elsewhere (
42
). The cell
lysis solution
contained 0.2% DDM in 50 mM AmBic.
Animal Care and Embryology
Sexually mature
X. laevis
frogs were sourced from Nasco or Xenopus1 (Dexter, MI). All procedures related to the humane care and use of
X. laevis
were authorized by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Maryland, College Park (Approval nos. R-FEB-21-07 and R-FEB-24-05). To minimize biological variability, all the embryos in this study were obtained through the natural mating of a single pair of parents. Embryos with stereotypical cleavage were chosen at the two-cell stage and cultured to Nieuwkoop-Faber stage 8 following our earlier studies. Each embryo and cell was labeled with a unique identifier, although this information was intentionally hidden during data analysis and only revealed to facilitate the interpretation of results.
Single-Cell Collection and Proteome Processing
Using sharpened forceps, the vitelline membrane surrounding the stage-8 embryos was carefully peeled away. As illustrated in
Figure 1
, the tissue covering the animal cap was identified based on pigmentation and location, dissected using a micro knife (No. 10318-14, Fine Science Tools), and transferred into a Petri dish filled with the Newport buffer (
Fig. 1
). The tissue was dissociated to a suspension of cells under gentle agitation with a hair loop. The resulting cells were rinsed of salts
via
quick transfer into HPLC water using a pipette tip, without visual lysis or damage to the cells confirmed under a stereomicroscope. Finally, these blastomeres were individually pipetted into an array on a fluorosilane-coated slide (microplate). Each cell was lysed by manual addition of 500 nl of the
lysis buffer
, with incubation at room temperature for 10 min. The extracted single-cell proteomes were denatured by rapid heating to 60 °C for 15 min, then digested with 500 nl of 0.1 μg/μl trypsin platinum (in 50 mM AmBic buffer) at 40 °C over 1 h. Liquid evaporation was compensated for by the addition of 400 nl of HPLC water to each sample every ∼5 min. The resulting single-cell proteome digests were allowed to dry at room temperature, before storage at −80 °C (the “samples”) until analysis.

CE-nanoESI-MS
The samples were reconstituted in the sample solvent for analysis using CE-MS. The CE-nanoESI platform was constructed and operated as detailed earlier (

versus

Eco-IMS was developed on 500 pg portions of the HeLa proteome digest (approximating to ∼2 cells), before testing performance on ∼200 pg of proteome amounts, or ca. a single cell. In the classical IMS, ions were analyzed between 1/K
0

−2

Only ions with +2 and +3 charges were selected for detection in this study. All other parameters were set to the default.

Data Analysis
The primary MS–MS/MS files were processed with MSFragger in FragPipe 20.0 (
44
). Proteins were identified against the Human proteome (UP000005640, downloaded from UniProt on July 9, 2023, containing 20,523 entries) or
Xenopus
proteome (UP000694892, downloaded from UniProt on January 10, 2022, containing 43,236 entries). The search parameters were the following:
m/z
tolerance for precursor ion; 20 ppm;
m/z
tolerance for fragment ion; 20 ppm; static modification, cysteine carbamidomethylation; dynamic modifications, methionine oxidation; minimum peptide length, 5 amino acids; maximum peptide length, 50 amino acids; and maximal missed cleavage sites, 2. Technical replicates were analyzed together with the match-between-runs (MBR) function disabled for the HeLa and enabled for the
X. laevis
samples. All other parameters were set to the default.
Experimental Design and Statistical Rationale
The HeLa proteome digest, used to develop–test–optimize–validate the method, was measured in 3 to 5 technical replicates (TRs, same sample analyzed repeatedly). To demonstrate proof-of-principle for single-cell proteomics, n = 9 different single blastomeres were isolated from embryos that were obtained from the natural mating of the same pair of
X. laevis.
The cells were isolated and measured in a randomized order. The custom-built CE-ESI platform was validated on the tims-TOF PRO mass spectrometer daily using the HeLa reference proteome digest. Peptides and proteins were filtered to <1% false discovery rate, calculated against a reversed-sequence decoy proteome reference database. From the generated report, the number of unique peptides (peptides that only mapped to a specific protein), total peptides (peptides that mapped to a specific protein group), and sequence coverage (%) were tabulated (
Supplemental Data
). For proteins that were identified based on one unique peptide, the tandem MS spectra were extracted with FragPipe-PDV (
45
) and are published in the accompanying
Supplemental Data
document. In agreement with established conventions, proteins are reported as protein groups (“proteins” from total peptides), unless there is sufficient information to identify proteoforms (unique peptides).
Safety
General safety measures were followed when working with chemicals and biological specimens. Capillaries and ESI emitters were carefully handled to minimize puncture hazards. All electrically conductive elements of the CE-ESI platform were shielded or Earth-grounded. Its protective enclosure with a safety interlock provided additional safety measure from an accidental electroshock.
Results and Discussion
Wet Ion Sorting is About to Go Dry
Our experimental design for developing Eco-IMS is presented in
Figure 1
. The approach was refined, and then validated on single-cell-equivalent amounts of HeLa proteome digests (∼200 pg). We demonstrated Eco-IMS for the proteomic profiling of single blastomeres in the mid-stage
X. laevis
embryo (stage 8). The animal cap was selected as model tissue in this study, as this layer of stem-like cells is fated to give rise to the embryonic ectoderm, yet remains susceptible to induction to different fates (reviewed in (
46
)). The proteome of each of n = 9 different blastomeres was extracted and digested in 500 nl droplets of trypsin in 50 mM AmBic, directly on a fluorosilane-coated glass slide. These resulting digests were analyzed on a custom-built μCE-ESI platform as per our protocols (
2
,
28
,
29
). The generated ions were measured on a timsTOF PRO mass spectrometer. Detection was executed under ddaPASEF control, selected to obtain a 100% duty cycle during tandem MS (
26
). The proteome profile was approximated by calculating the label-free quantification (LFQ) index of each protein. The resulting cell profiles were systematically compared through an unsupervised multivariate–statistical analysis. Discovery single-cell proteomics raised the potential to appreciate molecular differences between these pluripotent stem-like cell populations.
Our analytical hypothesis was empirically testable. We reasoned that separation based on electrophoretic mobility (μ
ef
) differences in the liquid phase during CE share mechanistic similarities to IMS leveraging ion mobility differences in the gas phase—that is the movement of a charged species (in a media) under an external electric field.
Figure 2
tests the theory through interconnected data analyses of 500 pg of the HeLa proteome digest standard, approximating to ca. two cells.

A
exemplifies electrophoretic sorting of the peptides into charge- and m/z-dependent trends, the basis of Eco-MS (
38

Fig. 2
B
) following an earlier model (
47

Fig. 2
B
). This knowledge was logically necessary to explore a relationship between the liquid-phase μ
ef
and gas-phase ion mobilities (1/K
0
). High Pearson correlation moments (ρ = 0.81–0.97) across the charge states validated a general correlation between the separation mechanisms (
Fig. 2
C
). This fundamental relationship in turn raised the potential to transfer CE-sorting to the domain of IM. Indeed, the measured 1/K
0

Figure 2

. This result validated the working hypothesis of this study.

This correlation between ion sorting practically “wet” and “dry” served as the basis for developing Eco-IMS in this work. We recently found MT-dependent scheduling of the m/z domain to benefit sensitivity and quantification (
39
). This led to our reasoning that MT-dependent framing would also advance detection along the domain of IM separation. Compared to the classical approach to scan the entire range of IM, we leveraged Eco-sorting to analyze only the ranges of IM that peptides actually occupied with separation. This in turn promised to improve ion accumulation, thus enhancing detection sensitivity.
Figure 3
A
presents the approach, showcasing only the +2 charges for simplicity. In agreement with our previous findings (
38
,
39
), this charge state accounted for ∼86% of all identifications and was considered sufficient for this study; the other charge states can, of course, be (also) included in future embodiments of the method to deepen sensitivity. We designed Eco-IMS to temporally schedule ddaPASEF along the following four frames tailoring to these +2 states (Frame no., range of separation time [min], range of ion mobility [V s cm
−2
]): #1, 20–27, 0.65–1.00; #2, 27–32, 0.60–1.20; #3, 32–40, 0.80–1.40; and #4, 40–45, 0.70–1.05. To alleviate technical complexity, Frame #2 was designed to encompass two subframes with an intentionally broader range of IM. As the benchmark of performance, the classical ddaPASEF was performed with the default parameters: the IM was sampled between the entire 0.6 to 1.6 V s cm
−2

We benchmarked the performance of proteome identification. Using identical cycle time, we projected narrower spans of IM to allow for slowing down the rate of gas-phase separation in Eco-IMS, therefore improving IM resolution. We anticipated other metrics building on resolution to also improve, including detection sensitivity. Indeed, Eco-IMS identified 1209 HeLa proteins, marking a 53% improvement from the 790 proteins detected under the classical approach (
Fig. 3
B). Ca. 94% of the proteins that were identified by the classical ddaPASEF were also measured by the Eco-driven IMS. The electronic

We, too, wondered about the origin of these clusters, especially among this supposedly similar set of pluripotent stem cells. On revealing the identity of each animal cap, no correlations were found with the timing of mating or the order in which the cells were isolated, processed, or analyzed. These molecular differences must afford functional significance, we concluded.
To probe deeper, we sharpened our single-cell proteomics lens to statistical confidence. Hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) was adapted as an orthogonal means to inspect the data. HCA grouped the samples into the same α, β, and γ clusters as UMAP.

Conclusions
Ths study developed and validated Eco-IMS for single-cell CE-ESI-MS proteomics. Using a timsTOF PRO mass spectrometer executing ddaPASEF, we found CE-driven Eco-framing of IM separation to improve protein identification and quantification. From single-cell equivalent HeLa proteome digests, Eco-IMS employing 4 IM–MT frames on this previous-generation mass spectrometer returned 962 proteins without MBR. This marked a 50% enhancement in proteome coverage compared to the classical ddaPASEF. There are several technological developmental opportunities to further Eco-MS. For example, automation to process cells would allow for exploring larger cell populations, thus beginning to complement already available single-cell transcriptome maps on embryonic cell differentiation. Analogously to our recent findings in Eco-driven DIA (
38
,
39
,
62
), we anticipate Eco-IMS proteome coverage to improve substantially when using a higher number of IM-MT frames; alternatively, the trends may be traced live.
Eco-IMS was able to find previously unknown proteomic differences between blastomeres in the embryonic animal cap tissue. Eco-IMS on our timsTOF PRO platform identified 1157 proteins from n = 9 single blastomeres that were manually isolated from the animal cap of the developing
X. laevis
embryo. UMAP and HCA, two orthogonal models of multivariate data analysis, independently organized these cells into the same 3 clusters based on the calculated LFQ profiles. HCA suggested these groups be driven, in part, by systematic differences in protein components related to the control of the cell cycle, development, protein translation, and energy production. Eco-IMS effectively sharpened our molecular lens to cell differentiation. As demonstrated here, new knowledge on the gene translational state of cells not only can help address existing questions but also generate new hypotheses to help better understand cell differentiation. We believe Eco-IMS to be an effective bioanalytical tool to this end both in a discovery and targeted proteomics setting.
