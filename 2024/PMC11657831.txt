Introduction
Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a hematologic malignancy characterized by genetic heterogeneity. Numerous genomic biomarkers have been revealed with the development of sequencing technology, and in the 2022 World Health Organization (WHO) classification, the 2022 recommendations of European LeukemiaNet (ELN) and International Consensus Classification (ICC) guidelines, AML with
NPM1
,
CEBPA
,
RUNX1
,
FLT3
, and
TP53
was listed as molecular risk categories, and among them,
RUNX1
,
TP53
, and
AXLS1
were considered to have a strong negative impact on the outcome of AML [
1
–
3
]. Despite these advances, there remains the occasion that some patients classified with favorable genetic features reflected dismal survival outcomes with standard intensive chemotherapy or hypomethylating agents [
4
]. Despite the extensive research on the genomic and transcriptomic features of AML [
5
–
7
], there is still a need for more comprehensive and accurate characteristics to precisely describe its prognostic risk. With the advancement of research methods, the application of proteomics and metabolomics in the exploration of disease molecular features has been increasing. Proteomics aims to delve deeper into the composition, structure, expression patterns, interactions of proteins, and their associations with different metabolic pathways. Proteins, as the direct executors of biological functions, can provide insights into the dynamic molecular behavior of cells through direct detection, thereby enhancing our understanding of the genotype-to-phenotype transition [
8
,
9
]. Furthermore, numerous studies in recent years have pointed out that tumor-related metabolic alterations have emerged as novel cancer biomarkers. For instance, AML blasts with high glycolytic activity are closely associated with poor prognosis [
10
]. Therefore, combining proteomics and metabolomics to further explore the molecular characteristics of AML is expected to provide deeper insights into its pathogenesis and the discovery of protein-based drug targets.
A tumor microenvironment (TME) is the environment where a tumor originates, growth, diffusion, and metastasis [
11
]. As a malignant tumor of the hematopoietic system, the TME of AML is the bone marrow microenvironment (BMM), which was first expounded by Schofield in 1978 [
12
]. It is widely considered that BMM plays a role in hematopoiesis, and leukemogenesis [
13
–
15
]. The BMM in patients with AML is characterized by an immunosuppressive milieu promoting tumor tolerance, immune escape, and disease growth [
16
,
17
]. Leukemia cells reside in the bone marrow niche, releasing soluble mediators that mediate communication with AML-supporting cells to promote the development of leukemia [
18
–
21
].
In our study, we investigated the profile of multi-omic bone marrow supernatant, an important part of BMM. We hypothesize that the expression levels of certain specific proteins and metabolites in bone marrow supernatant are closely associated with the relapse of AML. These molecules may serve as potential biomarkers, indicating the risk of disease recurrence, and may also play a pivotal role in the relapse process. By elucidating the relationship between these molecules and disease recurrence, we aim to provide new strategies and methods for predicting and treating relapsed AML. To validate this hypothesis, we conducted a series of rigorous experimental studies. Using mass spectrometry analysis, we determined the protein and metabolite expression profiles in bone marrow supernatant and screened out candidate molecules related to recurrence through bioinformatics methods. We further analyzed the functions of these molecules and explored their mechanisms in disease recurrence. Based on the above hypothesis and research design, our study provides a reasonable basis for deeply analyzing the role of bone marrow supernatant in disease recurrence. Through this research, we hope to discover new predictors and therapeutic targets for relapse AML, providing strong support for clinical diagnosis and treatment.
Materials and methods
Patient identification and collection
A total of 40 bone marrow supernatant were collected from 7 patients with relapsed AML and 33 patients with non-relapsed AML in Chongqing General Hospital and Chongqing Xinqiao Hospital from August 2019 to August 2021. All the patients were diagnosed according to the French–American–British (FAB) and WHO classifications and excluded blood diseases such as acute lymphoblastic leukemia and malignant histiocytosis; aged 15–80 years. The definition of relapse is the reappearance of leukemic cells in the peripheral blood or the presence of more than 5% primitive cells in the bone marrow (excluding other reasons such as bone marrow regeneration after consolidation chemotherapy) or the invasion of leukemic cells outside the bone marrow. Relapse-free survival (RFS) was used to indicate disease outcomes [
22
] and events were defined as hematological recurrence [
23
] All survival endpoints were censored at the date of last follow-up when relapse or death was not observed [
24
]. Among the 7 patients with recurrence, including 2 dead patients. Therefore, we classified death and recurrence into one group for research.
Sample preparation
The bone marrow was collected after clinical diagnosis and was centrifuged at 1500 rpm at 10 °C for 5 min. The bone marrow supernatant was stored in a -80° freezer without further processing.

Proteomics analysis
40 bone marrow supernatant samples were used to perform the screening assay by using proteomics. The proteomic analysis was performed by Shanghai OE Biotech. Co., Ltd. Briefly, each 40 µl sample of bone marrow supernatant was diluted tenfold and then washed through a chromatographic column. Samples with albumin and IgG removed were collected and freeze-dried. Subsequently, 300 µl of Sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) lysis buffer was added for redissolution, and the solution was centrifuged at 12,000×g for 10 min at room temperature. The supernatant was collected, and the centrifugation step was repeated to obtain the total protein solution. The Bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein concentration assay method was adopted [
1
]. The color reagent was prepared according to the instructions of the BCA kit, and the protein solution to be tested was diluted. A 96-well plate was prepared with gradient Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) standard protein solutions and the protein solution to be tested. After adding the color reagent to each well and allowing the reaction to occur, the absorbance values were measured using an enzyme labeling instrument. The protein concentration of the sample was then calculated based on the standard curve. Ten micrograms of protein from each sample were separated using 12% Sodium Dodecyl Sulfate PolyAcrylamide Gel Electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and stained with Coomassie Blue using the eStain LG protein staining instrument. The stained gels were then imaged using a fully automatic digital gel image analysis system. After enzymatic digestion and desalting, the samples were subjected to high-resolution Liquid Chromatography Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) mass spectrometry scanning. The mass spectrometry spectra were matched with theoretical spectra from the Fasta database, converting machine signals into peptide and protein sequence information. Spectrum libraries were established by integrating sequence, retention time, and fragment ion information. The raw LC-MS/MS data files were imported into the Spectronaut Pulsar software for data analysis.
Untargeted liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry analysis
40 samples of AML bone marrow supernatants were screened and measured using metabolomics, which was conducted by Shanghai OE Biotech. Co., Ltd. The samples preserved at -80℃ were thawed and 100µL was transferred to an EP tube. Then, a methanol-acetonitrile protein precipitating agent containing L-2-chlorophenylalanine was added, followed by vortex shaking, ultrasonic extraction in an ice-water bath, and standing. After centrifugation, the supernatant was volatilized, redissolved with methanol-water, and then rested and centrifuged again. The filtered supernatant was transferred to an LC sample vial and analyzed on a liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry system composed of an ACQUITY UPLC I-Class plus ultra-high performance liquid chromatography-tandem QE high-resolution mass spectrometer. For chromatographic conditions, we selected the ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 chromatographic column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm) and set the column temperature to 45℃. The mobile phase consisted of phase A water (containing 0.1% formic acid) and phase B acetonitrile (containing 0.1% formic acid), with a flow rate set to 0.35mL/min. The injection volume during the experiment was 2 µL. Experimental analysis was conducted on the liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry system according to the chromatographic conditions set above. The raw experimental data were processed on Progenesis QI v2.3 (provided by Nonlinear Dynamics, Newcastle, UK). The processing steps included baseline filtering, peak identification, integration, retention time correction, peak alignment, and normalization. The main parameters were set as follows: precursor tolerance was set to 5 ppm (using public databases) or 10 ppm (using self-built libraries), while product tolerance was set to 10 ppm (using public databases) or 20 ppm (using self-built libraries). After preprocessing the data, we identified compounds based on accurate mass numbers, secondary fragments, and isotope distributions. This identification process utilized public databases such as The Human Metabolome Database (HMDB), Lipidmaps (v2.3), and METLIN, as well as self-built libraries for qualitative analysis.
Gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) functional analysis
To explore the potential biological functions of differential proteins and related pathways, GO enrichment and KEGG pathway analysis were performed using the DAVID database (
https://david.ncifcrf.gov/
) [
25
]. The visualization of the enrichment results is performed by the “ggplot” and “dplyr” packages in R (version 4.2.3).
Statistical analysis
Kruskal-Wallis test was used to compare differences among various groups in the analysis of baseline information for the study subjects, with a
p
-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant. The proteins and metabolites with more than 15 missing values were excluded, and the remaining missing values were filled in using the mean imputation method. Subsequently, the levels of proteins and metabolites were normalized for use in subsequent research. Wilcox test was performed to calculate
p
-values, which were then corrected for False Discovery Rate (FDR).The Wilcoxon test was used to calculate the
p
-values, Proteins and metabolites with adjusted
p
-values (adj.
p
.Val) less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant and were selected as differential proteins and metabolites. COX proportional regression model was employed to assess the prognostic impact on patients’ relapse-free survival (RFS), with adjustments made for age and gender. The sensitivity and specificity of differential proteins in predicting AML recurrence were evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the area under the curve (AUC). All
p
-values were corrected using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method. All statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.2.3) and SPSS 25.0, ensuring scientific rigor and accuracy.

The overall landscape of proteomics
Data independent acquisition (DIA) technology was performed to identify proteins of AML bone marrow supernatant. In our study, 1688 proteins were identified in the AML bone marrow supernatant. Proteins with missing values exceeding 15 were excluded, and the remaining missing values were filled in with the mean. After normalizing the protein levels, a total of 996 reliable proteins were obtained for subsequent research. Based on the proteomic data, the “euclidean” method was employed to calculate the distances between different patients. Patients were grouped into 7 subclusters based on Manhattan distance (Fig.

Alterations in bone marrow supernatant metabolites profiling of the relapse AML
According to the results of functional enrichment of relapse-related protein, a large number of relapse-related proteins were concentrated in metabolic-related pathways, indicating that the regulation of metabolism may play an important role in the relapse of AML. Therefore, we further explored the metabolomic profile of the bone marrow microenvironment in AML. To illustrate the metabolic alterations in AML, we performed an untargeted metabolomics approach using bone marrow supernatant. In total, 4,831 metabolites were identified. After dividing the samples into relapse AML and non-relapse AML cohorts, the Wilcox test was conducted to assess statistical differences with an adjusted
p
-value < 0.05 as the threshold, totally 190 relapse-related metabolites were selected(Supplementary Table 3), the volcano plot(Fig.

Furthermore, we also conducted an analysis on the metabolites in the risk mutation group and the non-risk mutation group. The results showed that 222 metabolites were closely related to risk mutations, of which 95 were up-regulated and 127 were down-regulated(Supplementary Table 5). These differential metabolites were mainly enriched in critical processes such as alpha-linolenic acid metabolism, steroid hormone biosynthesis, and cancer pathways(Supplementary Fig. 2).

Discussion
The bone marrow microenvironment is a dynamic and complex ecology [
29
]. The current studies on the AML bone marrow proteome use nucleated cells in bone marrow, while we use bone marrow supernatant, which is not common in previous studies. Compared with bone marrow nucleated cells, bone marrow supernatant is rich in extracellular matrix and cytokines, which are the key components of BMM. More importantly, as an indispensable and easily accessible experimental sample in clinical diagnosis, bone marrow supernatant provides us with a unique research perspective. By comparing intracellular proteins with extracellular proteins, we can more clearly identify which proteins play a more critical role in the occurrence and development of AML in the extracellular microenvironment. In this study, we groundbreakingly depicted a bone marrow supernatant multi-omic landscape of relapse AML and identified relapsed-related proteins and relapse-related metabolites, through proteomic, metabolomic and genomic analyses, we revealed new protein and metabolite markers associated with known high-risk gene mutations (
ASXL1
,
TP53
, and
RUNX1
), which may provide new perspectives for the early diagnosis and treatment of relapsed AML. Of course, there are also some biases here, which need to be further explored in follow-up studies to further explore the relationship between bone marrow supernatant and bone marrow nucleated cells.
A recent proteomic and phosphoproteomic study on AML [
30
] established a comprehensive proteome and phosphoproteome database. It identified both known and novel dysregulated proteins in AML, revealing potential immunotherapeutic targets. These targets had been unattainable in previous genomic and transcriptome-level studies, thus facilitating further exploration of protein dysregulation in AML pathogenesis. Moreover, proteomic analysis has revealed subtypes of AML [
31
]. Notably, one subtype showed a poor prognosis despite lacking genomic markers of high risk. This subtype is characterized by high expression of mitochondrial proteins, which has not been described in previous genomic studies and is reflected exclusively in the proteome. In the 40 AML samples of our study, the frequency of
ASXL1
,
TP53
, and
RUNX1
mutations was 15%, 10.0%, and 7.5%, respectively. The overall three-mutation frequency was 30.0%. What’s more, we found that the expression of the proteins in the bone marrow microenvironment was closely correlated with
ASXL1
,
TP53
, and
RUNX1
by unsupervised clustering, which means the protein expression of the bone marrow microenvironment was tightly correlated with the prognosis of AML. The PCA results also confirmed that the proteins exist a separation between the patients with and without
ASXL1
,
TP53
, and
RUNX1
mutation. These three mutations also showed a close relationship with metabolism.
So far, relapse is still a common condition in the treatment of AML, occurring in 40–50% of young patients and the vast majority of elderly patients [
32
]. Previously, using single-cell analysis techniques, researchers such as Hope Mumme and Beena E. Thomas revealed alterations in the tumor microenvironment associated with relapse and remission of pediatric AML. They identified seven gene markers specifically associated with leukemia cells and observed significant transcriptomic differences by comparing leukemia cells from relapse and remission groups [
33
]. In this study, we explored the differences between the bone marrow supernatants of patients with relapsed AML and those with non-relapsed AML. Through analysis, we discovered 57 differential proteins associated with relapsed AML. Among these 57 proteins, we identified as being associated with relapse Thrombospondin-4(
THBS4
) exhibited the most statistically significant difference.
THBS4
, an extracellular glycoprotein involved in wound healing and tissue remodeling [
34
], showed a strong correlation with relapse in our study. Through multivariate Cox regression analysis, we investigated proteins associated with RFS. Although they did not exhibit significant differences (
p
< 0.05) after
p
-value correction using the same FDRprocedure, these proteins still showed a certain degree of correlation with RFS. Therefore, we presented these proteins in our results using the uncorrected
p
-values. In particular,
THBS4
exhibited a trend of association with RFS, and when combined with protein data related to relapse, this suggests to some extent that
THBS4
may be involved in disease relapse. Previous studies have linked high expression of
THBS4
with poor prognosis in hepatocellular carcinoma and gastric cancer [
35
–
37
]. In a study on the tumor microenvironment of gastric cancer, stromal cells expressing
THBS4
also expressed markers of CAFs [
34
].
THBS4
promotes the adhesion, migration, and proliferation of endothelial cells, and enhances angiogenesis in cancer models [
38
]. Therefore, we speculate that
THBS4
has the potential to become a prognostic marker for AML.
There has been a growing interest in recent studies related to the metabolism of AML. Many studies have focused on exploring the metabolic weaknesses of leukemia cells. For example, Cunningham et al. demonstrated that methionine depletion can be exploited for the treatment of AML, providing insights into the mechanisms of cellular metabolism and methionine recycling [
39
]. However, there is currently a lack of research on AML relapse that specifically focuses on the AML microenvironment, Therefore, our study further explored the metabolites associated with AML relapse. Using the Wilcox test, we screened out 190 metabolites associated with relapse, including Blumenol.C.glucoside, Guaiacol, and others.
Past research noted that lipid balance plays a very important role in LSC function [
40
]. In our study, a large number of AML-related metabolites and their functions are enriched in lipid metabolic pathways like Steroid hormone biosynthesis, which the metabolites associated with AML, AML with
ASXL1
,
TP53
, and
RUNX1
mutations, and relapse AML enriched in. The early research on the steroid hormone effect on the myeloid leukemia cell line of murine was conducted by Lotem and Sachs in 1974 [
41
,
42
]. In our study, Testosterone, a steroid hormone, drives the development of AML along with some proteins like
ALDOA
,
AOC3
, and
ENO1
, which were enriched in the metabolic pathway. The past study conducted by Romina P. Grinspon et al. has shown that the level of Testosterone in pediatric hematologic malignancy of testicular secretion fluctuates because of the effects of standardized treatment on reproductive secretion [
43
]. Our study found the level of Testosterone has a close association with
ASXL1
,
TP53
, and
RUNX1
mutations. These genes play crucial roles in the pathogenesis of AML, and their mutational status is closely associated with disease progression and prognosis. Consequently, changes in testosterone levels may influence the occurrence and development of AML through the regulation of these genetic mutations. This discovery not only contributes to our deeper understanding of the pathogenesis of AML but also potentially opens up new strategies for clinical diagnosis and treatment. For instance, by monitoring patients’ testosterone levels, we may be able to detect the risk of AML earlier or predict the progression and prognosis of the disease.
Our article also had some limitations. First, the follow-up period was not sufficiently long to evaluate long-term survival outcomes. Second, limited by the small sample size, we had not been able to establish a reliable prognostic model and were not able to perform external validation experiments.
In general, our study revealed for the first time the multi-omics characteristics of bone marrow supernatant in relapse AML patients, unearthed proteins and metabolites closely related to the relapse of AML. This discovery not only highlights the systematic differentiation of multiple metabolic pathways during disease recurrence, particularly glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and steroid hormone biosynthesis but also reveals the potential crucial roles of these proteins and metabolites in the recurrence mechanism of AML. These proteins and metabolites deserved further validation in a large-scale clinical trial, and these proteins and metabolic shared pathways were also potentially worthy of further studying the pathogenesis of AML.
