Summary
In this study, we investigated the role of DDIT4 in
H pylori
infection-induced gastric lesions using various cell lines, transgenic mice, and human tissue samples. We highlight that knockout of DDIT4 promoted
Helicobacter pylori
-induced gastric metaplasia through induction of metabolic ferroptosis and show that DDIT4 is an important protective gene in the pathogenesis of
Helicobacter pylori
.
Helicobacter pylori
(
H pylori
) is a microaerophilic gram-negative bacillus that is commonly acquired in childhood and persists throughout life if untreated.
1
As one of the most common infections,
H pylori
is considered as the major cause of various upper gastrointestinal diseases, including peptic ulcers and gastric cancer.
2
,
3
Pelayo Correa proposed a stepwise process of gastric carcinogenesis, known as the Correa’s cascade, starting from chronic gastritis often caused by
H pylori
infection, progressing to atrophic gastritis, gastric intestinal metaplasia (GIM), dysplasia, and ultimately gastric adenocarcinoma.
4
H pylori
infection initiates gastric inflammation and drives DNA damage such as oxidative damage via reactive oxygen species (ROS), and nitric oxide.
5
,
6
Sustained gastric inflammation and increased gastric tissue damage result in the development of spasmolytic polypeptide-expressing metaplasia (SPEM), further progressing to GIM lesions. Loss of acid-secreting parietal cells and reprogramming of chief cells into SPEM cells occurs in chronic atrophic gastritis, also known as pyloric or pseudopyloric metaplasia.
7
In the setting of atrophy and SPEM, cells with intestinal features can emerge. When that happens, it becomes GIM, which is most prominently by the presence of intestinal epithelial cells expressing MUC2 and CDX2.
8
At the GIM stage, gastric epithelial cells are replaced with specific intestinal cell populations, such as enterocytes, goblet cells, and enteroendocrine cells, which exhibit high expression of signature genes, such as MUC2.
9
It is widely recognized that GIM is a highly typical precancerous lesion, associated with an increased risk of gastric cancer.
10
Therefore, the molecular mechanism governing
H pylori
infection-related gastric metaplasia remains largely unexplored.
Ferroptosis is a newly discovered type of iron-dependent programmed cell death that is usually accompanied by the accumulation of lipid peroxides and ROS. Fe3
+
is imported by transferrin receptor 1 (TFR1) and reduced to Fe
2+
, which is stored in the cytoplasmic iron pool.
11
,
12
Excessive iron is spared in the iron storage complex ferritin, subsequently leading to the overproduction of ROS and triggering ferroptosis.
13
Ferroptosis involves multiple metabolic processes, such as lipid metabolism and glutathione (GSH) metabolism. The phospholipid peroxidation inhibitor glutathione peroxide 4 (GPX4), as a central molecule in ferroptosis, is crucial for bridging the connections among ferroptosis and lipid and GSH metabolism. The inhibition of GSH synthesis inactivates GPX4, which in turn results in lipid peroxide accumulation and induces ferroptosis.
14
,
15
It has been reported that various fatty acids promote lipid ROS generation and ferroptosis.
16
Previous studies have shown that
H pylori
infection increases the production of ROS and reactive nitrogen species.
6
,
17
However, the role of ferroptosis in
H pylori
infection-driven gastric pathogenesis and the underlying metabolic regulatory mechanism remain unknown.
The stress-response protein DNA damage-inducible transcript 4 (DDIT4), also known as DNA damage-response 1 (REDD1)
,
typically inhibits the mTOR signaling pathway by modulating TSC1/2 complexes. DDIT4 is activated in response to various cellular stressors, such as hypoxia and DNA damage.
18
Emerging evidence shows that DDIT4, a negative regulator of the mTOR signaling pathway, is involved in various cellular and metabolic processes. It has been reported that DDIT4 plays a crucial role in the noncanonical NF-κB signaling pathway and is linked to glucose metabolism and lipid formation.
18
A recent study indicated the significant role of DDIT4 inactivation in SPEM, a typical gastric mucosal lesion that promotes the proliferation of damaged cells.
19
Infection with
H pylori
causes gastric epithelial cell DNA damage responses and inflammation. However, the role of the stress-induced gene DDIT4 in the pathogenesis of
H pylori
infection has not been determined.
The present study provides the first data on the previously unknown role of DDIT4 in regulating metabolic ferroptosis in
H pylori
-associated gastric metaplasia. We showed that long-term
H pylori
infection led to inactivation of DDIT4. Deletion of
Ddit4
in mice significantly aggravated
H pylori
pathogenesis and resulted in severe gastric metaplasia lesions. DDIT4 deficiency caused the accumulation of lipid peroxidases and ROS, leading to ferroptosis. Compared with non-atrophic gastritis tissues, DDIT4 expression was significantly lower, and ferroptosis was increased in human GIM tissues. Our findings indicated that DDIT4 serves as a protective factor against
H pylori
-related gastric metaplasia and provides new insights into the mechanisms of ferroptosis and metabolic reprogramming in the progression of metaplasia.

H pylori
Infection Dynamically Regulates DDIT4 Expression in Gastric Epithelial Cells
To identify the DEGs related to
H pylori
infection, RNA sequencing was performed on 2 gastric epithelial cell lines, GES-1 and AGS, which were co-cultured with the
H pylori
strain 43504 for 6 hours (
Figure 1
A
). The genes with significantly upregulated expression were identified on the basis of a fold change ≥4 and a
P
-value less than .05. A Venn diagram revealed 36 significantly upregulated genes whose expression was significantly upregulated in both infected groups (
Figure 1
B
;
Table 1
). In particular, the stress response gene DDIT4, a negative regulator of mTOR activity through the TSC1/2 complex, was significantly increased following
H pylori
infection (
Figure 1
C
). To investigate the temporal effect of
H pylori
on DDIT4 expression, the additional
H pylori
strains PMSS1 and 7.13 were employed for co-culture with gastric epithelial cells at the indicated time points. Intriguingly,
H pylori
infection progressively upregulated the transcription of
DDIT4
from 3 to 6 hours, whereas the
DDIT4
mRNA levels gradually decreased from 6 to 12 to 24 hours after
H pylori
infection (
Figure 1
D

H pylori
infection (
Figure 1
E

H pylori
-infected epithelial cells at 6 hours, followed by a substantial suppression at 24 hours of treatment, even dropping below baseline levels (
Figure 1
F and G
).

To confirm our findings
in vivo
, we infected INS-GAS transgenic mice with the
H pylori

20
,

Ddit4 Deletion Results in Gastric Metaplasia in Animal Models of
H pylori
Pathogenesis
To determine the role of DDIT4 in
H pylori
pathogenesis,
Ddit4
deficient mice were infected with PMSS1 for 4 months. First, we confirmed that
DDIT4
mRNA levels were decreased in the gastric tissues of
Ddit4
-/-
mice infected with or without
H pylori
infection (
Figure 2
A
). Western blot analysis indicated that DDIT4 deficiency led to increased mTOR activity (
Figure 3
A
;
Figure 2
B
). Silver staining confirmed the colonization of
H pylori
in infected mice (
Figure 2
C
). There was no difference in the colonization level of gastric
H pylori
between
Ddit4
+/+
and
Ddit4
-/-
mice infected with
H pylori
infection (
Figure 2
D
). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of gastric tissues showed that
H pylori
infection induces gastric mucosal inflammation and mucus metaplasia (
Figure 3
B
); in mice with
Ddit4
deficiency, both the gastric antrum and corpus developed more severe gastritis and pronounced gastric metaplastic lesions (
Figure 3
B
). The pathologic scores of gastric tissues in these
Ddit4
knockout mice were also significantly higher than these wild-type (WT) mice (
Figure 3
C
). Moreover, Alcian blue (AB)/periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining revealed a substantial number of blue-stained goblet cells in the mouse gastric tissues of
Ddit4
-deficient mice (
Figure 3
D
), suggesting that
Ddit4
deficiency exacerbates the development of gastric metaplasia lesions. Immunofluorescence staining revealed that
Ddit4
deficiency in infected mice enhanced the expression of Ki67 in gastric tissues (
Figure 3
E
). Loss of acid-secreting parietal cells is a critical precursor in gastric metaplasia. We then examined the expression levels of ATP4A and ATP4B, the classical markers of parietal cells.
Ddit4
-/-
mice had significantly lower ATP4A and ATP4B expression in gastric tissues than did WT mice (
Figure 3
F
;
Figure 2
E
). These results were further confirmed through Western blot analysis (
Figure 3
G
). Moreover, infection of
Ddit4
-knockout mice led to a substantial increase of SPEM cells co-expressing GIF and GSII cells at the base of the gland (
Figure 3
H
). CD44V9, another specific SPEM marker, was also upregulated in the gastric tissues of infected
Ddit4
-/-
mice (
Figure 3
I
). We subsequently examined whether infected
Ddit4
-knockout mice manifest the gastric intestinal metaplasia phenotype, as indicated by the goblet cell marker MUC2 and enterocytes marker FABP1. Notably, only a minimal number of intestinal epithelial cells were observed in
Ddit4
-/-
mice (
Figure 2
F
). Taken together, these data indicated that
Ddit4
deficiency drives the development of gastric metaplasia in the stomach of
H pylori
-infected mice.

Ddit4 Deletion Results in Aberrant GSH Metabolism and Lipid Peroxide Accumulation
To elucidate the role of DDIT4 in the progression of
H pylori
infection-related gastric metaplasia, proteomic profiling was performed on the gastric tissues of infected
Ddit4
+/+
and
Ddit4
-/-
mice. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed distinct protein profiles between
Ddit4
+/+
and
Ddit4
-/-
mice (
Figure 4
A
). The complete list of differentially expressed proteins, including the official gene names, fold change values, and
P
-values is provided in
Supplementary Table 1
. The Gene Ontology (GO)-term set enrichment analysis indicated that, compared with those in the gastric tissues of WT mice, the proteins in the gastric tissues of
Ddit4
-/-
mice were significantly downregulated and were primarily enriched in the metabolic biologic processes (
Figure 4
B
). Similarly, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis also revealed that the downregulated proteins in
Ddit4
-deficient mice were enriched in multiple metabolism-related signaling pathways (
Figure 4
C
). To determine the association between the expression of DDIT4 and signaling pathway components in the proteomic profile results, we then performed a gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA). Significant suppression of multiple metabolic pathways, such as glycerolipid metabolism, cysteine and methionine metabolism, and oxidative phosphorylation was identified in
Ddit4
-/-
mice (
Figure 5
A
). Of those, we detected that the GSH metabolism and peroxisome pathways were significantly inhibited, and these pathways were the most highly downregulated pathways following
Ddit4
deletion (
Figure 5
B
). GSH, an essential non-enzymatic antioxidant, can maintain the cellular redox balance and protect cells from oxidative stress via the GSH/ glutathione disulfide (GSSG) cycle, which reduces intracellular ROS levels.
22
Glutathione S-transferases (GSTs) are a family of enzymes that catalyze the conjugation of GSH to various substrates, thereby facilitating their detoxification.
23
As shown in
Figure 5
C
, the expression of GST family members, such as GSTA1, GSTA2, GSTA3, and GSTA4 was significantly lower in the gastric tissues of
Ddit4
-/-
infected mice than in those of WT mice. Moreover, the GSH/GSSG ratio was reduced in the gastric tissues of DDIT4-deficient mice (
Figure 5
D
). Given that various metabolic processes were inhibited in
Ddit4
-/-
mice, we thereby examined the levels of adenosine triphosphate (ATP). As expected, ATP levels were reduced in
Ddit4
-knockout stomach tissues (
Figure 5
E
). We also showed that
Ddit4
deletion caused a significant accumulation of ROS in the gastric tissues (
Figure 5
F
). Notably, robust lipid peroxidation accumulation was detected in mice lacking
Ddit4
, as measured by the radiometric lipid peroxidation sensor BODIPY 581/591 C11 (
Figure 5
G
). Then, we further investigated the effect of DDIT4 on GSH metabolism and lipid peroxide levels in cells. Consistent with the findings from the animal experiments, DDIT4 knockdown via small interfering RNA (siRNA) transfection in AGS gastric epithelial cells. AGS led to a significant decrease in GSH/GSSG ratio (
Figure 5
H
;
Figure 4
D
) and ATP levels (
Figure 5
I
). In conclusion, these data demonstrated that DDIT4 is essential for the maintenance of cellular metabolic homeostasis. Loss of DDIT4 reprograms GSH metabolism, resulting in increased ROS levels and lipid peroxidation.

DDIT4 Deficiency Triggers Ferroptosis in
H
pylori
-infected Gastric Tissues
A decrease in intracellular GSH and peroxidase activity causes a low antioxidant capacity and ROS accumulation, which exacerbates the occurrence of lipid peroxidation and ultimately induces ferroptosis.
24
Given that ROS and lipid peroxidation accumulated in
Ddit4
-deficient mice, we further examined the relationship between DDIT4 and ferroptosis. GSEA analysis showed that the SDE gene expression in the gastric tissues of
Ddit4
-deficient mice was positively correlated with ferroptosis (
Figure 6
A
). The depletion of GSH or the inactivation of GPX4 can lead to metabolic imbalance, thus inducing ferroptosis. We found that
Ddit4
-/-
mice expressed lower mRNA levels of
GPX4
compared with
Ddit4
+/+
mice (
Figure 6
B
). Furthermore, there was a significant positive correlation between
GPX4
and
DDIT4
expression (Pearson correlation, r = 0.7075;
P
= .0101) (
Figure 6
C
). A heatmap was constructed to show the differentially expressed proteins involved in the ferroptosis pathway in gastric tissues between infected
Ddit4
+/+
and
Ddit4
-/-
mice. In detail, the pro-ferroptotic proteins such as arachidonic acid 15-lipoxygenase-1 (ALOX15), which possesses catalytic activity in peroxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids to generate lipid and ROS,
25
as well as the heme oxygenase (HMOX1) protein that catalyzes the degradation of heme to produce ferrous ion, were significantly upregulated in
Ddit4
-depleted mice. Moreover, the expression of GPX4 as the well-known marker against ferroptosis was downregulated after DDIT4 knockout (
Figure 6
D
). In addition, the greater expression of ALOX15 and HMOX1, as well as the lower expression of GPX4 in the gastric tissues of infected
Ddit4
-/-
mice than in those of infected WT mice, were validated via Western blot analysis (
Figure 6
E
;
Figure 4
E
). The inhibition of GPX4 in mouse gastric tissues were further confirmed by immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence staining (
Figure 6
F–G
). Because ferroptosis is closely linked to the accumulation of ferrous ions and mitochondrial dysfunction, we further measured Fe
2+
levels and the mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP). The results showed that intracellular Fe
2+
levels were enhanced significantly in the gastric tissues of infected
Ddit4
-/-
mice, compared with infected WT mice (
Figure 6
H
). Moreover, JC-1 staining revealed that the gastric tissues of infected
Ddit4
-deficient mice exhibited reduced MMP and green fluorescence, compared with those of infected WT mice, as indicated by red fluorescence due to the high MMP (
Figure 6
I
). Taken together, these data suggested that knockout of DDIT4 induces ferroptosis in gastric tissues, which may be responsible for the loss of parietal cells.

Discussion
Gastric metaplasia has been widely recognized as the major precursor of gastric malignancies.
7
In this study, we explored the role of DDIT4 in the progression of
H pylori
-induced gastric diseases and further elucidated a novel mechanism by which the loss of DDIT4 causes metabolic dysregulation to drive gastric metaplasia. We showed that DDIT4 may function as a protective guardian by metabolic reprogramming in the gastric microenvironment in response to
H pylori
infection. Loss of DDIT4 could suppress multiple metabolic pathways, and enhanced ROS and lipid peroxide levels, subsequently leading to the induction of ferroptosis in response to
H pylori
infection (
Figure 14
F
). Our findings provide novel insights into the significant protective role of DDIT4 in
H pylori
-induced gastric metaplasia, and present detailed evidence that host energy metabolism and ferroptosis are regulated by the inactivation of DDIT4, which contributes to
H pylori
-induced disease.
Our data indicate that short-term stimulation of
H pylori
infection caused a transient increase in DDIT4 to maintain homeostasis, whereas sustained
H pylori
infection led to a significant reduction in DDIT4. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting that rapid increases in DDIT4 in response to various physiologic and pathologic stimuli can restore metabolic to promote adaption and reduce damage by controlling mTORC1 activity.
18
H pylori
infection has been indicated to upregulate the expression of DDIT4 to promote gastritis.
29
The reported study elucidated the role of DDIT4 activation in gastritis induced by early
H pylori
infection. Additionally, we found that prolonged
H pylori
infection leads to the loss of DDIT4. Using Ddit4
-/-
mice, we further show that DDIT4 deficiency markedly promoted
H pylori
infection-related phenotypic changes, including inflammation and metaplastic lesions. These data suggest that DDIT4 plays a protective role following long-term
H pylori
infection-induced chronic gastric injury and the development of gastric metaplasia. The synergistic effect of
Ddit4
deficient and
H pylori
infection exacerbates gastric lesions, potentially through a mechanism whereby the induction of SPEM allows for
H pylori
colonization in deeper layers of the gastric unit.
30
,
31
,
32
Our findings are also supported by a recent study in which the loss of DDIT4 led to inappropriate activation of mTORC1 and promoted cell cycle re-entry of chief cells harboring DNA damage, ultimately contributing to gastric metaplasia.
19
,
33
Our study primarily clarifies that DDIT4 acts as a protective factor during the progression of gastric diseases following long-term
H pylori
infection. Indeed, there is additional evidence supporting DDIT4 as a potential tumor suppressor, due to the conclusion that DDIT4 deficiency can cooperate with RAS to drive tumorigenesis.
34
Paradoxically, however, several studies suggest that DDIT4 plays a detrimental role and is responsible for cancer cell survival and drug resistance.
35
,
36
Therefore, DDIT4 has been considered as a metabolic double agent, and its specific role depends on different physiologic and pathologic contexts.
Metabolic reprogramming is considered as a significant hallmark of tumorigenesis.
37
A few studies have explored the impact of
H pylori
infection on host cellular metabolism. The knockout of cystathionine γ-lyase (CTH), a key enzyme in mammalian reverse transsulfuration metabolism, has been found to alleviate acute and chronic
H pylori
-induced gastritis by regulating macrophage remodeling.
38
Spermine oxidase (SMOX), which catalyzes the conversion of spermine to spermidine and H
2
O
2
, is reported to promote
H pylori
-induced gastric carcinogenesis. The reason for this difference can be attributed to the induction of SMOX by
H pylori
infection, which promotes DNA damage and β-catenin activation.
39
Ferroptosis is described as a novel form of pathologic cell death that occurs as a result of ROS accumulation and lipid peroxidation.
14
,
24
,
40
Peng and colleagues reported that CagA, one of the most important virulence factors of
H pylori
, confers gastric cancer cells sensitivity to ferroptosis through regulating lipid peroxidation.
41
A recent study has also revealed that increased ferroptosis is involved in the development of gastric metaplasia.
42
In line with these findings, our work showed that ferroptosis is a critical metabolic feature in the progression from
H pylori
-induced gastric inflammation to metaplasia. Thus, these findings suggest the potential roles of peroxidation and ferroptosis as new directions for the development of drugs targeting
H pylori
-induced gastric metaplasia.
To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to demonstrate that loss of DDIT4 promotes gastric mucosal lesions caused by
H pylori
through induction of ferroptosis, as evidenced by upregulation of ALOX15 and HMOX1, thereby ROS and lipid peroxides accumulation. Although there is no direct evidence for ALOX15 and HMOX1 increasing ROS production, they have been reported to cause ROS accumulation and induce ferroptosis in cells.
43
,
44
It has been reported that mTOR inhibition suppresses ferroptosis through limiting oxidative stress.
45
Dysregulation of mTOR pathway caused persistent oxidative stress and excess lipid peroxidation, leading to the accumulation of irreversible DNA damage.
46
The resulting genetic alterations serve as driving forces for the progression from chronic inflammation to malignancies.
Our data revealed a high abundance of ROS in immune cells within the gastric tissues, raising the question of why DDIT4 loss in gastric epithelial cells would induce ROS accumulation in immune cells. The reason for this finding may be attributed to the regulation of HIF signaling pathway and energy metabolism reprogramming. It has been found that DDIT4 deficiency leads to a marked increase in lipid uptake and storage to meet the metabolic demands of the RAS mutant context to promote pancreatic tumorigenesis.
34
Moreover, DDIT4 and hypoxia-inducible factor HIF-1 exhibit negative feedback regulation. On the one hand, DDIT4 is an important downstream effector of HIF-1; on the other hand, genetic loss of DDIT4 has been shown to induce HIF-1 stabilization and glycolytic metabolism reprogramming through elevation of ROS, thereby contributing to tumorigenesis.
47
Moreover, the findings suggest that ROS and lipid peroxides accumulation in the gastric environment induce ferroptosis, causing the loss of parietal cells, and thereby advancing the development of gastric metaplasia. Recent studies indicate that myeloid cells-derived ROS increases H
2
O
2
secretion in the gastric microenvironment, thereby inducing epithelial mutagenesis.
48
Additionally, oxidative stress triggered the p38
MAPK
–p16
INK4a
/p19
ARF
signaling pathway selectively in parietal cells and the consequent induction of parietal cell loss.
49
This evidence may explain why ROS accumulation causes extensive alterations in gastric epithelial cells. Taken together, these findings indicate that DDIT4 may protect cells from lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis in
H pylori
-induced gastric metaplasia.
This study carries a limitation with regards to experiments involving human specimens. Gastric tissues sourced from human antrum region were utilized to link DDIT4 to intestinal metaplasia, a strategy that diverges from animal studies, which predominantly focus on lesions in the gastric corpus. Given the standard procedure of endoscopists who routinely procure gastric tissues from the antrum region for pathologic examination in cases of
H pylori
infection, it becomes particularly challenging to obtain tissues from the gastric corpus. This study suggests that persistent
H pylori
infection could potentially interact synergistically with genetic factors to drive lesion transitions between the antrum and corpus regions through the modulation of the gastric microenvironment.
In summary, the present study showed that DDIT4 expression dynamically changes in response to early and long-term
H pylori
infection. DDIT4 deficiency acts as a metabolic switch that drives gastric metaplasia through the elevation of ROS levels, lipid peroxidation, and ferroptosis. These findings extend our understanding of the mechanism of metabolic reprogramming during the development of
H pylori
-associated gastric metaplasia.
Materials and Methods
Patients and Tissue Samples
Human gastric tissue samples (20 cases of non-atrophic gastritis and 20 cases of GIM) were collected from patients who underwent gastroscopy and biopsy in the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University; all specimens were obtained from the gastric antrum of the patients. The diagnoses of the patients were based on histology. All subjects provided informed consent for obtaining the specimens. This study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University. All included cases were recorded in the Human Genetic Resources Center of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University.
H pylori
Strains
The WT CagA
+
H pylori
strains (ATCC43504, 7.13 and the rodent-adapted PMSS1) were used in this study.
H pylori
7.13 strain was kindly obtained from Prof Richard Peek, Jr (Vanderbilt University Medical Center).
H pylori
PMSS1 and its CagA-mutant were successfully constructed in our previous study.
50
Briefly,

were cultured on Brucella agar with 5% sheep blood (BD Bioscience) under microaerophilic conditions for in vitro passages as previously described.
50
Before
in vitro

H pylori
7.13 and PMSS1 strains were co-cultured with gastric epithelial cells at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 100:1.
Gastric Organoid Culture
Human gastric tissue was obtained from donor that underwent partial gastrectomy at the First Hospital of Nanchang University. This study was approved by the ethical committee of the First Hospital of Nanchang University ([2023] CDYFYYLK [01-009]), and informed consent was obtained from the donor.
The detailed experimental protocol for the gastric organoid construction and culture was carried out according to the manufacturer’s instructions (abs9736, Absin). Briefly, glands were extracted from 1 cm
2
of human tissue using EDTA in cold chelation buffer, digested in digestive solution at 37 °C for 30 minutes, cells were washed twice in Advanced Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) F12, seeded in Matrigel (M315066, BioGenous), and overlaid with culture medium. Propagation culture medium contained: Advance DMEM/F12 (12634028, Thermo Fischer Scientific) supplemented with penicillin/streptomycin, 10 mmol/L HEPES, Glutamax, 1 × B27, N-Acetylcysteine 1 mM (BioGenous). To the basal medium, growth factors were added. Final human stomach culture medium contained essential components EGF 50 ng/mL, Noggin conditioned medium 10%, R-spondin1 conditioned medium 10%, Wnt conditioned medium 50%, FGF10 200 ng/mL, Gastrin 1 nM, TGFβi 2 μM. Facultative component is Nicotinamide 10 mM. After seeding RHOKi, 10 μM was added. Additional tested components were: IGF 100 ng/mL, p38 inhibitor 10 μM, GSK3β inhibitor 3 μM, PGE2 500 nM (BioGenous).

Tamoxifen-treated Mouse Models
Male C57BL/6 mice aged 6- to 8-weeks were purchased from GemPharmatech and housed in a designated pathogen-free barrier facility with a 12-hour photoperiod. To generate the experimental model of gastric metaplasia, tamoxifen (Med Chem Express, HY-13757A) was dissolved in 10% ethanol and 90% sunflower oil, and mice were injected intraperitoneally (5 mg/20 g body weight).
27

Transfection of DNA plasmids and siRNA
All transfections were carried out in Opti-MEM (Gibco) using Lipofectamine 3000 following the manufacturer’s instruction (L3000015, Thermo Fisher). The Flag-DDIT4 Plasmid (GeneChem) and siRNAs (RIBOBIO) were performed in 6-well plates. The cells were collected for 48 hours for plasmids or 72 hours for siRNAs after transfection.
Evaluation of
H pylori
Colonization in Animal Models
Colonization levels of
H pylori
in mouse gastric tissues were evaluated by silver staining and colony-forming assay. The silver staining was performed using the Spirochete Silver Staining Kit (Warthin-Starry Method) (Solarbio-G1940) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, the paraffin-embedded tissues were deparaffinized in xylene and gradient ethanol. The Acid Sliver Solution was used to stain the slides in the water bath at 56 °C for 1 hour. Then the slides were re-dyed with Warthin-Starry Stain Solution in water bath at 56 °C until they turned into yellowish brown. All images were acquired under a Nikon Eclipse microscope.
For the colony-forming assay, the murine stomachs were opened along the inner curvature and divided into 4 parts. A quarter of the stomach was homogenized in brain heart infusion (BHI). Ten-fold serial dilutions were prepared in BHI broth, and aliquots of the dilutions were spread over Brucella agar plates. After 3 days’ culture, colonies were counted, and the number of colony-forming units was calculated per stomach. Colonies were confirmed to be
H pylori
by Gram staining.
RNA Sequencing

After determination of the RNA purity and quantification, the transcriptome sequencing and analysis were conducted by OE Biotech Co, Ltd. The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the DESeq R package functions estimateSizeFactors and nBinomTest. Fold change >2 or fold change <0.5 and

Immunofluorescence
Cells were washed with ice-cold PBS, fixed with 4% formaldehyde in PBS for 30 minutes, and then permeabilized with 0.5% Triton-100 at room temperature for 10 minutes. After blocking with 3% BSA in PBS for 1 hour, the cells were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with the corresponding secondary antibody conjugated with Alexa Fluor Plus 488 or Alexa Fluor Plus 555 (1:500 dilution, Thermo Fisher) in the dark for 1 hour. Cell nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). All slides were imaged using a fluorescence microscope (Leica Stellaris 5).
Cell Metabolism Measurement (ATP, GSH/GSSG)
ATP concentrations in mouse gastric tissue or gastric epithelial cells were determined using ATP assay kits (A095-1-1, Jiancheng) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, cell or tissue samples were homogenized and lysed in the ATP buffer, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 g for 5 minutes at 4 °C. Subsequently, they were loaded into a 96-well plate and mixed with the ATP detection working solution in recommended ratios. The plate was subsequently incubated in the dark for 10 minutes, and the optical density (OD) was measured at 636 nm using a Varioskan Flash system (Thermo Fisher). A standard curve was generated, and the total protein concentration of each sample was quantified using the Bradford protein assay. The ATP concentration was expressed as nmol/mg protein. Statistical analysis between groups was conducted using
t
-test, considering a
P
-value of less than .05 as statistically significant.
For measurement of GSSG and GSH
,
cells were collected in centrifuge tubes, immediately mixed with 3 volumes of ice-cold 5% metaphosphoric acid, frozen and thawed twice in liquid nitrogen and 37 °C water. The samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm and 4 °C for 10 minutes, and the supernatants were then harvested for GSH and GSSG determination. Total GSH, reduced GSH, and GSSG were measured using GSH and GSSG Assay Kit (S0053, Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The GSH/GSSG ratio were calculated using the following formula: GSH/GSSG = [Total GSH − (2 × GSSG)]/GSSG.
Intracellular ROS Level
The ROS levels of frozen tissues and cell samples were measured using the fluorescent probe DCFH-DA (C6827, Thermo Fisher). In brief, the cells or tissue sections were washed with PBS and incubated with ROS fluorescent dye DCFH-DA at the final concentration of 10 μM at 37 °C for 30 minutes. Nuclei staining was performed using Hoechst 33342 (C0031, Solarbio), and the fluorescence intensity was evaluated by fluorescence microscopy (Leica Microsystems) and quantified by ImageJ 1.53t software.
Assessment of Lipid Peroxidation
The BODIPY 581/591 C11 (D3861, ThermoFisher) sensor was used for lipid peroxidation that shifts its fluorescence emission from red to green upon oxidation. The tissue sections or cells were washed with PBS and incubated with 2.5 μM C11-BODIPY (581/591) at 37 °C in the dark for 30 minutes. Images at red and green light channels were taken using a fluorescence microscope, followed by the calculation of red/green fluorescence ratio using Image J software. On the other hand, the fatty acids in tissues or cells are stained with the fluorescent dye BODIPY 505/515. The frozen sections or cells were washed with PBS and incubated with 2 μM BODIPY505/515 at room temperature in the dark for 10 minutes. After a wash with PBS, the samples were subjected to a fluorescence microscope for the detection of green fluorescence signal.
Determination of Intracellular Fe
2+
Levels (FerroOrange)
FerroOrange (F374, DojinDo) was used to detect the intracellular Fe
2+

Assessment of Mitochondrial Membrane Potential (Δψm)
According to the manufacturer's protocol, mitochondrial membrane potential in cells or frozen sections was measured using JC-1 (M8650, Solarbio), a cationic, lipophilic fluorine probe. In healthy mitochondria (high membrane potential), JC-1 enters mitochondria and forms red fluorescent aggregates (JC-1 aggregates). When the membrane potential decreases (eg, upon mitochondria depolarization), JC-1 becomes a green fluorescent monomer (JC-1 monomer). Therefore, the higher the ratio of red fluorescence to green fluorescence, the higher the mitochondrial membrane polarization. The cells or frozen sections were washed with PBS and incubated with JC-1 at a final concentration of 1.0 μM at 37 °C for 15 minutes. The fluorescence was observed using a confocal microscope, and the red fluorescence/green fluorescence ratio was calculated using ImageJ.
