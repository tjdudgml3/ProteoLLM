1
Introduction
Periodontitis is one of the most common oral conditions worldwide (Chapple et al.
2018
), with an estimated prevalence of 10% in severe stages (Bernabe et al.
2020
). This disease impairs general and oral‐health‐related quality of life (Wong, Yap, and Allen
2021
). Moreover, it impacts on the systemic condition bidirectionally, being relevant to its relationship with cardiovascular diseases (Larvin et al.
2021
) and diabetes mellitus (Larvin et al.
2023
).
Nowadays, the gold standard diagnosis method for periodontitis is based on clinical parameters. However, the Classification of Periodontal Diseases and Conditions recognized the potential usefulness of biomarkers for accurate and prompt diagnosis of periodontitis (Tonetti, Greenwell, and Kornman
2018
).
In this regard, saliva is a medium composed of molecules that could be potential biomarkers. This is related to the pathogenesis of periodontitis based on dysbiosis of the oral microbiota and the host immune response. Overactivation of the immune system results in an imbalance of proteins, which can be reflected in the saliva composition (Buzalaf et al.
2020
). Accordingly, the diagnostic accuracy of several salivary proteins for detecting periodontitis has been analysed (Arias‐Bujanda, Regueira‐Iglesias, Balsa‐Castro, et al.
2020
; Blanco‐Pintos et al.
2023
).
However, there are probably other relevant molecules that have not yet been identified or whose relationship with the disease is unclear (Bostanci and Belibasakis
2018
). High‐throughput proteomic techniques allow an exhaustive study of the periodontal proteome, which is essential for the identification of new biomarkers (Gupta, Govila, and Saini
2015
).
The SWATH‐MS (sequential window acquisition of all theoretical mass spectra) technique has emerged as a valuable approach to discovering new protein biomarkers (Anjo, Santa, and Manadas
2017
). SWATH‐MS is a label‐free approach that is more affordable, has easier sample preparation and can support larger cohorts than labelling techniques (Anjo, Santa, and Manadas
2017
). Additionally, it combines a data‐dependent acquisition (DDA) strategy to identify the proteins with an untargeted data‐independent acquisition (DIA) quantification (Ludwig et al.
2018
).
DDA has a high proteome coverage potential but presents poor sensitivity for detecting proteins at low concentrations. Conversely, the untargeted and targeted DIA methods allow a more sensitive and accurate quantification of proteins (Chantada‐Vázquez et al.
2021
). Untargeted DIA is the optimal technique for discovering new biomarkers, while targeted DIA enables more precise, sensitive and specific quantifications (Smith
2012
). In the SWATH‐MS method, DDA is used to generate a good‐quality spectral library, as it is a critical factor in the correct quantification process, which is performed using an untargeted DIA strategy. Consequently, SWATH‐MS is a technology that combines deep proteome coverage capabilities with consistency comparable to DDA and quantitative accuracy comparable to targeted DIA (Ludwig et al.
2018
).
The salivary proteome has demonstrated different proteomic profiles depending on the periodontal status (Bostanci et al.
2018
; Hartenbach et al.
2020
; Shin et al.
2019
). However, this does not determine its ability to detect periodontitis, as it is mandatory to perform specific predictive analysis (Gürsoy and Kantarci
2022
). Additionally, although previous studies have shown that age and smoking status influence the diagnostic accuracy of salivary biomarkers such as interleukin‐1β (IL‐1β) and active matrix metalloproteinase‐8 (aMMP8) (Arias‐Bujanda, Regueira‐Iglesias, Blanco‐Pintos, et al.
2020
; Deng et al.
2021
), their impact on proteomic data has not yet been determined. Furthermore, to date, the SWATH‐MS technique has not been used to investigate the salivary proteome associated with periodontitis.
Thus, this cross‐sectional study had two objectives: (1) to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of salivary proteins quantified by SWATH‐MS to distinguish periodontitis from periodontal health, and (2) to evaluate the impact of the clinical variables age and smoking status on the predictive capacity of the salivary biomarkers identified.

2.1
Selection of Study Groups
Forty‐five periodontally healthy subjects (control group) and 45 with untreated periodontitis (periodontitis group) were recruited from 250 consecutive patients between 2018 and 2021. Five of the 90 subjects were excluded for non‐compliance with the project phases. Therefore, the clinical groups finally consisted of 44 periodontally healthy subjects and 41 periodontitis patients. The inclusion criteria are specified in Appendix
S1
.
Two experienced dentists made the periodontal diagnostics. Following the Classification of Periodontal Diseases and Conditions (Caton et al.
2018
; Tonetti, Greenwell, and Kornman
2018
), the control group included periodontally healthy subjects, whereas the periodontitis group comprised patients with stage III‐IV and grade B‐C generalized periodontitis.
‘Smoking habit’ was evaluated using a questionnaire, collecting information on its extent (non‐smoker, former smoker or current smoker), time as a former or current smoker and number of cigarettes consumed per day.
The research was conducted following the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (revised in 2000) on human experimentation studies (World Medical Association
2013
). The protocol was approved by the Galician Clinical Research Ethics Committee (registration number 2021/417) and the Instituto Superior de Ciências da Saúde‐Norte, CESPU (registration number 35/CEIUCS/2019). All the participants provided written informed consent to their involvement in the study.
2.2
Collection of Saliva Samples and Preparation for Analysis
Saliva samples were collected 1 or 2 weeks after the initial examination. Patients collected 1 mL of unstimulated whole saliva by the spitting method at least 1 h after the last meal and after the last brushing, and the saliva samples were stored at −80°C.
The protein concentrations were quantified using the reducing‐agent and detergent‐compatible (RC DC) Protein Assay Kit (BIO‐RAD). Fifty micrograms of proteins from each sample were precipitated using the methanol/chloroform (MeOH/CHCl
3
) method.
Purified salivary proteins were loaded on a 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate‐polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE) to concentrate them into a band for in‐gel digestion. We then proceeded to manual in‐gel tryptic digestion.
The gel pieces were rinsed with 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate in 50% MeOH (HPLC grade), dehydrated with acetonitrile (HPLC grade) and dried in a SpeedVac. Modified porcine trypsin (Promega) was added to the dried gel pieces. The resulting peptides were extracted three times, pooled, concentrated in a SpeedVac and stored at −20°C until use.
2.3
Protein Quantification by
SWATH
‐
MS
2.3.1
Construction of Spectral Libraries
Pools were prepared from each clinical group using equal amounts of individual digested samples to construct the spectral library necessary for the SWATH‐MS analysis. The pool of digested peptides was separated using reversed‐phase liquid chromatography (RP‐LC) and 40‐min gradients.
A TripleTOF 6600 high‐speed mass spectrometer (SCIEX) was employed for data acquisition using a DDA workflow. The proteins were identified using the ProteinPilot 5.0.1. software (SCIEX) against the human‐specific UniProt database (UniProt Consortium
2014
) and considering a false discovery rate (FDR) of ≤1% (Aggarwal and Yadav
2016
).
2.3.2
Relative Quantification
Subsequently, the samples were analysed individually using a DIA method. They were examined as described above but using the SWATH‐MS strategy. This consisted of 100 TOF MS/MS scans using sequential, overlapping precursor isolation windows of varying widths.
Data extraction was performed with the PeakView 2.2 software (SCIEX) using the previously created spectral library. Only those integrated peak area values based on signal intensity from 10 peptides/protein and seven fragments/peptide with a 30 ppm error were selected. These areas (processed PeakView mrkvw files) were exported to the MarkerView software for their relative quantification. To homogenize the data obtained, a most likely ratio (MLR) normalization was performed (Colangelo et al.
2015
).
The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium (Deutsch et al.
2023
) via the Proteomics Identifications Database (PRIDE) (Perez‐Riverol et al.
2022
) partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD043491.

Statistical Analysis
The statistical analysis for comparing clinical characteristics between the groups and calculating the sample size is detailed in Appendix
S1
.
2.4.1
Diagnostic Predictive Modelling Analysis
Predictive modelling was performed considering periodontally healthy subjects as the ‘control condition’ and patients with periodontitis as the ‘target condition’. The models obtained were single‐protein unadjusted and adjusted for age and smoking habit.
Considering smoking, it has been demonstrated that smoking duration alone provides more robust risk estimates of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease than the composite index of pack‐years (Bhatt et al.
2018
). Concerning the ex‐smokers, the subjects who quit smoking ≤2 years ago have the same probability of having periodontitis as smokers. The risk of periodontitis after ≥11 years without smoking is the same as in non‐smokers (Tomar and Asma
2000
). Based on these considerations, the smoking variable was analysed in two ways, considering (1) years of smoking only and (2) years of smoking and cigarettes smoked (pack‐year index). In both analyses, a correction factor was introduced for former smokers so that the more years the subject had stopped smoking, the lower the impact of the smoking factor.
The analyses were performed using the software R (version 4.2.2) (R Core Team
2023
) following the recommendations of the TRIPOD guidelines (Moons et al.
2015
).
The data of quantified proteins (integrated peak area values after MLR normalization) were logarithmically transformed. Box plots were created to compare protein levels between two clinical conditions before the modelling phases. For this graphical representation, the packages ggplot2 (version 3.4.0) (Wickham
2016
) and beeswarm (version 0.4.0) (Eklund and Trimble
2021
) were used. Predictive models of the proteins were developed using the generalized additive model (GAM) package (version 1.22) (Hastie
2022
).
The classification threshold for the maximum accuracy (ACC) value was determined using the InformationValue package (version 1.2.3) (Prabhakaran
2016
). The models with extreme classification cutoff points in the response variable (< 0.2/> 0.8) were discarded. The classification measures and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated by the pROC package (version 1.18.0) (Robin et al.
2011
). This package also calculated the area under the curve (AUC) and their CIs. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed using the Epi package (version 2.47) (Carstensen et al.
2023
).
Models with ACC values ≥ 70% were internally validated by bootstrapping, which allowed us to estimate the overfitting of the models by calculating the bias‐corrected (bc) performance parameters. We developed a script in R using the packages GAM, InformationValue, pROC and Epi (Carstensen et al.
2023
; Hastie
2022
; Prabhakaran
2016
; Robin et al.
2011
).
Single protein models with AUC values ≥ 0.850 were selected for combination with another protein (bivariate evaluation). The second predictor variable was chosen using an absolute correlation < 0.3. Classification parameters, ROC curves and bootstrap validations were performed using the same procedures.
The selection of models was made according to performance values and biological consistency. Interpretation of the parameters was performed using previous guidelines (De Luca Canto et al.
2015
; Hosmer, Lemeshow, and Sturdivant
2013
).

3.3
Predictive Modelling of Salivary Proteins
3.3.1
Single Proteins: Unadjusted and Adjusted for Age and Smoking Habit
Eight proteins had a bc‐AUC of 0.848–0.914 and bc‐ACC of 78.8%–86.8% to distinguish periodontitis from periodontal health without adjustment. The bc‐sensitivity and bc‐specificity ranged from 62.5% to 86.9% and from 60.9% to 98.1%, respectively.
The diagnostic accuracy of these biomarkers increased when accounting for age, demonstrating an exceptional bc‐AUC of 0.975–0.992 (Hosmer, Lemeshow, and Sturdivant
2013
), with a bc‐ACC of 94.1%–98.2% (bc‐sensitivity/bc‐specificity of 90.2%–98.6%/93.6%–97.2%), representing an improvement of 9.1%–19.4%.
The age‐adjusted proteins ordered from highest to lowest bc‐ACC (bc‐sensitivity/bc‐specificity) were as follows: protein S100‐A8 with 98.2% (98.1%/97.2%), lysozyme C with 97.6% (98.6%/94.1%), β‐2‐microglobulin with 97.3% (95.8%/95.4%), keratin, type II cytoskeletal 1 with 96.2% (96.3%/96.1%), isoform 2 of tropomyosin α‐3 chain with 96.0% (96.0%/94.9%), neutrophil defensin 1 and ubiquitin‐60S ribosomal protein L40 both with 95.2% (90.2%/95.6% and 94.7%/95.2%, respectively) and resistin with 94.1% (90.7%/93.6%) (Figures

The remaining four proteins had higher bc‐ACC values after adjusting for years and cigarettes smoked (pack‐year index), which were ordered from highest to lowest bc‐ACC (bc‐sensitivity/bc‐specificity): β‐2‐microglobulin with 90.4% (84.0%/96.4%), neutrophil defensin 1 with 87.0% (80.2%/88.4%), resistin with 86.6% (83.8%/83.5%) and isoform 2 of tropomyosin α‐3 chain with 86.2% (73.9%/93.2%). 4
Discussion
Eight salivary biomarkers demonstrated a bc‐ACC of > 78% to diagnose periodontitis. According to the indicators for interpreting diagnostic accuracy (De Luca Canto et al.
2015
), the bc‐sensitivity and bc‐specificity of these proteins ranged from poor or fair values around 60% to good or excellent > 85%. Adjusting for smoking status, bc‐ACC increased to > 83% with more balanced bc‐sensitivity and bc‐specificity from around 75% to > 89%. The age variable further improved the diagnostic accuracy, showing a bc‐ACC > 94% and all proteins obtaining excellent bc‐sensitivity and bc‐specificity > 90% (De Luca Canto et al.
2015
). These age‐adjusted results were comparable to those of two‐protein combinations, obtaining five bivariate associations with bc‐ACC > 92% and bc‐sensitivity and bc‐specificity > 88%.
Therefore, clinical variables and dual‐protein combinations improve the accuracy of salivary biomarkers to diagnose periodontitis, with single age‐adjusted proteins being more clinically applicable than two‐protein associations.
4.1
Comparison With Previous Proteomic Studies in Saliva: Methodological Differences
The protein quantification criteria in our study (integrated peak areas of 10 peptides/protein and seven fragments/peptide) were stricter than in previous investigations (Bostanci et al.
2018
; Grant et al.
2022
; Shin et al.
2019
). Consequently, the number of salivary proteins quantified was smaller than in other studies (Bostanci et al.
2018
; Shin et al.
2019
), 377 of the 427 proteins that comprised the spectral library. To detect less prevalent proteins, we could have applied online libraries (Rosenberger et al.
2014
) or software for library‐free DIA analysis (Demichev et al.
2020
). However, diagnostic biomarkers should be abundant and prevalent proteins. This facilitates their validation and subsequent translation into clinical practice.
Moreover, the sample size is considerably larger than in other publications (Haigh et al.
2010
; Hartenbach et al.
2020
; Mertens et al.
2018
; Salazar et al.
2013
; Tang et al.
2019
; Trindade et al.
2015
; Wu et al.
2009
). Previous investigations have focused on different expression analyses of salivary proteins in periodontitis using proteomic techniques (Hartenbach et al.
2020
; Salazar et al.
2013
; Shin et al.
2019
; Wu et al.
2009
). However, such studies are insufficient to consider a protein as a ‘biomarker’, as statistical analyses of predictive modelling are mandatory to determine its diagnostic accuracy (Gürsoy and Kantarci
2022
).
To date, limited research has used proteomic techniques to assess the diagnostic accuracy of the proteins to differentiate periodontitis from periodontal health (Rizal et al.
2020
). Moreover, proteomic studies have not assessed the impact of age and smoking status on the predictive capacity of biomarkers.
The few studies that have determined the diagnostic accuracy of salivary proteins using proteomic data directly have included non‐smokers and without significant age differences (Bostanci et al.
2018
; Tang et al.
2019
). These investigations demonstrated that combining salivary proteins increases the AUC (Bostanci et al.
2018
; Tang et al.
2019
). Conversely, besides AUC values, we provided other classification parameters which were internally validated. According to the TRIPOD statement, diagnostic accuracy values from a single discrimination measure are insufficient to provide reliable estimates (Debray et al.
2023
; Moons et al.
2015
).
In this regard, we demonstrated that, although keratin, type II cytoskeletal 1 obtained the highest bc‐AUC (0.914) and resistin the lowest (0.848), the bc‐ACC of resistin was higher (83%
vs
85%). This is because while both proteins showed excellent capacity to diagnose periodontitis (bc‐sensitivity > 80%), the capability for detecting periodontal health was higher for resistin (bc‐specificity of 88%
v
s
73%) (De Luca Canto et al.
2015
). These data highlight the importance of providing other classification measures besides the AUC.
Using other analytical techniques, both age and smoking habit have previously been shown to increase the accuracy in detecting periodontitis (Deng et al.
2021
; Grant et al.
2022
). Indeed, our research group had demonstrated that smoking influences diagnostic thresholds and the predictive ability of cytokines to diagnose periodontitis (Arias‐Bujanda et al.
2018
; Arias‐Bujanda, Regueira‐Iglesias, Blanco‐Pintos, et al.
2020
). Moreover, in contrast to these investigations, in the present study the smoking variable was analysed quantitatively using two different forms: years of smoking and years and number of cigarettes smoked (pack‐year index). This statistical approach was chosen considering the observations made by previous authors (Bhatt et al.
2018
).
4.2
Diagnostic Accuracy of the Protein Biomarkers Identified
Eight unadjusted salivary proteins showed excellent or outstanding bc‐AUC of 0.848–0.914 with a bc‐ACC of 79%–87% for diagnosing periodontitis (Hosmer, Lemeshow, and Sturdivant
2013
). However, bc‐sensitivity and bc‐specificity were divergent between proteins, ranging from a poor or fair value of around 60% (neutrophil defensin 1: bc‐sensitivity of 63%; and lysozyme C: bc‐specificity of 61%) to an excellent bc‐sensitivity of 87% (protein S100‐A8) and bc‐specificity of 98% (isoform 2 of tropomyosin α‐3 chain) (De Luca Canto et al.
2015
).

4.3
Impact of Age and Smoking Habit on the Biomarkers' Predictive Capacity
In this study, we showed that age increased the values more than smoking (age‐adjusted bc‐ACC increased by 9%–19% and smoking‐adjusted bc‐ACC by 1%–8%, except for isoform 2 tropomyosin α‐3 chain in the latter adjustment). The age‐adjusted proteins that obtained the highest classification measures were keratin, type II cytoskeletal 1, protein S100‐A8 and β‐2‐microglobulin with values > 95% (bc‐sensitivity/bc‐specificity of 96%–98%/95%–97%). These predictive values are similar to or even higher than those demonstrated by the two‐protein combination, with β‐2‐microglobulin with profilin‐1 and lysozyme C with ZG16B obtaining results > 90% (bc‐sensitivity/bc‐specificity of 93%–96%/92%–97%).
Considering smoking status, the most different bc‐ACC values between adjusting for years of smoking and pack‐year index were around 3% for lysozyme C (> bc‐ACC in years of smoking) and neutrophil defensin 1 (> bc‐ACC in pack‐year index). In both proteins, this difference was due to higher bc‐sensitivity (lysozyme C: 80%
vs
74%; neutrophil defensin 1: 80%
vs
75%) with similar bc‐specificity (94% and 88%, respectively). These results suggest that for some salivary proteins, years of smoking is more influential on their diagnostic accuracy, while others are more affected by the years and number of cigarettes smoked. Therefore, future proteomic studies should determine the impact of both smoking variables on protein expression levels and the diagnostic accuracy of biomarkers.
The impact of age on the predictive capacity of biomarkers may be related to the inflammageing effect. As the subject ages, the immune response changes, increasing the expression of inflammatory proteins; this may be an essential factor in modifying the periodontal proteome (Uçan Yarkaç, Babayiğit, and Gokturk
2024
; Zhang et al.
2022
). Tobacco impacts the immune system by exacerbating pathological responses but also by attenuating its normal defensive function (Qiu et al.
2017
), persisting even after smoking cessation (Saint‐André et al.
2024
). Furthermore, proteomics has found proteins differentially expressed in ex‐smokers compared to never‐smokers (Blanco‐Pintos, Regueira‐Iglesias, Kuz, et al.
2024
).
The fact that the salivary proteome's diagnostic predictivity is more influenced by age may be related to the paradoxical effect of tobacco on the immune system, possibly conditioned by the host response and heterogeneity of cigarette composition (Qiu et al.
2017
).
4.4
Clinical Applicability: Considerations, Limitations and Future Research Objectives
Clinical measures remain the gold standard for the diagnosis of periodontitis. However, because of their well‐known limitations in detecting the disease, Tonetti, Greenwell, and Kornman (
2018
) emphasized that biomarkers could be especially useful in identifying periodontitis, especially in the early stages.
This study showed that predictive modelling directly from proteomic data can identify biomarkers with an exceptional capacity for diagnosing periodontitis. This shows the clinical relevance of proteomic techniques as a diagnostic tool not only for clinicians but also for population screening or even for outpatient use (Mundt et al.
2023
; Bornes et al.
2023
). Nevertheless, these findings should be validated in an external patient group and using other techniques, for example, enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) (Shin et al.
2019
; Wu et al.
2009
) or other proteomic techniques (Bostanci et al.
2018
).
Moreover, although multivariate models increase the accuracy in diagnosing periodontitis, it is desirable to find simple biomarker combinations (Blanco‐Pintos et al.
2023
). In this regard, single proteins adjusted for age or tobacco and two‐protein combinations demonstrated outstanding diagnostic accuracy. However, undoubtedly, a single age‐adjusted protein in saliva could be more feasible to translate into clinical practice than combining two proteins.
If we compare the results obtained in saliva with those previously published by our group in gingival crevicular fluid (GCF), the diagnostic accuracy of GCF proteins is higher (Blanco‐Pintos, Regueira‐Iglesias, Relvas, et al.
2024
). However, when considering the clinical variables, the predictive capacity increases and becomes similar between both sample types, even higher for age‐adjusted salivary proteins. Thus, these results are very good from the perspective of clinical applicability, as saliva is easier to collect by clinicians and patients (Zhou and Liu
2023
). Furthermore, these proteomic biomarkers could represent a useful test in the secondary prevention of periodontitis, which is related to early diagnosis and rapid intervention (AbdulRaheem
2023
).
Because of the limited evidence for assessing the diagnostic accuracy of salivary proteins to detect periodontitis using proteomic data, we initially evaluated opposing periodontal conditions. Therefore, future research should consider heterogeneous periodontal conditions to determine the capacity of these proteins in detecting initial periodontitis stages. Additionally, because of the inflammatory factor of periodontitis, the capacity of these proteins to differentiate gingivitis from periodontitis should also be evaluated.
Although the sample size is larger than in many previous proteomic studies (Hartenbach et al.
2020
; Mertens et al.
2018
; Salazar et al.
2013
; Tang et al.
2019
), it was not sufficient to employ multivariate approaches considering age and smoking habit simultaneously in the predictive model. A larger and more heterogeneous sample size will make it possible to determine the impact of both risk factors.
In conclusion, salivary proteins quantified by SWATH‐MS allow the identification of various novel biomarkers with good or excellent ability to diagnose periodontitis. Age influences the accuracy of the single biomarkers more than smoking status, with predictive results comparable to or even higher than the two‐protein combinations.
The eight age‐adjusted biomarkers with bc‐sensitivity and bc‐specificity > 90% are keratin, type II cytoskeletal 1, protein S100‐A8, β‐2‐microglobulin, neutrophil defensin 1, lysozyme C, ubiquitin‐60S ribosomal protein L40, isoform 2 of tropomyosin α‐3 chain and resistin. Considering smoking status, although these proteins also have good to excellent values, they are lower than age‐adjusted ones. The protein combinations with the highest diagnostic accuracy (bc‐sensitivity and bc‐specificity > 90%) are β‐2‐microglobulin with profilin‐1 and lysozyme C with ZG16B. However, these results should be validated in an external patient group and using other analytical techniques.
