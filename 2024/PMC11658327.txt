Background
Targeting the immune checkpoints to unleash the natural immunity against cancer revolutionized cancer treatment approach [
1
,
2
]. Therefore, much hope was given to the implementation of PD-1/PDL-1 (programmed cell death protein 1/programmed cell death 1 ligand 1) blocking antibodies for treatment of cancers with little therapeutic improvements in the past decades, such as osteosarcoma (OS). OS is the most common type of bone cancer in humans, predominantly affecting children and young adults [
3
,
4
]. The 5-year survival rate for OS patients without metastatic disease is 60–70% but it dramatically decreases to less than 20% in metastatic disease, while the majority of patients manifest micrometastases in the lungs at the time of diagnosis [
5
–
8
]. OS patients still follow the therapeutic regimen based on surgical resection of the primary tumor and highly toxic neoadjuvant chemotherapy [
9
–
11
].
Although OS tumor cells express PDL-1, which is a prognostic marker for efficacy of PD-1/PDL-1 inhibition [
12
–
15
], clinical trials evaluating immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) for treatment of osteosarcoma appeared largely disappointing [
16
–
20
]. Several mechanisms were proposed to explain poor outcomes of ICIs in the treatment of OS. Primarily, it was linked to the presence of immunosuppressive immune cells including M2 macrophages [
21
–
24
], T regulatory cells or myeloid derived tumor suppressor cells, which suppress cytotoxic T cell response [
25
,
26
]. An alternative mechanism involves tumor immunogenicity. ICIs exhibit the most favorable outcomes in malignancies considered hot tumors characterized by high mutational burden and immune infiltrate, for example melanoma or non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [
27
–
30
]. In contrast, osteosarcoma is regarded as a cold tumor, characterized by limited infiltration by immune cells, which could make ICIs less effective [
31
]. Recent studies proposed that the limited response or unexpected cancer progression upon treatment with ICIs may also result from the functional expression of PD-1 not only by immune cells but also by tumor cells [
32
] in cancers such as NSCLC or colon cancer [
33
,
34
].
Currently, a growing body of evidence suggests the significance of cancer-expressed PD-1, but conflicting reports exist regarding the role and mechanism of cancer-intrinsic signaling. It was observed that PD-1 may play either a pro-tumorigenic role like in melanoma, glioblastoma and pancreatic cancer [
35
–
37
] or tumor suppressor functions as reported in NSCLC and colon cancer [
33
–
35
,
38
–
44
], suggesting that the PD-1 function depends on the tumor type. While PD-1 expression has been previously reported in bone and soft tissue sarcomas [
45
], its role in the limited response to ICIs in osteosarcoma has never been explored [
33
,
34
].
The PD-1 molecular pathway has been extensively studied in the context of immune cells, T cell functional exhaustion and therapeutic PD-1/PDL-1 blocking antibodies. Yet, it remains unclear whether cancer-intrinsic PD-1 acts as a tumor suppressor, or a tumor promoter and only scarce information is available regarding the cancer-intrinsic PD-1 molecular pathway. Given the limited number of patients who respond to immunotherapy and sometimes unexpected progress of the disease in response to ICIs, it becomes evident that we lack full understanding of the PD-1/PDL-1 axis in cancer [
16
,
17
]. Therefore, it is fundamental to investigate the PD-1 signaling network to clarify the debatable function of PD-1 pathway in cancer and to ensure safety of immunotherapy. Moreover, studying the PD-1 interactome has potential to identify new therapeutic targets and could improve the efficacy of ICIs across various types of cancer. Recently, an improved response to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) in cancer patients was observed in combination with AXL (tyrosine kinase receptor UFO) inhibitors. It was demonstrated that AXL signaling plays a role in tumor invasion, metastasis, and drug resistance. Upregulated expression of AXL was associated with poor prognosis in several cancers, making it an attractive therapeutic target [
46
]. Combining the immune checkpoint blockade with targeted therapies against AXL holds promise in overcoming resistance mechanisms and enhancing the efficacy of immunotherapy. The synergy between these approaches offers new avenues for precision cancer medicine, with potential benefits for patients across various malignancies. In this study, we implemented a cellular model of osteosarcoma to characterize PD-1 expression and PD-1 protein–protein interactions. Despite multiple roles of AXL in oncogenesis, cancer invasion, metastasis, and pathogenesis, the mechanistic link between AXL and PD-1 has not been reported previously. This is the first report which proves experimentally that AXL and PD-1 interact with each other. Molecular modeling and molecular dynamics simulations identified the PD-1/AXL interacting residues and demonstrated that the intracellular domains play the primary role in maintaining the PD-1/AXL interaction. Further implementation of comparative study approach revealed that the PD-1/AXL interaction also occurs in canine model, which is key for future studies into translational use of the finding and future in vivo validation. Sequence alignment indicated high conservation of amino acid residues at the interacting interface between dog and human, with significant similarity across other species.
Our study elucidates the molecular mechanisms governing the AXL/PD-1 interaction in cancer, highlighting the conservation of this interaction across species. These findings offer valuable insights for therapeutic targeting and drug design strategies in cancer immunotherapy.
Results
PD-1 is spontaneously expressed by osteosarcoma cells
Our objective was to elucidate the PD-1 protein–protein interactions in cancer cells. We hypothesized that PD-1 may be expressed spontaneously in OS cancer cells, which could be further used to study PD-1 protein–protein interactions in cancer by PD-1 pull-down and subsequent LC–MS/MS analysis validated by immunoblotting and proximity ligation assay (PLA) as depicted in Fig.
1
. We used U2OS human osteosarcoma cells as a cellular model of OS to confirm and characterize PD-1 receptor expression in this type of cancer cells. Western blot analysis confirmed that the PD-1 protein is expressed by U2OS cells and while the predicted molecular weight of PD-1 is 32 kDa, immunoblotting demonstrated that the PD-1 migration pattern is represented by two bands detected at 55 kDa and 70 kDa (Fig.
2
A). The 55 kDa band was previously described to represent heavily glycosylated, functionally active PD-1 [
47
,
48
]. The 70 kDa band was previously observed by Chen et al. [
49
] as PD-1 specific band in a study utilizing multiple monoclonal antibodies, but protein modifications responsible for increased molecular weight have not been investigated. We observed that the level of PD-1 expression in U2OS cells was low, which was corroborated by the immunofluorescent staining, where PD-1 detection was unsuccessful using a general staining protocol. PD-1 was only visualized by implementing Alexa Fluor™ 488 Tyramide SuperBoost™ staining, designed for the detection of low-abundance proteins (Fig.
2
B). Additionally, microscopy imaging revealed that PD-1 was expressed in the cytoplasm and did not localize specifically to the membrane. Therefore, we performed flow cytometry analysis to precisely assess the surface and intracellular expression of PD-1 in U2OS cells (Fig.
2
C and D, respectively). Data from four independent experiments demonstrated that nearly 100% of the cells expressed PD-1 intracellularly, while only 3.9% cells expressed PD-1 on their surface (Fig.
2
E). This percentage of cells expressing PD-1 on the surface corresponds to cancer-PD-1 expression observed in melanoma patients (depending on a study, 3%, ± 0.7%, n = 15 or 8.7% ± 1.5%, n = 8) or detected in NSCLC cell lines (4%, ± 0.3, n = 12) [
32
,
35
,
41
].

High-throughput screening of PD-1 interactome requires C-terminal protein fusion with the tag
To compensate for the low level of PD-1 expression by U2OS cells, we overexpressed the recombinant PD-1 protein using a plasmid vector. Before performing the pull-down assay for isolation of PD-1 interacting proteins, we optimized the PD-1 tagging approach to minimize any interference with PD-1 expression, stability, and protein–protein interactions. We tested various fusion tags including Twin-Strep-tag or Twin-Strep-tag coupled to V5 tag and inserted them either at the C-terminus or N-terminus of PD-1 protein (Fig.
3
A). Western blot analysis revealed that recombinant PD-1 protein migrated at the molecular weights corresponding to the wild-type protein with a slight shift corresponding to tag insertions. C-terminally tagged recombinant PD-1 was highly overexpressed and detected at a molecular weight of approximately 55 kDa, suggesting that it undergoes glycosylation. Fusion with the N-terminal tag resulted in the complete absence of PD-1 overexpression detected at 55 kDa, possibly due to N-terminal PD-1 modifications interference with protein N-glycosylation, as previously reported by Sun et al. [
48
]. Simultaneously, both N- and C-tagged proteins were weakly overexpressed in their non-glycosylated form detected at approximately 32 kDa. Notably, PD-1 overexpressing cells did not exhibit an elevation in the level of PD-1 form represented by the 70 kDa band, suggesting that the recombinant protein does not undergo similar PTMs. Taken together, the C-terminal V5-Twin-Strep-tag was less disruptive for the PD-1 expression pattern, therefore it was used in the subsequent experiments.

The phosphorylation of Y223 and Y248 tyrosine residues of PD-1, particularly Y248, was demonstrated to play a crucial role in downstream PD-1 signaling upon PDL-1 activation in T cells [
50
–
52
]. Therefore, we incorporated the Y223F and Y248F PD-1 mutants into our study to not only identify proteins interacting with PD-1, but also to examine if binding of these proteins depends on PD-1 phosphorylation state. Moreover, to perform a large-scale LC–MS/MS experiment, we generated three stable cell lines overexpressing: (1) wild-type PD-1 protein or (2) mutant variant, in which tyrosine Y223 was substituted with phenylalanine (referred to as Y223F mutant) or (3) mutant variant with Y248 substituted with phenylalanine (referred to as Y248F mutant) (Fig.
3
B-C). We measured the PD-1 overexpression in U2OS cells by flow cytometry analysis with surface and intracellular staining. Our initial findings demonstrated that endogenous PD-1 expression in U2OS cells was mostly restricted to the intracellular compartment. However, upon overexpression, wild-type PD-1 and its both mutant variants were markedly overexpressed both intracellularly and on the cell surface (Fig.
3
D and E).
Next, we used the U2OS cells overexpressing tagged PD-1 proteins for pull-down experiments to isolate PD-1 interacting proteins (schematic overview depicted by Supplementary Fig. 1). Since PD-1 mutations had no effect on either the level or localization of PD-1 protein, implementation of cells overexpressing the wild-type and mutated PD-1 to study the PD-1 interactome would allow to identify interactions with both membrane-bound and cytosolic proteins. Protein complexes were eluted from beads under reducing conditions and digested with trypsin via FASP (filter aided sample preparation) [
53
]. Subsequently, we performed quantitative bottom-up mass spectrometry. The data were searched against a protein database, quantified, and then subjected to bioinformatic and statistical analyses, ultimately providing a list of protein identities and their fold changes. We initially ranked proteins by their adjusted p-values of fold changes, reflecting the significance of protein enrichment in the pull-down samples when compared to the control. Selection criteria for protein candidates considered for validation were described in the methodology section.
After filtering the results against control, we obtained a list of 52 interacting protein candidates (FDR < 1%) for the WT PD-1 and 79 proteins for each of the phospho-tyrosine mutants (Fig.
3
F). 36 proteins were shared across all PD-1 forms (Table
1
), 8 proteins were unique for the WT PD-1, implicating that the latter require the functional phosphorylation of PD-1 tyrosine residues (Supplementary Table 1).

PD-1 pull-down and LC–MS/MS analysis identifies AXL as a cancer PD-1 interacting partner
From a pool of potential PD-1 interacting candidates shared across all PD-1 forms regardless of the mutations in tyrosine residues, the tyrosine kinase receptor UFO (AXL) emerged as a highly promising target. Notably, AXL has been reported to propagate epithelial to mesenchymal transition (EMT), cancer-induced angiogenesis, metastasis, and drug resistance, making it an attractive cancer therapeutic target. Moreover, it was observed that inhibition of AXL improves response to ICIs, but the mechanistic link between PD-1 and AXL has not been investigated before [
54
–
56
]. Prior to validation, we quantitatively analyzed AXL peptide spectra in Skyline software and confirmed the protein identity with two high confidence tryptic peptides (Supplementary Fig. 2). Then, we confirmed the presence of AXL in PD-1 protein complexes obtained from pull-down experiments by immunoblotting using AXL-specific antibodies, thereby verifying the physical interaction between these proteins (Fig.
4
A). Moreover, AXL was detected in the eluates of all samples with PD-1 overexpression, including the Y223F and Y248F mutants. This indicates that the interaction does not require PD-1 tyrosine phosphorylation and is supported by our LC–MS/MS findings. The PD-1 overexpressing cells facilitated the detection of PD-1 protein–protein interactions, especially when using methods like LC–MS/MS, which are only feasible when a protein is overexpressed. Subsequently, we performed a standard immunofluorescent staining in U2OS cells overexpressing recombinant PD-1 protein variants to optimize the experimental conditions prior to additional validation of the interaction between PD-1 and AXL by PLA [
57
]. The dual staining of PD-1 and AXL indicated potential co-localization in a subset of cells, observed as the orange signal in channel merged images (Supplementary Fig. 3). Next, we confirmed the interaction between PD-1 and AXL by PLA (Fig.
4
B), which indicated that mutations in tyrosine phosphorylation sites did not abrogate the interaction between PD-1 and AXL, consistently with the LC–MS/MS and immunoblotting data.

Notably, the interaction between PD-1 and AXL was not detected in the empty vector control despite endogenous expression of both proteins in the U2OS cells. The flow cytometry analysis and immunofluorescent staining demonstrated low level of PD-1 expressed spontaneously in U2OS cells, which could hamper the detection of the interaction under control conditions. However, upon overexpression, nearly all U2OS transfected cells demonstrated strong surface expression of PD-1 protein (see Fig.
3
D for reference), facilitating detection of the interaction. Furthermore, confocal microscopy employed to visualize the PLA revealed a potential localization of the interaction to the cellular membrane. This was observed in a single focal plane, but not when images were visualized as a Z-stack as depicted in Fig.
4
C.
Docking analysis reveals intracellular domain localization of the interaction between PD-1 and AXL
Having confirmed the PD-1/AXL interaction, we aimed at more in-depth characterization of its underlying mechanism. We performed molecular modeling and molecular dynamics simulations to investigate the specific binding sites involved in the interaction. Additionally, the analysis included studies on the impact of mutations in PD-1 tyrosine phosphorylation sites Y223 and Y248 on the binding affinity between PD-1 and AXL. To analyze the binding between AXL (ECD) and PD-1 (ECD), as well as between AXL (ICD) and PD-1 (ICD), we performed macromolecular docking simulations using HDOCK online server. To assess the potential interactions and binding strengths between AXL and PD-1, we examined the interactions between AXL (ICD) and PD-1 (ICD) WT and mutants (Y223F and Y248F). The resulting protein–protein complexes were subjected to analysis of their intermolecular interaction patterns using Schrodinger’s Maestro software.
The docking results summarized in Table

and visualized in Figs.

A-B and
6

The docking scores suggested favorable binding affinity between the two proteins, where a lower docking score value indicates a stronger predicted binding affinity. The identified interactions, including hydrogen bonds and salt bridges, described the nature of the intermolecular interactions and the potential stabilizing forces between AXL and PD-1 in the complex. The bond distances mentioned represent the spatial proximity between the interacting residues. Detailed description of the interacting residues, docking scores and chemical bonds characteristics is included in the Supplementary materials.

The docking scores indicated that the AXL-PD-1-ECD complex had the lowest binding affinity with a docking score of -220.02 kcal/mol. The AXL-PD-1-ICD (WT) complex had a slightly lower docking score of -296.67 kcal/mol, demonstrating relatively higher binding compared to the AXL-PD-1-ECD complex. The AXL-PD-1-ICD (Y223F) complex exhibited a docking score of -238.96 kcal/mol, suggesting a stronger binding affinity than the AXL-PD-1-ECD complex but still not as strong as the AXL-PD-1-ICD (WT) complex. Of those, the AXL–PD-1-ICD (Y248F) complex demonstrated the lowest docking score of -303.29 kcal/mol, indicating the highest binding affinity among the complexes analyzed.
Considering the comparison between the complexes formed between AXL and intracellular domains of PD-1 WT and mutant variants (Y223F and Y248F), it is evident that mutations in the intracellular domain of PD-1 led to variations in the specific interactions and docking scores. These alterations suggest different binding modes and potentially distinct effects on the stability and affinity of the AXL-PD-1 complex. However, in western blot and PLA experiments, we did not observe significant differences in the AXL-PD-1 interactions, indicating that tyrosine mutations do not abrogate the interaction between AXL and PD-1. Nevertheless, differences in the binding mode between conditions could impact the functional role of the interaction and require further validation.
The structure of PD-1 ECD exhibits higher thermodynamic stability when complexed to AXL
The Macromolecular HDOCK docking protocol lacks the ability to account for flexibility in protein receptors, therefore, to investigate the dynamic behavior of the AXL receptor upon binding of PD-1, we conducted MD simulations for four complexes, each simulated for a duration of 2 µs. To compare the structural changes during the simulation, we calculated the RMSD (Root Mean Square Deviation) of AXL and PD-1 proteins when they were complexed together. The RMSD value quantifies the average deviation between the positions of corresponding atoms in two structures. By calculating the RMSD, we assessed the extent of conformational changes that occur in the AXL and PD-1 receptors during the simulation.
We investigated the interactions between the ECD domain of AXL and the ECD domain of PD-1, and between the ICD domains of AXL and PD-1, including wild-type and PD-1 mutant variants (Y223F and Y248F). The study included two different scenarios: i) when both AXL and PD-1 were embedded within the same cell membrane (Fig.
7
A) and ii) when AXL and PD-1 were present in different cell membranes and separate cellular environments (Fig.
7
B). In the case of the interaction between ICD domains of AXL and PD-1, they were able to interact only when embedded within the same cell membrane (Fig.
7
A).

The RMSD calculated from the 2 µs of simulation data for the AXL-PD-1-ECD complex indicated that PD-1 exhibited higher thermodynamic stability when it was complexed with AXL. The averaged RMSD value obtained was 3.45 ± 0.19 Å throughout the simulation with PD-1 showing less structural deviation from its initial conformation when bound to AXL (Fig.
7
C).
During the initial 500 ns of the MD simulation of AXL, there was a notable decrease in conformational stability, as evidenced by a deviation of up to 17 Å in the RMSD. However, after this initial phase, the AXL structure stabilized, and its conformational stability was maintained throughout the remaining of the production run (up to 2 µs), with an average RMSD of 17.12 ± 2.65 Å (Fig.
7
C). The observed initial thermodynamic instability of the AXL structure could be attributed to the presence of a loop between the two immunoglobulin (Ig) domains and the two-fibronectin type III (FNIII) domains of AXL. This loop region likely undergoes significant conformational changes, leading to the observed higher RMSD values. Eventually, AXL reached a more stable conformation and remained relatively stable throughout the remaining simulation time. Notably, the PD-1 extracellular domain (ECD) remained complexed with the Ig domains of AXL likely contributing to the stabilization of the AXL structure after the initial phase (Supplementary Movie 1). Overall, these findings suggest that while the AXL structure exhibits instability during the initial phase of the simulation due to a loop region eventually, it reaches a more stable conformation and remains relatively stable throughout the remaining simulation time.
The ICD of AXL is thermodynamically stabilized when complexed to the ICD of PD-1 regardless of the phospho-tyrosine mutations of PD-1
The RMSD plot calculated for the interaction between the AXL-ICD and PD-1-ICD (WT) indicated a conformational stability of the AXL-ICD, with an average RMSD value of 5.39 ± 0.40 Å (Fig.
7
D). Notably, when the AXL-ICD was bound to the mutant variants of PD-1-ICD, the conformational stability of AXL-ICD further increased. Treatment with selective AXL inhibitor does not abrogate the PD-1/AXL interaction but decreases its stability
AXL inhibition was previously reported to sensitize cancer cells to immune checkpoint inhibitors treatment, although the precise mechanistic link has yet to be fully determined [

Our macromolecular docking analysis and molecular dynamics simulations indicated that the intracellular domain binding plays a crucial role in the strength of the PD-1/AXL interaction. Therefore, we chose bemcentinib, which is a potent and selective inhibitor of the AXL receptor that blocks its catalytic activity and inhibits downstream signaling to study its effect on the PD-1/AXL interaction [

Discussion
Contradictory reports regarding the role of cancer-intrinsic PD-1 signaling are available. Depending on the tumor type, PD-1 may act either as a tumor promoter or a suppressor and was proposed as a possible mechanism of resistance to ICB [
33
,
34
,
37
,
38
,
40
,
64
,
65
]. Since the current reports provide limited information about the pathways related to the PD-1 pathway and effects of cancer related mutational burden on PD-1 pathway signaling in cancer cells, the variations in the PD-1 interactome and effector functions in various cell types could be expected. Therefore, PD-1 interactome studies are essential to fully understand the mechanism of PD-1 signaling in cancer cells. In addition, finding new druggable targets could potentially improve the efficacy of immunotherapy in tumors with limited response to PD-1/PDL-1 blockade such as e.g. osteosarcoma.
Initially, we demonstrated that PD-1 is spontaneously expressed by human osteosarcoma cells at a low level. Our analysis indicated that an average of 4% of cells expressed PD-1 on the surface, however the intracellular expression of PD-1 was detected in almost all the cells. Such level of cancer-expressed PD-1 on the surface corresponds to the previous studies reporting from 3 to 9% PD-1 positive cells, however, intracellular expression of PD-1 was not described in the previous reports [
32
,
35
,
41
]. Moreover, our observations suggested that PD-1 protein could be post-translationally modified in malignant cells as evidenced by the migration at molecular weights of 55 kDa and 70 kDa detected by western blot analysis. These observations are in line with previous studies, suggesting that the 55 kDa molecular weight represents heavily glycosylated PD-1, described as the active form required for effective signaling in T cells [
47
,
48
].
We conducted a high-throughput PD-1 interactome study employing LC–MS/MS bottom-up quantitative proteomic approach to identify its binding partners isolated during pull-down. Apart from the wild-type PD-1 protein interactome, we analyzed changes in PD-1 interactome triggered by mutations in the tyrosine 223 and 248—residues reported as critical for the inhibitory function of PD-1 in T cells [
50
,
51
]. Therefore, in our study, we utilized PD-1 phospho-tyrosine mutants Y223 and Y248, in which the tyrosines were replaced by phenylalanine. The targeted tyrosine residues Tyr223 and Tyr248, have impact on downstream PD-1 signaling in T cells. Therefore, this approach allowed us to explore the largely uncharacterized cancer-related PD-1 interactome and better understand how PD-1 phosphorylation status might influence these interactions. Ultimately, we identified several PD-1 interacting candidates within cancer cells, with their binding characterized by either dependence or independence on the tyrosine mutations. This emphasizes the significance of PD-1 phosphorylation in some interactions. Among all studied interacting partners, we focused on AXL, a receptor tyrosine kinase increasingly recognized for its significance in cancer. AXL has not yet been reported as a PD-1 interacting partner [
66
–
69
]. We confirmed that AXL interacts with the PD-1 receptor using western blot and PLA as orthogonal methods. Furthermore, our findings indicated that AXL interacts with PD-1 specifically on the cellular membrane rather than within the cytoplasmic pool. These results are consistent with the known functions of both PD-1 and AXL as membrane receptors [
70
].
Subsequently, we characterized the mechanism of the interaction and binding characteristics between PD-1 and AXL by implementing in silico studies. The docking analysis was performed separately for complexes formed between the extracellular and intracellular domains of AXL and PD-1 (AXL-PD-1-ECD and AXL-PD-1-ICD, respectively). It also included Y223F and Y248F PD-1 mutant variants and revealed that the AXL-PD-1-ECD complex exhibited strong binding affinity, with specific hydrogen bonds and salt bridges stabilizing the complex. The AXL-PD-1-ICD (WT) complex showed potentially stronger binding compared to the extracellular domain complex, with distinct residues and bond types involved in the interaction. The mutant complexes, AXL-PD-1-ICD (Y223F) and AXL-PD-1-ICD (Y248F), demonstrated variations in interactions and docking scores, indicating altered but not abrogated protein–protein binding. These findings highlight the complex nature of the AXL-PD-1 interaction and emphasize the significance of domain-specific interactions and mutations in modulating binding affinity and stability.
Our MD simulation study of the conformational stability and dynamic behavior demonstrated that the complex formed between AXL and PD-1 exhibited higher thermodynamic stability, as evidenced by the lower RMSD values. Specifically, PD-1 showed less structural deviation from its initial conformation when bound to AXL, indicating a stable interaction between the two proteins. Furthermore, the AXL-ICD demonstrated increased conformational stability when bound to PD-1-ICD, especially in the case of the two mutant variants of PD-1 (Y223F and Y248F). The mutant variants displayed enhanced stability, as indicated by their lower averaged RMSD values compared to the wild-type PD-1-ICD. On the other hand, the initial phase of the simulation revealed a decrease in conformational stability of the AXL structure, attributed to conformational changes in a loop region between the Ig domains and FNIII domains of AXL. However, the AXL structure eventually reached a more stable conformation and maintained relatively stable structural characteristics throughout the remaining simulation time. Additionally, the extracellular domain of PD-1 and the intracellular domain of AXL exhibited higher structural stability during the simulation compared to the ECD of AXL and the ICD of PD-1. This comprehensive analysis of the intermolecular interactions provided valuable insights into the strength and stability of the binding between these domains. Also, we demonstrated that hydrogen bonds and salt bridges play a crucial role in stabilizing the interactions between the ECDs and ICDs of AXL and PD-1. The presence of pi-cation interactions and pi-pi stacking varied among the complexes, with the ICDs of AXL and PD-1 mutant (Y248F) demonstrating the most stable and consistent interactions (Supplementary Fig. 5).
Interestingly, AXL plays several functions in cancer, including but not limited to the propagation of EMT, cancer-induced angiogenesis, metastasis, and drug resistance. Therefore, it has recently emerged as an attractive cancer therapeutic target, with AXL blockade being reported to improve response to immunotherapy [
54
–
56
]. AXL inhibition was demonstrated to decrease production of proinflammatory cytokines, exerting better control over tumor associated macrophages [
71
]. It was also found to increase the number of PD-1 positive CD8 T cells in TME in NSCLC patients with STK11 mutations [
72
]. Importantly, AXL expression strongly correlates with PDL-1 expression by tumor cells, promoting immune evasion, while implementation of AXL inhibitors decreases PDL-1 expression, improving the efficacy of immunotherapy [
73
,
74
]. Nevertheless, the mechanistic link between PD-1 and AXL has not been reported thus far.
AXL activation is ligand dependent and requires ligation with GAS6 (growth arrest-specific protein 6), similarly to other tyrosine kinase receptors (RTKs) [
75
]. Notably, it was demonstrated that AXL could undergo non-canonical activation through heterodimerization with other RTKs such as EGFR or HER2, resulting in mutual activation. RTKs ability to activate AXL promotes cancer progression through downstream activation of PI3K/Akt/mTOR, RAS/RAF/MEK, or JAK/STAT signaling, thereby supporting tumor growth and survival [
67
,
76
,
77
]. This mechanism was proposed as AXL-driven resistance to HER or EGFR inhibitors [
78
–
81
]. Although no such correlation was previously reported for PD-1, it is tempting to speculate that the interaction between PD-1 and AXL could cross-modulate activity of both proteins. This hypothesis could be supported by the fact that inhibition of catalytic activity of AXL with bemcentinib, which we performed suggested decreased affinity of PD-1/AXL binding. PD-1 pull-down indicated that the PD-1/AXL interaction was affected, as evidenced by decreased presence of AXL after PD-1 pull-down. However, PLA did not indicate any visible changes upon AXL inhibition. Perhaps, bemcentinib treatment decreased PD-1/AXL binding affinity, making the interaction less stable, therefore less resistant to conditions of cell lysis and magnetic bead isolation. In contrast, the in situ conditions of PLA likely preserved the interaction, resulting in no observable changes in the detected interaction. However, more studies are needed to further elucidate the mechanism and significance of this interaction.
Additionally, to allow for quicker clinical evaluation of potential AXL/PD-1 axis modulators, using a comparative oncology approach we explored if this interaction also takes place in canine osteosarcoma. Canine osteosarcoma is a frequent, spontaneously occurring malignancy in dogs. In addition, it closely resembles human disease, both pathologically as well as genetically, making canine osteosarcoma an ideal model to study novel therapeutic approaches. Our studies confirmed endogenous expression of both PD-1 and AXL in canine osteosarcoma cell lines, while both PLA and PD-1 pull-down validated the presence of PD-1/AXL interaction in the dog model. Furthermore, sequence alignment analysis, revealed similarity between human and canine PD-1, with high conservation of AXL sequence in both humans and dogs. Also, the PD-1/AXL interacting residues identified for human proteins, were mostly preserved in both species. Given this high degree of homology between human and canine PD-1/AXL, we extended the sequence alignment analysis to more species. Although we observed moderate homology of PD-1 protein across species, AXL was highly conserved across all of them, with significant coverage of the residues involved in the interaction with PD-1. Interestingly, the interacting residues critical for the PD-1/AXL interaction showed closer homology between humans and dogs than between humans and rodents, further supporting the rationale for using canine model to study this interaction in human disease. Overall, our data imply that the interaction between PD-1 and AXL may be conserved across multiple species, suggesting that it could play a critical biological function.
In summary, the interaction between AXL and PD-1 in cancer presents a multifaceted scenario. Primarily, this interaction could exacerbate immune evasion mechanisms employed by cancer cells. AXL activation may upregulate PDL-1 expression on immune cells or tumor cells themselves, enhancing the suppression of anti-tumor immune responses. Conversely, if PD-1 engagement could potentiate AXL signaling pathways, it may result in promoting tumor cell survival and metastasis. It is worth pointing out that this interaction may depend on the tumor type and could also occur in immune cells expressing both PD-1 and AXL. Moreover, there are strong indications that the novel PD-1/AXL interaction may be conserved across larger number of species, emphasizing its significance in biological processes. Therefore, understanding these interactions may offer therapeutic opportunities. Combining AXL inhibitors with PD-1/PD-L1 blockade could potentially disrupt this axis, restoring anti-tumor immunity while impeding tumor progression and metastasis. This synergistic approach may enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy or other cancer treatment modalities by overcoming resistance mechanisms and augmenting anti-tumor immune responses. Consequently, our findings characterizing the interaction between PD-1 and AXL may potentially contribute to the discovery of new druggable targets and further studies describing the significance of the interaction between cancer-intrinsic PD-1 and AXL are urgently needed.
Materials and methods
Cell culture
U2OS human osteosarcoma cell line was obtained from Elabscience, cat. CL-0236. The cells were cultured in modified McCoy’s 5A medium (Gibco, cat. 16600082) supplemented with 10% heat inactivated FBS (Gibco, cat. 10500064) and 100UI/ml Penicillin–Streptomycin (Gibco, cat. 15140122). Canine osteosarcoma cell lines OSCA8 (cat. EMN001-FP) and OSCA78 (cat. EMN004-FP) were purchased from Kerafast. The cells were cultured in high glucose DMEM medium (Gibco, cat. 11965092) supplemented with 10% heat inactivated FBS (Gibco, cat. 10500064) and 100UI/ml Penicillin–Streptomycin (Gibco, cat. 15140122) and 100 mM HEPES (Gibco, cat. 15630056). All cell lines were maintained in a humidified atmosphere at 37 °C with 5% CO
2
. For AXL inhibition, cells were seeded onto cell culture dishes and allowed to adhere for 24 h prior to treatment with 2.5 µM bemcentinib for additional 24 h. Control cells were treated with an equivalent volume of DMSO. Following treatment, the cells were harvested for either pull-down assays or proximity ligation assay (PLA).
Attractene transfection of U2OS cells with plasmid vectors
Cells were seeded onto 6-well plate 24 h prior to the transfection. The transfection was performed using attractene transfection reagent (Qiagen, cat. 301005) with either empty vector control or human PD-1 WT, PD-1 Y223F or PD-1 Y248F mutant variant pcDNA 3.1( +) plasmid vector. Following the manufacturer’s protocol and the initially optimized transfection conditions, 1.8 µg of plasmid DNA was diluted with supplement free media to 100 µl. Then, 6.75 µl of Attractene transfection reagent was added and incubated for 15 min at RT (room temperature) to allow complex formation. Transfection complexes were added dropwise to the cells grown in 2.5 ml of cell culture media. Subsequently, for transient transfection, the cells were cultured for 48 h and collected for experiments. To create stable cell lines, the cells were incubated for 24 h before replacing the media with the antibiotic selection media. PD-1 vectors encoded the neomycin resistance gene, and successfully transfected cells were selected by 2-week incubation in 400 µg/ml Geneticin (Roche, cat. G418-RO) selection media. Empty vector control plasmid encoded hygromycin B phosphotransferase gene, and stably transfected cells were selected for 2 weeks in 100 µg/ml Hygromycin B (Gibco™, cat. 10687010) media. A similar protocol was followed for the transfection of canine osteosarcoma cells with vectors encoding the canine WT-PD-1 protein. Antibiotic selection of cells transfected with the empty vector was carried out using 50 µg/ml and 100 µg/ml Hygromycin for OSCA8 and OSCA78 cells, respectively. Cells transfected with canine WT-PD-1 vectors were selected using 300 µg/ml Geneticin.
Protein isolation for western blot
Cells were washed two times with PBS (phosphate buffered saline) and lysed directly on a cell culture plate with ice-cold CelLytic M lysis buffer (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. C2978) supplemented with appropriate amounts of protease inhibitor cocktails (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. P8340-1ML). Lysates were gently mixed on ice for 20 min and centrifuged for 15 min at 14,000 x
g
at 4 °C. Protein concentration in the supernatant was quantified by Bradford assay. Lysates were mixed with 4X reducing sample buffer and samples were denatured by boiling for 5 min at 95 °C.
Immunoblotting
Protein lysates or samples obtained through pull-down were resolved by 8% SDS-PAGE using standardized protein amounts of 20-50 µg per well. The resolved proteins were then transferred onto a nitrocellulose blotting membrane (Amersham Protran®, cat. 10600004) using the Trans-Blot® Turbo™ Transfer System (Bio-Rad). Subsequently, membranes were blocked overnight in 5% non-fat milk in 0.1% Tween 20 Phosphate buffered saline (PBST) at 4 °C. Next, membranes were washed three times with 0.1% PBST and incubated overnight at 4 °C with one of the following antibodies diluted in 2% blocking buffer: anti-PD-1 UltraMAB™ dilution 1:500 (clone UMAB199, OriGene, cat. UM800091) or anti-AXL dilution 1:1000 (clone C89E7, Cell Signalling, cat. #8661). Restore plus western blot stripping buffer (Thermo Scientific™, cat. 46430) was used when membrane reprobing with 1:10,000 dilution of anti-ß-actin (abcam, cat. ab6276) was performed. Following primary antibody incubation, membranes were washed three times with PBST and incubated for 1 h at RT with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies: goat anti-rabbit, dilution 1:10,000 (abcam, cat. ab97051) or rabbit anti-mouse, dilution 1:5000 (abcam, cat. ab6728). Finally, membranes were washed three more times with PBST, visualized by ECL substrate (Westar Antares, CYANAGEN, cat. XLS1420250) and ChemiDoc imaging system (Bio-Rad).
Alexa Fluor™ 488 Tyramide SuperBoost™ staining
800,000 cells were seeded on 18 mm coverslips in a 6-well plate. After 24 h incubation cells were rinsed two times with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, washed three times with PBS and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min. Slides were blocked with 10% goat serum for 1 h. Protein staining was performed following the manufacturer protocol using the reagents included in Tyramide SuperBoost
TM
Kit with Alexa Fluor
TM
488 (Invitrogen, cat.
B40941
). Briefly, endogenous peroxidase activity was inhibited by incubation with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 1 h at RT. Slides were washed and rinsed three times with PBS at RT, 5 min each time, then blocked for 1 h with the blocking buffer. Cells were stained for 1 h at RT with anti-PD-1 primary antibody UltraMAB™ (clone UMAB199, OriGene, cat. UM800091) diluted 1:100 in the blocking buffer and washed three times with PBS, 10 min each wash. Subsequently, samples were incubated for 7 min at RT with 100 µl of a tyramide working solution. The reaction was stopped by 10-min incubation with 100 µl of Reaction Stop Reagent, followed by three 10-min washes with PBS. Samples were mounted using ProLong™ Diamond Antifade Mountant with DAPI (Invitrogen, cat.
P36966
) and left overnight protected from light to dry. Specimens were stored at 4 °C until imaging, which was performed with Olympus Fluoview FV3000 confocal laser scanning microscope using 60X oil immersion lens. All images were acquired using the same settings for laser power, voltage, and gain.
Flow cytometry
1,000,000 cells were aliquoted into flow cytometry tubes, centrifuged for 5 min at 1500 rpm, washed with PBS and centrifuged for 5 min at 1500 rpm. Cells were stained for the surface expression of PD-1 with 5 µl of PE-Cy7 conjugated anti-PD-1 antibody (clone MIH4, Invitrogen, cat. 25–9969-42) for 30 min at RT, protected from light and washed two times with PBS. Cells were fixed-permeabilized for 30 min using eBioscience™ Foxp3/Transcription Factor Fixation/Permeabilization Concentrate and Diluent (Invitrogen, cat. 00–5521-00) and washed with Perm-Wash working solution. Intracellular PD-1 protein staining was performed with 5 µl of APC conjugated anti-PD-1 antibody (clone MIH4, Invitrogen, cat. 17–9969-42) for 30 min at RT, protected from light. Specimens were washed twice with Perm-Wash working solution by 5-min centrifugation at 1500 rpm. Cell pellets were resuspended in 500 µl of PBS and analyzed with BD FACSAria II cell sorter (BD Biosciences). Results were analyzed with FlowJo v10.8.1 flow cytometry analysis software (BD Biosciences). PD-1 expression bar chart and statistical analysis were performed in GraphPad Prism software, version 9.1.1.
Pull-down assay
For one biological replicate, the cells were directly lysed on two 80% confluent 15 cm cell culture dishes using 500 µl of ice-cold Pierce IP lysis buffer per plate (Thermo Scientific, cat. 87787) containing protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, cat. 11697498001). Cell lysates were incubated on ice for 25 min applying gentle swinging and centrifuged for 20 min at 14,000 x
g
at 4 °C. Total protein concentration in the supernatant was measured using Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific, cat. 23227) and 2 mg of total protein lysate was used for the pull-down experiment. Following the manufacturer protocol, 60 µl of 5% suspension of MagStrep “type3” XT magnetic beads (IBA-Lifesciences, cat. 2–1613-002) were equilibrated in the wash buffer (IBA-Lifesciences, cat. 2–1003-100) and added to the cell lysate. Samples were incubated for 1 h at 4 °C with gentle rotation. Lysates were placed in a magnetic separator, PD-1-bound proteins were washed three times and eluted in 75 µl of 1X reducing Laemmli buffer by boiling for 2 min at 95 °C. Eluted samples were further used for LC–MS/MS or western blot analysis.
Liquid chromatography – mass spectrometry analysis
Peptide generation through FASP
Proteins obtained through pull-down were processed into peptides following a modified filter-aided sample preparation (FASP) protocol described by Wisniewski et al. (2009) [
53
]. In brief, 200 µl of urea buffer and protein lysates were loaded onto Microcon Ultracel-10 columns (Millipore, cat. MRCPRT010), mixed and centrifuged at 20 °C, for 30 min at 17,000 x
g
(17,000 x
g
was applied in all centrifugation steps). Protein reduction was performed using 100 µl of urea buffer and 20 µl of 100 mM Tris (2-carboxyethyl) phosphine hydrochloride. Samples were incubated for 30 min at 37 °C with agitation at 600 rpm and centrifuged for 20 min. For protein alkylation, 100 µl of urea buffer and 20 µl of 300 mM iodoacetamide were applied and mixed for 1 min at 20 °C, 600 rpm. Samples were incubated for 20 min at room temperature, protected from light, and centrifuged for 20 min. Columns were washed three times with 100 µl of urea buffer, vortexed and centrifuged for 30 min. Three more washes were performed with 100 µl of 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate followed by 20 min centrifugation. Proteins were digested with 3 µl of 1 µg/µl trypsin in 100 µl of 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Peptides were eluted into fresh collecting tubes by 15 min centrifugation, then additionally with 50 µl of 0.5 M NaCl.
Peptide desalting
MicroSpin C18 columns (Harvard Apparatus, cat. 744601) were used to desalt tryptic peptides following a modified protocol described by Bouchal et al. [
82
]. Briefly, columns were conditioned three times with 200 µl of ACN (acetonitrile) with 0.1% FA (formic acid), centrifuged for 2 min at 200 rpm, equilibrated with 200 µl of water/0.1% FA and centrifuged for 2 min at 300 rpm. The slurry was hydrated with 200 µl of water/0.1% FA for 15 min and centrifuged for 2 min at 300 rpm. Peptides were transferred onto columns and centrifuged for 2 min at 500 rpm, washed three times with 200 µl of water/0.1% FA and centrifuged for 2 min at 500 rpm. Peptides were eluted with 200 µl of 50% ACN/0.1% FA, then twice with 80% ACN/0.1% FA, all centrifugation steps were performed for 2 min at 500 rpm. Specimens were dried by vacuum centrifugation using the Eppendorf Concentrator Plus system (Eppendorf) for approx. 3 h at 30 °C in VC-AQ mode and stored at -80 °C until analyzed.
Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) analysis
Dried samples were dissolved in 70 µl of loading buffer (0.08% (v/v) trifluoroacetic acid (TFA), 2.5% (v/v) ACN in water), vortexed and sonicated for 5 min. A mix of iRT (indexed Retention Time) calibration peptides (Biognosys, cat. Ki-3002–1) was added to each sample. Autosampler was set to inject 6 µl (approx. 1 µg) of peptides into the liquid chromatography (LC) system and separated using UltiMateTM 3000 RSLCnano System (ThermoFisher Scientific) coupled with Orbitrap ExplorisTM 480 mass spectrometer (ThermoFisher Scientific). Peptides were trapped and concentrated on PepMap™ Neo Trap C18 cartridge (ThermoFisher Scientific, cat. 174500), then separated on Acclaim™ PepMap™ RSLC 100 C18 reverse phase analytical column (ThermoFisher Scientific, cat. 164941) at 300 nl/min flow rate. Column was equilibrated for 10 min with 2.5 to 97.5 ratio of solvent B (0.1% (v/v) TFA in ACN) to A (0.1% (v/v) TFA in water). For peptide separation, a 90-min gradient increase of solution B from 2.5% to 40% was used, followed by a 2-min gradient increase of solvent B, reaching 99%. The column was equilibrated after each run with 2.5% solvent B for 8 min. Peptides were ionized with Nanospray Flex Ion Source and analyzed by mass spectrometer operating in a high voltage (2500 V) resolution positive ion mode, ion transfer tube temp. set to 250 °C. Data were acquired in DDA and DIA modes. For the MS1 in DDA mode, the Orbitrap resolution was set to 120,000, scan range 350-1200 m/z, AGC (automatic gain control) in custom mode, normalized AGC target of 300% and max. injection time in auto mode. To prevent repeated analysis of the same precursor ion, dynamic exclusion mode was set as custom, exclusion was made after one time and 20 s duration time, mass tolerance 10 ppm. Precursor ion selection for higher-energy C-trap dissociation (HCD) and MS2 scan was based on the precursor charge state set between + 2 and + 6, intensity threshold higher than 5.0e3. MS2 was set to perform 15 fragmentation scans, m/z isolation window was set as 2, HCD collision energy 30% with max. injection time of 40 ms and Orbitrap resolution of 15,000. In DIA mode, the full-scan resolution was 60,000, m/z scan range between 350 and 1450, AGC in custom mode, AGC target was set to 300% and max. injection time 100 ms. In the DIA scan parameters m/z precursor mass range was set between 350 and 1100, DIA window type to auto and m/z range mode. M/z isolation window was set to 12 and m/z window overlap to 1, HCD collision energy was 30% and in normalized mode, Orbitrap resolution 3000, AGC in custom mode and normalized AGC target set to 1000%. Max. injection time and scan range were in auto mode, microscan was set to 1.
Spectral library and MS data analysis
The raw DDA Mass Spectrometry data were searched with FragPipe (v15.0) against a human proteome database downloaded from UniProt (closed search). The database was accessed on October 14, 2021 and encompassed a total number of 78,120 entries, comprising 20,371 SwissProt reviewed entries and 57,749 TrEMBL unreviewed entries. Sequences for the recombinant PD-1 protein and iRT peptides (Biognosys, cat. Ki-3002–1) were manually incorporated into the database. Equal numbers of reverse and common contaminant protein sequences were generated in FragPipe and added to the search database. In the search settings, protein digestion was specified to trypsin enzymatic cleavage. Precursor mass tolerance was set to ± 8 ppm and fragment mass tolerance to 10 ppm. Fixed modifications comprised carbamidomethylation of cysteine residues and variable modifications included methionine oxidization and N-terminal acetylation. Trans Proteomic-Pipeline (TPP 6.0.0) [
83
] tools including PeptideProphet [
84
] and iProphet [
85
] were utilized for further analysis of the pep.XML search files and recalculation of their iprobabilities. Further analysis was performed in Skyline-daily (64-bit, 20.1.9.234) where the recalculated iprobability files were used to build a spectral library stored in .blib format. The iprobability cut-off score was set to 0.99 and the library used in the previous step for FragPipe search was used to match the remaining peptides to their precursors. Peptides matching multiple proteins were excluded from the spectral library. The spectral library was required to extract peptide intensities from DIA files and perform the quantitative analysis utilizing tryptic peptides of length between 2 to 60 amino acids. Peptide transition settings included precursor charges from + 1 to + 5, ion types y and b; and the product ion charges + 1 or + 2. Ion mass tolerance was set to 0.05 m/z, and only the peptides with minimum 3 transition product ions were considered for the analysis. If multiple product ions were detected, the 6 most intense were chosen for the quantitation. In the full-scan settings, DIA was set as the acquisition method and Orbitrap as the mass analyzer with resolving power 30,000 at 200 m/z. Retention time filter was set to 7 min of MS/MS IDs. The target transitions were refined by adding the corresponding number of reverse decoy transitions. Raw DIA data were imported into Skyline-daily, and both the target peptides and decoys were used to train the mProphet scoring mode to refine the peak group boundaries and obtain their mProphet q values [
86
]. Results were exported including all dependencies so they could be used for further analysis [
87
]. For validation, only the proteins with the adjusted
p
-value < 0.05 and less than 75% of missing values were considered. Then, a complete absence of the interacting candidate in the control conditions or at least a threefold increase (expressed as fold change) in protein abundance was required compared to the control to effectively filter out the unspecific binders.
Statistical analysis and data visualization
Data exported from Skyline-daily were processed in MSstats 4.0.1. R package (version 2022.12.0). The data were filtered out for any truncated or false positive peak groups with q-value > 0.01 (FDR < 1%), annotated, and peak group intensities were transformed to log2 scale and quantile normalized. Further analysis was performed using summed protein intensities. MSstats group comparison function of mixed-effect models was implemented for the pairwise relative comparison of protein abundance across all experimental conditions. The adjustment of p-values was performed with the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Custom Python 3.9.13 scripts were employed to annotate protein abundance comparisons. Unique and overlapping proteins significantly changed in the experiments were plotted with Deep-Venn diagrams [
88
]. The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE [
89
] partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD050950.
Proximity ligation assay
800,000 cells were seeded on 10 mm diameter round cover slips in 6-well plates 24 h before the experiment. Molecular modeling and molecular dynamics studies
Sequence retrieval, robetta and protein structure modeling
Due to the lack of the full crystal structures of PD-1 and AXL proteins in Protein Data Bank (PDB), the structure modeling approach had to be implemented to predict protein structures. The PDB crystal structure of the extracellular domain of PD-1 at 1.70 Å resolution was downloaded from PDB, ID: 6K0Y, while input sequences for the intracellular domain of PD-1 (PD-1-ICD), the extracellular domain of AXL (AXL-ECD) and the intracellular domain of AXL (AXL-ICD) were obtained from UniProt database. The UniProt IDs for the retrieved sequences were as follows:
Q15116
for PD-1-ICD,
P30530
for AXL-ECD and AXL-ICD. Subsequently, the structural models for AXL-ECD, AXL-ICD, and PD-1-ICD were generated with the ab-initio modeling approach using the ROBETTA Baker server. Specifically, the RoseTTAFold method was implemented, which is the default option provided by the server and is based on deep learning techniques [
90
]. RoseTTAFold method demonstrates superior performance in protein structure modeling compared to other available methods on the ROBETTA Baker server (
http://robetta.bakerlab.org
) [
90
]. Therefore, the RoseTTAFold method allowed us to obtain accurate and reliable structural models for AXL-ECD, AXL-ICD, and PD-1-ICD, despite the unavailability of their full crystal structures (Supplementary Fig. 4).
Macromolecular docking using HDOCK
The macromolecular docking of protein structures and prediction of their binding complexes and binding affinities was performed using HDOCK online server (
http://hdock.phys.hust.edu.cn/
)
for blind docking (
http://hdock.phys.hust.edu.cn/
). HDOCK predicts the intermolecular interactions at the interface of two proteins in binding complexes through a hybrid algorithm [
90
]. It generated ten top binding complexes along with their corresponding binding affinity scores. Those results were used to assess the potential interactions and binding strengths between AXL and PD-1, specifically the extracellular domain (ECD) and intracellular domain (ICD) of both receptors. The resulting protein–protein complexes were downloaded and subjected to analysis of their intermolecular interaction patterns using the Schrodinger’s Maestro software.
Long-scale full atomistic MD simulation
D. E. Shaw's Desmond software was utilized to perform all-atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulations lasting 2 µs [
91
]. The goal of these simulations was to investigate the binding potency or strength of the programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) intracellular domain (ICD) and extracellular domain (ECD) with the AXL intracellular domain (ICD) and extracellular domain (ECD). To create the simulation systems, all the complexes were placed in a solvated cubic box with dimensions of 10 Å, and periodic boundary conditions were applied. For solvation, the TIP3P water model was employed. Additionally, the total charges of the system were neutralized by including counter ions. Before the MD simulation, an energy minimization step was conducted using the steepest descent method while employing the OPLS 2005 force field. During the initial 400 ps of the simulation, all systems underwent linear heating in the NVT ensemble, gradually increasing the temperature from 0 to 300 K, where N denotes the number of atoms, V represents the volume, and T signifies the temperature. The system was then equilibrated using an NPT ensemble, operating at a pressure of 1 bar and a temperature of 300 K. To control the system's temperature, the Noose-Hoover chain thermostat method was employed, while the Martyna-Tobias-Klein barostat method with an isotropic coupling style was utilized to regulate the system's pressure. The equations of motion were integrated using the leap-frog approach with a time step of 2 fs. Additionally, a cutoff radius of 9.0 Å was set for the coulomb short-range interactions [
92
]. During the 2 µs of MD simulations, a total of 20,000 frames were captured for each of the complexes. Following the production run, trajectory analysis was conducted to evaluate the structural changes that occurred during the simulation. The analysis involved calculating the Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD).
