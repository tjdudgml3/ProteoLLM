Plain English Summary
Raloxifene is a breast cancer and osteoporosis drug used in postmenopausal women. Raloxifene is highly metabolized after absorption from the intestine, and the amounts of raloxifene and raloxifene metabolites in the blood vary markedly between individuals. Here, we studied the involvement of gut microbes and drug transporters in the movement of raloxifene in the body. We identified five transporters that move raloxifene metabolites across cell membranes in the liver and the intestine. Additionally, we showed that raloxifene metabolism is reversed by intestinal microbes at different rates between individuals. These results help us better understand the observed individual differences in raloxifene treatment.
1
INTRODUCTION AND BACKGROUND
Raloxifene is a second‐generation selective oestrogen receptor modulator (SERM) indicated for the treatment and prevention of osteoporosis in postmenopausal women.
1
Acting as an oestrogen receptor (ER) agonist in the bone, raloxifene decreases bone resorption and bone turnover, increases bone mineral density, and consequently decreases the incidence of vertebral fractures.
2
,
3
Raloxifene can also be used to reduce breast cancer risk in postmenopausal women at high risk for invasive breast cancer.
1
In the uterine and breast tissues, raloxifene is an ER antagonist and while it is not as effective in long‐term breast cancer risk reduction as tamoxifen, a SERM also used for the treatment of breast cancer, it is associated with a lower incidence of thromboembolic events, uterine hyperplasia and invasive uterine cancer.
4
,
5
The pharmacokinetics of raloxifene display high intra‐ and interindividual variability.
6
Raloxifene is extensively metabolized into three glucuronide conjugates, raloxifene 4′‐glucuronide, raloxifene 6‐glucuronide and raloxifene 4′,6‐diglucuronide (Figure
1
), and the unconjugated raloxifene comprises less than 1% of total raloxifene in plasma.
6
,
8
,
9
Because of the high degree of glucuronidation and the need for active or facilitated transport mechanisms for the disposition of glucuronide metabolites, genetic variability in transporter and metabolic enzyme genes have been investigated as underlying causes for the interindividual variability in raloxifene pharmacokinetics.
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
While the uridine 5′‐diphospho‐glucuronosyltransferase (UDP‐glucuronosyltransferase, UGT) enzyme isoforms responsible for raloxifene metabolism in the intestine (UGT1A1, 1A8, 1A9 and 1A10) and the liver (UGT1A1 and 1A9) have been characterized, there is still a lack of direct in vitro evidence for specific transporter involvement in the disposition of raloxifene and its glucuronides despite extensive previous research.
7
,
8
,
9
,
13
,

,

,
16
Regardless, pharmacokinetic studies have suggested a role for p‐glycoprotein (P‐gp, encoded by
ABCB1
) and organic anion transporting polypeptide 1B1 (OATP1B1, encoded by
SLCO1B1
) in the efflux and uptake of raloxifene species across cell membranes, respectively.
8
,
9
In these studies, the
ABCB1
c.3435C > T (rs1045642) and
SLCO1B1
c.388A > G (rs2306283) single nucleotide variations were associated with significant changes in pharmacodynamic parameters used to assess drug efficacy in osteoporosis treatment, which further highlights the importance of understanding transporter involvement in raloxifene disposition.

Structures of raloxifene and its glucuronide metabolites. Uridine 5′‐diphospho‐glucuronosyltransferase (UGT) 1A10 has been reported to form raloxifene 4′‐glucuronide, whereas UGT1A1, UGT1A8 and UGT1A9 produce both monoglucuronides.
7
The UGT enzyme isoforms responsible for raloxifene 4′,6‐diglucuronide formation have not been identified.
Raloxifene species undergo extensive biliary excretion with the majority of the dose excreted as unchanged drug in faeces and less than 0.2% and 6% excreted in urine as unchanged drug and glucuronide metabolites, respectively.
6
This biliary excretion enables the enterohepatic recycling of raloxifene, in which the drug is excreted via bile into the intestine and reabsorbed into the systemic circulation; consequently, prolonged elimination half‐life and secondary plasma concentration peaks of raloxifene are observed.
6
,

,

,

,
20
It is still unclear, however, which transporters facilitate the biliary excretion of raloxifene glucuronides in the human liver due to the previous studies relying on transporter inhibition, ATPase assays and genetic variant analyses of clinical data, although multidrug resistance‐associated protein 2 (MRP2, encoded by
ABCC2
) and breast cancer resistance protein (BCRP, encoded by
ABCG2
) are the most likely candidates based on previous literature.
8
,
16
,
21
Furthermore, many strains of intestinal bacteria produce β‐glucuronidase enzymes capable of cleaving glucuronide metabolites, which further promotes the enterohepatic recycling of the parent drug.
22
To our knowledge, however, no quantitative reports on the deglucuronidation of raloxifene glucuronides by human faecal bacteria have been published despite it playing an important role in the enterohepatic recycling of raloxifene. The deglucuronidation of raloxifene glucuronides may also represent an additional source of interindividual variability; for example, the release rate of SN‐38 (the active metabolite of irinotecan) from its glucuronide conjugate has been shown to be variable by 4‐fold in human faecal extracts.
23
To reliably predict and understand how individual factors affect drug pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics, it is essential to know the distinct transporter and metabolic enzyme isoforms involved in drug disposition. To this end, we first set out to identify efflux and uptake transporters involved in the disposition raloxifene and its metabolites by studying their interactions with BCRP, MRP2, MRP3 (
ABCC3
) and MRP4 (
ABCC4
) and OATP1B1, OATP1B3 (
SLCO1B3
) and OATP2B1 (
SLCO2B1
). We then determined the relative contribution of the studied transporters to the disposition of raloxifene species in the liver and the intestine. Finally, we measured the deglucuronidation of raloxifene glucuronides by faecal extracts to elaborate on the role of the gut microbiota in the variability of raloxifene pharmacokinetics and its enterohepatic recycling.

MATERIALS & METHODS
The study was conducted in accordance with the Basic & Clinical Pharmacology & Toxicology policy for experimental and clinical studies.
24
2.1
Materials
Raloxifene 4′‐glucuronide, raloxifene 6‐glucuronide and raloxifene 4′,6‐diglucuronide were purchased from Synthose (Vaughan, ON, Canada) and Toronto Research Chemicals (Toronto, ON, Canada). Raloxifene was from Sigma‐Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) and Toronto Research Chemicals, and raloxifene‐d4, raloxifene‐d4 4′‐glucuronide and raloxifene‐d4 6‐glucuronide were from Toronto Research Chemicals. Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), foetal bovine serum (FBS), Hank's Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) and the Pierce Bradford Protein Assay kit were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). Poly‐D‐lysine (PDL) hydrobromide (70 000–150 000 Da) used to coat well plates for cellular uptake assays was purchased either as a dry substance from Sigma‐Aldrich and dissolved in water in‐house or purchased as a ready‐made solution from Thermo Fisher Scientific. All other chemicals were from Sigma‐Aldrich unless otherwise stated. All water used was purified with a Milli‐Q purification system and filtered through a 0.22 μm filter (Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany).
2.2
Vesicular transport assay
The production of inverted membrane vesicles from
Spodoptera frugiperda
(Sf9) cells transfected to express MRP2, MRP3, MRP4, BCRP or MRP3def (an MRP3 mutant with impaired transport activity used as control
25
) as well as the cholesterol‐loading of BCRP and MRP3def vesicles were conducted as earlier described.
26
,
27
Likewise, the vesicular transport assays were carried out essentially as previously.
26
,
27
The probe substrates, incubation times and control inhibitors used in inhibition assays are presented in Table
S1
. Briefly, vesicles (50 μg of total protein per reaction), substrates and possible inhibitors were diluted in assay buffer (40mM MOPS‐Tris pH 7.0, 60mM KCl and 6mM MgCl
2
), added into a standard polystyrene 96‐well plate (Sarstedt, Nümbrecht, Germany) and incubated on a heated (+37°C) orbital shaker for 10 min. Transport was then initiated by the addition of Mg‐ATP at a final concentration of 4mM, whereas the addition of assay buffer or Mg‐AMP at a final concentration of 4mM served as the negative control. The final assay concentration of DMSO in vesicular experiments did not exceed 1.5%. After incubation at +37°C, transport was stopped by the addition of 200 μl of ice‐cold washing buffer (40mM MOPS‐Tris pH 7.0 and 70mM KCl) followed by filtration through a MultiScreen
HTS
FB 1.0/0.65 μm filter plate (MSFBN6B50, Merck Millipore) and five washes with 200 μl of ice‐cold washing buffer on a MultiScreen
HTS
vacuum manifold (Merck Millipore). After the filters were dried, the samples were eluted at room temperature (RT) by incubation with either 0.1 M NaOH (BCRP, MRP2 and MRP3 inhibition samples) for 10 min, 3:1 methanol: water with 0.1% formic acid (MRP4 inhibition samples) for 30 min, or 3:1 methanol: water with 30 ng/ml raloxifene‐d4, 15 ng/ml raloxifene‐d4 4′‐glucuronide and 15 ng/ml raloxifene‐d4 6‐glucuronide (raloxifene transport samples) for 30 min. The final eluates were then collected from the filter plate into a polystyrene 96‐well plate (Sarstedt) by centrifugation at 2750 × g for 2 min. Raloxifene samples were analysed with LC–MS/MS (for details see section
2.6
), whereas the detection methods of the probe substrates used in inhibition assays are described in Table
S1
.

Cellular transport assay
Baculoviruses carrying the wild‐type
SLCO1B1

SLCO1B3

Human Embryonic Kidney 293 (HEK293) cells were cultured in high‐glucose DMEM supplemented with 1 X GlutaMAX, 25mM HEPES and 10% FBS at standard cell culture conditions of +37°C and 5% CO
2
. For the cellular transport experiments, the cells were seeded at 50000 cells/well into PDL‐coated Nunc Cell‐Culture Treated 48‐well plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and incubated overnight. Culture medium was then replaced with fresh medium containing 5mM sodium butyrate and 30 μl baculovirus/well. The transport assay was performed 48 hours after transduction as previously described,
28
,
29
and details regarding the inhibition assay conditions are presented in Table
S1

3

Uptake was then initiated by removing the buffer and adding the substrate solution prepared in TP buffer with or without inhibitors. After the designated incubation time at +37°C, the transport was stopped by aspirating the substrate solution and washing the cells three times with 500 μl of ice‐cold TP buffer. After the last wash, the buffer was aspirated, the plates dried and the samples were treated at RT with either 0.1 M NaOH (inhibition assay samples) for 10 min, or 3:1 methanol: water with 30 ng/ml raloxifene‐d4, 15 ng/ml raloxifene‐d4 4′‐glucuronide and 15 ng/ml raloxifene‐d4 6‐glucuronide (raloxifene transport samples) for 30 min. The inhibition assay samples were moved into a polystyrene 96‐well plate (Sarstedt) and analysed as described in Table
S1
. The raloxifene samples were centrifuged at 14000 × g for 10 min at RT, and the supernatants analysed with LC–MS/MS (see section
2.6
for details). After the quantification of the transported substrate, uptake was standardized to the total protein amount measured with the Pierce Bradford Protein Assay kit using bovine serum albumin (BSA) as a reference standard.
2.4
Human faecal samples
To assess the deglucuronidation of raloxifene glucuronides by the human gut microbiota, faecal samples were collected from eight subjects (two male, six female) with a median age of 28 years (range 26–63). At the time of sample collection, all participants were in self‐reported good state of health and had no history of diagnosed intestinal disease. All participants reported a mixed diet of meat, dairy and vegetables, and the median body mass index (kg/m
2
) of the participants was 24.0 (range 21.8–28.3). The samples were self‐collected by the research subjects, stored at +4°C, if necessary, and frozen at −80°C within 24 hours after collection. The collection and analysis of faecal samples were approved by the ethics committee of the Helsinki University Hospital (HUS/12203/2022) and written informed consent was received from all participants.
2.5
Deglucuronidation assay
Deglucuronidation assays were performed using both pooled and individual faecal lysates prepared as described in the Supplementary
Materials & Methods
. For deglucuronidation experiments with pooled faecal lysate, lysates prepared from faecal samples of five subjects were combined at equal parts. Interindividual variability in deglucuronidation was assessed using the individual lysates of eight subjects. The deglucuronidation assay was performed on conical‐bottom polystyrene 96‐well plates (Greiner Bio‐One, Kremsmünster, Austria). First, 10 μl of 0.5 mg/ml faecal lysate was added to the well plate and kept on ice. The substrates were diluted with reaction buffer (25mM HEPES, 25mM NaCl, pH 6.5) to 1.25 X concentration. The lysate and substrate solutions were then preincubated at +37°C for 10 min, after which the reaction was initiated by adding 40 μl of the substrate solution to the lysate. The final assay concentrations were 0.1 mg/ml total protein for the faecal lysate, 1 X for the substrate (varying concentrations) and a maximum of 0.5% for DMSO. The plate was then incubated at +37°C on an orbital plate shaker with brief 500 rpm shaking. After the designated incubation time (3 min for raloxifene 4′‐glucuronide and 6‐glucuronide and 5 min for raloxifene 4′,6‐diglucuronide), the reaction was stopped by the addition of 100 μl of ice‐cold acetonitrile. The samples were then vortexed at full speed for 20 seconds, incubated at RT for 10 minutes and centrifuged at 17000 × g for 10 min at RT. The resulting supernatants were diluted with an equal volume of water and raloxifene was measured with ultra‐performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) (see section
2.6
for details).
2.6
Analytical methods
Quantification of raloxifene from the deglucuronidation assay samples was performed with an Acquity UPLC system from Waters (Milford, MA, USA) equipped with a photodiode array detector and a Luna Omega Polar C18 (1.6 μm, 2.1 × 50 mm) column from Phenomenex (Torrance, CA, USA). The column was kept at a temperature of 30°C and operated at a flow rate of 0.5 ml/min. Samples, kept at RT, were injected into the column at 10 μl using the full loop mode, and the needle was washed with 20% acetonitrile and 1:1 acetonitrile: methanol between each injection. The chromatographic eluents were 15mM KH
2
PO
4
at pH 2 (A) and acetonitrile (B), and the gradient was 0–5 min 20→80% B, 5–5.01 min 80→20% B and 5.01–6 min 20% B. Raloxifene was detected by measuring absorbance at 223 and 285 nm. The retention time of raloxifene was 1.60 min.
Quantification of raloxifene and its glucuronides from the transport assay samples was performed using the Sciex 6500 QTRAP with Nexera X2 liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry system (AB Sciex, Toronto, ON, Canada). The chromatographic separation of the analytes was achieved on an Atlantis T3 column (3 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm + 2.1 × 10 mm) from Waters using a gradient elution with 0.02% formic acid as mobile phase A and acetonitrile as mobile phase B at a 0.4 ml/min flow rate. The linear gradient of the mobile phase B started at 10% and was increased to 34% over 5.2 minutes followed by a column wash at 90% from 5.3 to 7 minutes and balancing at 10% from 7.1 to 9.5 minutes before the next injection. The mass spectrometer was operated in positive ionization mode (ESI+) and the multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) transitions [M+H]+ of the analytes were m/z 474/112.1 for raloxifene, m/z 650/473.9 for both raloxifene 4‐glucuronide and raloxifene 6‐glucuronide and m/z 825.9/473.9 for raloxifene 4,6‐diglucuronide. Monitored transitions for the deuterium‐labelled internal standards were m/z 477.9/116.2 for raloxifene‐d4, and m/z 654/478.2 for both labelled monoglucuronides, raloxifene‐d4 4‐glucuronide and raloxifene‐d4 6‐glucuronide. Data were analysed using the Analyst software version 1.6.3 (AB Sciex) using a peak area ratio between the analyte and corresponding deuterium‐labelled molecules, except for raloxifene 4,6‐diglucuronide, on which raloxifene‐d4 6‐glucuronide was used. The concentrations of the quality control samples were 8, 80 and 3000 nM for all the analytes. The inter‐day (n = 5) precisions (%CV) were between 4.3 and 13.1% and the accuracies were between 85% and 115% for all except one 8 nM raloxifene quality control sample for which the value was 79%.
The abundance of MRP2–4 and BCRP in Sf9 membrane vesicles and OATP1B1, OATP1B3 and OATP2B1 in HEK293 whole‐cell lysates (prepared as described in the Supplementary
Materials & Methods
) were quantified using a validated LC–MS/MS targeted proteomics approach using stable isotope labelled internal standards.
30
Absolute transporter protein quantification using proteospecific peptides (see Table
S3
) of BCRP, MRP2–4, OATP1B1, OATP1B3 and OATP2B1 as well as the reference protein Na/K‐ATPase (LSLDELHR) was conducted on a 7500 QTRAP triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (AB Sciex, Darmstadt, Germany) coupled to an Agilent Technologies 1290 Infinity system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). For each peptide, four mass transitions were monitored and the absolute protein abundance was assessed by using the stable isotope method. Details of the used chromatographic and mass spectrometric methods have been previously described.
30
To ensure comparability between the samples, the respective protein concentration was normalized to the individual sample's total protein content (pmol/mg protein).
2.7
Data analysis
All assays were conducted in at least triplicate samples, and the transport inhibition and deglucuronidation variability data were composed of three independent experiments. In the cellular transport inhibition assays, the solvent controls consisted of six replicates. Data from both single experiments as well as data compiled from independent experiments were presented as individual data points to visualize intra‐ and inter‐assay variability.
Vesicular transport experiments were performed in the presence of ATP and in the absence of ATP (inhibition assays) or the presence of AMP (transport assays). Cellular transport experiments were performed in OATP‐expressing cells as well as cells expressing eYFP (control cells). The transport rate was calculated as the amount of substrate transported into vesicles or cells (measured in moles or arbitrary fluorescence units) per incubation time (min) per total protein in the reaction (mg). The ATP‐dependent transport rate was calculated as the difference between the transport rates in the presence and absence of ATP. The OATP‐dependent transport rate was calculated as the difference between the transport rates in OATP‐ and eYFP‐expressing cells. The relative transport activity in the inhibition assays was determined as the solvent control‐normalized ATP‐ or OATP‐dependent transport of the probe substrate. In transport studies, a transport ratio (TR; expressed as fold over AMP or eYFP control) of ≥ 2.5 at both studied concentrations was set as a threshold for substrate identification.
The data were analysed and visualized using GraphPad Prism version 6.07 (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). Michaelis–Menten parameters and transporter protein abundance‐scaled clearances were calculated as described in Supplementary
Materials & Methods
. The coefficient of variation (CV), defined as the ratio of the standard deviation to the mean and expressed in percentage, was calculated to assess intersubject variability in the deglucuronidation of raloxifene glucuronides. Additionally, the fold variability was calculated as the ratio between the highest and lowest individual deglucuronidation rates.
3
RESULTS
3.1
Inhibition of efflux and uptake transporters by raloxifene species
Inhibition of BCRP, MRP2–4, OATP1B1, OATP1B3 and OATP2B1 transport was studied to identify interactions between raloxifene species and membrane transporters commonly involved in the enterohepatic recycling of glucuronidated drugs. At the studied concentration of 10 μM, most changes in the relative transport activity were minor (Figure
2
). The parent drug raloxifene appeared to decrease BCRP and MRP4 transport to approximately 50 and 40% of control, respectively, whereas raloxifene 4′‐glucuronide inhibited OATP1B3 to 50%. Out of all transporter‐inhibitor pairs tested, however, only MRP3 inhibition by raloxifene 4′‐glucuronide and raloxifene 6‐glucuronide was > 75%. Incubation with the control inhibitors benzbromarone (100 μM for MRP2 and MRP4, and 200 μM for MRP3), sulfasalazine (50 μM for BCRP and OATP2B1) and cyclosporine A (10 μM for OATP1B1 and OATP1B3) produced complete or near‐complete inhibition of their respective transporters (Figure
S1
).

3.2
Transport of raloxifene species by efflux and uptake transporters
Next, vesicular and cellular transport assays were conducted to examine the transport of raloxifene species by BCRP, MRP2–4, OATP1B1, OATP1B3 and OATP2B1 at two concentrations (Figures

and
4

However, in all vesicular transport samples, ≥ 50% of the raloxifene added to the reaction was recovered in the eluate. In contrast, both raloxifene 4′‐glucuronide and raloxifene 6‐glucuronide were identified as substrates of MRP2, MRP3, OATP1B1, OATP1B3 and OATP2B1 with TR values exceeding 2.5 at both studied concentrations of 1 μM and 10 μM (Figures

Raloxifene 4′,6‐diglucuronide was also identified as a substrate of MRP2 and MRP3; additionally, it appeared to be transported by MRP4, although only at the lower concentration of 2.5 μM (TR = 2.8 ± 0.5). Transport into control vesicles was mostly negligible with transport ratios ≤ 2.5, except for 2.5 μM raloxifene 4′,6‐diglucuronide (TR = 3.7 ± 0.1) and 10 μM raloxifene 6‐glucuronide (TR = 3.1 ± 0.2; cholesterol‐loaded control vesicles) (Table

DISCUSSION
In the present study, we have shown that MRP2, MRP3, OATP1B1, OATP1B3 and OATP2B1 are involved in the transport of raloxifene glucuronides. Additionally, we have characterized the deglucuronidation of raloxifene glucuronides using bacterial extracts prepared from faecal samples. We found all three raloxifene glucuronides to be transported by MRP2 and MRP3. MRP2 is expressed in the apical membrane of hepatocytes and enterocytes, where it transports its substrates to bile or the intestinal lumen.
31
The basolaterally expressed MRP3, on the other hand, transports its substrates towards blood both in the liver and the intestine. We also found raloxifene monoglucuronides to be substrates of OATP1B1 and OATP1B3, which take up their substrates into hepatocytes at the basolateral membrane, and OATP2B1, which is expressed both at the basolateral membrane of hepatocytes as well as in enterocytes.
31
All raloxifene glucuronides were cleaved back to the parent drug by faecal extracts, although there was a high degree of intersubject variability in the observed rates of deglucuronidation. Altogether, these results provide a mechanistic understanding of the pharmacokinetics and enterohepatic recycling of raloxifene, which helps identify and understand the sources behind the observed high interindividual variability in raloxifene pharmacokinetics in humans.
As expected, all raloxifene glucuronides were transported by both apically and basolaterally expressed intestinal and hepatic MRPs commonly associated with drug glucuronide disposition.
21
Although none of the raloxifene species notably inhibited MRP2, all three glucuronide metabolites were identified as MRP2 substrates in the subsequent transport assays exhibiting moderate transporter affinity (K
m
< 100 μM). Moreover, only the high‐affinity MRP3 substrates raloxifene 4′‐glucuronide and 6‐glucuronide exhibited notable inhibition of MRP3, whereas MRP3 inhibition by the lower‐affinity substrate raloxifene 4′,6‐diglucuronide was less remarkable. In an earlier study, MRP inhibitors decreased both apical and basolateral clearance of raloxifene glucuronide in Caco‐2 cells suggesting that MRP2 and MRP3 are involved in raloxifene disposition in the intestine.
16
Moreover, interactions between raloxifene glucuronides and MRP2 and MRP3 have been identified using the ATPase and transport inhibition assays, except for raloxifene 6‐glucuronide, which appeared not to interact with MRP2.
8
In this study, we were able to confirm some of the previous hypotheses on the role of MRPs in raloxifene species' disposition; however, our results simultaneously illustrate that indirect measurements of transporter activity, such as ATPase and inhibition assays, may often be insufficient for accurately determining whether a compound is a transporter substrate.
The parent drug raloxifene was not identified as a substrate of any of the efflux transporters included in this study, which may partly be explained by the incompatibility of the vesicular transport assay with lipophilic substrates.
32
Raloxifene has been previously identified as a BCRP inhibitor
33
; here, raloxifene appeared to inhibit BCRP by approximately 50% at 10 μM. Additionally, raloxifene inhibits P‐gp,
33
and transcellular permeability studies on Caco‐2 cells with P‐gp inhibitors have suggested that raloxifene is a P‐gp substrate.
8
,
16
Glucuronide conjugates of drugs, however, are unlikely P‐gp substrates due to the transporter having a negatively charged binding pocket,
34
,
35
and as raloxifene undergoes extensive glucuronidation in the intestine and the liver, P‐gp‐mediated biliary excretion of raloxifene likely has a minor role in the drug's total enterohepatic recycling. Furthermore, P‐gp inhibitors did not affect the efflux of raloxifene conjugates in Caco‐2 cells.
16
In a previous ATPase study, however, raloxifene 4′,6‐diglucuronide weakly activated ATPase in the presence of P‐gp membranes, and all raloxifene species inhibited activated P‐gp ATPase, indicating some degree of interaction with the transporter.
8
Moreover, the
ABCB1
c.3435C > T variation tended to be associated with increased plasma concentrations of all raloxifene species in a previous study, suggesting that P‐gp‐mediated raloxifene efflux affects the extent to which the drug is glucuronidated in the intestine.
8
In contrast, the
ABCC2
c.3972C > T (rs3740066) variation did not affect raloxifene species' plasma concentrations despite all raloxifene glucuronides being MRP2 substrates.
Raloxifene monoglucuronides were identified as substrates of OATP1B1, OATP1B3 and OATP2B1, whereas the 4′,6‐diglucuronide was not transported by any of these transporters. In line with previous findings,
19
raloxifene was not found to be a substrate of the studied OATPs. We also found raloxifene 4′‐glucuronide to weakly inhibit both OATP1B1 and OATP1B3; previously, OATP1B1 and OATP1B3 inhibition has been reported for raloxifene 4′‐glucuronide and 4′,6‐diglucuronide.
9
Moreover, individuals with the
SLCO1B1
c.388A > G variation have been reported to have significantly increased raloxifene, raloxifene 4′,6‐diglucuronide and total raloxifene serum concentrations.
9
Changes in the concentrations of the monoglucuronides, however, were visible but not statistically significant, despite both being identified as OATP1B1 substrates in this study. When we considered transporter abundance both in the in vitro system and the liver, we found OATP1B3 to likely have a larger role in the total liver uptake of raloxifene glucuronides than OATP1B1. OATP1B3 being the primary mediator of raloxifene monoglucuronide uptake into the liver could partly explain why the clinically significant no‐function OATP1B1 variant c.521 T > C (rs4149056)
36
did not significantly affect the plasma concentrations of raloxifene species in the previous study.
9
Regardless, when the authors analysed the plasma concentrations of individuals carrying a variant designated as
SLCO1B3
int7C > G (most likely referring to rs17680137) polymorphism, no statistically significant changes in the concentrations of the monoglucuronides were detected either. The small sample size and high interindividual variability in raloxifene pharmacokinetics, however, complicate the interpretation of these pharmacogenetic analyses.
Here, the main purpose of the transport inhibition assay was to identify transporter interactions, not clinically significant inhibitors of drug transport. We detected mostly moderate transport inhibition at 10 μM, whereas the plasma concentrations of raloxifene species in humans have been reported to be at the nanomolar range.

Based on our data, only MRP3 inhibition by raloxifene 4′‐glucuronide would warrant further investigation; for the rest, any clinically relevant transporter inhibition seems unlikely. However, raloxifene itself could be an object drug in transporter‐mediated food‐drug or drug–drug interactions leading to changes in raloxifene disposition by changing the extent to which raloxifene glucuronides undergo enterohepatic recycling. A recently published study showed that co‐administration of raloxifene with green tea decreases the AUC of raloxifene and its 4′‐ and 6‐glucuronide forms possibly through transporter inhibition.
19
As we have now established transporters partaking in the disposition of the main circulating raloxifene species, transporter‐mediated pharmacokinetic interactions with raloxifene as the object drug could be investigated in more detail.
Hydrolysis of raloxifene glucuronides by intestinal bacteria allows the reabsorption and enterohepatic recycling of raloxifene while also explaining the drug's excretion mostly as unchanged drug in faeces.
6
Previously, similar deglucuronidation rates of raloxifene monoglucuronides have been reported for β‐glucuronidase from
Helix pomatia,
7
while deglucuronidation by human gut bacterial β‐glucuronidases, to our knowledge, has not been characterized. Our results show that while all raloxifene glucuronides are deglucuronidated by gut bacterial extracts, interindividual differences in their deglucuronidation rates may serve as an additional source of variability in raloxifene pharmacokinetics. For raloxifene 4′,6‐diglucuronide, particularly, we observed notable (35‐fold) variability in the rate of deglucuronidation between the eight studied samples. Previously, the rate of SN‐38 glucuronide deglucuronidation has been reported to be variable by 4‐fold between seven samples,
23
whereas the highest rates of estrone‐3‐glucuronide and estradiol‐17‐glucuronide deglucuronidation were 19‐ and 89‐fold, respectively, compared to the lowest individual deglucuronidation rates in the analysis of faecal samples from 14 subjects.
37
Moreover, changes in the gut microbiota composition or deglucuronidation activity could also lead to intraindividual variability in raloxifene pharmacokinetics by changing the extent to which the drug undergoes enterohepatic recycling. For example, co‐administration of ampicillin reduces the peak raloxifene concentrations as well as its overall absorption by 28% and 14%, respectively, through the reduction of enteric bacteria, although systemic exposure and elimination rate remain unaffected.
1
One limitation of this study is the number of transporters evaluated. While the transporters included were chosen based on their relevance in the disposition of drug glucuronides,
21
a number of other efflux and uptake transporters could still participate in the pharmacokinetics of raloxifene and its glucuronides. For example, a possible contribution by organic anion transporter (OAT) was suggested by Jeong et al,
16
whereas Trdan Lušin et al reported interactions between raloxifene species and MRP1.
8
As recently reviewed, however, they appear to have a small role in the disposition of drug conjugates compared to OATPs and MRP2–3.
21
It should also be noted that in the scaling of clearance values, it is assumed that all quantified protein is active since the proteomic analysis cannot differentiate between inactive and active forms. Additionally, to study the deglucuronidation of raloxifene glucuronides in the gut, we collected faecal samples, which are enriched with bacteria originating from the lumen of the colon
38
and therefore unlikely to accurately represent deglucuronidation throughout the whole intestinal tract. However, faecal samples are the most abundant and readily available source of intestinal bacteria, and faecal lysates are well suited to estimate intersubject differences in the total deglucuronidation activity of the luminal gut microbiota. Finally, the test concentrations used in the experiments markedly exceed the reported plasma concentrations of raloxifene species.

However, for many of the transporters studied as well as for deglucuronidation, the relevant concentration is not the plasma concentration, but instead intracellular or gut luminal concentrations, which may well exceed plasma concentrations.
To summarize, we have shown that MRPs and OATPs together with the gut microbiota likely drive the enterohepatic recycling of raloxifene in humans. After oral administration, MRP3 may transport raloxifene glucuronides formed in the intestine into the portal vein, whereas the uptake of the monoglucuronides into hepatocytes is mediated mainly by OATP1B3 and OATP1B1. In hepatocytes, MRP3 pumps the glucuronides into the systemic circulation, while MRP2 is responsible for their biliary excretion. Back in the intestine, the glucuronides are cleaved back to raloxifene by bacterial β‐glucuronidases leading to the reabsorption of raloxifene. In light of this, we propose that individual variation in the function of these transporters as well as in the gut microbiota's deglucuronidation activity may explain some of the variability in raloxifene pharmacokinetics. To further estimate the contributions of these individual processes in humans, the protein abundance‐scaled data and information on deglucuronidation activity could be employed in physiologically based pharmacokinetic modelling. This data may also help us to better understand the pharmacokinetic variability related to other drugs undergoing enterohepatic recycling beyond raloxifene.
