Neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) is an autosomal-dominant cancer predisposition syndrome with up to 50% of patients afflicted by multiple benign peripheral nerve sheath tumors termed plexiform neurofibromas (PNs), characterized by biallelic
NF1
loss (
1
). NF1 patients with PNs have an 8 to 13% lifetime risk of malignant transformation to an aggressive soft tissue sarcoma known as malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor (MPNST) (
2
). MPNSTs harbor additional oncogenic events, which can include homozygous loss of
CDKN2A/B
,
TP53
mutations, alterations in components of the PRC2 complex (
EED
and
SUZ12),
or amplification of receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) genes. The mortality caused by MPNSTs is high, approaching 100% in patients with unresectable, metastatic, or recurrent disease, and these tumors do not respond to current anticancer therapies (
3
).
The protein encoded by the
NF1
gene, neurofibromin, is a RAS-GTPase activating protein. Homeostatic regulation of RAS protein by the Guanine nucleotide Exchange Factor/GTPase activating (GEF/GAP) cycle is disrupted in NF1 patients, resulting in elevated RAS-GTP levels and unregulated downstream RAF/MEK/ERK signaling. Preclinical studies of
Nf1
-mutant PN mouse models accurately predicted responses in clinical trials as exemplified by the success of single-agent MEK inhibitors (
4
,
5
). By contrast, several monotherapy-based clinical trials in MPNST have yielded disappointing results, likely reflecting the increased genomic complexity of these tumors (
6
).
The protein-tyrosine phosphatase SHP2 (encoded by
PTPN11
) functions as a positive signal transducer upstream of the RAS-GEF SOS1/2 and NF1 (
7
). SHP2 inhibitors block RAF/MEK/ERK pathway activation and tumor growth in RAS-mutant preclinical models of solid tumors (
8
–
11
), and several SHP2 inhibitors (SHP2i) are currently in clinical trials either as monotherapy or in combination with other agents (
12
,
13
). Given its position upstream of SOS1/2, we hypothesized that SHP2 inhibitors could more effectively decrease RAS-GTP levels and downstream signaling in
NF1
-mutant tumors. Here, we find that SHP2 inhibition can efficiently reduce RAS-GTP loading, block RAS-mediated RAF/MEK/ERK signaling, and abrogate tumor growth in NF1-MPNST. Furthermore, MPNSTs resistant to SHP2i exhibit markers of increased autophagic flux. Remarkably, combining SHP2i treatment with hydroxychloroquine (HQ), a pharmacological inhibitor of autophagy, enhances the effectiveness of SHP2 inhibition in mouse and human models of NF1-MPNST.

SHP2i Suppression of Orthotopic
NF1
-MPNST PDX.
To examine whether the SHP2i antitumor effects observed on GEMM-derived orthotopic allografts extend to the human disease, we generated

SHP2i monotherapy resulted in marked antitumor activity as illustrated by waterfall plots showing the percent change of tumor volume at day 21 relative to baseline (

To assess the durability of these antitumor effects, drug treatment was extended until tumors either reached a volume of 1,000 mm

In summary, in a majority of GEMM-derived orthotopic allograft models and PDX models tested, SHP2i monotherapy provided superior MPNST suppression and survival compared with MEKi monotherapy or combination therapy. Nevertheless, with some exceptions, SHP2i resistance and tumor regrowth eventually emerge.
In Vivo SHP2i Resistance.
Given the fidelity of the therapeutic response in MPNST GEMM-derived orthoptic allograft models to that seen with the more cumbersome PDX models, we opted to examine therapeutic resistance in the former model. A typical response to SHP2i monotherapy in
NP

), followed by a phase of

NP

V1 and

Thus, acquired resistance to SHP2i is observed only in vivo with native tumor stromal and microenvironmental components, and is not recapitulated in two-dimensional defined primary culture conditions, wherein the cells continue to exhibit SHP2i sensitivity. Whether these differential in vivo and in vitro effects are cell autonomous, microenvironmental, or combinatorial remains to be determined.

SHP2i-Resistant MPNSTs Display Elevated Autophagy Pathway Kinases.
The eventual manifestation of in vivo SHP2i resistance by MPNST could reflect specific transcriptional adaptations or alternatively, posttranslational adaptations that might affect signaling networks. To examine potential changes at the posttranslational level, we performed a kinomic analysis of fresh frozen tumor samples from drug-sensitive and drug-resistant MPNST. Tumors from mice treated for 5 d with SHP2i represent early therapeutic responses (tumor shrinkage) (
Fig. 3
A
for
NP
-mutant MPNST allografts). To identify tumors in the earliest phases of therapy resistance (late tumor stasis), tumors were selected once palpable evidence of resumed tumor growth became apparent but prior to the development of large tumors (20 to 28 d;
Fig. 3
A
for
NP
MPNST). For each time point analyzed, we selected three independent tumor-bearing animals. To identify candidate kinases associated with resistance to SHP2i (SHP099), we leveraged a chemical proteomic approach that utilizes multiplexed kinase inhibitor beads (MIBs), in which Sepharose beads are covalently coupled to broad-spectrum, Type I kinase inhibitors (
27
–
30
). MIB gravity-flow affinity chromatography, combined with mass spectrometry (MIB/MS), enables capture and identification of kinases in lysates on a kinome scale (
27
–
30
). Kinase binding to MIBs is influenced by their protein expression levels, by the affinity of a given kinase for the inhibitors used for MIB capture, and by their activity (
30
).
Snap-frozen tumor samples were subjected to MIB/MS kinome analysis, and the results are depicted as volcano plots (
SI Appendix
, Fig. S5
A
). Notably, increased MIB binding of PIK3C3 was detected in SHP2i-treated
NP
, and LRRK2 was detected in
NI
MPNSTs. Both these kinases are linked to autophagy regulation (
SI Appendix
, Fig. S5
A
and
B
). Dysregulated autophagy mediated by LRRK2 mutants has previously been linked to increased kinase activity of LRRK2. PIK3C3 (a.k.a., hVPS34; vacuolar protein sorting 34) is the only mammalian class III PI3K, and together with hVPS15 and BECLIN, catalyzes production of PI3 phosphate, a lipid important for autophagosome membrane formation (
31
). An obligate regulatory subunit of PIK3C3, PIK3R4 (a.k.a. hVPS15) was also recovered in the bead pulldowns (
SI Appendix
, Fig. S5
A
). Increased MIB binding of PIK3C3 was especially pronounced in
NP
early resistant tumors (SHP2i at 20 to 28 d) compared with vehicle-treated control tumors or tumors treated with a short course of therapy (SHP2i at 5 d;
Fig. 3
D
).
We next examined autophagy activity by IF staining for p62 which is degraded during autophagy (
32
,
33
). Consistent with autophagy induction, tumors treated for 21 to 28 d when early therapeutic resistance manifests (late
tumor stasis
), showed decreased p62 staining (relative to 5-d treated tumors) (
Fig. 3
E
for
NP
mutant MPNST allografts and;
SI Appendix
, Fig. S5
D
for
NI

,
33

We also conducted RNA-sequencing on the
NP
MPNST mouse tumor samples as well as PDX#2 tumor samples.
NP
MPNST mouse tumor samples were treated with SHP2i for 5 d (early therapeutic response) and 20 to 28 d (evolved therapeutic resistance). PDX#2 tumor samples develop therapy resistance at later time points and therefore, tumors were harvested after treatment with SHP2i for 5 d (early therapeutic response) and 40 to 50 d (evolved therapeutic resistance). We specifically focused on autophagy-related genes, consisting of the essential genes involved in autophagy initiation, nucleation, and expansion (

Differential expression and pathway enrichment analysis demonstrated that consistent with the kinomic data, essential autophagy-related gene mRNAs were significantly higher in the tumor samples treated with SHP2i for 20 to 28 d (evolved therapeutic resistance) relative to tumors treated with SHP2i for 5 d (early therapeutic response) in both

HQ Improves Antitumor Effectiveness of SHP2 Inhibition in NF1 MPNST.
We next investigated whether the observed appearance of autophagy-associated activated kinases in incipient SHP2i resistance might reflect an activity in resistance. We therefore therapeutically targeted autophagy using clinically available inhibitors. PIK3C3-IN1 is a highly potent, selective PIK3C3 inhibitor, whereas HQ, an FDA-approved drug, inhibits lysosomal acidification and suppresses autophagy by affecting autophagosome fusion and degradation (
35
). To assess the combined effect of autophagy inhibition with SHP2i, primary MPNST cultures were treated with PIK3C3-IN1 (2.5uM) or HQ (25uM) in the presence or absence of SHP2i for 4 d and cell numbers were counted (see
Materials and Methods
for details).
NP

NI

SI Appendix
, Fig. S6
A
–
C

We next examined the LC3II/I ratios in
NF1

Materials and Methods

Immunoblots of chloroquine (CQ)-treated cells (4 h of CQ treatment prior to harvesting cells), when compared to vehicle, demonstrated increased LC3-II/-LC32-I ratios, consistent with autophagy suppression owing to failure of lysosomal degradation of LC3-II. SHP099-treated cells also demonstrated increased LC3-II/-LC3-I ratios (indicative of increased LC3II generation), which were significantly accentuated upon cotreatment with chloroquine (lysosomal inhibitor), supporting the idea that SHP2 inhibition of NF1 MPNST cultures is accompanied by induced autophagic flux (
SI Appendix

We next assessed the effects of combination therapy in mice bearing

Nude mice were orthotopically implanted with five thousand MPNST cells, divided into four cohorts, and treated with vehicle (control), HQ (50 mg/kg daily), SHP2i (SHP099 50 mg/kg daily), or the combination of SHP2i plus HQ (SHP099 50 mg/kg daily and HQ 50 mg/kg daily), all of which are equivalent to human doses in the clinic (

Discussion
Genetically engineered mouse models of NF1 have exhibited remarkable fidelity in replicating the disease hallmarks (
41
,
42
). Such models have permitted otherwise impossible investigations into the origins of tumors, their interplay with the microenvironment, and more recently, prediction of clinical outcomes to PN therapies (
4
,
30
). NF1 mouse models have also contributed much insight into the origin, natural history, and genetic properties of MPNST, but no experimental therapies have yet been effective in clinical trials (
3
,
43
,
44
). We were prompted to reinvestigate RAS pathway inhibition for MPNST in the wake of recent preclinical and clinical success in the treatment of NF1 mutant PNs (
45
,
46
). The clinical impact of molecular therapies targeting the RAS/ERK pathway in other malignancies is highly variable dependent on the type of molecular alteration and/or the cellular and tissue context in which cancers arise (
47
). Certainly, in genomically complex and clinically aggressive diseases, combination with additional anticancer agents tends to increase efficacy (
47
). Given the well-established phenomenon of compensatory feedback upstream activation following RAS pathway inhibition (
16
,
48
–
52
), we reasoned that use of inhibitors upstream of RAS could perhaps mitigate such secondary activation in MPNST (
30
). Therefore, we reexamined MEKi in comparison with SHP2i in multiple NF1 MPNST mouse models. Only SHP2i monotherapy or combined therapy exhibited sustained RAS pathway inactivation and clear antitumor efficacy. We extended and validated these findings by using several recently developed orthotopic PDX models of NF1 MPNST with diverse mutational spectra. Several NF1 MPNST clinical trials are underway (
NCT03433183
) or planned (
NCT05253131
) which use a diverse array of combination therapies with MEKi as the backbone. Our results suggest that SHP2i (or blocking RAS or nodes upstream of RAS such as with SOS1 inhibitors) may provide a more effective backbone to test rational combinatorial treatments in lieu of MEKi.
As is the case following most anticancer therapies in the clinical setting, GEMM-derived orthotopic allografts and PDX tumors frequently recurred following initial SHP2i monotherapy-induced regressions (
53
,
54
). When therapy-resistant MPNSTs were placed in primary culture, they regained SHP2i sensitivity, even though when retransplanted orthotopically, the tumors remained SHP2i-resistant and expanded aggressively. Thus, in MPNST, either cancer cell-intrinsic properties reconfigure in vivo to resume therapy resistance, or possible extrinsic/nonautonomous factors including hypoxia, tumor stroma, or microenvironment cells (which here would be the myeloid, NK, or B cell compartments given that all experiments were performed in nude mice) may exert unique antitumor influences such as the secretion of a growth factor that is not present in the culture medium and that bypasses SHP2i. With a focus on adaptive intrinsic tumor cell responses, we examined transcriptional and proteomic kinome states. Concordant with transcriptional upregulation of autophagy pathway genes in early resistant tumors, the MIB/MS platform kinome analysis revealed differential activation of critical components of autophagy signaling. In the context of specific
BRAF
V600E
and RAS mutant tumor cell lines, use of RAF or MEK inhibitors elevated autophagy as an acquired resistance mechanism (
55
–
58
). In our study, cotargeting of SHP2 and the autophagy pathway using HQ enhances therapeutic efficacy in vitro and in vivo.
Our screening system for NF1-MPNST holds promise as a much-needed predictive preclinical platform. Tumor-derived primary cultures can be used to triage promising mono- or combination therapies followed by progression through orthotopic mouse tumors for rapid in vitro and in vivo screening and culminating in validation in orthotopic PDX models. As exemplified here, in vitro results do not always hold when extended to in vivo studies accentuating the need for progressive validation of results across the platforms. The use of mouse models in these studies further yielded critical insights into the tumoral mechanisms of drug resistance to allosteric SHP2 inhibition. The GEMM-derived orthotopic allografts provide an added advantage in future investigations into the potential role of the microenvironment in contributing to adaptive resistance to therapy. While here we show that tumor cell-autonomous activation of autophagy is critical for the SHP2i-resistant state, it is likely that external microenvironmental forces, including the immune and inflammatory systems, may also contribute and will require further investigation (
45
,
59
). While broad in spectrum, the kinome assay is not comprehensive and therefore may capture some kinases associated with autophagy but not all kinases.
A recent study reported that SHP099 monotherapy was only modestly effective at halting MPNST growth, while genetic
PTPN11
(encoding SHP2) knockdown was more effective (
20
). Further, the combination of MEKi plus SHP2i had better efficacy in their cell culture systems and xenografts. Several differences in our experimental approach could account for the differential outcomes. Wang et al. primarily employed long-established, high-passage MPNST cell lines maintained in RPMI or DMEM with supplements including fetal calf serum and presumably incubated in 20% O
2
(
20
). Our cell experiments exclusively employed low passage primary cultures directly derived from MPNST GEMMs or from patient resections, and maintained orthotopically in mouse SN, a common location for spontaneous MPNST. Our primary cultures are maintained in defined, serum-free medium with growth factor supplements and 5% O
2
. Multiple in vivo MPNST models demonstrated that combination of MEKi with reduced dose SHP2i on a continuous schedule was poorly tolerated (
8
) and inferior to SHP2i alone both in SN implanted GEMM-derived orthotopic allograft tumors or PDXs. Taken in aggregate, we attribute the effects seen in combination therapy primarily to the action of SHP2i. During the preparation of this manuscript, a study reported that combined SHP2 inhibition and cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK4/6) inhibition provide antitumor efficacy in subcutaneous PDX models. The results of early-phase clinical trials assessing the safety and toxicity of combined SHP2i and MEKi or CDK4/6i, which have overlapping toxicity profiles, in patients with RAS mutant tumors await critical evaluation (
NCT03989115
and
NCT04000529
) and underscore the importance of identifying the optimal combination regimens in relevant preclinical model systems prior to initiating multicenter studies in a rare disease cohort such as
NF1
MPNST.
While our manuscript was in review, Miao et al. (
46
) reported that SHP099, but not clinical agents such as TNO-155 or RMC-4630, has a SHP2-independent effect of inhibiting autophagy including antitumor effects on RAS(G12C)-driven malignancies. However, our results and previous studies (
20
,
60
) do not support this mechanism in MPNST. We and others have demonstrated no single-agent HQ antitumor effect at the same dose on MPNST (
Fig. 4
A
). Also, unlike in the studies by Miao et al., Wang et al. reported that TNO-155 had greater efficacy than SHP099 in their MPNST models. Future studies will be required to understand the apparent tumor context-dependent differences in SHP099 on- and off-target effects.
This study focused on cell-autonomous MPNST responses to anti-RAS pathway therapy. The conclusion that RAS-upstream targets may have stronger and more durable antitumor effects warrants further investigation including delineation of the precise sources of feedback activation that are counteracted by SHP2i in these tumors. Some well-known feedback mechanisms to ERK suppression include derepression of RTKs and growth factors as well as loss of ERK-mediated negative feedback of SOS1/2 and RAF, all of which can be blocked with SHP2i. The fact that in vivo, SHP2i plus HQ combination therapy overcomes SHP2i acquired resistance is consistent with induction of autophagy leading to acquired resistance and points to a promising therapeutic avenue in a space where none exists.
Our findings indicate that SHP2i has strong suppression activity for NF1-MPNST. Further, combined targeting of SHP2i with HQ might offer better efficacy and less toxicity compared with drug combinations currently being tested to provide a meaningful weapon against MPNST. These results merit evaluation in a prospective clinical trial.
Materials and Methods
Primary Cell Cultures and Reagents.
Cells were cultured in serum-free medium supplemented with B27 and N2, plus EGF and FGF (10 ng/mL each) in 5% oxygen at 37C. Every culture was passaged for no longer than 4 wk. Each primary cell culture was tested for
mycoplasma
contamination by PCR 5 to 7 d after each thawing. SHP099 (HY-100388A) was purchased from Med-ChemExpress, and Trametinib (GSK1120212-S2673) was purchased from Selleckchem. The human MPNST lines 90-8 and ST-8814 used in the in vitro studies were obtained from ATCC.
Biochemical Assays.
RAS activity measurements.
RAS activity was assessed by GST-RBD pulldown, followed by immunoblotting with pan-RAS or RAS isoform–specific antibodies. Whole-cell lysates were resolved by SDS-PAGE, followed by transfer to Nylon membranes. Briefly, 1 mL of lysis buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.2, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl
2
, 5% glycerol, and 1% NP40) containing protease inhibitors was added to treated cells for 15 min on ice, and lysates were scraped from the plate and centrifuged at 14,000×
g
rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. Clarified lysates (0.25 to 3 mg) were added to prewashed GST-tagged RBD glutathione agarose beads (30 μL) for 1 h at 4 °C under constant rocking. Beads were then centrifuged, washed once, and eluted in 30 μL of 2× SDS-PAGE sample buffer. Immunodetection of RAS proteins was carried out with pan-RAS (Ab-3; Calbiochem; 1:1,000) antibodies, with ERK-2 (sc-1647; Santa Cruz Biotechnology; 1:1,000) used as a loading control for total RAS blots.
Immunoblotting.
Immunoblots were performed with the indicated primary antibodies, followed by IRDye-conjugated secondary antibodies and visualization by LI-COR. Whole-cell or tumor lysates were generated in modified radioimmunoprecipitation buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, 1% NP-40, and 0.1% SDS), supplemented with protease (40 µg/mL PMSF, 2 µg/mL antipain, 2 µg/mL pepstatin A, 20 µg/mL leupeptin, and 20 µg/mL aprotinin) and phosphatase (10 mM NaF, 1 mM Na
3
VO
4
, 10 mM β-glycerophosphate, and 10 mM sodium pyrophosphate) inhibitors. Total lysate protein was resolved by standard SDS-PAGE and transferred in 1 × transfer buffer containing 15% methanol. Membranes were incubated with their respective primary and secondary antibodies labeled with IRDye (680 nm/800 nm) and visualized by using a LICOR. Immunoblots were performed from tumor tissue lysed as described below for MIB/MS kinome profiling. Equal amounts of total protein (by the Bradford assay) were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to nitrocellulose membranes, probed with the indicated primary antibody and anti-rabbit or anti-mouse secondary antibodies coupled to HRP, detected by ECL, and imaged on a BioRad ChemiDoc. Densitometry was performed using BioRad ImageLab. The following antibodies were purchased from Cell Signaling: pMEK 1/2 (#9121; 1:1,000), MEK1 (61B12; #2352; 1:1,000), pAKT (Thr308) (#4056; 1:1,000), pAKT (Ser473) (#9271; 1:1,000), panAKT (#2920; 1:1,000), pSTAT3 (Tyr705), (#9145; 1:1,000), LC3B Antibody (#2775S), PI3 Kinase Class III (D4E2) Rabbit mAb (#3358 T), BECLIN-1 (D40C5) Rabbit mAb (#3495 T), and SQSTM1/p62 (#5114). AntiSHP2 (sc-280; 1:1,000), antiGAPDH (sc-365062, 1:1,000), and anti-ERK2 (sc-1647; 1:1,000) antibodies were purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology. Anti-STAT1 antibody (#NB500-514) was purchased from Novus.
Histology, IHC, and IF Analysis.
Hematoxylin and eosin (H & E), p62 IF, cleaved caspase-3 immunohistochemistry (IHC) (Cell Signaling, D3E9), and Ki67 IHC (Spring Biosciences, SP6) staining were performed on paraffin sections by the Experimental Pathology Shared Resource at Perlmutter Cancer Center (PCC).
GEMM Tumor Dissociation.
Primary tumors were dissected and minced into small tissue blocks and then were dissociated by using a gentleMACS Octo Dissociator (Miltenyi Biotec) and 1 g/mL collagenase (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# C8051), following the preset protocol. The dissociated cells were washed in PBS three times and treated with blood cell lysate buffer (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# R7757).
Quantitative RT-PCR.
Total RNA was isolated by using the Qiagen RNeasy kit. cDNA was generated by using the SuperScript IV First Strand Synthesis System (Invitrogen). qRT-PCR was performed with Fast SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems), following the manufacturer’s protocol, in 384-well format in C1000 Touch Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad). Differential gene expression analysis was performed with CFX Manager (Bio-Rad) and normalized to GAPDH expression. Quantification and statistics were performed. Primers used are listed in
SI Appendix
, Table S1

MPNST cells (5,000) from the

When 50% cell confluency was noted in each well, DMSO (vehicle control), Trametinib (25 nM), SHP099 (10 µM), or a combination of SHP099 (10 µM) and Trametinib (25 nM) was added. Following 96 h of drug treatment, the Cell-Titer Glo assay (Promega, catalog no. G7570) was utilized to assess cell viability. Statistical analysis between groups was performed using a two-tailed unpaired Student’s
t

MPNST cells (5000) derived from the

Then DMSO (vehicle control), HQ (25 µM), PIK3C3-IN1 (2.5 µM), SHP099 (10 µM), SHP099 (10 µM) plus HQ (25 µM), or SHP099 (10 µM) plus PIK3C3-IN1 (2.5 µM) were added. NF1-MPNST Xenografts.
PDX generation and model validation.
Patients or their designated legal guardian provided consent to collection of tissue. The study was approved by the Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center IRB. Tumor tissue was processed by a member of the Precision Pathology Biobanking Center (
64

3

A skin incision over the right hind-leg cut was used to expose the gluteus superficialis and biceps femoris muscles. A small scissor was used to cut the connective tissue that connects the muscles, revealing a small cavity traversed by the SN. A piece of tumor was fixed to the surface of the nerve using absorbable sutures taking care not to breach the epineurium. For the interior muscular layer pocket, simple interrupted absorbable sutures were used, and the skin incision was closed with sterile stainless-steel wound clips. Primary tumors were grafted orthotopically in at least three to five different mice in the first passage. Implanted mice were monitored closely for tumor engraftment and growth. When tumors reached a volume (TV) of ~1,000 mm
3

Representative tumor fragments were obtained from the initial passage (P0) and subsequent passages (P1-5) and processed for sequence validation, viable cryobanking for future model reestablishment, and other downstream applications. All procedures described are performed under sterile, barrier conditions.
PDX samples were sequenced by using MSK-IMPACT, a hybridization capture-based next-generation sequencing clinical assay (
65

In summary, this assay consists of the following standard workflow: Reads are mapped using BWA MEM and indel-realigned and baseQ-recalibrated using GATK; then mutations are called using MuTect (v1.1.4) and SomaticIndelDetector (GATK v2.3.9). Copy number is computed by using the total counts computed on bins of 100b and GC-normalized. Then the log tumor/normal ratio was segmented using the Circular Binary Segmentation method (

https://github.com/soccin/seqCNA

Multiplexed Inhibitor Bead Chromatography and Mass Spectrometry.
MIB affinity chromatography and MS were performed on snap-frozen tissue from tumor-bearing mice treated with vehicle, Trametinib, SHP099, HQ, or drug combinations. Briefly, tissue was crushed by mortar and pestle in ice-cold MIB lysis buffer (50 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5% Triton X-100, 1 mM EDTA, and 1 mM EGTA, pH 7.5) supplemented with complete protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche) and 1% phosphatase inhibitor cocktails 2 and 3 (Sigma). Extracts were sonicated 3 × 10 s, clarified by centrifugation, and filtered with a syringe (0.22 µm) before quantifying protein concentration by Bradford assay. Equal amounts of total protein were gravity-flowed over MIB columns in high-salt MIB lysis buffer (1 M NaCl). The MIB columns consisted of a 125-µL mixture of five type I kinase inhibitors: VI-16832, PP58, purvalanol B (
67

Bound protein was eluted twice with 0.5% SDS, 1% β-mercaptoethanol, 100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 6.8 for 15 min at 100C, and then treated with dithiothreitol (5 mM) for 25 min at 60 °C and followed by 20 mM iodoacetamide for 30 min in the dark. Following centrifugation with Amicon Ultra-4 filters (10-kDa cutoff) to ~100 µL, samples were precipitated with methanol–chloroform, dried in a SpeedVac, and resuspended in 50 mM HEPES (pH 8.0). Tryptic digests were performed overnight at 37 °C, extracted four times with 1 mL ethyl acetate to remove detergent, and dried. Peptides were passed through C18 spin columns according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Pierce), resuspended in 0.1% formic acid, and equal volumes (approximately 20 to 0% of the final peptide suspension for each experimental set) were injected onto a Thermo EASY-Spray 75 μm × 25 cm C18 column and separated on a 120-min gradient (2 to 40% acetonitrile) using an EASY-nLC 1,200 instrument. Thermo Orbitrap Exploris 480 MS ESI parameters were as follows: Full Scan – Resolution 120,000, Scan range 375 to 1,500 m/z, RF Lens 40%, AGC Target - Custom (Normalized AGC Target, 300%), 60 s maximum injection time, Filters – Monoisotopic peak determination: Peptide, Intensity Threshold 5.0e3, Charge State 2 to 5, Dynamic Exclusion 30 s, Data Dependent Mode – 20 Dependent scans, ddMS2 – Isolation Window 2 m/z, HCD Collision Energy 30%, Resolution 30,000, AGC Target – Custom (100%), maximum injection time 60 s. Raw files were processed for label-free quantification (LFQ) by MaxQuant (version 1.6.11.0) LFQ using the UniProt/Swiss-Prot human or mouse database, as appropriate, with fixed carbidomethyl (C) and variable oxidation (M) and acetyl (protein N-terminal) modifications, match time window 3 min. LFQ intensities for all annotated kinases with at least two razor+unique peptides were imported into Perseus software (version 1.6.10.50), log2-transformed, and filtered to include annotated kinases with at least three valid values in one treatment group. Missing values were imputed from the normal distribution for each column using default parameters (width 0.3, down shift 1.8). RNA Sequencing.
RNA was extracted from mice with
