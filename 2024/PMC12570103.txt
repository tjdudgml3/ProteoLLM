Introduction
Chronic heart failure (CHF), a progressive condition with a high mortality rate, results from diverse organic or functional heart diseases.
1
Currently, the limited availability of treatment options and the unclear pathophysiological mechanisms underlying CHF are significantly influencing the survival rates and prognoses of CHF patients suffering from this condition.
1
,
2
,
3
Consequently, there is a pressing need to investigate the etiology of CHF and explore more effective treatment strategies to improve the quality of life and survival rates of these patients. Traditionally, the treatment of CHF has primarily centered on modulating the neurohumoral and sympathetic system, such as using angiotensin converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors, beta (β)-blockers, and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs), etc.
3
,
4
,
5
However, current
anti
-CHF drugs have serious side effects,
6
,
7
necessitating the timely development of new
anti
-CHF drugs.
Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) is one of the important resources for developing new
anti
-CHF drugs. Formulas like Danshen Yang Sheng Pill, Danshen Drops, Shengxian Decoction, and Ginseng Injection have shown promise in CHF treatment.
4
,
8
Given the miscellaneous etiology and complex pathophysiological mechanisms underlying CHF, TCM formulas, with their multicomponent, multi-targeting, and multi-pathway advantages, may offer a good therapeutic potential for addressing this condition.
4
For example, Qishen Yiqi dropping pills (QSYQ), a TCM formula designed to invigorate Qi and promote blood circulation, composed of
Angelica sinensis
,
Salvia miltiorrhiza
Bunge,
Panax notoginseng
, and
Dalbergia odorifera
.
4
According to reports, the combination of QSYQ with Western medicine, such as trimetazidine and clopidogrel, shows a more effective CHF treatment effect than Western medicine alone.
4
Furthermore, Danggui Buxue Decoction (DBD), a herbal formula documented in “Neiwaishang Bianhuo Lun”, has long been utilized in ancient China to treat blood deficiency syndrome.
9
,
10
This study pays special attention to DBD which is composed of two herbs,
Angelica sinensis
and
Astragalus mongholicus
Bunge, in a ratio of 1:5. The chemical constituents show that saponins, flavonoids, volatile oils, organic acids, and polysaccharides are the primary components of DBD.
10
Modern pharmacological studies have demonstrated that DBD can protect the cardiovascular system, antagonize diabetic nephropathy, accelerate cardiac angiogenesis, enhance anti-tumor activity, and protect against cardiomyocyte injury.
9
In our previous study, we observed that DBD intensified the efficacy of Western medicine (digoxin, doxorubicin hydrochloride, benazepril hydrochloride, and metoprolol tartrate) on myocardial tissue with heart failure (HF) by modulating endothelin (ET), calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and Malondialdehyde (MDA).
11
Despite these promising findings, the protective effect of DBD on CHF and its underlying therapeutic mechanism is not fully understood. This study conducts further investigation in this area, hoping to promote the development of novel diagnostic and therapeutic strategies and further improve CHF outcomes.
This study uses myocardial proteomics and hepatic metabolomics to explore the action targets and pathways of DBD in improving CHF (or CHF accompanied by liver injury) with a CHF animal model. DBD alleviated damaged myocardial and liver function in the CHF animal model by enhancing the activity of catalase (CAT), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), cytochrome oxidase (COX), and succinate dehydrogenase (SDH). In fact, the therapeutic effect of DBD on CHF was comparable to that of positive drugs (metoprolol tartrate, captopril, and digoxin) and the combination group (DBD and positive drugs). The myocardial proteomics analysis revealed that DBD can alleviate CHF by improving energy metabolism and reducing oxidative damage. Concurrently, hepatic metabolomics demonstrated that DBD also had a protective effect in relieving liver injury in CHF patients by regulating various metabolic pathways, including lipid, vitamin, amino acid, and nucleotide metabolism. These findings highlighted the unique advantages of TCM formulas in
CHF
treatment and provided novel mechanistic insights that supported the clinical application of DBD in
CHF
.

2.2
Preparation of Danggui Buxue decoction
Angelica sinensis
(100 g, powder) and
Astragalus mongholicus
Bunge (500 g, powder) were added to 10 folds pure water, and then soaked for 1 h (h). The mixtures were decocted 3 times for 1.5 h each time. The decoction was collected and concentrated under reduced pressure. The decocting liquid was then concentrated and freeze-dried to obtain DBD, which contained 1.90 mg/g of
Astragaloside
IV and 1.23 mg/g of ferulic acid by HPLC.

2.3

Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (180–220 g, 40 pieces) were obtained from the Lanzhou Veterinary Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (SCXK, 2021-0001), and were kept in a breeding room with controlled environmental conditions (20–25 °C, 50–60 % humidity).
After one week of adaptive feeding, all animals were randomly divided into 5 groups (control group, model group, positive group, DBD-L + Positive group, and DBD-H group) after 1 week of adaptive feeding (
Table 1
). The ISO was subcutaneously injected into rats in each group at a dose of 6 mg/kg for 21 days except the control group.
13
The lower and higher doses of DBD were 1.23 g/kg BW/d and 2.46 g/kg BW/d, respectively, which was equivalent to 5 and 10 folds of the adult dose per kilogram body weight. Control group rats were given an equal volume of sterile saline. The dosages of positive drugs are shown in
Table 1
. All rats received the above reagents by tube feeding for 21 days, once daily (
Table 1
).

Observation of histopathology
Briefly, the heart tissues were collected and immersed in 4 % paraformaldehyde. 2.6
Myocardial proteomics research
2.6.1
Biological sample preparation
Proteins (model group, DBD-H group, and DBD-L + Positive group) in the heart samples (n = 6) were collected using a Rat Spin Column. The supernatant was collected after centrifugation at 12,000×
g
and 4 °C for 10 min. Bicinchoninic Acid (BCA) protein kit was used to determine the concentration of proteins in each sample.
19
The protein samples (100 μg) were mixed with DTT (dithiothreitol, 25 mmol/L), which was incubated at 55 °C for 20 min. Then, the mixtures were alkylated with 50 mM IAA, followed by overnight digestion with sequencing grade trypsin.
20
The digested peptides were collected and labeled with tandem mass tags (TMT) Isobaric Label Reagent Set (Thermo Scientific). The digested samples were desalted using a small C18 column and separated by reversed phase high performance liquid chromatography (RPUPLC) column (150 mm × 2.1 mm，C18 XP Column).
21
2.6.2
LC-MS/MS analysis and data processing
The TMT-labeled peptides were detected by liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). The mobile phase consisted of an acetonitrile-water-formic acid system, with phase A comprising 2 % acetonitrile and phase B containing 80 % acetonitrile and 1 % formic acid, respectively.
22
The mass spectra were run in data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode for 90 min in positive ion detection mode. The 20 ions with the highest intensities in a primary scan were screened by a quadrupole, followed by fragment ion scanning using higher-energy collision dissociation (HCD) cleavage.
22
The built-in Sequest HT search engine and Proteome Discoverer software (Version 2.4.0.305) were used to analyze raw mass spectrometry data files. Statistical methods were used to screen differential expressed proteins, and followed by bioinformatic analysis.
2.7
Hepatic metabolomics research
2.7.1
Biological sample preparation
Briefly, liver samples (25 mg) from each group (model group, DBD-H group, and DBD-L + Positive group) were mixed with the extraction fluid of metabolite (500 μL, methanol: acetonitrile: water = 2 : 2: 1, mixtures containing isotope-labeled internal standards), and then vortexed for 30 s (s), respectively. The mixtures added steel beads, were ground for 4 min at 35 Hz, and sonicated for 5 min in an ice water bath.
23
Repeated 3 times. The extractions were centrifuged (12,000×
g
, 15 min, 4 °C) after standing at −40 °C for 1 h. The supernatant (400 μL) was removed into a new 1.5 mL Eppendorf (EP) tube and vacuum-dried, then mixed with 50 % acetonitrile (200 μL). The mixtures were vortexed for 30 s, sonicated in an ice water bath for 10 min, and centrifuged at 4 °C (15 min, 12,000×
g
). The supernatant (75 μL) from each sample was transferred into the injection bottle for detection. In addition, quality control (QC) samples were prepared to contain biological information from all samples.
24
2.7.2
UPLC-QTOF-MS/MS analysis and data processing
QC samples were randomly inserted into the analytical batch for stability and performance checks. The samples were separated chromatographically using an ultra-performance liquid chromatograph (Agilent 1290) and an Acquity UPLC BEH Amide (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.7 M) liquid chromatographic column. For liquid chromatography, phase A consisted of an aqueous phase containg 25 mmol/L ammonium acetate and ammonia, while phase B was acetonitrile. The ions were automatically identified, and their secondary mass spectrometry data were collected using the data collection program (Analyst TF 1.7) of Triple TOF 6600 high resolution mass spectrometry based on the primary mass spectrometry data.
25
For secondary mass spectrometry, ions with ion intensities greater than 100 were selected with collision-induced dissociation energy and a cycle time of 30 eV and 0.56 s, respectively. The mass spectrometry data were
log
-transformed and UV-formatted using SIMCAV 16.0.2. The biological background of the generated differential metabolites was analyzed using gene ontology (GO) and kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG), among others.
26
2.8
Determination of CAT, GSH-Px, and MDA
Rat serum samples were stored at −80 °C and thawed at 4 °C. Rat myocardial tissues (0.2 g) were thawed at 4 °C and chopped up. After the tissues were thoroughly ground in a glass homogenization tube which had containing 9 folds of pre-cooled saline, they were centrifuged at 3500×
g
for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was carefully collected and stored at a low temperature. The protein concentration was assayed by the BCA method.
27
The test solutions were prepared following the kit's instructions to detect CAT, GSH-Px, and MDA levels in the rat's serum and tissue samples.
2.9
Analysis of ATP, COX, and SDH level in myocardial tissue
Rat myocardial tissues were processed as 2.8. Following the kit's instructions, the levels of adenosine 5′-triphosphate (ATP), COX, and SDH in the myocardial tissue were assessed.
28
2.10
Western blotting assay
The rats' cardiac tissues were lysed in pre-cooled radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (1 mL), and mechanically homogenized on ice for 10 min. Total proteins were detected using the BCA protein assay kit. The proteins were separated by 10 % SDS-PAGE and transferred to a PVDF membrane (Millipore, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5 % skimmed milk powder for 2 h at room temperature, then incubated with the primary antibodies (β-actin, adenosine 5′-monophosphate (AMP)-activated protein kinase (AMPK), phosphorylated AMPK (pAMPK), silent mating type information regulation 2 homolog-1 (SIRT1), peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1 alpha (PGC-1α), and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor beta (PPAR β), Abcam Company) for 24 h at 4 °C. After washing with 1 × TBST, the membranes were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with the secondary antibody and subjected to immunoblot analysis using Image Lab Software.
29
2.11

All data were expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) with indicated “n” value. The data were analyzed by the GraphPad Prism 8.0.

Results
3.1
DBD alleviated ISO-induced CHF in rats
The pharmacological effects of DBD on CHF induced by ISO were investigated. BNP, a cardiac hormone secreted by ventricular myocardial cells serves as a biomarker for clinical diagnosis, treatment, and assessment of CHF.
30
Compared with that in normal rats, the BNP level in ISO-induced CHF rats was notably elevated (
Fig. 1
E), indicating severe myocardial damage. This was an ideal source for the successful establishment of CHF rats’ model. After the administration of DBD or positive drugs, the secretion of BNP in CHF rats significantly reduced (
P
< 0.01). Meanwhile, the change in body weight in the control group was comparable to that of the other groups indicating that DBD posed no potential safety hazards (
Fig. 1
A).

In addition, the histomorphology alterations of the heart tissues, cardiac hypertrophy (CH), cardiac index (CI), ALT, and AST activity were assayed to investigate the influence of DBD on CHF rats. The results showed that DBD significantly alleviated myocardial hypertrophy in ISO-induced CHF rats. Particularly, the effect was more prominent in the DBD-H group compared with the positive drugs (

These results demonstrated that DBD could ameliorate myocardial damage and liver injury in ISO-induced CHF rats.

Discussion and conclusion
In recent years, CHF has garnered increasing global attention as a complicated clinical syndrome that appears in various severe stages of heart diseases. Unfortunately, progress in clinical therapeutic drugs has been limited due to the absence of reliable biomarkers.
4
DBD offers a significant potential in HF treatment due to its advantages of multi-targeting, multi-composition, multi-pathways, and lower-toxicity.
9
This study proves that DBD can significantly alleviate myocardial and liver damage by reducing serum levels of BNP and the activities of ALT and AST. The effect of a high dose of DBD is similar to that of a low dosage of DBD combined with conventional medications, namely captopril, digoxin, and metoprolol tartrate. These findings imply that DBD not only exhibits standalone therapeutic efficacy on CHF, but also has synergistic effects when combined with Western medicine.
There is growing evidence that a series of factors can cause CHF, such as oxidative stress, inflammation, endoplasmic reticulum stress, apoptosis, metabolism remodeling, and angiogenesis.
4
,
32
,
33
,
34
,
Among these factors, metabolism remodeling and oxidative stress are the two that appear most important. It has been reported that the metabolism remodeling of cardiomyocytes is responsible for cardiac structural remodeling
4
,
35
and oxidative stress can induce the generation of Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS), reducing the activity of antioxidant enzymes and triggering CHF. Mitochondria is the metabolic signaling center and energy sensor,
36
and its changes can exacerbate myocardial energy metabolism disorders
37
and ROS levels.
38
Meanwhile, peroxisome, as another source of ROS, can help maintain cellular energy balance and redox homeostasis.
39
,
40
In the present study, myocardial proteomics results indicated that differential proteins in each group were precisely located in mitochondria and peroxisomes. This observation indicates a disruption in energy metabolism (mitochondria or peroxisome) and alterations in the antioxidant system (glutathione metabolism is predominant) in cardiomyocytes of CHF rats. GO and KEGG enrichment analysis further supports the finding that DBD can alleviate symptoms of
CHF
by regulating fatty acids catabolic process, lipid metabolism, and intracellular oxidoreductase activity through the fatty acid metabolism pathway, β-alanine metabolism pathway, and PPAR signaling pathway. Thus, it can be concluded that DBD can relieve CHF by modulating energy metabolism and oxidative stress primarily through mitochondria and peroxisomes.
Moreover, Qin et al. once reported that CAT can effectively reduce myocyte hypertrophy, myocyte apoptosis, and interstitial fibrosis, thereby preventing the progression to overt heart failure.
41
Consistently, our study observed an enhancement in CAT activity in both the serum and myocardial tissues of CHF rats treated with DBD. Furthermore, we found that DBD treatment can also increase the activity of GSH-Px in CHF rats, aligning with proteomic results indicating glutathione metabolism as the primary metabolic pathway involved. In addition, the existence of CHF can give rise to mitochondrial dysfunction within cardiomyocytes, subsequently exacerbating cardiomyopathy.
42
,
43
COX and SDH are the two pivotal rate-limiting enzymes within the mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation respiratory chain.
44
,
45
In subsequent work, the ATP content, COX, and SDH activities in the myocardial tissues of CHF rats were reduced, providing evidence of impaired myocardial mitochondrial respiratory function and a decreased ability to synthesize ATP in rats with cardiac mitochondria. Therefore, DBD exerts a therapeutic effect on rats with CHF by enhancing antioxidant activity and improving mitochondrial function.
The signal pathway involving SIRT1, AMPK, p-AMPK, and PGC-1α is important for the regulation of mitochondrial energy metabolism and initiation of mitochondrial biogenesis.
46
It has been reported that the regulation of the transcriptional coactivator PGC-1α by both AMPK and SIRT1 may impact the mitochondrial level of cardiomyocytes. Activation of AMPK/SIRT1/PGC-1α signaling pathway can ameliorate mitochondrial and oxidative damage induced by CHF.
46
,
47
In the current study, the administration of DBD can enhance the expression levels of AMPK, SIRT1, and PGC-1α proteins in the myocardial tissues of rats with CHF. Additionally, within cardiomyocytes, PPAR β exhibited the capability to enhance fatty acid oxidation and overall fatty acid utilization,
48
,
49
thus exerting its effect of alleviating CHF. In addition, the activation of PPAR β can protect cardiac myocytes by inhibiting oxidative stress.
50
The proteomics analysis shows that PPAR β signal pathway plays an important role in the treatment of CHF by DBD, which is consistent with the later results that show an increase in the expression of PPAR β protein by DBD. Altogether, it has been determined that the restoration of energy metabolism and oxidative stress in cardiomyocytes of CHF rats by DBD can be attributed to the mitochondria- and peroxisome-mediated AMPK/SIRT1/PGC-1α/PPAR β signaling pathway. This may offer new insights into potential therapeutic strategies for CHF.
A clinical investigation demonstrated that liver dysfunction often occurs in CHF patients, correlating with disease severity and prognosis.
51
Consistent with these findings, our study proved that the liver injury occurred in CHF rats is related to changes of AST and ALT levels. However, DBD treatment can successfully restore the liver functions. Untargeted metabolomics results show that DBD significantly adjust hepatic metabolism in CHF rats, including the downregulation of metabolites associated with vitamins and amino acids, such as α-linolenic acid,
N
-acetyl-L-alanine, xanthine, pantothenate, heptadecanoic acid, tauroursodeoxycholic acid, taurochenodeoxycholate. In the KEGG pathway enrichment analysis, these differential metabolites were involved in a series of metabolic pathways. Among these, lipid, vitamin, amino acid, nucleotide, and other metabolism pathways are particularly important in the treatment of CHF with DBD. This proves that DBD can address liver dysfunction in CHF rats through these metabolic pathways.
In summary, DBD can effectively alleviate CHF by regulating mitochondria- and peroxisome-mediated energy metabolism and restoring oxidative damage via the AMPK/SIRT1/PGC-1α/PPARβ signaling pathway. Moreover, DBD can also relieve liver injury in CHF rats through a series of metabolic pathways. This study provides a potential drug for the clinical treatment of CHF and further enhances the understanding of the clinical applications of DBD.
5
Limitations of the study
Although this experiment verified the protective effect of DBD on in rats with CHF, the specific active ingredient responsible for this therapeutic action remains unknown due to the complex nature of TCM. Additionally, while this study has explored the molecular targets and pathways of DBD for the treatment of CHF via non-targeted metabolomics and proteomics, deeper and more specific experimental validation is needed to enhance the understanding of DBD for the treatment of CHF.
