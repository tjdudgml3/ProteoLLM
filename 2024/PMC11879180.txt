Significance statement
Gut microbiota influences brain function and adult neurogenesis. Our study shows that combining 3 probiotic strains (ProB3) enhances adult neurogenesis in the mouse hippocampus. ProB3 colonization in germ-free mice promotes neural stem cell proliferation and ameliorates maturation arrest. ProB3 increases blood metabolites, including theanine and imidazole peptides, fostering neurogenesis in human neural stem cells. The results suggest that ProB3 colonization promotes adult neurogenesis, providing potential avenues for improving adult hippocampal neurogenesis.
Introduction
Gut microbiota is essential for maintaining mammalian health; gut microbiota dysbiosis is associated with the progression of various human diseases. Indeed, gut microbiota plays a key role in several neurological disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Parkinson’s disease, and psychiatric disorders.
1
Regarding AD, antibiotic treatment-induced alterations in the gut microbiota reduce amyloid-β plaque deposition and decrease the reactivity of glia localized to the plaques in a mouse model of AD.
2-4
Numerous reports have also associated gut microbiota with psychiatric disorders. For example, an increase or decrease in a specific species of intestinal bacteria is characteristically observed in patients with schizophrenia, and the transplantation of that bacteria from the feces of patients with schizophrenia in germ-free (GF) mice caused them to exhibit behavioral abnormalities relevant to schizophrenia.
5
Moreover, the intake of a probiotic preparation containing 3 strains of
Bacillus subtilis
TO-A (BS),
Enterococcus faecium
T-110 (EF), and
Clostridium butyricum
TO-A (CB) improves bowel movements in patients with schizophrenia and alleviates psychotic symptoms.
6
,
7
However, although regulating gut microbiota using probiotics is a potential therapeutic strategy for psychiatric disorders, the mechanisms these bacteria use to target cell types in the nervous system, contributing to disease amelioration, remain largely unknown.
Adult neurogenesis of the hippocampus plays an essential role in emotion and higher-order brain functions such as memory and learning.
8
Moreover, adult neurogenesis is closely related to diseases such as AD and psychiatric disorders.
9
,
10
Furthermore, genetic association studies have shown that the protein disrupted-in-schizophrenia 1, a risk factor for schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, major depression, and autism, is important in hippocampal neurogenesis.
11
,
12
Additionally, several antipsychotic drugs have been implicated in promoting hippocampal neurogenesis in mice.
13
Adult neurogenesis is sensitive to environmental influences such as alcohol intake, diet, smoking, and social stress; gut microbiota participates in this regulation.
14
,
15
Indeed, antibiotic treatment attenuates adult neurogenesis,
16
while GF mice have reported abnormalities in neurogenesis.
17
,
18
Therefore, schizophrenia treatment through the intake of a BS, EF, and CB formulation is possibly mediated by modulating adult neurogenesis; however, this has not been investigated in detail. Moreover, the mechanism through which probiotics coordinate adult neurogenesis is unclear.
In this study, we examine whether the colonization of the intestinal tract by 3 probiotic strains (BS, EF, and CB; hereafter referred to as ProB3) impacts adult neurogenesis regulation of the dentate gyrus (DG) in the hippocampus and reverses its dysregulation in GF mice. Moreover, we investigate whether the levels of metabolites that promote human neural stem cell (hNSC) proliferation and neurogenesis increase in the blood after ProB3 colonization in mice.
Materials and methods
Probiotic bacteria
BS, EF, and CB were acquired from TOA Biopharma Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan) and have been assigned the accession numbers FERM BP-07462, FERM BP-10867, and FERM BP-10866, respectively, by the International Patent Organism Depositary of the National Institute of Technology and Evaluation. The culturing methods for each bacteria are described in the
Supplementary Material and Methods
.
Experimental animals
All murine experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Experiment Committee of National Institute of Advanced Industrial Science and Technology (AIST) and the University of Tokyo. GF BALB/c mice were bred at the Laboratory of Veterinary Public Health, the University of Tokyo. A schematic diagram of mouse breeding and administration of ProB3 and BrdU is shown in
Supplementary Figure 1A
. The GF mice were kept in flexible vinyl isolators in a room at 24 °C and 60% relative humidity, under a 12/12 hours light/dark cycle, and were fed a CMF-pelleted diet (Oriental Yeast Co.) sterilized using γ-irradiation at a dose of 50 kGy. After dividing the 12-week-old GF mice into GF and ProB3 groups, the ProB3 group included GF mice orally administered the ProB3 mixture adjusted with saline to contain 5 × 10
8
colony forming units/mL each of BS, EF, and CB at a dose of 200 µL/animal once weekly for 4 consecutive weeks (total 5 times) or 7 consecutive weeks (total 8 times) (
Supplementary Figure S1A
). The GF group received saline under the same conditions.
The mice in each group were administered 50 mg/kg 5-bromo-2ʹ-deoxyuridine (BrdU) intraperitoneally, once daily for 3 consecutive days, after 4 weeks (28 days) from the start of ProB3 or saline oral administration (p.o.) (
Supplementary Figure S1A
). The day after the last BrdU administration, perfusion fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) was performed after deeply anesthetised by isoflurane (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation), and brains were collected from each group of mice (
Supplementary Figure S1A
). Samples from the GF mice administered saline for 4 weeks (GF-4W) and GF mice administered ProB3 for 4 weeks (ProB3-4W) were analyzed. Furthermore, 3 weeks after BrdU administration, that is, 7 weeks (51 days) after p.o. of the ProB3 mixture (ProB3-7W) or saline (GF-7W), perfusion with 4% PFA was performed, and the brains of the mice in each group were collected. To confirm whether ProB3 colonization occurred in the intestinal tract, the faeces of the mice were collected 7 days after ProB3 oral administration at weeks 2 and 4. The collected faeces were homogenized and spread on agar plates containing tryptic soy agar with 3% NaCl for BS and ABCM (Eiken Chemical) for EF and CB and incubated at 37 °C under aerobic and anaerobic conditions, respectively. The colonization of the administered probiotics in the mouse intestine was determined by counting the characteristic colonies of each of the 3 bacteria (
Supplementary Figure S1B
). The GF mice were also checked for GF status the day before dissection to confirm they were in an abacterial state.
For specific-pathogen-free (SPF) mice, 12-week-old BALB/c mice were purchased from Japan SLC, Inc. and housed for the same duration as GF and ProB3 mice without saline p.o. at AIST; BrdU was administered intraperitoneally, at a concentration of 50 mg/kg once daily for 3 consecutive days (
Supplementary Figure S1A
). The day after the last BrdU administration, perfusion fixation with 4% PFA was performed after adequate anesthesia and brain samples were collected from SPF mice as SPF-4W. Three weeks after BrdU administration, perfusion with 4% PFA was performed, and the brains were collected as samples from SPF-7W mice. 10-week-old C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Japan SLC, Inc. and housed at AIST.
Details on immunohistochemistry methods and metabolome analysis are described in the
Supplementary Material and Methods
.
Culturing of hNSCs and maNSCs
The human neural stem cells (hNSCs) were purchased from PhoenixSongs Biologicals, Inc. (Donor Lot CxB-3). The cell line was derived from the cerebral cortex of a male human fetus at embryonic week 14. The hNSCs were cultured as described previously
19
,
20
and the neural aspects of this cell line were confirmed. In brief, the hNSCs were cultured in an undifferentiated medium composed of N2-supplemented Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium with F12 (DMEM/F-12; Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing a 0.1% B27 supplement (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 20 ng/mL human basic fibroblast growth factor (PeproTech, Inc.), and 20 ng/mL human epidermal growth factor (PeproTech, Inc.) on culture dishes precoated with poly-l-ornithine (Sigma-Aldrich) and laminin (Corning). Details of the evaluation methods of metabolites are described in the
Supplementary Material and Methods
. The effects of UCPH-101 on the proliferation of hNSCs were evaluated in a similar manner; details regarding the evaluation methods are described in the
Supplementary Material and Methods
. The mouse adult hippocampal neural stem cells (maNSCs) used in this study were kindly provided by Dr Taito Matsuda and Dr Kinichi Nakashima of Kyushu University. Methodological details for culturing maNSCs
21
and measuring proliferative potential and neuronal differentiation, as indicated in
Supplementary Figures 4 and 6
, are described in the
Supplementary Material and Methods
.
Statistical analysis
R and SPSS software were used to perform all statistical analyses. Statistical analysis of the immunohistochemical staining was performed using Tukey’s range test. In the metabolome analysis, equal variances were first verified using Levene’s test, followed by Student’s
t
-test if not rejected, and Welch’s
t
-test if rejected. Dunnett’s test was used to evaluate the experiments using hNSCs. Results are shown as mean ± standard deviation of at least 3 experiments;
P
< .05 was considered statistically significant in all experiments.
Results
ProB3 colonization promotes adult neural stem cell proliferation and neurogenesis
To investigate the effect of bacterial colonization by ProB3 on adult hippocampal neurogenesis, we prepared SPF mice, GF mice, and ProB3-colonized mice (
Supplementary Figure S1A
). In adult hippocampal neurogenesis, quiescent neural stem cells (Type-1 cells) become active neural stem cells (Type-2 cells), newborn premature neurons, and differentiate into mature neurons in a stepwise manner (
Supplementary Figure S1C
).
22-24
We first assessed whether colonization by ProB3 affected the proliferation of adult neural stem cells in the hippocampal DG. To this end, the BrdU-positive (BrdU
+
) cells were enumerated in the subgranular zone (SGZ) of the DG of ProB3-4W, GF-4W, and SPF-4W mice (
Supplementary Figure S1A
). BrdU
+
cells were most abundant in ProB3-4W mice (
Figure 1A
), while similar abundances were observed in SPF-4W and GF-4W mice (
Figure 1B
). Furthermore, immunohistochemical analysis of Ki67—a marker of proliferative cells—revealed that the most Ki67
+
cells were observed in the DG of ProB3 mice, with a statistical difference compared to SPF mice (
Figure 1C
and
D
). These results indicate that ProB3 colonization promotes the proliferation of adult neural stem cells in the hippocampal DG of mice.

Reduction of quiescent neural stem cells in the DG of GF mice and their restoration via ProB3 colonization

and

Identification of metabolites that increase in the blood after ProB3 colonization
Alterations in the gut microbiota lead to quantitative changes in the levels of various metabolites in the blood. Moreover, adult hippocampal neural stem cells have direct access to molecules circulating in the blood,
29
,
30
and the levels of various molecules in the blood can increase and decrease during adult neurogenesis.
31
To elucidate the mechanism through which ProB3 colonization promotes adult neurogenesis, we identified the metabolites in the blood that increased after ProB3 colonization. The blood metabolome analysis results revealed that the number of metabolites involved in glycolysis tended to decrease in ProB3-4W mice compared to GF-4W mice (
Supplementary Table S1
). The analysis of water-soluble metabolites using CE-FTMS (capillary electrophoresis-Fourier transform mass spectrometry) showed that 2,6-diaminopimelic acid (mesoDAP) derived from the cell wall of the ProB3 bacteria, theanine—considered to be produced by the ProB3 bacteria—along with carnosine, homocarnosine, and anserine of the imidazole peptide group, were higher in ProB3-4W mice than in GF-4W mice (
Supplementary Table S1
). The D-Ala-D-Ala and 3-hydroxybutyric acid (3-HB)—a hydroxy acid implicated in brain function and derived from bacteria—were also higher in ProB3 mice. However, the increase was not statistically significant. In fat-soluble substance analysis using LC-TOFMS (liquid chromatography time-of-flight mass spectrometry), long-chain fatty acids, 17-hydroxyprogesterone, and ricinoleic acid levels were higher in ProB3 mice than in GF mice (
Supplementary Table S2
). Thus, these metabolomic analyses allowed us to identify metabolites increased in the blood by ProB3 colonization.
Theanine, carnosine, and 3-HB promote the proliferation of HNSCs
Based on the increase in the metabolites in ProB3 mice, we evaluated the effects of theanine and imidazole peptides—involved in neural differentiation in the brain
32
—on the proliferation and differentiation of neural stem cells. We also examined 3-HB, mesoDAP and D-Ala-D-Ala, which had increased by more than twice their original concentrations in the ProB3 mice compared to GF mice. In addition, as muramyl dipeptide (MDP) derived from the bacterial cell wall has been associated with the modulation of brain function in mice,
33
we investigated its effects on neural stem cells. To this end, we utilized neural stem cells derived from human fetuses.
19
,
20
As a primary evaluation of the effect on proliferation, hNSCs were cultured on chamber slides. The concentration-dependent effects of each metabolite on the proliferation of these cells at a wide range of concentrations (1 nM to 1 mM) were evaluated based on the percentage of cells that incorporated 5-ethynyl-2ʹ‐deoxyuridine (EdU) (
Supplementary Figure S3A
). The proportion of EdU
+
cells was higher at lower concentrations of theanine, carnosine, and 3-HB compared to the untreated control cells, indicating a tendency to promote the proliferation of hNSCs (
Supplementary Figure S3B, D, F
).
The effects of these metabolites on hNSC proliferation were further examined at a narrow concentration range in 24-well plates (
Figure 5A
). The results showed that the proportion of EdU
+
cells was significantly higher in theanine- and carnosine-treated cells than in the controls (
Figure 5B–E
), indicating that treatment with these metabolites promoted the proliferation of hNSCs. For 3-HB-treated cells, a significantly higher percentage of EdU-incorporated cells was observed at the 0.5 nM concentration compared to the controls (
Figure 5F
,
G

Next, we examined the effects of the metabolites on the neuronal differentiation of hNSCs. Similar to the evaluation of effects on proliferation, hNSCs were cultured on chamber slides for primary evaluation (

+

+

+

Discussion
In this study, we demonstrated that the hippocampus of GF mice exhibited impaired maturation of newborn neurons and decreased quiescent neural stem cells. Meanwhile, probiotic ProB3 colonization effectively restored these abnormalities. Moreover, ProB3 colonization enhanced the proliferation of neural stem cells in the adult hippocampus. Specifically, circulating metabolites, enhanced by ProB3 colonization, promoted the proliferation and neuronal differentiation of hNSCs and maNSC and the maturation of newborn neurons. These results indicate that the role of the gut microbiota, considered essential for adult neurogenesis in the hippocampus, can be mostly complemented by ProB3, a combination probiotic containing only 3 strains (BS, EF, and CB) (
Supplementary Figure S8
).
In this study, the abundance of DCX
+
neural progenitor cells and immature neurons was higher in GF mice than in SPF mice. Similarly, a previous study reported an increased abundance of DCX
+
cells in GF mice compared with conventionally colonized mice.
17
Additionally, we demonstrated that DCX
+
cells failed to mature into DCX
+
neurons with prolonged branching dendrites, CR
+
postmitotic neurons, or NeuN
+
neurons. This impaired neuronal maturation may involve microglial inactivation in GF mice. Indeed, phagocytosis of newborn neurons by the microglia is essential for adult neurogenesis.
34
Diaz-Aparicio et al. reported that the suppression of microglial phagocytosis in adult stages increases the number of proliferative DCX
+
cells without altering the abundance of NeuN
+
mature neurons,
35
which is consistent with the GF mouse results in the current study. Therefore, to assess microglial activation, their abundance and morphology were evaluated in the GCL and SGZ of DG in SPF-4W, GF-4W, and ProB3-4W mice. Immunostaining for ionized calcium-binding adapter molecule 1 (Iba1)—a maker for microglia—revealed no significant differences among mice (
Supplementary Figure S9A and B
). However, the microglia morphology in GF-4W mice contained more complex branched projections than in other mice (
Supplementary Figure S9C
). Additionally, the Iba1
+
cell area in images was significantly larger in GF-4W mice than in the other mice (
Supplementary Figure S9D
). Microglia in the resting state have intricately branched projections; upon activation, the cell body becomes smaller, and the number of projections decreases.
36
,
37
Hence, our results suggest that microglia in GF mice are in a resting state and become activated by ProB3 treatment to the same level as in SPF mice. Meanwhile, no difference was observed in
Aif-1
(Iba1-coding gene) expression in the hippocampi of mice. This suggests that a strong inflammatory response was not induced in the brains of mice treated with ProB3 (
Supplementary Figure S9E
). Thus, considering our results and previous reports showing that the function of microglia is altered in GF mice,
36-38
the increased number of DCX
+
cells and the decreased abundance of NeuN
+
cells in GF mice may be attributed to decreased microglial phagocytosis. Given that the proportion of BrdU
+
and DCX
+
cells was lower in ProB3 mice than in GF mice, the increased number of NeuN
+
mature neurons in ProB3 mice likely resulted from restoring normal phagocytosis in the microglia. Future detailed analyses of whether microglial phagocytosis is restored by ProB3 treatment are warranted to support this hypothesis.
NeuroD1, a basic helix-loop-helix transcription factor expressed in neuroblasts and immature neurons, plays an important role in newborn neuron maturation.
39
When we examined the expression of NeuroD1 by immunohistochemistry, although an age-related decrease in NeuroD1 expression was observed (
Supplementary Figure S10
), significantly more NeuroD1
+
cells were detected in ProB3 mice than in SPF mice (
Supplementary Figure S11A–C
). When comparing GF and ProB3 mice, NeuroD1
+
cells tended to increase more in ProB3 mice, although this was not significant (
Supplementary Figure S11A–C
). These results imply that ProB3 colonization promotes neurogenesis and maturation of newborn neurons through NeuroD1 expression. Interestingly, the number of NeuroD1
+
cells in GF-4W mice increased significantly compared to SPF-4W mice (
Supplementary Figure S11A, B
), similar to DCX
+
cells. Hence, neuronal differentiation in adult neural stem cells is not inhibited in GF mice and is promoted more in GF mice than in SPF mice. However, further analysis is needed as our study lacks direct evidence that ProB3-induced maturation is due to increased NeuroD1 expression.
This study showed that Type-1 cells in the DG of the hippocampus were reduced in GF mice. This decrease in Type-1 cells may have resulted from insufficient pooling during development or failure to maintain a quiescent state at the adult stage. Moreover, the number of BrdU
+
cells one day after administration was comparable to that in SPF mice, while DCX
+
newborn neurons were also abundant in GF mice. Additionally, the abundance of Ki67
+
-activated neural stem cells with radial morphology was similar among all mice, indicating that the transition of quiescent NSCs to activated NSCs was not prevented. A recent report has shown reduced quiescent NSCs and increased DCX-positive cells in the DG of mice with heterozygous deletion
Setd1a
, an established risk gene for schizophrenia (SCZ).
40
Hence, we hypothesize that, in GF mice, neural stem cells cannot maintain a quiescent state and instead become activated and arrested as young neurons. Interestingly, we found that the depletion of Type-1 cells was restored by ProB3 colonization. Accordingly, we hypothesize that the depletion of Type-1 cells in GF mice is caused by the decrease in cell-extrinsic factors that are essential for maintaining quiescence in the adult stage and that ProB3 colonization upregulates the expression of these extrinsic factors and restores their depletion. Cell-extrinsic factors, such as extracellular matrix,
41
Notch signaling,
42
,
43
and WNT,
44-46
comprising the niche, are important in the maintenance of the quiescent state of neural stem cells in the adult hippocampus. Future studies exploring how the gut microbiota maintains Type-1 cells through expressing cell-extrinsic factors are necessary.
In this study, using hNSCs, we found that anserine accelerates the maturation of newborn neurons, suggesting that the ProB3-induced increase in serum anserine contributes to the release of arrested young neurons in the DG. Anserine, similar to carnosine and homocarnosine, is an imidazole peptide that promotes the neuronal differentiation of hNSCs. Adult hippocampal neural stem cells are located where they are directly exposed to metabolites in the blood circulation.
47
Therefore, promoting adult neurogenesis by ProB3 colonization may be partly due to increased imidazole peptides. Moreover, an increase in serum theanine levels was observed in ProB3 mice. Theanine facilitates the proliferation of neural stem cells derived from the adult hippocampus
48
and can effectively improve memory in SAMP8 mice—a mouse model of accelerated aging—and depression in humans.
49
Moreover, clinical studies have reported that l-theanine and l-carnosine intake improves the symptoms of schizophrenia.
50-52
There is some evidence that theanine induces the elevated expression of excitatory amino acid transporters and modulates their activity.
53
Using hNSCs, we observed that the addition of UCPH-101—an excitatory amino acid transporter subtype 1 (EAAT1) inhibitor—inhibited theanine-induced proliferation (
Supplementary Figure S12
), suggesting that theanine mediates neural stem cell proliferation by activating EAAT1. Additionally, our metabolic analyses revealed an increase in 3-HB, which promotes adult neurogenesis and restores memory deficits associated with histone methyltransferase lysine (K)-specific methyltransferase 2D (KMT2D) deficiency.
32
Overall, although the mechanism through which ProB3-induced metabolites exhibit beneficial effects remains unclear, our findings suggest that ProB3 intake may increase the abundance of these metabolites in humans.
Conclusion
In this study, we showed that aberrant adult neurogenesis in GF mice could be restored via colonization of the intestinal tract by 3 probiotic strains: BS, EF, and CB (ProB3). These bacteria also stimulate the proliferation of adult neural stem cells. Furthermore, we identified blood metabolites increased by ProB3 colonization and found that they promoted hNSC proliferation and neurogenesis. This study demonstrates that the intake of specific probiotics can promote adult neurogenesis, providing novel insights into the role of gut microbiota in the brain–gut axis.
