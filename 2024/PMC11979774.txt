Introduction
The idea that gene expression drives development and maintains cellular function is a well-established maxim in our post-genomic era. The problem now is to describe the molecular pathways that orchestrate the cell-specific and temporal readout of the genome.
Caenorhabditis elegans
is especially well suited to this task. Its small size and relative simplicity are certainly useful attributes. But the consequent detailed and comprehensive descriptions of the
C. elegans
body plan and development are uniquely valuable for precisely mapping gene expression to defined cell fates. Coupled with a powerful suite of genetic tools and imaging methods, accurate gene expression maps that match the single-cell resolution of
C. elegans
anatomy and lineage are key to unlocking the genomic blueprints for cellular differentiation and function. Here, we describe approaches for measuring gene expression in
C. elegans
. Our goal is to provide both newcomers to
C. elegans
research and established investigators an up-to-date review of the field that we hope will inspire new directions for gene expression research.
We begin by outlining the conventional method of using transcriptional reporters to detect gene expression. Our review of transgenic methods and CRISPR/genome engineering is brief owing to recent comprehensive reviews of these topics (
Dickinson and Goldstein 2016
;
Nance and Frøkjaer-Jensen 2019
;
Martin and Calarco 2022
). Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) methods for directly quantifying transcripts with single-cell resolution are summarized with special emphasis on newer more sensitive techniques and the exciting development of multiplexing strategies for monitoring transcript abundance in situ. To complement methods for quantifying steady-state levels of gene expression in fixed tissue, we review new techniques for monitoring transcripts in vivo, including live-cell reporters for detecting alternative splicing.
To highlight the advent of powerful RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) methods for gene expression analysis, we provide a comprehensive review of the field for
C. elegans
. We note the importance of deep sequencing of whole worm–derived RNA samples in the adult and during development for validating gene models inferred by analysis of the genomic sequence. We review approaches for isolating RNA from specific cell types for conventional “bulk” RNA-seq of samples pooled from a population of target cells. Strengths and weaknesses of these cell-specific methods are outlined. Protocols for RNA amplification and library construction are discussed. Alternative sequencing technologies (i.e. short read vs long read) are reviewed. Methods for processing and analyzing bulk RNA-seq data are discussed including the challenge of accurately cataloging alternatively spliced transcripts.
We describe single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) techniques in the next section. scRNA-seq is especially useful because accurate profiles of individual cell types can be obtained post hoc and do not depend on methods for isolating RNA from specific cells. Moreover, the fully described anatomy and development of
C. elegans
are excellent fits for interpreting scRNA-seq data that crucially requires independent validation of cell-specific gene expression. Readers are walked through methods for generating and analyzing scRNA-seq datasets. To complement our review of methods for direct detection of transcripts, we summarize related methods for mapping RNA polymerase binding sites (e.g. RAPID) and open chromatin (e.g. ATAC-seq) both of which are typically associated with transcriptionally active genes. We also describe ribosomal profiling strategies for detecting actively translated transcripts. In a final section, we review methods for cataloging protein expression. Although antibodies are available for direct detection of a limited number of
C. elegans
proteins, more widely used methods rely on either epitope tagging or labeling endogenous proteins with fluorescent reporters for live-cell imaging. We then describe high-throughput proteomic methods for detecting protein abundance from whole worms as well as specific tissues and cell types. In a final section, we highlight the daunting task of extending RNA profiling and proteomic analysis to single individual worms for the purpose of cataloging potential differences in gene expression among genotypically identical individuals.
Fluorescent reporters for detecting gene transcription
We have included a brief overview of fluorescent reporter constructs (e.g. GFP) to complement detailed and comprehensive reviews of this topic in WormBook (
Boulin 2006
;
Nance and Frøkjaer-Jensen 2019
).
Transcriptional reporters
Historically, transgenic reporters have been used as proxies to monitor gene expression in
C. elegans
. In the simplest version of this approach, a genomic fragment from the upstream region of a target gene is fused to a fluorophore (GFP) and microinjected into the hermaphrodite gonad to produce a transgenic strain (
Fig. 1
). In the compact
C. elegans
genome, upstream intergenic regions are typically short (1–2 kb) and can be fused to a GFP reporter either by subcloning into an expression vector (see Fire Lab
C. elegans
Vector Kit, Addgene) or by overlap PCR (
Hobert 2002
). Because transgenic DNA is carried as a multicopy extrachromosomal array that may not be transmitted in the germ line, these “transcriptional” reporter constructs are usually microinjected with a co-selectable marker convenient for identifying transgenic progeny (
Fire and Waterston 1989
;
Mello
et al
. 1991
). Bright promoter::GFP fusions that are reliably expressed in specific tissues or cell types (e.g. coelomocytes, pharyngeal muscle) are convenient co-selectable markers (
Okkema
et al
. 1993
;
Loria
et al
. 2004
). Alternatively, a temperature-sensitive lethal allele of
pha-1
can be used for positive selection of transgenic arrays carrying a wild-type copy of the
pha-1
gene. In this case, maintenance at 25°C ensures highly penetrant inheritance of the transgenic array (
Granato
et al
. 1994
).

Advantages and limitations of transcriptional reporters
Although useful for many additional applications (see below), transgenic strains may not provide an accurate readout of endogenous gene expression due to the uncertain location of native gene regulatory regions. For example, intrinsic mechanisms that regulate mRNA stability (e.g. miRNA sites in 3′ UTRs) may be excluded from transgenic reporters that fuse GFP to presumptive promoter region. These problems can be overcome by using fosmid reporters that contain intact genomic flanking regions (
Sarov
et al
. 2006
;
Tursun
et al
. 2009
). However, transgenic arrays of either plasmids or fosmids can result in missing or ectopic expression likely due to mis-regulated expression from high copy number arrays.
Bicistronic transcriptional reporters that exploit SL2 trans-splicing
An additional indirect approach for detecting gene expression relies on the phenomenon of trans-splicing. In
C. elegans
, ∼70% of transcripts are spliced in
trans
(“trans-splicing”) at the 5′ end with a spliced leader (SL) sequence provided by a small nuclear ribonucleoprotein particle. Most of these transcripts are spliced to the SL1 sequence. However, for
C. elegans
operons, in which multiple genes are transcribed in tandem from a common promoter, downstream genes are trans-spliced with the SL2 sequence. The net effect of SL2 trans-splicing is the production of separate mRNAs for each gene in the operon (
Blumenthal 2018
). A series of bicistronic reporters have been developed to exploit SL2 trans-splicing for indirect monitoring of gene-specific transcripts (
Fig. 1c;
Tursun
et al
. 2009
). In this approach, an intergenic region from an operon that contains the SL2 trans-splicing site is inserted after the stop codon of a target gene and fused to the coding sequence for a fluorescent reporter (e.g. GFP). In this configuration, transcription of the native upstream gene results in a separate downstream fluorescent reporter transcript that is detected when it is translated. Notably, the native 3′ UTR is fused to the fluorescent reporter transcript and thus is expected to preserve intrinsic RNA regulatory mechanisms. In addition, the downstream reporter can be tagged with a nuclear localization sequence to facilitate cell identification (
Fig. 1c
).
The SL2 trans-splicing site can also be placed upstream of the target gene coding region such that the first transcript in the synthetic bicistronic operon encodes the fluorescent reporter followed by the SL2-tagged native gene transcript. Although initially designed for transgenic reporters (
Tursun
et al
. 2009
), a recent paper (
Sun
et al
. 2023
) demonstrated that the SL2-tagging construct can also be inserted at native loci by genome engineering (
Dickinson and Goldstein 2016
;
Nance and Frøkjaer-Jensen 2019
;
Martin and Calarco 2022
). In this study, a histone (H2B) protein fusion to GFP consistently yielded a brighter nuclear-localized signal than a canonical nuclear localization signal (NLS) sequence for a series of neuropeptide encoding genes (
Sun
et al
. 2023
).
Advantages and limitations of bicistronic transcriptional reporters
Genome engineering (
Dickinson and Goldstein 2016
;
Martin and Calarco 2022
) obviates potentially artifactual expression arising from multicopy transgenic arrays. However, SL2 trans-spliced transcripts may be expressed at lower levels than upstream SL1 trans-spliced transcripts and thus could underestimate expression for the native gene (
Cutter
et al
. 2009
;
Nance and Frøkjaer-Jensen 2019
). As noted below, bicistronic cassettes can also be utilized to monitor translation of native protein.
Direct detection of transcripts in situ
For direct measures of gene expression activity, in situ hybridization methods can be used to quantify transcript abundance (
Femino
et al
. 1998
;
Pichon
et al
. 2018
). A shared goal of these techniques is to detect specific transcripts throughout development in identified cells and in subcellular compartments. In all cases, oligonucleotide probes that are complementary to the target transcript are labeled with either an enzymatic or fluorescent marker to amplify the hybridization reaction for a locally resolved signal (
Albertson
et al
. 1995
;
Seydoux and Fire 1995
).
Enzymatic method for detecting transcripts in situ
An enzymatic method used for
C. elegans
relies on the incorporation of a plant steroid digoxigenin (DIG) into a labeled complementary DNA (cDNA) probe which was then binds an alkaline phosphatase–coupled antibody to DIG. A darkly colored enzymatic product can be detected in the microscope in specific cells (
Fig. 2
). Although the enzymatic method is highly sensitive, it is not quantitative and does not resolve transcripts in subcellular compartments. More recently, the enzymatic-labeled strategy has been largely supplanted by FISH methods which are equally sensitive but provide higher resolution and can be used for quantitative analysis.

Single-molecule FISH
First utilized to detect beta-actin transcripts in cultured cells, the original FISH protocol relied on a limited number of individual probes each one labeled with multiple fluorophores (
Femino
et al
. 1998
). An alternative and now widely used method utilizes multiple transcript-specific probes each labeled with a single fluorophore. The combined signal from multiple probes hybridized to a single RNA transcript can be detected as a bright individual punctum hence the moniker, “single-molecule FISH” (“smFISH”) (
Raj and Tyagi 2010
;
Fig. 2
). smFISH probes (20 bp) are readily synthesized and can be obtained from commercial sources (e.g. Biosearch Technologies), and hybridization protocols are well established (
https://biosearch-cdn.azureedge.net/assetsv6/bti_custom_stellaris_celegans_protocol.pdf
) (
Raj
et al
. 2008
;
Lee
et al
. 2017
;
Parker
et al
. 2021
). Importantly, the hybridization method is compatible with GFP and other protein fluorophores that can be used to label landmarks in situ. For example, a commonly used method is to express cytosolic GFP in a target cell and then to count smFISH puncta that reside within the circumference of the GFP signal (
Fig. 2
). The original smFISH protocol called for 48 transcript-specific probes (
Raj and Tyagi 2010
) but significantly fewer probes (20–25) can produce a detectable signal (R. Collings, D. Miller, unpublished). Downstream processing of confocal images (e.g. deconvolution) can significantly enhance the reliability and sensitivity of smFISH quantification (
Miller-Fleming
et al
. 2021
). The smFISH method is now well established for
C. elegans
and has been used for a range of applications including transcript quantification in somatic cells and in the gonad, and subcellular localization in the embryo and in neurons (
Arey
et al
. 2019
;
Winkenbach
et al
. 2022
;
Table 1
).

Single-molecule inexpensive FISH
At $800–$1,000 for a complete set of smFISH probes, cost can be limiting for experiments to detect multiple transcripts. “Single-molecule inexpensive FISH” (“smiFISH”), a recently developed variant of the original smFISH method, offers a less costly and more flexible approach. In this case, the transcript-specific probes include an adapter sequence for hybridization to a universal fluorescently labeled probe (
Fig. 2
;
Tsanov
et al
. 2016
). With this arrangement, the more expensive fluorescently labeled probe can be used for multiple different transcripts. This 2-step method has been used successfully to detect transcripts in embryos but has been less effective for postembryonic measurements perhaps due to limited permeability of the relatively large primary + secondary probe complex which is preannealed before hybridization (
Parker
et al
. 2021
;
Table 1
).
Methods for amplifying smFISH signals
An important limitation of the smFISH method for
C. elegans
is that many transcripts are too short (<1,000 nts) to yield the multiple (e.g. 25–48) oligonucleotide probes required for a robust signal. This consideration may be especially important for long noncoding RNAs which are typically <1,000 nts (
Akay
et al
. 2019
). More sensitive FISH techniques are available to circumvent this problem. The hybridization chain reaction (HCR) method relies on a nonenzymatic mechanism for amplifying a fluorescent signal arising from probes annealing with target RNA. In the HCR method (
Fig. 2
), an initial initiator probe hybridizes with the target RNA. An adapter sequence on the initiator probe then hybridizes with a fluorescently labeled hairpin probe to trigger HCR (
Fig. 2
;
Choi
et al
. 2018
). Because HCR-FISH is at least 10 × more sensitive than smFISH, it should be possible to detect shorter RNAs by using a small number of initiator probes (
Trimmer
et al
. 2023
) (
https://www.molecularinstruments.com/subcellular-singlemolecule-comparison
). Additional highly sensitive techniques are available for testing in
C. elegans
. In the “branched DNA” amplification method, sensitivity is enhanced by hybridizing the sample with a succession of oligonucleotides to produce a highly branched superstructure of fluorescent probes (
Player
et al
. 2001
;
Sinnamon and Czaplinski 2014
). A variation of this technique, RNAScope, uses “split” oligonucleotide “Z probes” for the initial hybridization step to enhance signal to noise (
Wang
et al
. 2012
). A more sensitive RNAScope protocol, “BaseScope,” is reported to detect alternatively spliced RNAs with single pairs of Z probes for each variant (
Erben
et al
. 2018
).
smFISH methods (multiplexed error-robust FISH, seqFISH) for simultaneous detection of multiple transcripts
By using different colored fluorophores, all of the above FISH methods are compatible with simultaneous detection of multiple transcripts, but the total number is limited to available channels (typically ≤5) in a light microscope. New methods, multiplexed error-robust FISH (MERFISH) (
Chen, Boettiger,
et al
. 2015
) and seqFISH (
Shah
et al
. 2016
), are designed for highly multiplexed imaging that in principle can detect 1,000 s of distinct RNAs in a single sample (
Eng
et al
. 2019
;
Xia
et al
. 2019
). Both MERFISH and seqFISH use a combinatorial labeling scheme to produce a distinct overall signal for each specific transcript and error correction methods to exclude noise. In the MERFISH approach, native transcripts are initially hybridized with RNA-specific probes, each one containing adaptor or “readout sequences” (
Fig. 2
). Each readout sequence can be detected with a fluorescently labeled complementary probe. Imaging from multiple rounds of hybridization with readout sequence probes detects the location of each fluorescent punctum to build a “digital code” (e.g. 001010…) of the combined signals that is unique to a given transcript. The MERFISH probes are designed to facilitate an error correction scheme that limits incorrect assignments (
Chen, Tillberg, et al. 2015
).
Limitations of MERFISH and seqFISH assays
Although the MERFISH and seqFISH schemes afford high-throughput detection of multiple unique transcripts, they are also limited by the requirement that each hybridization punctum must be spatially resolved in the light microscope for unambiguous detection (
Pichon
et al
. 2018
). This limitation is especially significant for
C. elegans
due to the small size of most somatic cells. Super-resolution microscopy could be used to resolve closely spaced puncta. In addition, cell size can be enlarged by expansion microscopy to resolve closely spaced puncta (
Chen, Tillberg, et al. 2015
;
Xia
et al
. 2019
). A related approach, exSeq (
Alon
et al
. 2021
), that relies on the combination of expansion microscopy and in situ sequencing has been recently applied to
C. elegans
in the Boyden lab (Zhang
et al.
, personal communication). The successful implementation of these additional techniques would be especially useful in
C. elegans
, for example, for correlating temporal gene expression with cell lineage or with specific subcellular compartments (
Table 1
).
Detecting RNA localization in live animals with MS2/PP7 reporter systems
Although smFISH methods are useful for imaging transcripts at steady-state levels, observing the dynamics of RNA localization and accumulation on short timescales of seconds to minutes requires imaging in live animals. A well-established fluorescent reporter-based approach that has been successfully implemented in
C. elegans
makes use of viral coat proteins MS2 and PP7 and their interaction with RNA stem loop structures (
Fig. 3a
;
Le
et al
. 2022
). In the original design, the coat protein is fused to a fluorescent protein (FP) (e.g. MS2::GFP) and multiple RNA stem loops are inserted into a gene of interest typically in either 5′ or 3′ UTRs. The stem loops engineered into the target RNA then capture the coat protein reporter to produce a local fluorescent signal.

Designing an MS2/PP7 reporter system for detecting RNA in vivo
Several considerations are important for implementing this system. In most strategies, a nuclear localization signal is included in the MS2/PP7 coat protein::GFP fusion to trap most of the intrinsic GFP fluorescence in the nucleus (
Yan
et al
. 2009
;
Li
et al
. 2021
). As newly transcribed RNA is produced (
Fig. 3a
), the coat protein::GFP marker binds and is exported along with the RNA into the cytoplasm, thus allowing visualization of individual transcripts in the cytoplasm. In one example, localization of
cebp-1
mRNA to touch neuron synapses was visualized by incorporating 6 MS2 stem loops in the 3′ UTR and co-expressing the MS2::NLS::GFP fusion protein (
Yan
et al
. 2009
).
cebp-1
localization was found to depend on its 3′ UTR and was influenced by
DLK-1
signaling, an established regulator of axon regeneration (
Yan
et al
. 2009
). In a more recent example, the subcellular localization and dynamics of mRNAs were studied in polarized epithelial cells (
Li
et al
. 2021
). Here, 24 copies of the PP7 stem loop were inserted into the 3′ UTR of candidate mRNAs and co-expressed with nuclear-localized PP7::mCherry fusion protein. With this approach, mRNA transcripts encoding a variety of apical, basolateral, and junctional localized proteins were monitored. Interestingly, these transcripts displayed biased localization trajectories and accumulated at membranes in close proximity to their corresponding proteins (
Li
et al
. 2021
), a phenomenon also observed in
C. elegans
embryonic cells (
Winkenbach
et al
. 2022
).
Single-copy insertion of MS2::GFP fusion protein for monitoring RNA dynamics
In most cases, candidate stem loop containing mRNAs and coat protein fusions were expressed from multicopy extrachromosomal arrays. However, to reduce potential overexpression artifacts, single-copy transgenes can be utilized. For example, in one study, mosSCI transgenesis (
Nance and Frøkjær-Jensen 2019
) was employed for single-copy expression in the germ line of an MS2::GFP fusion protein and a candidate mRNA (
sygl-1
, a notch target gene) engineered with 24 copies of the MS2 stem loop (
Lee
et al
. 2019
). This approach detected transcriptional bursting dynamics in germ line stem cells proximal to the distal tip cell and showed how these dynamics are altered in Notch signaling defective mutants (
Lee
et al
. 2019
).
Enhancing the sensitivity of the MS2 system for RNA detection
Another consideration when designing these reporters is a determination of the number of stem loops required for a robust signal. As mentioned above, in the case of overexpression, 6 stem loops proved sufficient (
Yan
et al
. 2009
). However, 24 stem loops are likely required for strong signal enrichment at physiologically relevant RNA expression levels (
Lee
et al
. 2019
). A recent study enhanced the sensitivity of the MS2 system by incorporating the SunTag peptide (
Hu
et al
. 2023
). In this strategy, 24 copies of the SunTag epitope are fused to an individual MS2 protein, with each MS2 fusion protein binding to an MS2 stem loop (
Fig. 3b
). Each SunTag can then recruit a genetically encoded single-chain fragment variable antibody (scFv) fused to GFP, effectively binding up to 48 GFP molecules per stem loop (
Fig. 3b
). In this assay, insertion of only 8 stem loops into an endogenous target gene by CRISPR genome editing (
Dickinson and Goldstein 2016
;
Nance and Frøkjaer-Jensen 2019
;
Martin and Calarco 2022
) was sufficient to track
C42D4.3
and
mai-1
transcripts, which are induced after wound generation in epidermal cells (
Hu
et al
. 2023
). In this configuration, both the MS2::SunTag and scFv–GFP fusion proteins were delivered as multicopy extrachromosomal arrays, but nonetheless resulted in high signal-to-noise ratios (
Hu
et al
. 2023
).
Limitations to MS2 reporter system and possible solutions
A recent study using the MS2 reporter system devised additional modfications that are needed for monitoring endogenous transcripts (
Tocchini and Mango 2023
). In this case, genome engineering (
Dickinson and Goldstein 2016
;
Nance and Frøkjaer-Jensen 2019
;
Martin and Calarco 2022
) to insert 24 stem loops into 3′ UTR sequences destabilized target mRNAs. Cytoplasmic expression of stem loop-tagged transcripts, however, could be rescued in nonsense-mediated decay (NMD)-deficient mutant backgrounds (
Tocchini and Mango 2023
). In addition, a single-copy insertion of the MS2::GFP fusion protein was used to achieve low level expression. This approach obviated the need for attaching an NLS to the MS2::GFP construct that might interfere with normal mRNA localization in the cytoplasm (
Tocchini and Mango 2023
;
Table 1
).
These results suggest that multicopy expression of the MS2::GFP protein is possible, but should either be nuclear-localized or paired with the SunTag amplification system for adequate signal-to-noise discrimination. Alternatively, modest single-copy expression of MS2::GFP in the cytoplasm combined with 24 stem loop repeats can be an effective strategy. In addition, reporter localization patterns in genetic backgrounds of interest should be compared with corresponding strains lacking an intact NMD pathway.
Detecting RNA processing
Transcriptome profiling experiments in
C. elegans

Gerstein
et al.
2010
;
Ramani
et al.
2011
;
Blazie
et al.
2015
,
2017
;
Kaletsky
et al.
2018
;
Steber
et al.
2019
;
Warner
et al.
2019
;
Koterniak

). An important goal now is to investigate the mechanisms regulating the spatiotemporal implementation of these RNA processing steps.
In situ hybridization methods for detecting alternative splicing
The HCR protocol (
Fig. 2
) and the BaseScope approach from Advanced Cell Diagnostics have resolved splice variants and individual splice junctions in other organisms (
Selvaraj
et al
. 2020
;
Bunda and Andrade 2022
;
Traenkner
et al
. 2023
) and could be applied to
C. elegans
. Monitoring alternative splicing patterns directly and in situ should offer deeper insights into the regulation of alternative isoform usage and localization.
Reporters for detecting RNA processing
In contrast to in situ approaches, the ability to monitor alternative splicing or alternative cleavage and polyadenylation in live animals opens the door to tractable and scalable genetic screening strategies to identify candidate regulators of these processes. Several reporters have been developed to monitor alternative splicing patterns. The simplest reporters fuse FPs in frame with specific isoforms, which can then be tested individually. Alternatively, a combination of different colored FPs can be transgenically co-expressed to demonstrate preferred usage of one isoform over another.
Two-color reporter assay for alternative splicing events
A second type of reporter utilizes 2 tandemly arranged FPs which are each translated in different reading frames (
Fig. 4a
;
Kuroyanagi
et al
. 2010
,
2013
;
Norris
et al
. 2014
;
Calarco and Pilaka-Akella 2022
). A candidate alternative splicing event, usually encompassing an alternative exon and its adjacent introns and exons (also known as a “mini-gene”), is cloned upstream of the FP sequences. Generally, alternative exons are encoded by a multiple of 3 nucleotides (i.e. frame-preserving). Thus, to induce a reading frame shift in the reporter, an extra nucleotide is inserted into the alternative exon. When the alternative exon is included in the final reporter mRNA, the reading frame encoding one FP (e.g. RFP) is used. When the alternative exon is skipped, a second reading frame is utilized, resulting in the translation of the other FP (e.g. GFP) (
Fig. 4a
). In both reporter designs, the FPs are used as an indirect readout of mRNA splicing. Importantly, similar live-cell splicing reporters have been successfully utilized in genetic screens to uncover regulators of tissue- and even neuron subtype–specific alternative splicing patterns, or to validate genome-wide measurements of splicing variation (
Kuroyanagi
et al
. 2007
,
2013
;
Norris
et al
. 2014
;
Tan and Fraser 2017
;
Thompson
et al
. 2019
).
Fig. 4.

An alternative exon (yellow) and its flanking introns and constitutively spliced exons (gray) are located upstream of the FP ORFs. A single nucleotide is inserted into the alternative exon, such that exon inclusion vs skipping results in 2 different reading frames for either mCherry or EGFP. Each FP is flanked by an N- and C-terminal nuclear localization signal (light blue), which concentrates the reporter signal in the nucleus, and an upstream 2A peptide (pink), which uncouples the 2X NLS-fused FP from the translated reporter. The relative amounts of red/green fluorescence provide an indirect readout of alternative splicing. b) A bicistronic dual 3′ UTR readout reporter. Two UTR isoforms of the same gene (yellow and extended light blue; short and long UTR variants created by alternative polyadenylation, respectively) are positioned downstream of one of 2 FPs fused to H2B. After transcription, the reporter pre-mRNA is trans-spliced through an SL2 regulatory sequence (pink), yielding 2 mature mRNAs, each containing a unique FP::H2B::3′ UTR fusion. Limitations of the RNA splicing reporter assay
Generally, splicing reporters are most useful as multicopy transgenes to afford sufficiently bright signals for microscopy and screening. The requirement for a reading frameshift to toggle between the fluorescent reporters, however, introduces some limitations. In particular, the “mini-genes” used in the constructs may not fully recapitulate endogenous alternative splicing patterns, since splicing regulatory elements may be positioned in distal locations. It is therefore recommended to implement an independent method of measuring endogenous isoform levels to confirm whether a reporter is recapitulating physiological expression patterns. Typically, these measurements are derived from cell-specific transcriptome profiling experiments (see below).
Reporters for alternative splicing of 3′ UTR sequences
In some cases, the choice of different cleavage and polyadenylation sites can lead to 3′ UTRs of varying lengths (
Tian and Manley 2017
). Different 3′ UTR sequences may interact with unique sets of regulatory factors such as RNA-binding proteins and miRNAs, which in turn can influence RNA localization, stability, and translation (
Mayr 2017
). In the case of alternative polyadenylation leading to varied 3′ UTR lengths, 2 elegant reporter systems have been developed to monitor the translational output of variant 3′ UTRs. These reporters utilize a bicistronic architecture to express 2 FPs from the same transcribed pre-mRNA, but rely on SL2 trans-splicing to produce 2 separate mature mRNAs with distinct 3′ UTRs (
Fig. 4b and c
). In a first design, the FPs are fused to 2 3′ UTR isoforms that are normally generated by alternative polyadenylation (
West
et al
. 2018
;
Fig. 4b
). As an example, cloning alternative 3′ UTRs from the
air-2
locus into this reporter configuration resulted in distinct patterns of red vs green fluorescence in the germline and in embryos, reflecting separate roles for each UTR variant in controlling protein expression in different tissues (
West
et al
. 2018
). In a second design, the upstream FP (red) is used as a reference signal for normalizing expression of the downstream fluorescent (green) protein, encoded by an mRNA tethered to a 3′ UTR that can be mutated to test for function (
Blazie
et al
. 2017
;
Fig. 4c
). Using this reporter design, 2 important miRNA binding sites (for miR50 and miR85) were identified in the longer alternative 3′ UTRs of
tct-1
and
rack-1
transcripts. For both reporter strategies, although 3′ UTR choice is not directly monitored, the impact of each UTR variant on final reporter translation can be assessed, providing functional readouts of cell type–specific activity. Thus, these reporters can serve as useful tools for the screening of cis elements (by mutagenesis) and regulatory factors (by forward or reverse genetics).
Whole-worm RNA-seq
Although the
C. elegans
genomic sequence was largely complete in 1998, a comprehensive map of transcribed regions (i.e. “genes”) required many additional years of work (
C. elegans
Sequencing Consortium 1998
;
Hillier
et al
. 2005
). Candidate
C. elegans
coding genes were initially identified in the genomic sequence by GeneFinder (P. Green, L. Hillier, unpublished), software that relied on a training set of validated coding regions for ab initio predictions (
Spieth and Lawson 2006
). The EST and Orfeome (
Reboul
et al
. 2003
;
Salehi-Ashtiani
et al
. 2009
) projects directly validated GeneFinder results and identified additional transcribed regions. With the advent of high-throughput sequencing methods, the balance of likely transcribed sequences was defined by whole-animal RNA-seq of different developmental stages and conditions (
Hillier
et al.
2009
;
Boeck
et al.
2011
;
Engelmann
et al.
2011
;
Warner
et al.
2019
).
Prior to the development of high-throughput RNA-seq (see below), microarrays were widely used for global analysis of gene expression and transcript discovery in
C. elegans
(
Jiang
et al.
2001
;
Portman 2006
;
von Stetina, Fox,
et al.
2007
;
Lu
et al.
2011
;
Spencer
et al.
2011
). With its higher dynamic range, reproducibility, and single-nucleotide resolution, RNA-seq has now largely supplanted microarray analysis. For whole-body RNA-seq, sufficient quantities of total RNA (∼10 μg) can be readily purified by conventional techniques (
Portman 2006
). In this case, it is convenient to isolate polyA + RNA and use a combination of poly-deoxythymidine (poly-dT) and random primers for RNA-seq library construction to achieve broad gene body coverage (
Hillier
et al
. 2009
). Long noncoding RNAs (lincRNAs) and pseudogenes, which are polyadenylated, are also captured by this approach (
Gerstein
et al.
2010
;
Nam and Bartel 2012
). Additional methods are required, however, to detect other classes of noncoding RNAs that are not polyadenylated (i.e. snoRNAs, miRNAs, tRNAs, piwi RNAs, endo-siRNAs). The use of random primers for cDNA synthesis, for example, effectively captures many nonpolyadenylated ncRNAs, but it is also typically necessary in this case to deplete ribosomal RNA (rRNA) sequences which comprise ∼95% of total RNA before sequencing (
Barrett
et al
. 2021
). Isolation methods that target small RNAs (<26 nts) are required for detecting miRNAs, tRNAs, piwi RNA, and endo-siRNAs (
Ambros
et al
. 2003
;
Ruby
et al
. 2006
;
Billi 2014
).
RNA-seq to detect tissue- and cell-specific gene expression
With its well-defined cell lineage and anatomy,
C. elegans
is especially amenable to the key goal of linking gene expression with cell fate determination and function. Two complementary strategies have been used to build cell-specific RNA-seq libraries. In one approach, RNA isolated from pools of defined cell types is used for RNA-seq library construction. With this “bulk” strategy, the specificity of the RNA-seq library is defined by the purity of the cell preparation. Alternatively, heterogeneous populations of cells can be profiled by scRNA-seq methods. In this case, specificity is achieved by sequencing each cell separately. Computational approaches are then applied to segregate the single-cell profiles into separate groups or “clusters” according to shared patterns of gene expression. We will review methods for bulk RNA-seq first and then summarize newer approaches that exploit scRNA-seq techniques.
Bulk RNA-seq of embryonic cells
In an early project to profile single embryonic cells, fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) was used to isolate a homogeneous population of 1-cell embryos, each marked with a GFP-tagged protein that is uniquely visible at the 1-cell stage. In this case, RNA-seq analysis was limited to small, noncoding RNAs (miRNAs, 26G-RNAs) (
Stoeckius
et al
. 2009
). A later effort used FACS to isolate differentiated embryonic cells labeled with specific fluorescent markers to generate an RNA-seq time series of 7 different tissues or lineages. This effort revealed dynamically expressed and tissue-specific transcripts and cataloged alternatively spliced exons (
Warner
et al.
2019
). More precise transcriptomic profiles of specific cells have been generated either by RNA-seq of manually dissected embryonic cells or by scRNA-seq technology (see below).
Bulk RNA-seq of postembryonic cells isolated by FACS
The notoriously tough cuticle surrounding larval and adult animals initially confounded efforts to isolate specific postembryonic cells for bulk RNA-seq. A solution to this challenging problem was first proposed in a landmark paper from Jeff Kuhn's lab that described a practical method for generating dissociated preparations of viable cells from larvae (
Zhang
et al
. 2011
). The dissociation protocol is straightforward and involves 2 basic steps: (1) treatment with detergent (SDS) plus a reducing agent (DTT) to permeabilize the cuticle and (2) application of a broad-spectrum protease (Pronase) accompanied by physical trituration to release viable cells (
Fig. 5a and b
;
Zhang and Kuhn 2013
). Immunostaining and tissue-specific GFP markers confirmed that muscle cells and neurons were released and could be maintained in culture for at least 1 week (
Zhang
et al
. 2011
). Although neurons were under-represented in these cultures relative to their in vivo abundance, a subsequent study established that multiple classes of viable larval neurons are initially released at the expected ratios by this cell dissociation method (
Spencer
et al
. 2014
). To demonstrate the utility of this approach for RNA-seq, the serotonergic NSM pharyngeal neurons were isolated by FACS directly from a preparation of dissociated L1 larvae. Bulk RNA-seq analysis confirmed enrichment of known NSM markers (e.g.
tph-1
,

,

et al.

Marking target cells for FACS isolation
A first requirement is to identify a cell-specific marker, usually a protein fluorophore that can be genetically engineered (e.g, GFP, RFP, etc.). In the simplest case, a single reporter is uniquely expressed in the target cell and thus can be readily isolated by FACS (
Spencer
et al
. 2014
). As a rule of thumb, a useful GFP marker, for example, should be bright enough that it can be easily detected by eye in a stereo-dissecting microscope equipped with epifluorescence (e.g. Leica M165). Off-target cells showing a visibly weaker signal can be excluded by FACS (
Fig. 5c
). Because cellular extensions (e.g. neuronal axons, excretory cell ducts) are typically severed by the cell dissociation method and can be detected in FACS scatter plots, nuclear-localized markers are preferred for accurate cell counts (
Arey
et al.
2019
;
Taylor
et al.
2021

Melentijevic
et al.
2017
;
Taylor
et al.
2021
,
2024

For cases in which no single marker is exclusively expressed in the target cell, it is frequently possible to devise a strategy involving 2 different colored markers (e.g. GFP vs RFP) that afford FACS isolation of the desired cell type. For example, if GFP and RFP are uniquely co-expressed in the target cell, dual-labeled (GFP + RFP) cells can be readily identified in the FACS profile and isolated for RNA-seq. Other dual-color combinations are also possible. For example, FACS can readily resolve a target cell that uniquely expresses one of the markers (e.g. GFP) vs all other cells that may express a different color (e.g. RFP) or both colors (e.g. GFP + RFP) (
Barrett
et al.
2021
;

et al.
2021
;
Taylor
et al.
2021

Spencer

Affinity-based methods for tissue-specific mRNA expression profiling
In addition to the use of FACS-based approaches to obtain cell-specific transcriptome data, several affinity purification-based methods have been developed to achieve similar goals. In general, an advantage of these affinity purification methods is that they do not require a flow cytometer for enrichment of RNA from cell types of interest. Another advantage of these methods is that samples are rapidly flash frozen prior to the purification of target transcript populations, enabling detection of dynamic changes in expression that may occur over time scales of minutes (
Table 2
).

Controls for PAT-seq
Like most immunoprecipitation experiments, important controls are required to assess nonspecific association of RNA with the affinity resin in the mRNA tagging method. Expression values from mRNA tagging/PAT-seq experiments are often presented as relative measurements of enrichment over whole-animal lysates or as comparisons between tissues. It can be useful to prepare a “mock” IP dataset from a strain that does not express the

Translating ribosome affinity purification
Initially developed for use in the

This arrested state is believed to be stable in the presence of the drug, thus eliminating the need for additional crosslinking steps prior to immunoprecipitation. After immunoprecipitation, the repertoire of actively translated mRNA from a particular tissue is subjected to high-throughput sequencing (
Fig. 6b
).
Since its initial development, the TRAP approach has been used in most model organisms, including

(

;

;

;

;

;

. 2023
). In an initial study in

, TRAP was used to capture mRNAs across 3 broad tissues and from several neuromodulatory neuron subtypes (

et al

). Enriched mRNAs were well correlated with independent measures of gene expression, included new tissue and neuronal subtype-enriched transcripts, and led to the identification of a novel regulator of serotonergic-dependent feeding behavior (

). Since this work, several variations of the approach and different transgenic strains have been developed expressing different ribosome subunits with a variety of epitope tags (

;

;

Advantages and limitations of the TRAP method
An advantage of the TRAP approach, as with other affinity-based methods [i.e. mRNA tagging/PAT-Seq, isolation of nuclei tagged in specific cell types (INTACT)], is that animals are flash frozen in liquid nitrogen within minutes of collection as a live sample. Rapid collection ensures that the repertoire of mRNAs isolated from the target cell should represent the state of the animal at the time of collection and thus obviates potential artifactual changes in transcripts that could occur during extended cell dissociation and FACS protocols. For example, recent studies have applied TRAP to profile the transcriptional responses of specific neurons to defined stimuli (
Rhoades
et al
. 2019
;
Liu
et al
. 2022
;
McLachlan
et al
. 2022
;
Harris
et al
. 2023
;
Wu
et al
. 2023
). One limitation of the TRAP approach is that only actively translated mRNAs are recovered from the immunoprecipitation step. In addition, noncoding RNAs are excluded. Thus, RNAs that are not translated are not captured, as well as any RNA species not engaged by the ribosome during translation. An additional limitation of TRAP is that profiling a new cell type requires the construction of a transgenic strain expressing an epitope-tagged ribosome subunit, as opposed to the FACS technique which can utilize a large array of available fluorescent marker strains (
Barrett
et al.
2021
;
Taylor
et al.
2021
;
Table 2
).
Isolation of nuclei tagged in specific cell types
Originally developed for purifying nuclei from the root epidermis of plants (

), the INTACT has been utilized to produce cell-specific profiles. In this approach, the nuclear pore protein
NPP-9
is fused with a biotin ligase recognition peptide (BLRP). With expression of

::BLRP fusion protein can be targeted for biotinylation (

). Expression of

::BLRP fusion protein under the control of different promoters enables the biotinylation of nuclei from specific cell and tissue types (

et al
. 2012
;
Fig. 6c
). In the initial implementation of INTACT in

, muscle nuclei were purified for analysis of nucleosome occupancy and gene expression (

et al

). Recently, a modified version of the protocol was used to detect gene regulation by heterochronic genes (

and

) in developing post mitotic neurons (

) and to identify transcripts regulated by the Cut family of homeodomain proteins in the nervous system (
Leyva-Díaz and Hobert 2022
).
As described above, an advantage of the INTACT method is that both gene expression and chromatin (i.e. chromatin IP or nucleosome occupancy) profiling experiments can be performed. One limitation of this approach, however, is that captured RNA is exclusively derived from the nucleus and thus would not reflect post-transcriptional regulation in the cytoplasm contributing to the final abundance of RNA (
Table 2
).
Strategies for enrichment of RNA species and cDNA library preparation
Here, we highlight methods for the selective enrichment or depletion of selected RNAs and the construction of cDNA libraries for high-throughput sequencing.
3′ biased sequencing of cDNA derived from mRNA
Several cDNA synthesis strategies have been employed in
C. elegans
for RNA-seq analysis. All of these approaches exploit the polyadenylated (polyA) tail added to nearly all eukaryotic mRNAs and some long noncoding RNAs. In the simplest strategy, poly-dT oligonucleotide is annealed at the 5′ end of the polyA tail to prime first-strand cDNA synthesis by reverse transcriptase (
Fig. 7a
). An advantage of this approach is that mRNA purification is not required, and thus, even small amounts of total RNA can be utilized for cDNA synthesis. In addition, poly-dT priming affords reliable coverage of 3′ end cleavage and polyadenylation sites. However, poly-dT priming can result in biased coverage of 3′ ends and the consequent under-representation of reads from the 5′ region. Despite this caveat, poly-dT priming is the simplest method for most RNA-seq experiments such as quantification of differentially expressed (DE) transcripts. Commercially available kits, such as the QuantSeq Library prep kit from Lexogen, make use of this strategy (
Corley
et al
. 2019
;
Ma
et al
. 2019
).

Full-length sequencing of cDNA derived from mRNA
Switching mechanism at the 5′-end of the RNA transcript (SMART) (
Zhu
et al
. 2001
) relies on poly-dT priming but utilizes a phenomenon known as template switching to enrich for full-length cDNAs.
Moloney murine leukemia virus
reverse transcriptase enzymes can add nontemplated deoxycytidines to the 3′ termini of synthesized cDNA (i.e. first strand) (
Fig. 7b
). This novel polyC region can then be exploited by base pairing with a template switching oligonucleotide. By adding known sequences to the template switching oligonucleotide and the poly-dT reverse transcription (RT) primer, full-length cDNA can be enriched by PCR and further tagged by “tagmentation” using transposases that both fragment the DNA and add adapters for short-read sequencing (
Fig. 7b
;
Hagemann-Jensen
et al
. 2020
). SMART is the method of choice for generating full-length transcripts from small amounts of RNA as low as 10 picograms, such as from sorted cells, affinity-purified mRNA, or for scRNA-seq experiments. Commercial kits that typically yield good results in
C. elegans
are available (e.g. SMART-seq from Takara).
Purification of polyA + RNA for cDNA synthesis and library construction
mRNA can be isolated by poly-dT oligonucleotide affinity purification. Because other more abundant RNA species (e.g. rRNAs) are excluded by this approach, cDNA synthesis can proceed with both poly-dT and random primers (
Fig. 7c
). This strategy ensures a more uniform distribution of reads across the transcript than protocols that depend on 3′ end priming from intact mRNA and have been utilized for quantification of regulated alternative splicing and alternative promoter/first exon usage. A disadvantage with mRNA purification protocols is that significantly more total RNA is needed to ensure isolation of sufficient quantities of polyA + RNA for the downstream steps. Commercially available options for this strategy include the TruSeq (Illumina) and NEBNext (New England Biolabs) series of kits (
Rhodes
et al
. 2014
).
rRNA depletion to achieve whole transcriptome coverage and detection on nonpolyadenylated RNAs
Although random priming can be used to generate cDNAs from isolated RNAs, cDNAs for rRNA, which constitutes ∼95% of total RNA, are the dominant product. To circumvent this problem, methods have been developed for selectively depleting rRNA before sequencing. A recently developed method relies on a custom pool of oligonucleotides targeting
C. elegans
rRNA sequences for removal by nuclease digestion (
Barrett
et al
. 2021
;
Fig. 7d
). In comparison with a library generated by poly-dT priming (SMART-seq), rRNA depletion (Solo Ovation) more reliably detected longer transcripts. The Solo Ovation method also detected nonpolyadenylated RNAs which can function as key regulators of development and cellular physiology. Another strategy that could be tested for
C. elegans
utilizes CRISPR/Cas9 to cleave cDNA from rRNA samples, preventing amplification of these species during library construction (
Gu
et al
. 2016
).
Small RNA cloning strategies for high-throughput sequencing
C. elegans
has served as a pioneering model system for studies of small regulatory RNAs, including the identification of the first microRNA (miRNA), the discovery of small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) and the phenomenon of RNA interference (
Lee
et al
. 1993
;
Wightman
et al
. 1993
;
Fire
et al
. 1998
). Since these initial breakthroughs, it is now well established that small RNA species can regulate many facets of gene expression.
Initial cloning efforts revealed that small RNAs can be modified with small adducts, such as a 5′ triphosphate or a 5′ cap (
Dai and Gu 2020
;
Li, Dai,
et al
. 2020
). Most commercial kits recommend the use of specific enzymatic treatments to remove RNA modifications to produce a 5′ monophosphate group and 3′ hydroxyl group for ligation with adapters (
Fig. 8
). Enzymes such as RNA 5′ pyrophosphohydrolase (RppH) can remove pyrophosphate from a triphosphate group (
Deana
et al
. 2008
), while the human decapping enzyme can remove the 5′ cap (
Grudzien-Nogalska and Kiledjian 2017
). Other approaches ligate an adapter to the 3′ end of the RNA and then perform a RT reaction to synthesize first-strand cDNA. The cDNA can then be ligated with a second adapter at its 3′ end (
Pak and Fire 2007
) and circularized (
Kwon 2011
), or a template switching strategy can be employed for second strand synthesis (
Ko and Lee 2006
). Recently, a single test tube approach has been developed to perform all enzymatic manipulations which remove 5′ modifications and ligate the necessary adapters to enrich and amplify small RNA populations (
Li, Dai,
et al
. 2020
;
Fig. 8
). This approach appears to afford versatility and specificity, while enabling the purification of small RNA populations from as little as 20 ng of total RNA.

Next-generation sequencing methods
Next-generation methods can be classified as either short-read or long-read sequencing. Because different methodologies are employed for generating and analyzing short- vs long-read sequences, we will review each method separately.
Short-read sequencing (Illumina)
The “sequencing by synthesis” approach, initially developed by Solexa and subsequently acquired by Illumina, can generate hundreds of millions of short sequence reads from libraries of cDNA reverse-transcribed from a starting pool of RNA (
Uhlen and Quake 2023
). This high-throughput sequencing approach is currently the most used method for differential gene expression and splice variant analysis and offers the highest base call accuracy, depth of coverage and throughput, and the lowest cost per nucleotide (
Logsdon
et al
. 2020
).
To provide enough read depth for detecting and measuring differences in local splice variation at exon junctions across different samples, 50–100 million mapped reads per sample are recommended. This estimate is based on assessing the ability to detect alternative splice junctions and differential splicing patterns as a function of read depth, both from whole-animal and cell type–enriched transcriptome data (
Ramani
et al.
2011
;
Koterniak
et al.
2020
). The higher sequencing depth is needed, in this case, to capture the small subset of reads that span splice junctions among the total sequences mapped. Given that 26 billion reads can be generated in a single flow cell of the state-of-the-art NovaSeq X instrument (specifications from Illumina website), in principle, more than 250 individual
C. elegans
transcriptome samples could be sequenced in a multiplexed fashion.
Analysis of bulk transcriptome data
Several methods are available for processing and analyzing bulk RNA-seq data derived from short-read sequencing. Given the large number of available options and the emergence of new tools each year, we will focus on examples of commonly used approaches with useful documentation and used in our own studies (
Fig. 9
). At the end of this section, we direct the reader to software with graphical user interface (GUI) options for those who prefer to avoid command line formats. We focus on analysis tools developed for short-read data keeping in mind that additional specialized tools should emerge for the analysis of long-read sequences (see below) (
Amarasinghe
et al
. 2021
).

Quality control and preprocessing of sequencing data
The quality of sequencing libraries should be confirmed before beginning any analysis pipeline (
Fig. 9
). Common problems include a drop in base calling quality at later cycles of sequencing runs, over-representation of specific sequences, shorter than expected cDNA inserts, and adapter dimers. A popular tool to check for some of these potential problems is FastQC (
https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/
), which provides users with visual plots and graphics to assess the quality of sequencing data. If sequencing adapters are retained at the ends of reads, which can happen when library inserts are short, they can be trimmed with programs like Cutadapt (
Martin 2011
) or Trimmomatic (
Bolger
et al
. 2014
). Trimmomatic also offers additional processing features such as filtering reads with poor quality scores. After processing, the user can proceed to map reads and generate gene or transcript-centered counts from the sequencing data. In many instances, quality control software is routinely integrated into services provided by institutional sequencing cores which should be consulted before initiating RNA-seq projects.
Read mapping
Identifying DE genes among distinct experimental conditions, developmental stages, or genotypes is a common goal of many RNA-seq experiments. Several steps are required to obtain candidate lists of DE genes (
Fig. 9
). Fortunately,
C. elegans
is a well-studied metazoan, with a fully sequenced genome and an active community that continues to refine gene models. Indeed,
C. elegans
was the first animal with a completed genome sequence (
C. elegans
Sequencing Consortium 1998
), with the most up-to-date genome assembly and gene annotations maintained on databases such as WormBase (
Davis
et al
. 2022
) and Ensembl (
Martin, Amode,
et al
. 2023
). These resources can be utilized for read mapping to the current genome build and assignment to correct gene structures. Popular alignment algorithms include Spliced Transcripts Alignment to a Reference (STAR) (
Dobin
et al
. 2013
) and Hierarchical Indexing for Spliced Alignment of Transcripts 2 (HISAT2) (
Kim
et al
. 2019
). Both STAR and HISAT2 are “splicing aware” aligners capable of mapping both exon body and splice junction-spanning reads (
Corchete
et al
. 2020
). A recently developed software package, Salmon, is less memory intensive than STAR or HISAT2 (
Patro
et al
. 2017
) and functions as an all-in-one mapping and transcript abundance pipeline.
Gene counts
For alignment-based software, outputs in the form of SAM/BAM files are used to generate gene-centered counts quantified for each gene (
Fig. 9
). Two commonly used packages, HTSeq and FeatureCounts, offer multiple parameters for adjusting the stringency of gene assignments and counting (
Liao
et al
. 2014
;
Anders
et al
. 2015
). At the end of this process, each sample is described in a tabular format of raw counts associated with rows of individual genes. With short-read data, an additional consideration when counting and assigning reads to particular gene loci is whether to include multimapping reads (i.e. reads that can be mapped to multiple sites in the genome). The simplest and most conservative approach is to omit these reads and keep only uniquely mapping sequences to gene models. Other strategies include equally dividing these reads across all matching loci, or proportionally assigning them based on information learned from any uniquely mapping reads. For a more detailed discussion of this topic, we recommend a recent review (
Deschamps-Francoeur
et al
. 2020
).
Normalization and differential expression
Once raw read counts are obtained, some form of normalization is required to account for technical variability between samples and variability due to sampling bias inherent in short-read data (
Fig. 9
;
Evans
et al
. 2018
). The simplest form of normalization involves correcting for factors such as library size and the length of the mRNA of individual genes. These considerations convert read count data into commonly used RPKM/FPKM (Reads/Fragments per kilobase of exon model per million mapped reads) and TPM (Transcripts Per Million) (
Conesa
et al
. 2016
). This form of normalization is useful for assessing the relative expression of genes within a sample and assigning confidence to whether a particular gene is actually expressed.
Given the importance of quantifying differential expression, this problem has attracted much focus and many algorithms have been developed, each using distinct normalization and differential gene expression calling strategies (
Fig. 9
;
Conesa
et al
. 2016
). Two of the most commonly used packages, DESeq2 and EdgeR, employ somewhat more sophisticated normalization strategies and statistical approaches, taking into account the non-Gaussian distribution and high variance found in RNA-seq short-read count data (
Robinson
et al
. 2010
;
Love
et al
. 2014
). In addition, these packages can perform pair-wise comparisons or multisample comparisons and work well with small numbers of replicate samples and are thus good starting points.
Alternative splicing analysis
In addition to steady-state transcript differences shaping cellular identity and physiology, alternative splicing, alternative promoter choice, and alternative cleavage and polyadenylation are mechanisms that also diversify the transcriptome (
Fig. 9
;
Nilsen and Graveley 2010
). Indeed, several studies have demonstrated that genes that are differentially expressed between identified cell types typically define a separate group vs genes that undergo differential splicing (
Pan
et al.
2004
;
Koterniak
et al.
2020
). Thus, identifying transcript isoforms and characterizing their differential expression across cells and as an organism develops or responds to its environment are important goals of RNA-seq analysis.
Short-read sequencing can detect local alternative splicing
Despite its advantages, one limitation of the Illumina platform is the short length of the sequencing reads. Illumina reads are currently limited to 2 × 150–300 nucleotides (i.e. one read from each end of the sequenced DNA for paired-end reads). Because most full-length cDNAs exceed this limit, individual isoforms with differing 5′ and 3′ ends or with multiple internal alternative splice junctions are usually not captured in a single read (
Fig. 10
). For example, the
slo-1
locus undergoes extensive alternative splicing, and encodes at least 12 different curated isoforms through utilization of multiple alternative exons (
Glauser
et al
. 2011
;
Johnson
et al
. 2011
). As a result, for Illumina sequencing, abundance measurements are typically gene centered (i.e. aggregating all isoforms) and splicing variation is measured locally (i.e. for adjacent exons) (
Fig. 10
). For most applications, the short-read length format is sufficient to obtain reliable mapping and quantification of gene expression and local alternative splicing (
Fig. 9
) and has been used in a large number of studies of
C. elegans
(for several recent examples, see:
Cao
et al.
2017
;
Kaletsky
et al.
2018
;
Warner
et al.
2019
;
Koterniak
et al.
2020
;
Taylor
et al.
2021
;
Weinreb
et al
. 2024
;
Wolfe
et al
. 2024
).

Software packages for quantifying alternative splicing
Two reliable software packages with good documentation for quantifying alternative splicing variation are Majiq/Voila (
Vaquero-Garcia
et al
. 2016
) and Vertebrate Alternative Splicing and Transcription tools (VAST-tools) (
Irimia
et al
. 2014
). VAST-tools uses curated isoform libraries from a variety of species, including
C. elegans
, and classifies different types of alternative splicing events (intron retention, exon skipping, alternative splice site choice, and microexon regulation). Majiq/Voila includes the added ability to identify local splicing variation among both annotated and previously unannotated splice junctions, enabling the discovery of novel splice variants. Each package also incorporates modules for data visualization.
Clustering, Gene Ontology, pathway enrichment, and interactome analysis
After identifying DE or alternatively spliced transcripts, it is important to extract functional information from these gene lists. By discovering common features among the repertoire of regulated genes, it may be possible to pinpoint biological pathways or networks contributing to cell differentiation and physiology.
Clustering genes from shared expression profiles can reveal functional groups
Clustering approaches can group genes based on shared expression profiles, which often reflect shared biological functions (
Eisen
et al
. 1998
). Available packages in common coding languages like R and Python perform standard clustering approaches through Bioconductor or Biopython (
Cock
et al
. 2009
;
Huber
et al
. 2015
). After clustering, data are commonly displayed in a heatmap, which allows for further exploration of individual modules (
Fig. 9
). An excellent package for creating and modifying heatmaps is the “ComplexHeatmap’ package in R (
Gu 2022
). Alternatively, the TreeView software package offers a convenient GUI to perform clustering and visualization (
Keil
et al
. 2018
). Two commonly used clustering techniques are hierarchical clustering and k-means clustering. In hierarchical clustering, both genes and sample conditions can be clustered or ordered in a dendrogram- or tree-like fashion by assessing their relative similarity (by metrics such as the Pearson correlation) to all other genes and sample conditions, respectively. In k-means clustering, the user defines how many clusters should be formed, and this can dictate the granularity at which networks of co-regulated genes can be grouped together. In both approaches, further exploration of clustered groups is required to assess biological relevance. For more details about clustering approaches, see some recent reviews (
Altman and Krzywinski 2017
) (
https://www.intechopen.com/chapters/73567
).
Gene Ontology and gene set enrichment analysis
Another strategy for identifying functional modules within larger gene lists is to detect over-representation of Gene Ontology (GO) terms or to use the related technique of Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) (
Fig. 9
). Several software packages, such as the Database for Annotation Visualization and Integrated Discovery (
Sherman
et al
. 2022
), web-based GEne SeT AnaLysis Toolkit (WebGestalt) (
Liao
et al
. 2019
), and ClusterProfiler (
Wu
et al
. 2021
) can perform multiple types of comparisons to map genes from a transcriptome experiment onto biological processes and pathways. A recently developed
C. elegans
-centric gene set analysis tool called WormCat (
Holdorf
et al
. 2020
) utilizes creative visualization strategies to accommodate the multidimensional nature of functional annotations. Another method called Deep EXtraction Independent Component Analysis used independent component analysis and a compendium of transcriptome profiling experiments to define 209 gene modules based on co-expression signatures derived from extant microarray experiments (
Cary
et al
. 2020
).
Comparison to protein interaction data
Co-expressed genes, in addition to potentially belonging to shared pathways, may also associate in protein complexes. Thus, mapping differentially regulated genes onto protein interaction datasets is another useful way to probe for functional modules in RNA-seq data. Several curated protein interaction databases are available, including the well-maintained STRING database (
Szklarczyk
et al
. 2023
). Using STRING, a user can query proteins of interest and visualize network diagrams incorporating various forms of protein interaction data (e.g. biochemical evidence, yeast 2 hybrid results), co-expression data, and even text mining co-occurrence (
Fig. 9
). Interactome data from orthologs are also incorporated into the query network, which can be useful when direct evidence from
C. elegans
is limited (
Szklarczyk
et al
. 2023
). An available plugin for STRING offers access to the popular network visualization tool Cytoscape for additional analysis (
Shannon
et al
. 2003
).
GUI software for transcriptome analysis
Some of the tools described above require expertise with command line programing. Several options are available for users who prefer GUIs. Galaxy is a freely available web-based platform with a full suite of quality control, alignment, count, normalization and differential expression calling tools controllable through the GUI (
The Galaxy Community 2022
). CLC Workbench from Qiagen (
https://digitalinsights.qiagen.com/
) also offers an integrated GUI for genomics analysis although this software is available for a fee. As mentioned above, TreeView is a stand-alone GUI accommodating several clustering methods and heatmap visualization (
Keil
et al
. 2018
). All the GO and gene set enrichment tools described above are also GUIs. Finally, a recent analysis pipeline called RNAlysis (
Teichman
et al
. 2023
) has been developed to perform most of the common processing and analysis steps in a user-friendly interface tailored to those without any coding experience.
Long-read RNA-seq
Currently, most isoform quantification experiments have relied on short-read RNA-seq data to detect “local” splicing or exon junction usage variation (
Fig. 10
). As noted above, however, short-read data are usually insufficient to resolve the co-occurrence of specific alternative splice junctions at distant locations within the same mRNA molecule.
Long-read sequencing methods, primarily offered by Pacific Biosciences (PacBio) or Oxford Nanopore Technologies, can generate long reads ranging from tens of kilobases (PacBio) to several million bases (Nanopore) (
Amarasinghe
et al
. 2020
;
Logsdon
et al
. 2020
;
Fig. 10
). These read lengths can easily accommodate full-length transcripts in
C. elegans
and are thus particularly useful for detecting exon usage across individual transcripts.
Nanopore long-read sequencing of cDNA and RNA
Nanopore cDNA sequencing has confirmed that the majority of
C. elegans
transcripts are trans-spliced with either SL1 or SL2 spliced leader sequences at the 5′ end. Interestingly, transcripts that are not trans-spliced contain 5′ RNA hairpin structures that mimic the trans-SL conformation (
Bernard
et al
. 2023
).
An added advantage of Nanopore sequencing is that either DNA or RNA can be directly sequenced, eliminating the strict requirement of preparing cDNA libraries for transcript detection (
Amarasinghe
et al
. 2020
). For example, thousands of novel full-length isoforms were identified by sequencing
C. elegans
RNA from different developmental stages and from male-enriched populations. Additionally, extensive variation was detected at the 3′ ends of mRNAs, including the use of alternative 3′ UTR sequences and differences in the length of polyA tails (
Li, Ren,
et al
. 2020
;
Roach
et al
. 2020
). A recent study used direct RNA-seq to capture degradation intermediates in the NMD pathway and revealed key roles for
SMG-5
and
SMG-6
in endonucleolytic cleavage (
Viscardi and Arribere 2023
). Thus, additional sequence features of transcripts, full-length splice variants, and even degradation intermediates can be characterized using long-read sequencing.
Detecting chemical modifications by Nanopore direct RNA-seq
Another distinct advantage of direct RNA-seq is that chemical modifications of RNA can be identified by the Nanopore platform. This application was used in
C. elegans
to detect the N
6
-methyl adenosine (m
6
A) modification in specific pre-mRNAs and to show that the m
6
A modification resulted in alternative splicing and eventual degradation of these transcripts (
Watabe
et al
. 2021
). Interestingly, these m
6
A-modified transcripts are encoded by a family of
S
-adenosyl-
L-
methionine (SAM) synthetase (“
sams
”) genes, which provide the substrate (SAM) for the m
6
A modification. Additional analysis revealed an autoregulatory loop that couples RNA splicing, RNA methylation, and SAM synthetase levels: when SAM levels are high, increased deposition of m
6
A occurs, which in turn leads to altered splicing and degradation of SAMS transcripts, eventually reducing SAM production (
Mendel
et al.
2021
;
Watabe
et al.
2021
). These results thus demonstrated that m
6
A post-transcriptional modifications can alter RNA function and metabolism in
C. elegans
as previously shown for other animals (
Jiang
et al
. 2021
). In principle, other RNA modifications could be mapped using Nanopore technology and may have further application in
C. elegans
.
Limitations of long-read sequencing strategies
As mentioned above, long-read sequencing strategies are capable of full isoform reconstruction and annotation. Long-read sequencing approaches, however, are currently less useful for measurements of differential gene expression, largely due to the higher cost of generating sufficient coverage for quantitative analysis (
Joglekar
et al
. 2021
,
2024
;
Pardo-Palacios
et al
. 2024
). However, the PromethION platform from Oxford Nanopore Technologies is closing the cost gap (
Logsdon
et al
. 2020
).
Single-cell RNA sequencing
As noted above, a major benefit of bulk RNA-seq is the opportunity to obtain deep, whole transcriptome coverage for defined tissues and specific cell types (
Fig. 9
). When considering the alternative of scRNA-seq, it may be useful to consider disadvantages of the bulk RNA-seq approach. First, methods for isolating specific cell types (e.g. FACS, INTACT) depend on the availability of markers for the target cell type, which may not be available (
Figs. 5
and
6
). Second, data accumulation is time-intensive. For example, at the rate of 1 sample/week, a month is required to generate 3–4 replicates using the FACS method for each individual tissue/cell type. Third, although FACS or other cell-specific isolation methods can enrich for the cell type of interest, nontarget cells and the resultant contaminating RNA are almost always collected with the sample (
Barrett
et al
. 2022
). In contrast, scRNA-seq does not depend on selective isolation of a pool of target cells, can be implemented quickly, and is stochastic, effectively achieving “purity” by randomly profiling individual cells. scRNA-seq can also capture heterogeneity in gene expression within a given cell type (
Table 2
).
Physical dissection of individual cells for scRNA-seq
Early attempts to document transcriptomes from single cells in
C. elegans
relied on physical dissection. Embryos are readily dissociated by treatment with chitinase and individual cells can be captured with a micropipette for single-cell sequencing (
Hashimshony
et al
. 2012
,
2015
;
Nishimura
et al
. 2015
;
Tintori
et al
. 2016
;
Cole
et al
. 2024
). For postembryonic profiling, fluorescently labeled cell types were physically dissected (
Schwarz
et al
. 2012
;
Lockhead
et al
. 2016
;
Nath
et al
. 2016
;
Hsueh
et al
. 2017
), then pooled for RNA isolation, followed by methods similar to bulk RNA-seq. The technical difficulty of physical dissection from larvae and adults meant that only a small number of cells (≤20) could be profiled. A recent report demonstrated that femtosecond laser microdissection can also be used to isolate individual neurons for transcriptome profiling of >40 cells. While labor-intensive and requiring a costly laser setup, this approach has the advantage of capturing single cells from individual worms with known experimental phenotypes. For example, in this case, single-cell profiles could be correlated with the axon regeneration phenotype of each targeted neuron (
Zhao
et al
. 2023
;
Table 2
).

Labeling and amplifying cell-specific transcripts for scRNA-seq
In scRNA-seq, the RT reaction appends unique barcodes to cDNAs from each individual cell (
Table 3
). The cDNA from many cells is then pooled for amplification, library construction, and sequencing (
Fig. 11b
). During data analysis, the unique cell barcodes permit the assignment of reads to the cell of origin, thus resolving the transcriptomes of each individual cell. Various single-cell technologies are available. They primarily differ in the methods of partitioning cells into individual compartments for RNA capture and the RT reaction (see below). The small amount of RNA in a given cell requires multiple rounds of amplification to produce sufficient cDNA for sequencing. To reduce bias arising from unequal amplification, most scRNA-seq platforms use unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) to label each individual transcript. After sequencing, each UMI is counted only once so that the overall abundance of a transcript is determined by the sum of corresponding UMIs in the dataset (
Fig. 11b
).
Table 3.
Definitions for scRNA-seq analysis.
Term
Definition
Barcode
A predefined oligonucleotide added to each cDNA during RT or PCR amplification that labels the transcript from a specific cell or experimental sample. Barcodes are distinguished from cDNA in the analysis pipeline. AKA, “cell index or sample index.”
Unique molecular identifier (UMI)
A unique sequence (10–12 nucleotides) added to each cDNA molecule as it is synthesized. Reads containing the same UMI, cell barcode, and RNA sequences come from the same transcript and are collapsed into 1 count.
Empty droplets
Droplets that do not contain a cell, but may contain background RNA molecules from lysed cells.
Normalization
Mathematical transformation to account for differences in sequencing coverage between cells, e.g. variation in RNA capture or PCR amplification between cells. A commonly used method: Scale the UMI counts for each cell by a cell-specific scaling factor.
Integration
Combining data from multiple experiments. This can include replicates (technical or biological) or data from different experimental groups (ages/genotypes/treatments).
Dimensionality reduction
Algorithms that reduce the complexity of the dataset by compressing the information about multiple features (e.g. genes) into a smaller number of values typically in 2 steps: (1) PCA, followed by (2) nonlinear methods such as t-SNE or UMAP.
Principal component analysis (PCA)
Reduces dataset complexity by condensing multiple correlated features into a smaller number of values known as principal components (PCs). PCA typically used to reduce data from tens of thousands of genes to 50–100 PCs that capture most of the variability in the original dataset.
Visualization
Graphing the relationships between single-cell expression data, usually with t-SNE or UMAP plots.
Clustering
Defining groups of cells with similar gene expression typically by unsupervised learning. Most commonly, clustering is performed on the reduced dimensionality data (either PCA or t-SNE/UMAP).
Cell annotation
Assigning an anatomical identity to cells in scRNA-seq datasets that depends on independently derived biological knowledge of gene expression in specific cell types. Often the first step is to determine enriched genes for each cluster, followed by comparison to known gene expression profiles for anatomical cell types.
Doublet identification
Identifying droplets containing 2 or more cells that could be mistaken for unique cell types.
How many cells are needed for a robust scRNA-seq profile?
scRNA-seq techniques typically capture only a fraction of the RNA from an individual cell. The latest version of the 10X Genomics platform, Chromium v3, for example, is estimated to capture ∼30% of mRNA transcripts per cell (
Zheng
et al
. 2017
) (10X Genomics website). The under-sampling problem is mitigated by sequencing many cells of each type. As RNA capture is stochastic, single cells corresponding to an individual cell type will feature partially overlapping transcriptional profiles. Thus, aggregating the expression of genes across all the cells corresponding to an individual cell type generates a more robust transcriptional profile. An important consequence is a higher fraction of false negatives in gene detection for cell types that are lowly represented in a dataset. For example, down-sampling of 51 neuronal cell types represented by >500 individual cells each indicated that ∼25 cells were required to capture at least 50% of the predicted maximal number of genes per cell type (
Taylor
et al.
2021
). It is important to emphasize, however, that these calculations may underestimate gene number. Improvements in sequencing technology and analysis are needed for more accurate determinations of ground truth for genes expressed in each cell (
Barrett
et al
. 2022
).

Capturing fixed cells for droplet-based scRNA-seq
As an alternative to live-cell scRNA-seq, dissociated cells can be fixed in methanol and stored at ≤ −20°C (

et al.
2023

et al.
2023

In addition, background RNA levels (i.e. ambient RNA in all droplets) are significantly higher for methanol-fixed cells (∼25%) than for live cells (∼5–10%) (

et al.
2021

Long-read sequencing obviates 3′ bias of droplet-based scRNA-seq methods
The 10X Genomics platform has been widely used for scRNA-seq in

Fig. 10

Typically, cDNA is split into different pools for short-read vs long-read sequencing. This approach has generated RNA isoform atlases from

et al

;

et al

C. elegans
(A. Weinreb, M. Hammarlund, personal communication). Some limitations include low sequencing depth and the higher error rates which may account for the low fraction (∼1/3) of reads with assigned cell barcodes and UMIs (
Lebrigand
et al

et al

Combinatorial indexing strategies for scRNA-seq
As an alternative to droplet-based methods, combinatorial indexing schemes have also been used for scRNA-seq profiles of
C. elegans
cells (
Fig. 11b and c
). For example, one version of combinatorial indexing, sci-RNA-seq, was used to produce an expansive scRNA-seq dataset from L2 larvae (
Cao
et al.
2017
). As an alternative to partitioning cells on a microfluidic device, the combinatorial indexing approach uses a series of separate barcoding reactions to label each individual cell with a unique sequence tag. In the first step, fixed and permeabilized cells are randomly distributed to a multiwell microtiter plate in which each well contains a unique barcode. After cDNA synthesis, cells are pooled and then redistributed (or “split”) to a new multiwell plate for an additional round of the barcoding reaction. Using this approach, the cDNAs for each individual cell acquire a unique combination of barcodes (
Fig. 11b and c
;
Cao
et al.
2017
;
Rosenberg
et al.
2018
;
Martin, Qiu,
et al.
2023
). Commercialized split-pool barcoding kits are available from Scale Biosciences and Parse Biosciences. The Parse protocol uses both random and oligo-dT priming for cDNA synthesis (
Rosenberg
et al
. 2018
) for capture of nonpolyadenylated RNA species and whole gene body coverage. Although this approach should enable analysis of alternative splicing (
Fig. 11c
), it may also require additional methods for removing rRNA before sequencing (
Gu
et al
. 2016
).
Alternative scRNA-seq methods for whole transcriptome coverage
Some classes of small noncoding RNAs, such as micro-RNAs, are not profiled by most widely used droplet-based or combinatorial indexing technologies. This is largely due to methods of RNA capture and size selection steps in library construction. Plate-based methods to capture full-length or total RNA from single cells have been developed, including SORT-seq (
Muraro
et al
. 2016
), SMART-seq2 (
Picelli
et al
. 2014
), and SMART-Seq-Total (
Isakova
et al
. 2021
) and VASA-Seq (
Salmen
et al
. 2022
). To capture all RNA species, SMART-Seq-Total and VASA-Seq use
E. coli
poly(A) polymerases to append polyA tails to RNA and then capture all RNA species by oligo-dT priming. SORT-seq, SMART-seq2, and SMART-Seq-Total involve sorting cells into individual wells of multiwell plates and thus have limited throughput compared with droplet-based methods. A recent publication using VASA-Seq achieved high-throughput (>30,000 cells) capture of full-length RNA reads from all RNA species (
Salmen
et al
. 2022
) but requires custom microfluidic chambers for droplet capture. VASA-Seq is offered by a commercial provider, Single Cell Discoveries, but currently only for
mouse
and human samples (
Tables 2
and
4
).
What does it cost?
Cost is a significant factor in deciding whether to perform scRNA-seq experiments and in selecting an approach. For 10X Genomics, cost is largely determined by the number of samples. The standard approach involves loading each individual sample (genotype or replicate) into an individual channel on the microfluidics device. This can cost $3000–$4,000 per sample, including library preparation and sequencing, for up to 10,000 cells. PIPseq kits from Fluent Biosciences cost less per sample but are sold in multisample kits that still present a significant up-front cost of thousands of dollars, with shelf-lives of ∼6 months.
For large-scale sequencing (hundreds of thousands of cells), combinatorial indexing can offer substantial cost savings over droplet-based approaches. This is because droplet-based approaches scale linearly with cell number, whereas combinatorial indexing methods scale sublinearly, thus reducing the cost per cell. Newer multiplexing strategies (e.g. 10X Genomics CellPlex) reduce cost by attaching unique barcodes to individual cells, allowing for loading a greater number of cells into each channel (
Mylka
et al
. 2022
). For additional considerations on selecting appropriate scRNA-seq technology for specific experiments, we refer readers to recent reviews (
Regan and Preall 2022
;
Conte
et al
. 2024
).

Read mapping and quality control
In the first step, raw sequencing reads in fastq files must be assigned to individual experimental samples (via sample barcodes) and to individual cells (via cell-specific barcodes) (
Fig. 12

C. elegans

Davis
et al
. 2022

With 3′ counting approaches such as 10X Genomics, incomplete annotations of 3′ UTRs can result in an underestimation of gene counts, since reads mapping beyond the annotated UTR will not be used for quantification. To obviate this problem, empirical methods have been developed for utilizing scRNA-seq data to extend 3′ UTR annotations (

;

2021
;

2024

Fig. 12
and

Normalization and clustering
One commonly used approach for normalization, inspired by library size normalization of bulk data, calculates a “size factor” for each cell based on the total UMI count per cell and uses this value to scale gene expression, thereby accounting for differences in RNA capture across cells. In general, once datasets are normalized and integrated, dimensionality reduction is performed, first using principal component analysis (PCA), followed by algorithms such as t-SNE, UMAP, or PHATE (

Detecting DE transcripts in scRNA-seq data
The method of differential expression testing (
Fig. 12
) is of particular interest and should be considered before data collection, as it strongly influences the initial experimental design. The major consideration is the number and type of biological replicate samples needed. Several popular methods for testing differential expression have treated each cell as an independent observation, but recent work has shown that this approach can produce false positives especially among highly expressed genes (
Lun and Marioni 2017
;
Crowell
et al
. 2020
;
Squair
et al
. 2021
;
Zimmerman
et al
. 2021
;
Murphy and Skene 2022
). A straightforward solution to this problem is to collect multiple biological replicates (i.e. independently prepared samples) for each condition. “Pseudobulk” aggregate data (either summed or averaged) are then generated for each cell type within each replicate. In this way, more robust statistical tests (e.g. edgeR) can be implemented to limit the false-positive rate (
Lun and Marioni 2017
;
Tung
et al
. 2017
;
Squair
et al
. 2021
). An alternate approach is to use statistical models known as mixed models that consider individual cells while also accounting for the experimental sample when testing for differential expression (
Crowell
et al
. 2020
;
Zimmerman
et al
. 2021
). Although generating multiple independent biological samples for scRNA-seq can be labor-intensive and costly, it should strengthen confidence in the results. Additionally, the development of multiplexing technologies and competition in the single-cell market should lead to decreased costs in the future. With numerous available high-fidelity endogenous fluorescent reporters for individual genes and the ease of generating new reporters, it should be relatively straightforward to validate, in vivo, differential expression patterns generated from scRNA-seq comparisons.

Genome-wide methods for studies of gene regulation
To complement methods for directly measuring gene expression by RNA-seq, other approaches have emerged that catalog gene regulatory mechanisms, including the association of RNA polymerases with active genes, chromatin accessibility, and translational states of mRNAs. In this section, we describe recent applications of these additional methods in
C. elegans
. Although chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) is a widely used approach for mapping transcription factor and chromatin binding sites, we do not discuss the ChIP method owing to recent comprehensive reviews of this topic (
Araya
et al
. 2014
;
Askjaer 2014
;
Sen
et al
. 2021
).
RAPID: RNA polymerase DNA adenine methylation identification to identify actively transcribed genes in specific cell types
A recent study described a method for foot printing RNA polymerase binding sites (
Gómez-Saldivar
et al.
2020
). Dubbed “RAPID” for RNA polymerase DNA adenine methylation identification (DamID), the protocol was adapted from the DamIDapproach originally developed in the Henikoff lab (
van Steensel and Henikoff 2000
). In this strategy,
E. coli
Dam methyltransferase, which methylates adenine in GATC recognition sequences, is fused to
RPB-6
, a common protein subunit of all 3 RNA polymerases (
Fig. 13a
). Selected tissues were targeted in a combinatorial approach: cell-specific expression of Cre-activated RBP-6::Dam from a constitutively active heat-shock promoter (at 25°C) (
Gómez-Saldivar
et al.
2020
). When animals are grown on Dam-negative
E. coli
, GATC sites in close proximity to RNA polymerases containing RPB-6::Dam are selectively methylated and can be detected by DNA sequencing. In all tested cell types from embryos and adults, RAPID effectively identified labeled known marker genes and identified new genes specifically expressed in the single pair of XXX neuroendocrine cells. Additional studies have used RAPID to target other cell types with comparable specificity (
Katsanos
et al
. 2021
;
Fragoso-Luna
et al
. 2023
).

Advantages and limitations of the RAPID method
RAPID has several advantages. First, cell-specific methylation profiles can be obtained from whole-worm extracts, without the need for sorting by FACS or affinity purification. Second, small amounts of starting material (e.g. a few thousand animals or cells) are required in comparison with other tissue-specific profiling methods. One limitation is that transgenic strains driving expression of Cre or FLP in specific cell types are needed, although a number of these recombinase driver lines already exist. Moreover, RNA polymerase association is not always representative of the full transcriptome due to RNA species inherited from parental cells. In addition, RNA-seq measurements span a broader dynamic range than RAPID results. Finally, methylation occurs at GATC sites which may limit detection of RNA polymerase binding to some genomic regions (
Gómez-Saldivar
et al.
2020
).
Genome-wide detection of open chromatin domains
Actively transcribed genes and their regulatory domains exhibit more open chromatin states, which are hypersensitive to endonucleases (e.g. DNaseI) and also accessible to transposases. DNaseI hypersensitive sites can be identified across the genome by sequencing (DNase-seq) to reveal regulatory modules such as enhancers and promoter elements (
Boyle
et al
. 2008
;
Song and Crawford 2010
;
Ho
et al
. 2017
). In assay for transposase-accessible chromatin with sequencing (ATAC-seq), intact chromatin (i.e. in isolated nuclei) is treated with a prokaryotic transposon (TN5) that specifically cuts genomic DNA in physically accessible regions (
Fig. 13b
). The cut ends are ligated with adapters for detection by sequencing. Because open chromatin is correlated with gene expression, ATAC-seq sites are expected to mark regulatory regions for actively transcribed genes (
Buenrostro
et al
. 2013
).
Use of ATAC-seq to detect transcriptionally active genes
Given its ease of implementation, ATAC-seq has been utilized more frequently than DNAse-seq in
C. elegans
(
Daugherty
et al
. 2017
;
Jänes
et al
. 2018
;
Serizay
et al
. 2020
;
Durham
et al
. 2021
). In 2 cases, ATAC-seq revealed a dynamic chromatin landscape across development and indexed these data with orthogonal histone modification and ChIP-seq datasets to identify the subset of accessible regions corresponding to enhancer and promoter elements (
Daugherty
et al
. 2017
;
Jänes
et al
. 2018
). A more recent study performed ATAC-seq and RNA-seq on sorted nuclei from the germline and 4 major somatic tissues (neurons, hypodermis, muscle, and intestine) (
Serizay
et al
. 2020
). Intriguingly, these datasets revealed that over half of the ATAC-seq sites are uniquely accessible in 1 tissue and that genes expressed in germ line and somatic cells show distinct maps of regulatory architecture and promoter features. Interestingly, ATAC-seq revealed that chromatin accessibility differences can drive left/right asymmetry during embryonic development of the sensory neurons ASEL and ASER (
Charest
et al
. 2020
). In this case, binding sites for the transcription factors
TBX-37
/38 upstream of the microRNA locus were uniquely accessible in the ABa lineage, which gives rise to ASEL and expresses
lsy-6
. These results highlight the utility of ATAC-seq for detecting permissive chromatin states that specify terminal fates.
Single-nucleus ATAC-seq analysis
A recent study used single-nucleus ATAC-seq (snATAC-seq) to map chromatin accessibility in L2 larval cells (
Durham
et al
. 2021
). Over 24,000 of these nuclei could be subdivided into 37 different cell type clusters. Computational analysis detected specific cell types that were independently identified by scRNA-seq data (
Cao
et al.
2017
). Moreover, snATAC-seq hits also encompassed putative transcription factor binding sites identified by ChIP-seq data (
Kudron
et al
. 2018
). Thus, these findings suggest that the snATAC-seq method can be utilized in
C. elegans
to identify gene regulatory regions that are activated in specific cell types. This pioneering approach opens the door to comprehensive integration of multimodal single-cell data from
C. elegans
to generate unified descriptions of cell types, as in other organisms (
Lee and Li 2023
).
Polysome and ribosome profiling to measure the translation status of mRNA
mRNA and protein levels are not well correlated for some genes, a disparity indicative of robust post-transcriptional regulation (
Buccitelli and Selbach 2020
). Ribosome profiling techniques (or Ribo-seq) have been used in
C. elegans
to measure the translational status of mRNAs with the goal of investigating translational regulatory mechanisms (
Stadler and Fire 2011
;
Stadler
et al
. 2012
;
Stadler and Fire 2013
;
Aeschimann
et al
. 2015
,
2017
;
Lan
et al
. 2019
;
Shaffer and Rollins 2020
;
Stein
et al
. 2022
).
In one approach, whole-animal lysates are fractionated on sucrose gradients to resolve transcripts engaged by a single ribosome (monosomes) vs those with 2 or more ribosomes (polysomes) (
Ding and Grosshans 2009
;
Shaffer and Rollins 2020
). RNA-seq of the isolated fractions was used to identify actively translated transcripts in the polysome fraction vs quiescent transcripts in the monosome fraction. Several recent studies applied this approach to investigate mechanisms regulating translation of specific genes under conditions that promote lifespan extension (
Lan
et al
. 2019
;
Rollins
et al
. 2019
;
Ma
et al
. 2023
).
Ribosome profiling can also be used to measure translation rate vs ribosome occupancy (
Ingolia 2014
). In this approach, a sample is resolved on a sucrose gradient to obtain monosome and polysome fractions as in polysome profiling, but then digested with RNases. Regions of mRNA occupied by a ribosome are protected from digestion, and these protected fragments are identified by RNA-seq to obtain transcriptome-wide ribosome occupancy maps at codon-level resolution (
Ingolia 2014
). In parallel, an aliquot of the RNA from the sample is separately processed to obtain steady-state transcript measurements. Comparisons of ribosome occupancy vs steady-state transcript levels from the same sample can be used to assign transcripts to a range of translational activity. Ribosome profiling has been used to measure slowing ribosome elongation rates at wobble codons (
Stadler and Fire 2011
), dynamic translation upon entry and exit at L1 diapause (
Stadler and Fire 2013
), miRNA-mediated regulation and translational repression (
Stadler
et al
. 2012
;
Aeschimann
et al
. 2017
), and increased ribosomal pausing with aging, which can lead to deterioration of ribosome quality control pathways and proteostasis (
Stein
et al
. 2022
).
To date, both polysome profiling and ribosome profiling have been exclusively performed with whole-animal lysates. For tissue-specific snapshots of translation, TRAP-seq could be used or perhaps a combined protocol of first purifying polysome vs monosome fractions and then performing TRAP on each fraction.
Detecting protein expression and localization
Several genetic options are available for detecting expression of specific proteins (
Figs. 14
and
15
). All of these strategies involve the addition of epitope or FP fusions, either at endogenous loci via genome editing, or through transgenes introduced as single-copy or multicopy arrays. A recent excellent review of transgenesis methods in
C. elegans
has covered approaches for genome editing, single- and multicopy expression strategies, and guidance on gene architecture for constructing expression transgenes (
Nance and Frøkjaer-Jensen 2019
). We direct the reader to this important and useful resource. In this section, we will discuss contemporary approaches for detecting protein expression and localization and compare their advantages and limitations.

Indirect monitoring of protein expression with SL2 reporters
As described above, bicistronic reporters that rely on trans-splicing with SL2 are useful for detecting native gene expression (
Fig. 1
). With this experimental design, each trans-spliced mRNA is independently translated such that the fluorescent reporter protein is uncoupled from the endogenous protein of interest (
Fig. 1
). Although the SL2 method relies on translation, it is formally a method for detecting transcription since the translated gene product is assumed to be directly proportional to the abundance of each trans-spliced transcript. In vivo, however, protein expression may not be directly related to transcript levels due to post-transcriptional mechanisms that regulate endogenous translation (
Grün
et al
. 2014
;
Walther
et al
. 2015
).
Detecting protein translation with reporters
An alternative approach for directly measuring endogenous protein translation relies on the viral 2A peptide. Viral 2A peptides are short sequences (18–23 amino acids) that induces ribosomal “skipping,” a phenomenon that terminates translation at the 2A peptide consensus sequence and then restarts translation at the next encoded amino acid (
Fig. 14a
;
Atkins
et al
. 2007
;
Doronina
et al
. 2008
). The net effect is the production of 2 separate proteins. For a bipartite reporter of endogenous translation, the 2A peptide encoding sequence, fused to a FP coding sequence, can be inserted either upstream or downstream of the coding region of interest. Upon mRNA translation, both the target and FPs are produced at stoichiometric levels, but as separate, untethered peptides. Remarkably, at least 5 different proteins can be expressed from single reporter constructs containing multiple interspersed 2A sequences (
Fig. 14a
;
Ahier and Jarriault 2014
).
Advantages and limitations of trans-splicing (SL2) and ribosome “skipping” (2A) reporters
An advantage of both the SL2 and 2A reporter designs is that endogenous protein function should be preserved by uncoupling the reporter proteins (
Figs. 1
and
14a
). An additional advantage is that reporters can be localized to discrete locations such as the nucleus which can aid cell identification (
Yemini
et al
. 2021
). One caveat, of course, is that the uncoupled reporter does not reveal the in vivo location or stability of the endogenous protein (see below). Additionally, in both the SL2 and 2A designs, potential post-transcriptional regulation of the native transcript and protein may not be preserved. For example, the fluorescent reporter proteins may perdure much longer than either native transcripts or proteins with short half-lives (
Bounoutas
et al
. 2011
). Interestingly, a recent direct comparison of the relative amount of reporter produced by the 2A and SL2 approaches found that in most cases, the bicistronic SL2 cassette led to brighter reporter signal in comparison with the equivalent 2A design (
Sun
et al
. 2023
). Therefore, when signal is expected to be low, especially in endogenous tagging and single-copy expression experiments, it may be wiser to choose the SL2 strategy.
Direct detection of protein expression and localization
Immunological methods such as immunofluorescence staining and western blotting are useful strategies for directly measuring protein expression and localization (
Fig. 14b
;
Miller
et al
. 1983
;
Shakes
et al
. 2012
). However, antibody reagents are available for only a limited number of
C. elegans
proteins (Developmental Studies Hybridoma Bank,
https://dshb.biology.uiowa.edu/
) (
Hadwiger
et al
. 2010
), and generating these resources can be costly. Moreover, with the advent of efficient genome editing strategies (
Dickinson and Goldstein 2016
;
Nance and Frøkjaer-Jensen 2019
;
Martin and Calarco 2022
) and the tested use of several common epitopes (3xFLAG, HA) and a wide array of FPs (
Breimann
et al
. 2019
;
Shah
et al
. 2022
), “tagged’ transgenic strains can offer the simplest approach for detecting endogenous proteins.
Epitope tagging endogenous proteins for detection by immunostaining
Epitope-tagged endogenous proteins can be directly visualized in live animals (with fluorescent reporter tags), localized in fixed samples by immunofluorescence (
Shakes
et al
. 2012
) or by immuno-electron microscopy (
Weimer
et al
. 2004
;
Gracheva
et al
. 2010
), and quantified by biochemical methods such as western blotting. However, a known caveat of any tagging strategy is that fusion of an epitope or fluorescent reporter could alter target protein function. It is thus critically important to utilize functional assays (e.g. visible phenotypes) to assess the status of the fusion protein in vivo. Some solutions to this problem include introducing the tag at alternative locations such as N- vs C-termini or in disordered linker regions. Alpha-fold can be useful for guiding this strategy (
Jumper
et al
. 2021
).
Detecting endogenous protein expression in specific cell types
In some applications, it is advantageous to visualize protein expression and localization in a small subset of cells. Several strategies have been developed for this purpose that presumptively do not interfere with key endogenous regulatory signals such as promoters and UTRs. In every case, however, it is important to confirm that adducts facilitating detection (e.g. epitopes, GFP) do not disrupt native protein function in vivo.
Using recombinases (Cre, FLP) to drive cell-specific gene expression
One strategy employs the site specific recombinase enzymes Cre and FLP to achieve conditional expression in selected cell types (
Fig. 15a and b
;
Hubbard 2014
;
Schwartz and Jorgensen 2016
). Generally, these approaches involve 2 elements: (1) cell type–specific expression of either Cre or Flp in specific cells and (2) a target gene engineered for Cre or FLP-dependent recombination that results in expression of the endogenous protein fused to a fluorescent reporter (
Fig. 15a and b
). For conditional C-terminal fusions, an “off cassette” flanked with FRT sites (for FLP recombinase) is inserted by genome editing at the 3′ end of the target gene to block transcription of a downstream GFP coding sequence. Cell-specific expression of Flp, in this case, excises the off cassette to fuse GFP to the target protein C-terminus (
Fig. 15a
). For N-terminal fusions, GFP fused to a FLP-on cassette is inserted between the target gene promoter and start codon. In the absence of FLP, the GFP tag is proteolytically degraded due to an attached PEST sequence and the downstream gene is normally expressed from a trans-spliced SL2 transcript included in the FLP-on cassette (
Fig. 15b
). With FLP-dependent excision, the PEST sequence and SL2 trans-splicing signal are removed and GFP is fused in frame to the N-terminus of the native protein (
Schwartz and Jorgensen 2016
;
McDonald
et al
. 2020
;
Miller-Fleming
et al
. 2021
;
Fig. 15b
).
Split FP method for detecting cell-specific expression
An additional strategy to detect cell-specific expression makes use of split FP. This approach is based on the discovery that GFP can be asymmetrically divided between the 10th and 11th strands of its beta barrel structure and can be expressed as 2 components—a larger GFP
1–10
and a smaller GFP
11
fragment—which spontaneously anneal in vivo to restore fluorescence (
Cabantous
et al
. 2005
). In
C. elegans
, several studies have used split-GFP (green) and split-wrmScarlet (red) FPs to detect cell-specific protein expression (
Fig. 15c
;
Noma
et al
. 2017
;
He
et al
. 2019
;
Hefel and Smolikove 2019
;
Goudeau
et al
. 2021
;
Cuentas-Condori
et al
. 2023
). The smaller FP
11
fragment can be fused to an encoded protein of interest by genome editing strategies. Fluorescence can then be controlled by expression of the FP
1–10
fragment from a different promoter, such that only cells expressing both the FP
1-10
and FP
11
fragments will produce a signal (
Fig. 15c
). Although transgenes are convenient for driving expression of the FP
1–10
fragment, this experimental design should not lead to overexpression artifacts because the FP
11

11
to the target protein to produce a concatenated array of reconstituted FP proteins that is significantly brighter than a single-copy FP signal (
Kamiyama
et al
. 2016
;
He
et al

;
Hefel and Smolikove 2019
;
Goudeau

Kamiyama
et al.
2016
;
He
et al.
2019

High-throughput detection of proteins

;

;

;

), phosphorylation sites (

et al
. 2009
), normal aging vs long-lived

mutants (

et al
. 2007
;
Narayan
et al
. 2016
), heat-shock responses (
Larance
et al
. 2011
), transcription factor mutants (

Methods for detecting cell-specific protein expression
As outlined above, multiple RNA-seq methods have been developed to detect cell-specific gene expression. Here, we describe an emerging list of recently developed approaches that use MS to quantify cell-specific protein expression.
Bio-Orthogonal Noncanonical Amino Acid Tagging
Bio-Orthogonal Noncanonical Amino Acid Tagging (BONCAT) can be used to label proteins from defined cell types (
Yuet
et al
. 2015
;
Aburaya
et al
. 2020
;
Fig. 16a
). Tagging is accomplished with a mutant phenylalanine tRNA synthetase that incorporates the amino acid variant azide phenylalanine (Azf). Tissue specificity for Azf incorporation is achieved by expressing the mutant Phe-tRNA with cell-specific promoters. Proteins labeled with Azf can be enriched from whole-animal lysates by affinity to alkyne-agarose and identified by MS. This approach has been used to label proteins in pharyngeal muscle cells (
Yuet
et al
. 2015
), from all neurons and from a single neuron class, the AFD sensory neuron (
Aburaya
et al
. 2020
).

(

), other caveats should be considered. These include the efficiency of Azf incorporation (≤50% of phenylalanine residues in whole-worm total protein extracts), nonspecific detection of protein [968 proteins were detected in strains lacking mutant Phe-tRNA synthetase (
Aburaya

Proximity labeling methods
An alternative method for generating cell-specific proteomic profiles, known as “proximity labeling,” involves selective biotin labeling of proteins by exogenous enzymes such as ascorbate peroxidase (APX) or biotin ligases (BioID, TurboID) (
Samavarchi-Tehrani
et al
. 2020
;
Qin
et al
. 2021
;
Xu
et al
. 2021
;
Fig. 16a
). Biotin-labeled proteins can then be purified from lysates by affinity to streptavidin (
Fig. 16c
). Biotinylating enzymes can be transgenically expressed in specific cells or tissues to sample the entire proteome. Alternatively, restricting expression of the biotinylating enzyme to a selected subcellular compartment or attaching it to a specific protein can limit the labeling reaction to adjacent (e.g. interacting) proteins (
Fig. 16b
;
Reinke
et al
. 2017
;
Artan
et al
. 2021
).
Proximity labeling with APX
In the presence of hydrogen peroxide, APX catalyzes the covalent addition of biotin from exogenously supplied biotin–phenol to nearby proteins (
Fig. 16a
). This strategy has been used to detect proteins expressed in several major
C. elegans
tissues, including intestine, body wall muscle, epidermis, and pharyngeal muscle (
Reinke
et al
. 2017
). Limitations of this approach include variable protein detection across tissues of different sizes, with more efficient protein detection in larger tissues (e.g. body wall muscle vs pharyngeal muscle). In addition, genetic modifications (
bus-8
knockdown) are required to increase cuticle permeability to the biotin–phenol substrate (
Reinke
et al
. 2017
).
TurboID
A modified version of the
E. coli
biotin ligase BirA, known as TurboID, can also be used for proximity labeling (
Branon
et al

;
Sanchez
et al

). In this method, the transgenically expressed TurboID enzyme covalently attaches biotin to endogenous proteins located within a few nanometers of the enzyme (
Fig. 16a and b

Advantages and limitations of proximity labeling methods
All of proximity labeling methods detect substantial levels of background protein in conditions with no cell-specific labeling (i.e. absence of biotin labeling enzyme), thus requiring the use of enrichment thresholds vs controls for confident detection of bona fide tissue-specific protein expression. Studies with APX and TurboID profiled many of the same tissues, but different enrichment quantification criteria and subcellular localizations (i.e. nuclear or cytoplasmic samples for APX vs no localization for TurboID) confound direct comparisons of these approaches. However, the number of proteins detected was similar, both overall and within each of the commonly sampled tissues, which suggests that these methods are robust (
Reinke
et al
. 2017
;
Artan
et al
. 2021
).
Protein detection methods are less sensitive than RNA-seq
These methods are uniquely useful for detecting protein abundance, subcellular localization, and predicting protein–protein interactions. However, RNA-based approaches are more sensitive, likely due to available methods for amplifying RNA for RNA-seq library construction. For example, a study measuring mRNA and protein from whole worms detected transcripts for 19,549 genes, but only 9,162 proteins (
Grün
et al
. 2014
). This difference is even more pronounced for individual tissues or cell types. For example,
Reinke
et al.
(2017)
found that confidently detected proteins in specific tissues constituted 7–21% of the number of mRNAs detected in the same cell types (
Spencer
et al.
2011
). Similarly, 111–1,834 proteins were confidently detected for the sensory neuron AFD (
Aburaya
et al
. 2020
;
Artan
et al
. 2021
), whereas scRNA-seq detected 5351–5,824 genes for AFD (
Lockhead
et al.
2016
;
Taylor
et al.
2021
). The dynamic range of ∼5 orders of magnitude for MS is substantially lower than the estimated variance ∼12 orders of magnitude in protein concentration for some samples (
Timp and Timp 2020
). In an approach similar to the removal of abundant rRNAs in RNA-seq experiments, highly abundant proteins can be depleted prior to MS analysis (e.g. endogenously biotinylated proteins in TurboID experiments) to facilitate detection of lowly expressed proteins (
Artan
et al
. 2022
).
Single-cell proteomics and transcript analysis
Paralleling single-cell methods for RNA detection, several groups are developing strategies for MS-based detection of proteins from single cells (

;

Specht

Ahmad and Budnik 2023
;
Bennett
et al
. 2023
). With no currently available commercial platforms, access to single-cell proteomics is limited to a few specialized laboratories. New developments in this field aim to improve multiplexing to increase the number of cells measured simultaneously, and much of the work has focused on large cells with high protein content. To date, these methods have not been applied to
C. elegans
, but as technology advances, will likely become a powerful tool for profiling protein expression at the resolution and levels currently available for transcripts. In an important step toward single-cell proteomic profiling, a recent study reported robust protein expression atlases of dissected tissues (intestine, gonad) from adult hermaphrodites (
Tan
et al
. 2024
).
Single-worm RNA-seq and proteomics
Most applications of either bulk or scRNA-seq and proteomic analysis involve pooling cells from many animals for each experimental condition. The pooling approach, however, may mask potential variation among individual cells or animals. Recently, methods have been developed for single-worm RNA-seq and proteomics.
Single-worm transcript analysis
Building on protocols first implemented for insect parasitic nematodes (
Lu
et al
. 2017
;
Chang
et al
. 2021
),
Wang
et al
. (2022)
demonstrated that cDNA libraries from single
C. elegans
animals were comparable to those prepared from pooled samples containing RNA from many animals. A parallel comparison of single-worm and 5 pooled samples (i.e. 5 worms vs 5 pooled samples/condition) in response to pathogen infection showed that both methods detected DE genes. However, fewer DE genes were identified in the single-worm samples, which could be due to high worm-to-worm variability (
Wang
et al
. 2022
).
Single-worm proteomic analysis
Recent proteomic studies detected 3,000–5,000 proteins from individual worms (
Bensaddek
et al
. 2016
;
Steinbach
et al
. 2022
;
Zhu
et al
. 2024

Larance
et al
. 2015
). The substantially greater number of genes detected by RNA-seq of individual worms (>24,000) (
Wang
et al
. 2022

Interestingly, in both sequencing and proteomic approaches, many genes (∼7,000) or proteins (602) were detected only in single worm but not in pooled samples (
Bensaddek
et al
. 2016
;
Wang
et al
. 2022

Bensaddek
et al

et al
. 2016
;
Steinbach
et al
. 2022

Zhu
et al
. 2024
). Overall, single-worm gene expression and proteomic methods could be useful for elucidating mechanisms that drive phenotypic diversity between isogenic animals (
Bensaddek
et al

;

et al

;
