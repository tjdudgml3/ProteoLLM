Introduction
Osteoarthritis (OA) is a major contributor to a growing burden of musculoskeletal disease.
1
Globally, 595 million people had OA in 2020, equal to 7.6% of the world’s population, representing a 132% increase in total cases since 1990.
2
OA affects all tissues of synovial joints and is regarded as a primarily degenerative condition with an inflammatory component. Its slow progression and a paucity of reliable biomarkers currently preclude a clear definition of early OA and the OA timeline. The lack of biomarkers has impeded the identification of drug targets and the development of disease-modifying OA drugs. Together, these needs motivate continued investigation of fundamental OA mechanisms.
Since articular cartilage breakdown by proteolysis is a hallmark of OA, proteolytic biomarkers could help to better define OA onset and progression, and measure treatment response, but only a limited number are currently available. Proteolysis destroys structural joint proteins and generates protein fragments whose new biological activities may drive inflammation in OA.
3
Defining proteases involved in joint destruction could uncover relevant drug targets. Identification of proteolytically-derived protein termini using N-terminomics,
4
along with other proteomic analyses,
5
,
6
have suggested a massive scale of proteolysis in the OA joint. The underlying proteolytic mechanisms are poorly understood and their clinical utility is yet to be fully exploited. N-terminomics was previously used to elucidate the activity of serine protease HtrA1, which was consistently detected in knee OA cartilage.
4
Thus, specific proteolytic events occurring in OA cartilage could be attributed to HtrA1 by combining forward degradomics (proteome-wide determination of proteolysis) with reverse degradomics, which maps individual protease activities, in this case, HtrA1.
4
N-terminomics has also identified mast cell tryptase-mediated lubricin breakdown, which impaired the frictional properties of the joint.
5
However, the targets and cleavage sites of most proteases remain unknown.
As a proxy for diverse joint tissues that offers a window into joint health and diseases,
7
,
8

Here, N-terminomics of matched OA cartilage and synovial fluid was used to elicit a synovial fluid degradome and define the extent to which proteolytic cartilage breakdown was reflected in synovial fluid composition. We also defined specific targets of proteases with well-established roles in OA cartilage breakdown, i.e., ADAMTS5 and MMP13, as well as of mast cell chymase (CMA1), a product of mast cells, whose contribution to OA cartilage breakdown is presently undefined.
Methods
Human knee OA cartilage and synovial fluid
Synovial fluid and cartilage were obtained from 5 male patients undergoing primary total knee arthroplasty (TKA) (age range 56–69y, median 61y) after informed consent (Cleveland Clinic IRB 20–857). Synovial fluid aspiration was performed during TKA, performed by a medial parapatellar approach under tourniquet. Cartilage was dissected with a razor blade from osteochondral cuts taken during arthroplasty, snap-frozen and stored at −80 °C until analysis. Synovial fluid was centrifuged at 1500 g for 10 min. The supernatant was aliquoted and frozen at −80 °C. Synovial fluid and cartilage used for analysis lacked visible blood contamination.
Sample preparation for N-terminomics
Synovial fluid was recentrifuged after thawing and protein from 500 μl was obtained by chloroform-methanol precipitation. Protein concentration was determined by the Bradford assay. 250 μg of proteins from each sample was used for Terminal Amine Isotopic Labeling of Substrates (TAILS) as previously described.
4
A portion of the labeled cartilage and synovial fluid peptides was used for shotgun proteomics without negative selection of tryptic peptides (pre-TAILS sample). TAILS and pre-TAILS peptides were fractionated offline by high pH reverse phase high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC).
4
Cartilage was thawed and processed for TAILS as previously described.

Digestion of healthy human cartilage with MMP13, ADAMTS5 and CMA1
Non-diseased human cartilage was dissected from talar articular surfaces from a healthy donor (provided by Arnavaz Hakimiyan and Dr. Susan Chubinskaya, Rush Medical Center).
4
After methanolchloroform precipitation of proteins and proteoglycans from ~1 mg wet weight pulverized cartilage, 100 μg was divided into 3 tubes for digestion with active protease and 3 with inactivated protease. Thus, 16.6 μg of cartilage protein was used per channel for labeling. 0.33 μg of protease was used in each digest, achieving a 1:50 protease to total substrate library ratio. The tissue was finely diced with a razor blade and immersed in liquid nitrogen for 30 s and pulverized (BioSpec Products 59014H Tissue pulverizer, Cole–Parmer, IL, USA, catalog no. EW-36903–15). 200 μl of digestion buffer [for recombinant human (rh) MMP13 (catalog number 511-MM-010, Bio-Techne, Minneapolis, USA) - 100 mm Tris pH 7.5, 150 mm NaCl, 10 mm CaCl2, 0.01% (w/v) Brij35

10
rhCMA1 - mast cell chymase (catalog number 4099-SE-010, Bio-Techne, Minneapolis, USA) - 50 mM Tris, 200 mM NaCl, 5 mM CaCl2,.02% Brij, pH 7.5)] was used for each digest. MMP13 was activated using p-aminophenylmercuric acetate (final concentration 1 mM in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO)) at 37 °C for 4 h and 5 nM of active protease or heat-in-activated protease (control digests) was used for digestion at 37 °C for 16 h. The digest supernatants were collected after centrifugation at 5000 g for 10 min and 50 μg protein was taken for TAILS.
Samples digested with active proteases were labeled with isotopically heavy formaldehyde (resulting in an adduct of 34 Da to N-termini or E-amines of Lys) and control digests with light formaldehyde (resulting in an adduct of 28 Da). Pairs of active and control samples for each protease were combined for subsequent trypsin digestion and peptide off-line fractionation prior to LC-MS/MS.
4
Protein mass spectrometry
For LC-MS/MS, we used a Bruker TimsTof Pro2 Q-Tof mass spectrometer in positive ion mode, coupled with a CaptiveSpray ion source (both from Bruker Daltonik GmbH, Bremen). The HPLC column was a Bruker 15 cm x 75 μm id C18 ReproSil AQ, 1.9 μm, 120 Å reversed-phase capillary chromatography column. One μl volumes of the samples were injected. Peptides eluted from the column by an acetonitrile/0.1% formic acid gradient at a flow rate of 0.3 μl/min were introduced into the source of the mass spectrometer in-line. Parallel Accumulation–Serial Fragmentation (PASEF) data-dependent acquisition was used to select precursor ions for fragmentation with a TIMS-MS scan followed by 10 PASEF MS/MS scans. The TIMS-MS survey scan was acquired between 0.60 and 1.6 Vs/cm2 and 100–1700
m/z
with a ramp time of 166 ms. The total cycle time for the PASEF scans was 1.2 s and the MS/MS experiments were performed with collision-induced dissociation with energy ranging from 20 eV (0.6 Vs.cm2) to 59 eV (1.6 Vs/cm2). Precursors with 2–5 charges were selected with the target value set to 20,000 a.u and intensity threshold to 2500 a.u. Precursor ions were dynamically excluded for 0.4 s
LC-MS/MS data analysis
.d files from the TimsToF were imported into FragPipe V20.0, integrated with MSFragger-3.8, IonQuant-1.9.8 and Philosopher 5.0.0 and searched against a human proteome database containing 20,437 entries including reviewed sequences, common contaminants, and protein isoforms. Analysis parameters were set as precursor mass tolerance of 10 ppm, and fragment mass tolerance of 0.6 Da. Static modification was set as carbamidomethyl (C), whereas dynamic modifications included the light/heavy (28/34 Da) dimethyl adducts (N-terminal, Lys), oxidation (Met, Pro), deamidation (Asn, Arg), acetylation (N-terminal), and Gln to pyro-Glu N-terminal. Since the working protease for LC-MS/MS was trypsin, a semi-tryptic search was used. A decoy database was created to calculate false discovery rate and percolator with strict cut off at 1% was applied. Common contaminants such as hemoglobin and keratins were removed, although albumin and immunoglobulins were retained for synovial fluid analysis. Unlabeled tryptic peptides were filtered out since they resulted from trypsin digestion after labeling, whereas unlabeled semi-tryptic peptides were retained.

Discussion
We used matched human cartilage and synovial fluid from knee OA to determine the proteolytic landscape of each sample
de novo
, defining proteolytic cleavages and proteases occurring in human knee OA. The samples were subjected to extensive peptide fractionation prior to LC-MS/MS to overcome the proteome complexity of cartilage and improve identification of cleavages in less abundant components, expanding degradome depth and breadth. Only a few of the peptides in these degradomes had been previously identified and attributed to specific proteases in MEROPs. In addition to reflecting the dominance of cleaved ECM and circulating molecules in cartilage and synovial fluid, respectively, the analysis identified several novel cartilage ECM-derived proteolytic products in the synovial fluid. We further experimentally elucidated contributions of MMP13 and ADAMTS5 in OA cartilage breakdown and identified protease-specific cartilage breakdown products in synovial fluid. Mast cell tryptase-mediated lubricin/PRG4 breakdown has a deleterious impact on joint rheology
5

6
CMA1 is thought to be mast cell specific
29

The unique CMA1 cleavages and substrates (
Table S9

21
and for evaluation of therapeutics targeting mast cells. Previously, a phage-displayed random nonapeptide library defined a CMA1 substrate recognition profile with a strong preference for Tyr and Phe at P1.
30
Here, a library of cartilage substrates in their natural complexes, indicated an additional preference for Arg and Leu at P1. The former is not an artefact of the TAILS procedure, since the MMP13 degradome, obtained by an identical approach, selected against Arg at this position. A significant advantage of TAILS is that cleavages resulting in Arg or Lys at the P1 position are detected because of N-terminal labeling, whereas they are missed by degradomics employing semi-tryptic/non-tryptic searches of shotgun proteomics data.

Collagen II was the second-most preferred MMP13 substrate (after PRELP), but not in the top ten CMA1 substrates and was low on the list of ADAMTS5 substrates. Identification of ADAMTS5 cleavages in collagen II does not necessarily imply that it is a bona fide collagenase, for which a triple helix-unwinding property apparently resident only in select MMP hemopexin domains is required.
33
The collagen II peptides observed in the ADAMTS5 reverse degradome may arise from further cleavage of collagenase-cleaved (e.g., MMP-13) collagen, i.e, gelatin. MMP-13 preferentially cleaves collagen II among the proteases we investigated, but it is also not solely a collagenase. CMA1, MMP9 and ADAMTS5 reverse degradomes identified unique substrates (
Table S9
), but these could well become redundant with the activity of additional proteases investigated by reverse degradomics in future.

Comparison of the activities of ADAMTS5, MMP13 and CMA1 with HtrA1,
4
demonstrated little overlap. Furthermore, the proteolytic landscape of OA also had limited overlap with the reverse degradome of the four proteases defined to date. Even if ~30–40% cleavages attributable to ‘ragging’ by exopeptidases are excluded, most cleavages observed in cartilage and synovial fluid thus still remain unattributed, suggesting that cartilage degradation in human knee OA likely results from the activity of multiple proteases whose products may accumulate over the OA timeline. Indeed, neither ADAMTS5 nor CMA1 were detected in OA cartilage taken at the time of arthroplasty, yet their proteolytic products were detected. Furthermore, the initially generated proteolytic fragments may then be further modified by other proteases. Indeed, a recent peptidomics analysis attributed observed fragments to 192 proteases by cross-comparison with the MEROPS database of proteases and their activities.
6
In contrast, studies in mouse genetic models have shown that the lack of ADAMTS5, MMP13 or HtrA1 individually protects against cartilage breakdown.
31
,
34
,
36
,
40
Since the OA synovial fluid proteome varies during disease progression
41
it will be necessary to ascertain whether different proteases dominate cartilage breakdown at distinct points along the OA timeline-for example, ADAMTS5 in early OA and HtrA1 in late-stage OA. As with prior TAILS analysis of OA cartilage, ragging was rampant, probably because proteolytic fragments remain in the joint where they are exposed to exopeptidases, many of which were detected. The precise identification of primary cleavages of individual proteases by reverse degradomics is thus a major advantage of integrating forward and reverse degradomics. Degradomics of healthy knee cartilage and synovial fluid are presently unreported and it is possible that some of the observed cleavages result from physiological turnover occurring in healthy tissues.
Limitations of the present work reflect technical limitations of LC-MS/MS and TAILS, namely, stochastic nature of peptide detection in LC-MS/MS which is affected by protein dynamic range, inability to detect very small and very large peptides, and a potential bias for trypsin-like proteases stemming from searches for tryptic and non-tryptic specificity. The experimental digestion of cartilage with CMA1, MMP13 and ADAMTS5 does not exclude the possibility that the observed cleavages could arise from proteases activated by them. However, the broad agreement of consensus sequence site preferences for each protease with that previously determined using purified proteases and peptide substrates suggests that possible “indirect” cleavages are likely to be in the minority.
In summary, many fragments released from cartilage during its proteolytic breakdown are present in synovial fluid and appear to result from the activities of distinct proteases. The translational impact of this work lies in the potentially expanded and clinically useful biomarker repertoire that could be based on this information, because of molecular exchange between synovial fluid and blood. Indeed, assays using neoepitope antibodies for cleaved proteins are used to detect aggrecan, COMP and collagen fragments in blood and urine. The protease-specific cleavage sites uncovered here will also be useful for testing the efficacy and specificity of protease inhibitor therapeutics.
