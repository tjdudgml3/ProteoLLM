1
Introduction
In nature, viruses infect almost all forms of living organisms on earth and their obligate parasitism in space, time, and continuity of a host gives rise to the possibility of co‐ or multi‐infections between or among viruses. The coexistence of two or more viruses through a simultaneous or successive invasion is common in plants and can lead to diverse interactions between them (Rentería‐Canett et al.
2011
; Sadras et al.
2024
; Rybicki and Foster
2024
). Synergism, antagonism, and coexistence are three categories used to characterise the enhanced (Vance et al.
1995
; Niraula et al.
2024
), reduced (Syller
2012
; Niraula et al.
2024
), or unaffected (Untiveros, Fuentes, and Salazar
2007
; Borgolte, Varrelmann, and Hossain
2024
) viral virulence, mobility, multiplication, and other viral activities. However, plant virus interactions are complex, depending on the host species and their growth stages, on the sequence of invasions by each individual virus, and on the environmental conditions (Aguilar, Sánchez, and Ponz
2000
; Wintermantel et al.
2008
; Gómez et al.
2010
; Elvira González et al.
2021
), which also offers a wealth of challenges and opportunities for researchers in plant virology.
The antagonism of plant viruses refers to the influence of different viruses on each other's transmission and duplication through competition, inhibition, or exclusion when co‐infections occur on the same host plant such as cross‐protection and mutual exclusion (DaPalma et al.
2010
; Syller
2012
). Cross‐protection, also known as superinfection exclusion or homologous interference, occurs when a mild strain of the virus in its host plant is capable of inhibiting further infections by severe strains of the virus within the same genus or a closely related one (Julve et al.
2013
; Bergua et al.
2014
). This phenomenon is most commonly observed between different viruses of the same genus or between different mutant strains of the same virus (Mahmood and Rush
1999
; Komar et al.
2008
; Vigne et al.
2009
). On the other hand, mutual exclusion, also known as mutual suppression or mutual competitive suppression, exists not only between different viruses within the same genus and different strains of the same virus (Chen et al.
2012
), but also between two completely different viruses, such as DNA and RNA viruses (Pohl and Wege
2007
; Crespo et al.
2020
). However, the mechanisms involved in virus–virus, and virus–host interactions are underexplored and inadequately understood due to the complexity of multiple viral infections and difficulty to understand behaviours of viruses invaded throughout the course of the infestation (Koskella, Giraud, and Hood
2006
).
Earlier studies indicate that the overexpression of genes encoding viral capsid proteins (CPs) of the primary virus can coat or encapsulate the RNA of the secondary viruses (Beachy, Loesch‐Fries, and Tumer
1990
; Lu, Stubbs, and Culver
1998
). Transgenic plants genetically engineered with a viral CP gene can effectively resist further infections of the same virus, suggesting that an insertion of viral CP gene to plants and the accumulation of the CP interfere with the assembly and proliferation of the target viruses in host plants at an early stage of the infection (Park et al.
2007
). However, the CP‐mediated antagonism exists not only among homologous viruses, but also among unrelated viruses. For example, the infection, mobility and symptoms induced by distantly related viruses such as cucumber mosaic virus, potato virus Y, and potato virus X (PVX) are found to be somewhat attenuated in transgenic plants in which the CP of tobacco mosaic virus (TMV) is transcribed, indicating an impediment of replication and movement of heterologous viruses by the TMV CP (Anderson et al.
1989
; Bazzini et al.
2006
). Similar antagonistic phenomena can also be observed between viruses, satellite RNAs, viroids, and CP‐deficient viruses (Gal‐On and Shiboleth
2006
). Therefore, CP‐mediated defensive resistance plays a key role in antagonistic interactions among various viruses.
Tomato yellow leaf curl virus (TYLCV) (family
Geminiviridae
, genus
Begomovirus
) and tomato spotted wilt virus (TSWV) (family
Tospoviridae
, genus
Orthotospovirus
) are both devastating pathogens in tomato production worldwide (Scholthof et al.
2011
; Bupi et al.
2024
). TYLCV is transmitted persistently by whiteflies (
Bemisia tabaci
) and contains a monomeric circular single‐stranded DNA of approximately 2.7–2.8 kb in length, comprising six open reading frames (ORF), namely V1, V2, C1, C2, C3, and C4, among which the ORF V1 encodes a coat protein with a molecular weight of approximately 29 kDa (Zhang, Gong, and Zhou
2009
; Kamal et al.
2024
). TSWV is transmitted persistently by thrips (
Frankliniella occidentalis
) and its genome contains three RNA strands, designated as L coding for RdRp, M coding for GP and NSm, and S coding for N (CP) and NSs proteins. The N protein is primarily involved in forming the viral nucleoprotein complex and regulating various processes of viral replication, transcription and movement (Li et al.
2015
). Our earlier research findings demonstrated that TSWV inhibits TYLCV significantly in tobacco seedlings co‐inoculated with both viruses (Qiao et al.
2023
). Therefore, this study was specifically designed (1) to evaluate the antagonistic effect of the TSWV N protein on TYLCV's multiplication and virulence, and (2) to understand the underlying mechanism involved in interactions between two viruses through their specific interactions between proteins in transgenic
Nicotiana benthamiana
seedlings through a co‐immunoprecipitation method followed by mass spectrometry analysis (Co‐IP‐MS).

To observe the effect of TSWV N on the V1, C3 and C4 proteins of TYLCV in
N. benthamiana

The plasmid TSWV N‐GFP or its corresponding control plasmid were transfected into
N. benthamiana

Agrobacterium

Leaves of transgenic seedlings were taken and total DNA extracted as the template to test for the presence of GM‐N and GM‐CK via PCR using a pair of 35S‐F and YBA‐1132‐R primers. A nontransgenic
N. benthamiana
(non‐GM) seedling was used as the negative control. Eight lines of GM‐CK and seven lines of GM‐N seedlings obtained from the screening process were further evaluated for their relative expression level of corresponding genes by RT‐qPCR (Figure
4a
) (Perveen et al.
2021

4c
). The confirmed GM‐N plants were found to produce a strong immune reaction with the corresponding antibody, with the molecular weight of the fusion protein estimated to be approximately 57 kDa (30 kDa TSWV N and 27 kDa GFP). The subcellular localisation of GFP in the GM‐CK (#7) and TSWV N‐GFP in the GM‐N (#1) line was finally examined using laser confocal microscopy. GFP in the GM‐CK plant was observed in both cytoplasm and nucleus while TSWV N‐GFP in the GM‐N plant accumulated mainly in the cytoplasm (Figure
4d
).

The effect of TSWV N on the virulence of TYLCV was further examined by inoculating TYLCV into the transgenic seedlings through five combinations: GM‐CK inoculated with TYLCV, GM‐N inoculated with TYLCV, non‐GM inoculated with TYLCV, non‐GM inoculated with TSWV and TYLCV, and healthy control. Compared to GM‐CK and non‐GM plants that were inoculated with TYLCV at 7 and 14 days post‐inoculation (dpi), the viral symptom shown on GM‐N plants inoculated with TYLCV was much less severe with only slightly curled upper leaves. Non‐GM plants co‐inoculated with TYLCV and TSWV exhibited considerably more severe symptoms than all the other three treatments, which is consistent with the findings of the preceding study (Qiao et al.
2023
) (Figure
5a
). The newly emerged leaves collected from each treated seedling were used for qPCR analysis (Figure
5b,c
) and western blot detection (Figure
5d,e
) to confirm systemic migration of TYLCV from the infected leaves to those leaves and determine the TYLCV accumulation in those leaves. TYLCV was less abundant in the newly emerged leaves of GM‐N transgenic seedlings inoculated with TYLCV than that in GM‐CK and non‐GM plants inoculated with TYLCV, and this reduction was observed to increase from 51% to 63% at 7 and 14 dpi, respectively. However, the lowest amount of TYLCV was found in the non‐GM plants co‐inoculated with TSWV and TYLCV. Furthermore, the reduction in TYLCV was only 70%–78% in these plants, which was a smaller increase than in the GM‐N TYLCV‐inoculated plants (12%). Therefore, it was clearly demonstrated that TYLCV was significantly inhibited in
N. benthamiana
seedlings with transient and stable expression of TSWV N.

TSWV

The transgenic
N. benthamiana
materials were used for three treatments: GM‐CK, GM‐N, and GM‐N seedlings inoculated with TYLCV, TYLCV, and an empty vector. The total plant proteins with each treatment were extracted under non‐denaturing conditions and the protein extracts were obtained by the Co‐IP method. The results derived from the western blot assay demonstrated the presence of clear, specific target bands with each treatment (Figure
6a
), and the total ion chromatograms were obtained by the MS (Figure
S1
). The three treatments described above rendered 36, 452, and 585 host‐interacting proteins, respectively. The analysed protein profiles (Figure

Furthermore, 28 proteins were found in the GM‐CK plant inoculated with TYLCV and GM‐N plant inoculated with TYLCV. The corresponding unique proteins of each treatment were 8, 244, and 116, respectively. With the aim of identifying the host proteins of TSWV N affecting the pathogenicity of TYLCV, the 244 unique differential proteins (Table
S1
) present in the GM‐N inoculated with TYLCV treatments were therefore chosen for bioinformatics analysis.

2.6
Gene Ontology Enrichment and Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes Pathway Analysis of Differential Proteins
Functional annotation and enrichment analyses of gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) were carried out for the differential proteins, using the
Nicotiana tabacum
proteomics database as a reference. In terms of biological process, the top 15 GO terms significantly enriched for differential proteins mainly included photosynthesis, glycolytic process and translation. The corresponding cellular component was chloroplast, cytoplasm, and its related components. The molecular function was mainly the translation‐related ribosome (Figure
7a
).

The top 20 KEGG pathways with significantly enriched and differentiated proteins were primarily involved in carbohydrate metabolic pathways, including glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, pyruvate metabolism, and the chloroplast‐associated carbon fixation. Additionally, chloroplast‐related pathways such as carbon fixation in photosynthetic organisms and porphyrin and chlorophyll metabolism were also correlated with the presence of TSWV (Figure

). Many proteins of plant viruses participate or are involved in plant gene expression and metabolic pathways; analysis of TSWV N transgenic
N. benthamiana
infected with TYLCV demonstrated that the N protein is primarily involved in carbohydrate metabolism related to photosynthesis, amino acid metabolism, and other energy metabolism pathways of the host during the process of interactions with TYLCV based on GO enrichment and KEGG pathway analysis.
3
Discussion
The molecular weight of the TSWV RdRp protein is approximately 330 kDa, which renders the external production of such a large protein inherently inefficient and challenging, even with codon optimisation (Feng et al.
2020
; Liu et al.
2023
). In the current research, several attempts failed to construct transient expression vectors for RdRp. The TSWV GP is required for the maturation of the viral particles and exists as a spiny protrusion on the surface of viral envelopes (Kikkert et al.
1999
; Ribeiro et al.
2008
). An optimisation of the
GP
gene sequence is essential for activating transcriptional activities of M RNA (Feng et al.
2020
). In this study, a transient expression vector that was constructed based on a wild‐type TSWV
GP
gene was observed to slightly reduce TYLCV pathogenicity in inoculated
N. benthamiana
, suggesting that the wild‐type GP is also functional and needs further studies to determine whether the optimised GP could be more effective in reducing viral virulence. Co‐infection of TSWV and iris yellow spot virus in
Datura stramonium
results in more severe symptoms, indicating that the interaction of the two viral NSs can be more effective in suppressing the silencing mechanism of the host (Bag et al.
2012
). NSs can replace the silencing suppressor HC‐Pro of turnip mosaic virus (TuMV) and promote systemic infection and symptom development of TuMV (Garcia‐Ruiz, Gabriel Peralta, and Harte‐Maxwell
2018
). Within this research, TYLCV was transiently inoculated into
N. benthamiana
seedlings that contained TSWV NSs, causing a significant increase of TYLCV. Overall, the N protein is one of the major structural proteins involved in the formation of ribonucleoprotein complexes and possesses multiple biological functions throughout the viral life cycle (Pappu, Jones, and Jain
2009
; Guo et al.
2017
). The results derived from this research have indicated that the NSs, N, GP, and NSm proteins of TSWV can be transiently expressed in
N. benthamiana
seedlings, together with inoculated TYLCV, and N protein had the most significant inhibitory effect on TYLCV. However, the absence of infectious clones of TSWV in this experiment significantly constrains a full understanding of antagonistic mechanisms of TSWV against TYLCV. Nevertheless, a successful construction of the reverse genetics system of TSWV should provide a great potential for further in‐depth research (Feng et al.
2020
).
Research on resistance against plant viruses was attempted in transgenic plants containing TSWV
N
gene (Pang, Slightom, and Gonsalves
1993
; Jan et al.
2000
). For instance, transgenic tobacco expressing the N proteins of TSWV, tomato chlorotic spot virus, and groundnut ringspot virus exhibits extensive resistance to these viruses (Prins et al.
1995
). Another study found that virus resistance in plants transgenic for the TSWV
N
gene was predominantly characterised by post‐transcriptional gene silencing, confirming that the
N
gene is an effective inducer of RNA‐mediated cross‐protection (Sonoda
2003
). Furthermore, overexpression of the TSWV N protein by the PVX vector triggers a hypersensitive reaction and programmed cell death in the host pepper and inhibits systemic infection by PVX, indicating that this is a characteristic of the TSWV
N
gene and not a common feature of all tospovirus
N
genes (Lovato et al.
2008
). In this study, the relative multiplication of TYLCV was significantly reduced in TSWV N transgenic
N. benthamiana
. TSWV N was effectively antagonistic against TYLCV in transgenic
N. benthamiana
and reduced TYLCV‐induced symptoms, indicating that the TSWV N transgenic plants are not immune to TYLCV, but the TSWV N protein somehow inhibits the viral replication of TYLCV.
A transfection approach was applied to investigate the effect of TSWV N protein against each of TYLCV proteins in
N. benthamiana
seedlings. The results demonstrated that the TSWV N protein significantly inhibited the accumulation of the V1, C3, and C4 proteins, but not of V2, C1, and C2. In addition, no interactions between TSWV N and each of TYLCV proteins were also validated through a yeast two‐hybrid assay (Figure
S2
). According to previous studies, the TYLCV V1 protein is primarily involved in the capsid formation of single‐stranded DNA, viral particle formation, cellular transport, virus DNA accumulation, and insect transmission (Hallan and Gafni
2001
; Harrison and Robinson
2002
) and an absence of V1 can cause a reduction in viral ssDNA assembly (Padidam, Beachy, and Fauquet
1996
). The C3 protein, as a replication enhancer, promotes replication of viral DNA (by about 50‐fold) and symptom development (Cantú‐Iris et al.
2019
), while the C4 protein participates in regulating pathogenicity, suppression of host defence, and disease progression (Jupin et al.
1994
). A disruption of C4 synthesis may result in a reduction of TYLCV‐induced symptoms, reduction of viral DNA replications, hindered disease progress (Kim et al.
2016
), while an overexpression of the C4 protein induces abnormal and viral‐like symptoms in hosts (Luna et al.
2012
; Saeed et al.
2015
). Therefore, the TSWV N protein is probably involved in regulating TYLCV's capsid formation, replication capacity and symptom development through suppressing V1, C3, and C4 proteins of TYLCV. However, further studies are required to elucidate the underlying mechanism involved.
In plant cells, the transiently expressed TSWV N protein is dispersed in a form of granules throughout cytoplasm (Ribeiro et al.
2009
), which was also confirmed in this study (Figure
3a
). However, in TSWV N transgenic
N. benthamiana
, the N protein was evenly distributed throughout the cytoplasm (Figure
4c
) where the
N
gene was constantly expressed. Among the proteins encoded by TYLCV, the V1 protein reportedly localises in the cytoplasm and nucleus of
N. benthamiana
(Zhao et al.
2020
) and is involved in regulating the movement of viral DNA between nucleus and cytoplasm (Gorovits et al.
2013
). In our experiment, TYLCV V1 in the cytoplasm co‐transfected with TSWV N was significantly inhibited, suggesting a possible blockage of V1 transport between nucleus and cytoplasm, and consequently a discontinuity of viral protein synthesis and assembly. In addition, TYLCV C3 is mainly localised in the nucleus and forms irregular granular particles within it (Li et al.
2020
), while C4 is localised at the plasma membrane or around the cell and can interact with plasmodesmata (Rojas et al.
2005
; Mei et al.
2018
). These observations are consistent with the results of the subcellular localisation of C3 and C4 observed with this study. Moreover, it was found that the subcellular localisation of C3 and C4 remained unaltered when they were co‐transfected with the TSWV N in transgenic
N. benthamiana
seedlings.
The Co‐IP‐MS technique has been used to study the interactions between various viruses and host proteins (Watanabe et al.
2014
; Ramage et al.
2015
; Crow and Cristea
2017
; Batra et al.
2018
; Medina‐Puche and Lozano‐Durán
2024
), which provides a tool not only to detect proteins that directly bind to the bait protein but also to screen for proteins that bind indirectly (Gillen and Nita‐Lazar
2019
; Gomaa et al.
2024
). The technique has facilitated research in analysing interactions between TSWV NSs and plant defence‐related proteins such as calmodulin, importin, carbonic anhydrase, and two heat shock proteins (Zhai, Gnanasekaran, and Pappu
2021
). With this study, we also employed this technique to identify a number of host proteins that may interact with the TSWV N protein. Through the Venn analysis to exclude the self‐interactions of the TSWV N protein and GFP with the host, 244 unique differential proteins were identified in TSWV N transgenic
N. benthamiana
plants inoculated with TYLCV. A subsequent bioinformatics analysis revealed that these proteins are primarily involved in carbohydrate metabolic pathways related to photosynthesis or respiration, such as glycolysis, the TCA cycle, and mitochondrial energy transfer, which is basically consistent with the findings obtained by other study using peptide fingerprinting (Corpillo et al.
2004
). The status of carbohydrates has a significant impact on the mobilisation and synthesis of intracellular energy storage materials, symptom development, and defence functions and its changes are a primary basis for the disruption of photosynthesis and respiration (Reinero and Beachy
1989
; Stare et al.
2015
). The accumulation of sugars in plant cells infected by viruses can lead to an imbalance in the nitrogen:carbon ratio, which in turn triggers feedback transcriptional regulation of photosynthesis‐related genes and occasionally inhibits photosynthesis (Balachandran, Osmond, and Makino
1994
; Rahoutei, García‐Luque, and Barón
2000
; Sampol et al.
2003
). In a separate study, 15 host proteins interacting with TSWV N were identified by a yeast two‐hybrid method in
N. benthamiana
plants; these proteins are primarily involved in photosynthesis, plant defence response and ribosome biogenesis (Miao et al.
2021
), which is largely consistent with those identified in the present study and their biosignature analyses. GO functional enrichment and KEGG pathway analysis of the differential proteins, as well as the interaction between TSWV N and TYLCV proteins (Figure
2
), further indicate that TSWV N may inhibit TYLCV infection by competing for or regulating energy within host cells, because all pathways involved in energy metabolisms and regulations are directly or indirectly related. This provides a preliminary understanding of virus–virus and virus–host interactions and establishes a foundation for further studies. However, these results have not yet elucidated or detailed the specific processes by which TSWV and TYLCV interact.
Traditionally, antagonism among microbes has been widely used to control plant diseases caused by pathogenic fungi and bacteria but rarely applied to those caused by plant viruses (Elena et al.
2022
; Elango et al.
2024
). The concept and practice of virus‐to‐virus antagonism in the control of plant viruses thus far have relied on the use of weakly virulent strains of viruses to inhibit more severe strains of similar viruses within the same genus. There is little research information on using specific proteins of viruses belonging to different genera to protect host plants from more devastating viral infections. The findings presented here provide the first theoretical basis and practical evidence on potential uses of the TSWV N protein in combatting TYLCV and offer an understanding of mechanisms involved in the antagonistic effect between TSWV N and TYLCV with a particular focus on the interaction between the two viral CPs. Because this is the first report on the protein of a plant virus to effectively inhibit another different virus, employing TSWV N or other viral proteins in further studies could be a novel research approach in plant viral epidemiology, control strategies, viral protein transgenic plants for tolerance against viral invasions.

Experimental Procedures
4.1
Plant Material and Virus Inoculation
All
N. benthamiana
plants were cultivated in a greenhouse at a temperature of 25°C, a photoperiod of 16 h/8 h (light/dark), and a relative humidity of 70%–80%. TYLCV infectious clones were provided by Dr. Xueping Zhou (Zhejiang University). As a standard inoculation protocol,
Agrobacterium
cultures carrying TYLCV were suspended in the infiltration buffer (10 mM MES, 10 mM MgCl
2
, 200 μM acetosyringone, pH 5.7), adjusted to OD
600
= 1.0, and then infiltrated into the
N. benthamiana
leaves using a 1 mL needleless syringe. The TSWV strain (named as YN) was obtained from a previously reported source (Hu et al.
2011
), revived and maintained in
N. benthamiana
plants. TSWV symptomatic leaves were homogenised in a phosphate‐buffered saline solution (PBS; 0.01 M Na
2
HPO
4
, 0.01 M NaH
2
PO
4
, pH 7.5) at a ratio of 1:5 (wt/vol), and mechanically inoculated by rubbing the viral suspension onto leaf surface dusted with the carborundum powder (Qiao et al.
2023
).
4.2
Nucleic Acid Extraction and
RT
‐
qPCR
The total DNA and RNA of
N. benthamiana
were extracted using the Ezup Spin Column Super Plant Genomic DNA Extraction Kit and Spin Column Plant Total RNA Purification Kit (Sangon Biotech), respectively, following the instructions provided by the manufacturer. One microgram of total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA in a 20 μL system using the HiScript II first Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme). The quantity of DNA was determined using an ND‐2000c spectrophotometer (NanoDrop, Thermo), and the DNA was diluted to a final concentration of 50 ng/μL to standardise the DNA levels in all samples to be tested (Gil‐Salas et al.
2012
). Both qPCR and RT‐qPCR amplifications were performed on the quantitative real‐time PCR Master Cycler ep realplex (Eppendorf). A total of 1 μL of the extracted DNA or cDNA was used as the template in the qPCR/RT‐qPCRs of 20 μL, which consisted of a solution containing 10 μL of 2×SG Fast qPCR Master Mix (Sangon Biotech), 0.2 μL of forward primer (10 μM), 0.2 μL of reverse primer (10 μM), and 8.6 μL of RNase‐free water. Four replicates of each sample were performed for relative quantification of TYLCV
V1
gene,
GFP
gene in GM‐CK, and TSWV
N
gene in GM‐N under the following conditions: 5 min at 95°C; then 40 cycles of 10 s at 95°C, 20 s at 55°C, and 30 s at 72°C. The relative expression values of the genes were calculated using the 2
−ΔΔ
C
t
method with the
NbEF‐1‐α
gene as an internal control (Livak and Schmittgen
2001
). All primers including those for qPCR were synthesised by Sangon Biotech and are listed in Table
S2
.
4.3
Vector Construction
The construction of all plasmids in this study was based on the ClonExpress Ultra One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme). For transient expression analysis, the coding sequences of the
N
,
GP
,
NSs
and
NSm
genes of TSWV were amplified and cloned into the plant expression vector pCB301 (provided by Dr. Xiaorong Tao, Nanjing Agricultural University). Similarly, the genes of each protein of TYLCV were cloned into the pGD‐3FLAG vector (provided by Dr. Tao Zhou, China Agricultural University) to obtain the recombinant clones TYLCV‐V1/V2/C1/C2/C3/C4‐FLAG. In contrast, the TSWV
N
gene was cloned into the pGD‐3Myc vector (provided by Dr. Tao Zhou, China Agricultural University) to obtain the TSWV‐N‐Myc recombinant plasmid. To observe the subcellular localisation, the TSWV
N
gene was cloned into the plant expression vector pYBA‐1132‐GFP (provided by Dr. Jie Liu, Weifang University of Science and Technology), resulting in the recombinant plasmid TSWV N‐GFP. The
V1
,
C3
and
C4
genes of TYLCV were cloned into the pGD‐mCherry vector (provided by Dr. Tao Zhou, China Agricultural University) to obtain the recombinant clones TYLCV‐V1/C3/C4‐mCherry. The construction of all plasmids was confirmed by DNA sequencing, transformed into
Agrobacterium tumefaciens
GV3101, and stored in the laboratory for further uses.
4.4
Gene Expression in
N. benthamiana
Seedlings
In order to conduct transient transfection assays in
N. benthamiana
seedlings, the relevant recombinant plasmid‐containing
Agrobacterium
cells were initially collected by centrifugation and then suspended in infiltration buffer, after which the concentration was adjusted to OD
600
of about 1.0–1.2. Subsequently, the prepared suspension was infiltrated into the dorsal part of the leaf blades of 3‐ to 4‐week‐old
N. benthamiana
seedlings using a 1 mL syringe. To generate transgenic
N. benthamiana
plants that could stably express TSWV N, the constructed TSWV N‐GFP was introduced into
N. benthamiana
seedlings via
Agrobacterium
‐mediated leaf disc transformation (Clemente
2006
). Total plant DNA was extracted from each of transgenic lines separately, and 25 μL of the PCR mixture contained 1 μL of DNA, 0.2 μM each of the 35S‐F and YBA‐1132‐R primers, and 2× Taq PCR Master Mix (Vazyme) under thermocycling conditions of 94°C for 5 min; 30 cycles of 94°C for 15 s, 51°C for 15 s, 72°C for 50 s; and a final extension at 72°C for 10 min. Based on the results, the confirmed transgenic lines were initially screened for subsequent studies.
4.5
Western Blot Analysis
Total proteins were extracted using protein lysis buffer (625 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 10% glycerol, 2% SDS, 5% β‐mercaptoethanol), and the supernatant was collected by centrifugation and subjected to gel electrophoresis by 12% SDS‐PAGE, followed by transfer to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes by electroblotting. Following the transfer, primary antibodies, including anti‐GFP, anti‐MYC, anti‐FLAG (Sangon Biotech) and anti‐TYLCV V1 derived from mouse serum (provided by Dr. Xueping Zhou, Zhejiang University), were employed, while anti‐mouse IgG conjugated with alkaline phosphatase served as the secondary antibody for immunoblotting. The colour development reaction was conducted using the BCIP/NBT alkaline phosphatase colour development kit (Cowin Biotech).
4.6
Co‐
IP
‐
MS
Assay
A Co‐IP analysis was conducted following the previously described methodology (Chen et al.
2017
; Medina‐Puche and Lozano‐Durán
2024
). Leaves of transgenic
N. benthamiana
seedlings with the different treatments were collected and ground in liquid nitrogen. Total plant proteins were extracted from approximately 1.5 g ground leaf powder in 1.5 mL of non‐denaturing protein extraction buffer (50 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM dithiothreitol, 5 mM EDTA, 0.5% Triton X‐100, 1× protease inhibitor cocktail). The supernatant was filtered through a 0.45 μm filter, and an equal amount of total protein was incubated with 30 μL of anti‐GFP nanoantibody agarose beads (AlpalifeBio) on a rotator at 4°C overnight. The resulting coprecipitated complexes were washed four times with precooled PBS and subsequently immunoblotted as previously described. The samples from lanes 3, 5, and 7 in Figure
6
were sent to OE Biotech Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China) for MS analysis. The data obtained from the samples belonging to different treatment groups were subjected to protein profiling using the proteomic database of
N. benthamiana
to ensure the quantity of host proteins present in each case. Following this, the selected proteins were analysed in terms of their GO and KEGG pathway based on the
N. tabacum
proteomics database.
