1
Introduction
Male infertility has emerged as a growing and significant medical concern, affecting approximately 50 % of infertile couples worldwide [
1
]. While most infertile men can produce sperm, many suffer from low sperm quality, manifested as oligospermia, asthenospermia, and teratospermia [
2
,
3
]. Studies have shown that oxidative stress, marked by an imbalance between reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and the antioxidant response, detrimentally impacts sperm structure and function, contributing to male infertility [
4
,
5
]. However, the regulatory mechanisms governing oxidative stress during spermatogenesis remain elusive.
Hydrogen sulfide (H
2
S), historically known as a hazardous, colorless, flammable gas with a characteristic rotten egg odor, is now recognized as the third gasotransmitter, followed by nitric oxide (NO) and carbon monoxide (CO) [
6
]. As an antioxidant, H
2
S can protect cells from oxidative damage by directly or indirectly neutralizing intracellular ROS [
7
]. In mammalian cells, H
2
S can be produced by enzyme-dependent or enzyme-independent pathways [
8
]. Enzyme-independent processes primarily arise from the decomposition of inorganic substances, whereas enzyme-dependent processes are mainly mediated by three enzymes: cystathionine β-synthase (CBS), cystathionine γ-lyase (CSE), and 3-mercaptopyruvate sulfurtransferase (MST) [
8
]. The endogenous steady-state level of H
2
S mainly depends on its oxidation and catabolism [
9
], with the initial step of the sulfide oxidation pathway catalyzed by sulfide-quinone oxidoreductase (SQOR), which prevents H
2
S accumulation and produces highly reactive persulfide species [
9
].
ASB1 belongs to the ankyrin repeat and suppressor of cytokine signaling (SOCS) box (ASB) family, which comprises 18 members (ASB1-18) containing two conserved domains, i.e., N-terminal ankyrin (ANK) repeats and a C-terminal SOCS box [
10
]. ANK repeats are essential for substrate recognition, while the SOCS box domain interacts with Elongin B/C. Consequently, Elongin B/C recruits Cullin-2/5 and the RING-box proteins Rbx1/2, assembling the Elongin–Cullin–SOCS (ECS) ubiquitin ligase complex [
11
]. Studies have shown that specific methylation of the
ASB1
gene is associated with ischemic cardiomyopathy [
12
] and anxiety disorders [
13
]. Moreover, ASB1 is crucial for the inflammatory response during microbial infection and colitis by targeting the nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathways [
14
]. An
Asb1
knockout (
Asb1
-KO) mouse model exhibited normal development; however,
Asb1
-KO testes exhibited diminished spermatogenesis with less complete filling of the seminiferous tubules [
15
]. Nevertheless, the specific mechanism by which ASB1 regulates spermatogenesis and the downstream substrates catalyzed by ASB1 remain unknown.
In the present study, we confirmed that the
Asb1
gene is predominantly expressed in the mouse testis.
Asb1
-KO mice exhibited impaired fertility characterized by oligospermia, asthenospermia, and teratospermia. We observed significantly elevated levels of oxidative stress in the testes of
Asb1
-KO mice, accompanied by decreased levels of the antioxidant factor H
2
S. Mechanistically, SQOR was identified as a specific substrate of ASB1, and ASB1 was found to cooperate with Elongin B (ELOB) to promote its K48-linked ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation of residues K207 and K344. Importantly, the impaired fertility observed in
Asb1
-KO mice could be significantly reversed by supplying NaHS, a H
2
S donor. These findings suggest that ASB1 is crucial for maintaining redox homeostasis during mouse spermiogenesis.

Results
2.1
ASB1 is a highly conserved testis-enriched gene
We initially analyzed the conservation of the ASB1 protein and found it to be highly conserved among species (
Fig. S1A
). The conservation of its ANK and SOCS motifs suggested that it may have a conserved function.
To validate the expression pattern of
Asb1
transcripts, we conducted real-time q‒PCR on mouse tissues. The results revealed abundant expression of
Asb1
transcripts in the testis (
Fig. S1B
). To characterize
Asb1
distribution in mouse testes, we employed a fluorescence
in situ
hybridization (FISH) approach since all commercial antibodies against ASB1 failed to yield promising results. As depicted in
Fig. 1
A,
Asb1
was predominantly located in steps10-15 spermatids. Our FISH results were consistent with a previous report using an
Asb1
-KO mouse model [
15
] in which the endogenous
Asb1
locus was replaced by the β-galactosidase gene. Based on these findings, we speculate that
Asb1
might function during spermiogenesis.

2.2
Knockout of Asb1 impairs male fertility and semen quality in mice
To elucidate the role of
Asb1 in vivo
,
Asb1
global KO mice were generated using CRISPR/Cas9-based gene editing. Two gRNAs were designed to target exon 3 of
Asb1
, leading to a frameshift mutation due to a 20-bp deletion (
Fig. S2A
). The mutation was confirmed by sequencing PCR products obtained from WT and KO mice (
Fig. S2B
).
Asb1
transcripts showed a moderate decrease in KO testes when compared with wild-type (WT) (
Fig. S2C
), suggesting that
Asb1
mutation caused partially decay of the relevant mRNA. Western blotting further confirmed the complete absence of the ASB1 protein in
Asb1-
KO testes (
Fig. S2D
). Collectively, we successfully generated an
Asb1-
KO mouse line.
The
Asb1-
KO mice were viable and showed no overt abnormalities in development or behavior. Male fertility was assessed by mating
Asb1-
KO, heterozygous (HET), or WT males with WT females for 3 months, and the average number of offspring per litter was recorded. WT and
Asb1
-HET mating pairs had 11.9 ± 0.7 and 12.3 ± 1.2 pups per litter on average, respectively, while
Asb1-
KO males sired an average of 4.1 ± 0.8 pups per litter (
Fig. S2E
), indicating that
Asb1-
KO males were subfertile and demonstrating the requirement of
Asb1
for male fertility in mice.
The testes of
Asb1-
KO mice were much smaller in weight than those of WT mice (
Fig. 1
B). Compared with those in the control group, the sperm count in the cauda epididymis of
Asb1
-KO mice was significantly lower (
Fig. 1
C). Computer-assisted sperm analysis (CASA) was used to evaluate the semen quality of
Asb1-
KO males, and the results revealed decreased sperm motility and the sperm progressive motility rate compared with those of WT mice (
Fig. 1
D and E).
Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was conducted to evaluate the morphology of mature spermatozoa in
Asb1-
KO and WT mice. In the WT mice, more than 90 % of the spermatozoa exhibited the typical hooked-head and long, smooth tails, whereas 92.4 ± 2.6 % and 27.9 ± 3.2 % of the spermatozoa observed in the
Asb1-
KO mice exhibited deformed heads and tails (including amorphous, small or tapered heads and coiled/bent tails), respectively (
Fig. 1
F and G).
Ultrastructurally, WT sperm showed well-formed acrosomes at the tips, large, condensed nuclei in the head, and typical “9 + 2” microtubule structure of flagellum (
Fig. 1
H and I). In contrast, most of
Asb1-
KO sperm exhibited misshapen heads with thin or disrupted acrosomes and barely recognizable membranes (
Fig. 1
H). Additionally, a lower incidence of abnormal flagella was observed in
Asb1-
KO sperm, characterized by disorganized or absent fibrous sheaths, outer dense fibers, and axonemes (
Fig. 1
I). Overall, deficiency of
Asb1
leads to oligospermia, asthenospermia, and teratospermia in mice, which may contribute to
Asb1-
KO male subfertility.
2.3
Impaired spermatid nuclear shaping in Asb1-KO mice
To further characterize the etiology of subfertility in
Asb1-
KO mice, we initially conducted periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining to examine testes from
Asb1
-KO and WT mice. Histologically, WT testes displayed intact seminiferous tubules containing all stages of spermatogenic cells, whereas deformed spermatids were evident in
Asb1
-KO testes (
Fig. S3A
). These deformed spermatids may undergo apoptosis, as indicated by significantly increased apoptotic signals in the cauda epididymis of
Asb1-
KO mice compared to those in the control group (
Fig. S3
, B and C).
Spermatogenesis is a cyclic process characterized by sequential and stepwise germ cell development. In mice, the seminiferous epithelium cycle comprises 12 stages (equivalent to 16 steps during spermiogenesis), distinguishable by PAS staining based on acrosome development dynamics and spermatid nucleus morphology [
16
]. Using PAS staining, we assessed the number and nuclear morphology of postmeiotic spermatids at each step of spermiogenesis. At steps 1–9 of spermiogenesis, similar numbers of spermatids were observed between the WT and
Asb1-
KO mice (
Fig. 2
A–C). However, from step 10 onward, a cumulative decrease in the number of spermatids was evident in the testes of
Asb1-
KO mice compared to those of WT mice (
Fig. 2
A–C). Additionally, while the morphology of
Asb1-
KO spermatids appeared normal up to step 9 of spermiogenesis, from steps 10–16, the normal ratio of spermatid nuclei in
Asb1-
KO testes significantly decreased compared to that in WT testes (
Fig. 2
, A, B, and D). Furthermore, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis of
Asb1-
KO testes revealed no abnormalities in spermatids up to step 9 of spermiogenesis; however, gross abnormalities in spermatid nuclei shapes were subsequently observed (
Fig. 2
E). At steps 10–11, WT spermatids continued to elongate and flatten bilaterally at the head, whereas
Asb1-
KO spermatids exhibited nuclear protrusion (
Fig. 2
E). Similar abnormalities were observed in steps 13–14 of
Asb1-
KO spermatids, where the nuclei, despite normal condensation levels, were highly misshapen in
Asb1-
KO testes (
Fig. 2
E). Overall, these findings indicate that ASB1 regulates spermatid nuclear shaping from step 10 onward, which is highly consistent with its distributions during spermiogenesis (
Fig. 1
A).

2.4
ASB1 deficiency exacerbates testicular oxidative stress and sperm DNA damage
The sperm head is known to carry the entire paternal DNA, which combines with maternal DNA upon fertilization, thereby forming a unique genetic blueprint for the offspring [
17
]. A study reported a significant positive correlation between sperm DNA fragmentation (SDF) and the teratozoospermia index [
18
]. Additionally, other studies have also shown that men with teratozoospermia may face a greater risk of sperm DNA damage [
19
,
20
]. Consequently, we conducted an acridine orange staining assay to quantify SDF. The results revealed a significant increase in SDF due to ASB1 depletion compared to that in the WT (
Fig. 3
A and B). Oxidative stress is one of the primary factors triggering DNA oxidative damage [
21
]. ROS are produced as byproducts of oxidative metabolic processes [
22
], while malondialdehyde (MDA) serves as a main biomarker for lipid peroxidation [
23
]. Accordingly, we measured ROS and MDA levels in the testes of WT and
Asb1-
KO mice (
Fig. 3
, C to E and
Fig. S4
). Both ROS and MDA levels were notably greater in
Asb1
-KO testes than in WT testes (
Fig. 3E and Fig. S4
, A and B), whereas antioxidant indicators, glutathione (GSH) and superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity, were significantly decreased in
Asb1-
KO testes (
Fig. 3
F and G). Based on flow cytometry, we subsequently analyzed ROS contents in haploid, diploid, and tetraploid cells of testes, respectively. The results revealed a significant increase of ROS levels in
Asb1-
KO haploid spermatids when compared with WT (
Fig. 3
C and D), while ROS levels in diploid and tetraploid cells remained unchanged between WT and
Asb1-
KO mice (
Fig. S4
, C to F). Furthermore, using DCFDA and DHE staining, excessive ROS levels were also observed in mature spermatozoa (
Fig. 3
H–K) and elongating spermatids (
Figs. S5, A, C, D and F
), but not in round spermatids (
Figs. S5, A, B, D and E
) of
Asb1
-KO mice. Overall, our results underscore the requirement of ASB1 for maintaining redox homeostasis during spermiogenesis.

2.5
ASB1 interacts with SQOR and promotes its polyubiquitination in mouse testes
Given that ASB1 is the substrate recognition subunit of the ECS ubiquitin ligase complex, identifying its potential substrates is essential. To achieve this goal, we conducted immunoprecipitation-liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (IP-LC‒MS/MS) analysis on purified endogenous ASB1 complexes immunoprecipitated from mouse testes (
Fig. 4
A).
Asb1-
KO testes served as the negative control. The testicular lysates were subjected to lysis, followed by IP using an anti-ASB1 antibody and subsequent IP-PAGE, in-gel digestion, liquid chromatography separation, and mass spectrometry analysis (
Fig. 4
A). Two independent experiments revealed 96 overlapping proteins (
Fig. 4
B and
Table S1
). The candidate proteins were functionally classified based on GO function and the MGI mouse phenotypic database (
https://www.informatics.jax.org/
), with a particular focus on events closely linked to the phenotype of
Asb1-
KO mice, including “ubiquitin ligase complex”, “oxidation”, and “spermatogenesis”. Consequently, ASB1 itself and ELOB were classified under the “ubiquitin ligase complex” event (
Fig. 4
C). As a component of the ECS complex, ELOB acts as an adaptor binding Cullin as well as a SOCS-box protein [
11
]. Through reciprocal coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays, we observed that ELOB could interact with CUL2, but not with CUL5 in mouse testes (
Fig. S6
). Therefore, these results suggested the involvement of ASB1 and ELOB in the formation of the ECS complex, which functions as an E3 ubiquitin ligase in mouse testes.

,

,

]. However, the numbers of spermatocytes (
Fig. S7
, A and B), round spermatids (

Fig. S7

Asb1-

Asb1

Within the “oxidation” event, only SQOR was identified (
Fig. 4
C). SQOR is capable of activating the sulfide oxidation pathway to clear H
2
S in mammals [
9
]. Consequently, we measured the levels of H
2
S in mouse testes, and observed a lower level of H
2
S in
Asb1
-KO mice (
Fig. 4
D). Furthermore, we detected the levels of H
2
S in mature spermatozoa, elongating spermatids, and round spermatids, respectively, using three specific H
2

2
S in mature spermatozoa (
Fig. 4
E and F, and
Fig. S9
) and elongating spermatids (
Figs. S10, A, C, D, F, G and I
) were significantly decreased in
Asb1
-KO mice compared with those with WT mice, while the levels of H
2
S in round spermatids (
Figs. S10, A, B, D, E, G and H
) remained unchanged between the two groups. Combined with the fact that ASB1 deficiency causes excessive oxidative stress and sperm DNA damage (
Fig. 3
), these findings suggest that SQOR might serve as a crucial substrate of ASB1 during spermiogenesis.
To validate the MS results, reciprocal Co-IP was performed to confirm the endogenous interactions between ASB1 and SQOR, as well as the interactions between ASB1 and ELOB in mouse testes (
Fig. 4
G). In addition, the knockout of
Asb1
dramatically weakened the ELOB-SQOR interactions (
Fig. 4
G). Moreover, Western blotting, real-time q-PCR, and immunofluorescence assays revealed that
Asb1
deletion significantly enhanced SQOR protein expression without affecting its mRNA expression (
Fig. 4
H–L), suggesting that ASB1 may promote SQOR protein destabilization.
Next, to determine whether ASB1 functions as the substrate recognition subunit for SQOR, we assessed SQOR ubiquitination in mouse testes. SQOR-IP followed by Western blotting with anti-panubiquitin (pan-Ub) and anti-K48-specific (K48-Ub) antibodies revealed significantly lower levels of poly-pan and poly-K48 ubiquitination of SQOR in
Asb1
-KO testes than in WT testes (
Fig. 4
M). As K48-linked chains play essential roles in proteasomal degradation [
30
], this accumulation of poly-ubiquitinated SQOR would predictably result in SQOR protein degradation. Taken together, these findings indicate that SQOR is a substrate of ASB1 in mouse testes and that ASB1 ubiquitinates SQOR through K48-linkedubiquitin chain.
2.6
ASB1 interacts with SQOR and destabilizes it
in vitro
To further elucidate the molecular mechanisms connecting ASB1 and SQOR, we used the HDOCK [
31
] server to predict integrated protein‒protein docking interactions. The three-dimensional (3D) structure of ASB1-SQOR interactions was visualized using PyMOL software, as previously described [
32
]. As illustrated in
Fig. 5
A and Movie S1, the docking interface comprised residues 47–280 of ASB1 and 218–445 of SQOR. The key residues involved in protein‒protein interactions are listed in
Table S2
. As expected, most of the key residues were within the ANK motif in ASB1. However, which sites on the SQOR protein are responsible for ASB1-SQOR interactions? As indicated in
Table S2
, SQOR K218 and M429 exhibited the closest proximity (1.09 Å and 1.69 Å, respectively) to the relevant residues of ASB1. We therefore cotransfected human embryonic kidney HEK-293T cells with expression vectors containing Flag-tagged ASB1 or GFP-tagged SQOR. Co-IP analysis confirmed the reciprocal interactions of Flag-tagged ASB1 and GFP-tagged SQOR (
Fig. 5
B). Subsequently, two SQOR mutants (K218G and M429G) and wild-type (WT) SQOR (SQOR-WT) were cotransfected with ASB1 into HEK-293T cells. Co-IP results demonstrated that SQOR-WT, but not the two other mutants, strongly interacted with ASB1 (
Fig. 5
C). Overall, these findings confirm that SQOR K218 and M429 are critical for ASB1-SQOR interactions.

Discussion
It has been estimated that in mice, more than 2300 genes are abundantly expressed in male germ cells [
37
]. Examining the precise roles of these genes in spermatogenesis has the potential to unveil the intricate molecular mechanisms that drive the formation of mature spermatozoa. This knowledge can further our understanding of the causes of male infertility and contribute to the development of effective contraceptive methods. However, it is worth noting that the specific functions of numerous genes that are mainly expressed in the testes remain largely unknown.
As well-established substrate recognition subunits of E3 ligases, ASB family proteins are generally implicated in regulating cytokine signaling and tumor-associated pathways by promoting the polyubiquitylation of specific substrates and subsequent proteasomal degradation [
[38]
,
[39]
,
[40]
]. Recent studies by us and other groups have revealed that several ASBs, including
Asb3
[
15
],
Asb4
[
41
],
Asb8
[
42
],
Asb9
[
43
],
Asb12
[
44
],
Asb15
[
45
] and
Asb17
[
46
,
47
], are abundantly expressed in mouse testes. Using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene editing technology, we generated mice with ubiquitous knockout of
Asb12
[
44
],
Asb15
[
45
], and
Asb17
[
46
]. Unfortunately,
Asb12
-KO and
Asb15
-KO mice exhibit normal spermatogenesis and fertility [
44
,
45
]. The disruption of
Asb17
has only a minor influence on spermiation but not on fertility [
46
]. In the present study, we identified
Asb1
as a testis-enriched gene in mice.
Asb1
-KO mice exhibited impaired spermatid nuclear shaping, excessive sperm DNA damage, and oxidative stress, ultimately leading to severe oligoasthenoteratozoospermia and subfertility. Taken together, our results indicate that ASB1 is a key regulator crucial for spermiogenesis in mice.
H
2
S is a gas signaling molecule that plays a crucial role in regulating various physiological functions, including vasorelaxation, the regulation of inflammatory mediators, the protection of mitochondrial function, and the inhibition of cell apoptosis [
[48]
,
[49]
,
[50]
]. Studies have verified that H
2
S can decrease the synthesis of inflammatory factors by blocking multiple signaling pathways, such as the NF-κB/MAPK and prolyl hydroxylase 2 (PHD2)/hypoxia-inducible factor-1alpha (HIF-1α)/MAPK pathways [
51
,
52
]. In addition, H
2
S can induce the expression of antioxidant genes and eliminate excess ROS in organisms, thereby effectively functioning as an antioxidant
in vivo
[
53
,
54
]. In this study, a decrease in H
2
S levels was observed in the testes of
Asb1
-KO mice, and this decrease was accompanied by an increase in ROS and MDA levels. However, when we administered the H
2
S donor NaHS to
Asb1
-KO mice, we observed a significant decrease in oxidative stress levels, leading to the restoration of spermatogenesis. These findings strongly indicate that ASB1 plays a crucial role in male fertility by regulating the balance between oxidation and antioxidation in the testes. To our knowledge, for the first time, we identified ASB1 as a unique substrate recognition subunit of the E3 ubiquitin ligase involved in the regulation of oxidative stress during spermiogenesis.
SQOR, also known as sulfide-quinone reductase-like (SQRDL) protein, acts as a major catalyzing enzyme in H
2
S metabolism, playing a crucial role in modulating the levels of H
2
S in organisms by oxidizing H
2
S [
55
]. In this study, based on the IP-LC‒MS/MS results, we identified that SQOR potential interacts with ASB1. In
Asb1
-KO testes, the expression of the SQOR protein was significantly increased, accompanied by an obvious reduction in H
2
S content. Our
in vivo
and
in vitro
experiments further demonstrated that ASB1 interacted with SQOR largely through SQOR 218Lys and 429Met and thereby catalyzed the K48-linked polyubiquitination of SQOR on lysine residues 207 and 344 followed by proteasomal degradation (
Fig. 9
).
Fig. 9
A proposed model for ASB1-regulated spermiogenesis in mouse testes. In WT mouse testes, ASB1 participates in the formation of the ECS ubiquitin ligase complex alongside Elongin B (ELOB). ASB1 primarily interacts with SQOR through its K218 and M429 residues, leading to the stimulation of K48-linked polyubiquitination of SQOR on lysine residues 207 and 344. This interaction facilitates the subsequent proteasomal degradation of SQOR, thereby maintaining redox homeostasis during spermiogenesis. In
Asb1
-KO testes, excessive SQOR accumulation occurs, resulting in an imbalance between oxidation and antioxidation. This imbalance eventually leads to sperm DNA damage and impaired spermatogenesis.
Fig. 9
Numerous studies have highlighted the significant impact of the testicular microenvironment on spermatogenesis [
56
]. The excessive accumulation of ROS within the seminiferous epithelium can be detrimental to the membrane and DNA integrity of spermatids, leading to cellular apoptosis and the disruption of spermatogenesis, eventually impairing male fertility [
5
,
57
]. It has been estimated that oxidative stress may be the cause of a decrease in semen quality in 30 %–80 % of male patients with subfertility [
58
]. Therefore, antioxidants are currently being marketed to treat male infertility. However, several widely used antioxidants, such as vitamin E, folic acid, and zinc, have shown underwhelming effectiveness in improving the semen quality of infertile men [
59
,
60
]. H
2
S is a potentially versatile therapeutic agent, and several H
2
S donors have been in clinical trials for treating cardiovascular and inflammatory diseases [
[61]
,
[62]
,
[63]
]. Moreover, in this study, H
2
S supplementation significantly ameliorated the altered phenotypes observed in
Asb1
-KO testes. We therefore assume that H
2
S supplementation may also be a promising strategy for treating male infertility.
Indeed, while our study has provided significant insights into the role of ASB1 in spermatogenesis, it is important to acknowledge several limitations that warrant further investigation. Firstly, despite our efforts to prepare a new ASB1 antibody, including multiple rounds of immunization and screening, we were unable to obtain an effective antibody for immunostaining. We attribute this failure to the high degree of similarity between ASB1 and other related proteins, which may lead to cross-reactivity issues. To address this, future work could focus on generating ASB1-tagged knock-in mice, such as those with Flag or HA tags, to more definitively determine the localization of ASB1 protein in the testis.
Secondly, previous studies have reported significant heterogeneity in the phenotypes of seminiferous tubules in
Asb1
-KO mice on the C57BL/6 background, with some tubules showing relatively normal spermatogenesis and others displaying a complete absence of spermatogenesis [
15
]. This variability in phenotypic expression posed a significant challenge to accurately investigating the role of ASB1 in spermatogenesis. In our study, we chose
Asb1
-KO mice on the CD-1 background, which demonstrated homogeneous phenotypes consistently showing defects in the spermiogenesis process. This uniformity in phenotype allowed us to more confidently elucidate the specific role of ASB1 in spermatogenesis. However, the variability observed in different genetic backgrounds suggests that the phenotypic outcomes of
Asb1
-KO may differ across strains. Therefore, future studies should explore whether
ASB1
mutations exist in human populations and whether these mutations impact male spermatogenesis and fertility. Such investigations will provide a more comprehensive understanding of the physiological functions of ASB1 and its potential role in male infertility.
Lastly, our study found that knocking down
SQOR
in HEK-293T cells alleviated the excessive oxidative stress caused by
ASB1
knockdown. While this finding is promising, it is unclear whether the same effect would be observed
in vivo
. Future work should investigate whether the knockout of
Sqor
can rescue the phenotype in
Asb1
-KO mice, as this would provide critical evidence for the functional interaction between ASB1 and SQOR in spermatogenesis.
In conclusion, our study defines a mechanism wherein ASB1 maintains redox homeostasis through the proteasome-mediated degradation of SQOR during spermiogenesis. In the future, H
2
S could emerge as a promising therapeutic option for male infertility.

Materials and methods
4.1
Mice and ethics
CD-1 mice were randomly divided into cages under specific pathogen-free conditions, with a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle at 22–28 °C and 50–70 % humidity, and provided food and water ad libitum at the experimental animal center of Nanjing Medical University. For the fertility test, adult male mice (8 weeks–4 months old) were individually housed with two WT CD-1 females in a suitable environment for 2 months, and the number of pups born per litter was recorded for subsequent statistical analyses. For NaHS administration, 8-week-old
Asb1
-KO males were equally randomized to receive drinking water supplemented with or without 30 μM NaHS for 2 months, according to a reported protocol [
36
]. To maintain a consistent and stable concentration of NaHS, the drinking water was changed daily. The study was conducted in strict accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and was approved by the Animal Ethical and Welfare Committee of Nanjing Medical University (license number: 2004020).
4.2
Generation of Asb1-KO mice
Asb1
-KO mice were generated using CRISPR/Cas9-based gene editing methods as previously described [
64
]. In brief, two single guide (sg) RNAs were designed to target exon 3 of
Asb1
; the sequences were as follows: sgRNA-1: 5'-GGCTGTAGTGAACGGGCACTTGG-3' and sgRNA-2: 5'-GTGTACCATGCCTCTCGTGTGGG-3'. Cas9 mRNA and sgRNAs were microinjected into CD-1 mouse zygotes, which were subsequently transferred into the oviducts of pseudopregnant CD-1 females. The founders were backcrossed with WT CD-1 mice for five generations to eliminate potential off-target effects. Genotypes were verified by PCR amplification and subsequent Sanger sequencing using the forward primer 5'-CAGGAGATGGAACACAGAA-3' and reverse primer 5'-ACGAAGAGCACTCATAAGG-3'.
4.3
Histological analysis
For light microscopy analysis, testicular tissues from WT and
Asb1-
KO mice were fixed in modified Davidson’s fluid for at least 48 h, dehydrated in ethanol, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned (5 μm thick). Deparaffinized sections were subjected to PAS staining, and images were captured using a bright-field microscope (Axioskop2 Plus, Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). For electron microscopy analysis, testicular tissues and sperm were fixed in 2 % (
v
/
v
) glutaraldehyde overnight and immersed in 2 % (
w
/
v
) OsO4 for 3 h. The samples were then embedded in araldite, sectioned (80 nm thick), and analyzed using a transmission electron microscope (JEM-1410, JEOL, Tokyo, Japan).
4.4
Immunofluorescence analysis
Immunofluorescence staining was performed on paraffin-embedded sections following previously described methods [
65
,
66
]. Briefly, sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval. After blocking with 1 % bovine serum albumin, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the indicated antibodies (
Table S3
). After three washes with PBS, the slides were incubated with Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for 2 h at room temperature. Finally, the samples were counterstained with 5 μg/mL 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Beyotime, Nantong, China), and images were captured using a Zeiss laser confocal microscope (LSM800, Carl Zeiss).
4.5
FISH analysis
FISH was carried out using a FISH Kit (RiboBio, Guangzhou, China) following the manufacturer’s guidelines. A specific probe designed for
Asb1
was synthesized by RiboBio. Peanut agglutinin (PNA; Vector Laboratories, Newark, CA, USA) was used to label the sperm acrosome. Images were acquired using a Zeiss laser confocal microscope (LSM800, Carl Zeiss).
4.6
Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL)
Cellular apoptosis was detected using a TUNEL Detection Kit (A113, Vazyme, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, deparaffinized sections were rehydrated and treated with 10 μg/mL proteinase K for 10 min, while cultured cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde (PFA) and treated with 0.1 % Trition X-100 for 5 min. Samples were then incubated with equilibration buffer and BrightRed buffer, and stained with DAPI (Beyotime) before mounting. Images were captured with a confocal laser microscope (LSM800; Carl Zeiss).
4.7
Sex hormone measurement
Blood collected from the mouse eyeball was allowed to clot at room temperature for 1 h, followed by centrifugation for 15 min. The resulting serum was stored at −80 °C. Serum follicle stimulating hormone (FSH), luteinizing hormone (LH), testosterone (T), and estradiol (E2) levels were measured using ELISA kits (Bioswamp, Wuhan, China).
4.8
ROS measurement
Testicular ROS levels were measured as described previously [
67
]. Briefly, testes from adult WT and
Asb1-
KO mice were collected and processed to obtain single-cell suspensions. After two-step enzyme digestion with 1 mg/mL collagenase and 0.25 % trypsin, the suspension was incubated with 10 μg/mL Hoechst 33342 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and 10 μM 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFDA; Invitrogen) for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. The cells were then washed with PBS three times and immediately analyzed by flow cytometry (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). Fluorescence intensities were quantified using FlowJo software (version 10.8.1).
To measure the ROS levels in sperm and cultured cells, the samples were stained with 5 μg/mL DAPI, 10 μM DCFDA or 10 μM Dihydroethidium (DHE; Beyotime) [
68
,
69
] for 30 min at 37 °C, and then analyzed using a confocal laser microscope (LSM800; Carl Zeiss).
4.9
Testicular MDA measurement
Testicular MDA levels were assessed using a Lipid Peroxidation MDA assay kit (S0131S, Beyotime) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, testicular supernatant was prepared using lysis buffer (P0013, Beyotime) and incubated with 0.09 % (
w
/
v
) thiobarbituric acid for 15 min at 95 °C. The mixture was cooled to room temperature and centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 min. Subsequently, the supernatant was added to a 96-well plate, and the absorbance at 532 nm was measured on a microplate reader.
4.10
H
2
S measurement
Testicular H
2
S levels were measured using an H
2
S assay kit (A146-1-1, Jiancheng, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, H
2
S extract solution was added to testicular tissues. After homogenization and centrifugation, the testicular supernatant was incubated with zinc acetate, N,N-dimethyl-p-phenylenediamine, and ammonium ferric sulfate. Finally, the absorbance of aliquots was determined using a microplate reader at 665 nm.
H
2
S levels in sperm and cultured cells were measured using the specific H
2

Testicular GSH measurement
GSH levels were quantified using a GSH Assay Kit (BC1175, Solarbio, Beijing, China). SOD activity assay
SOD activity was determined using a SOD Assay Kit (BC5165, Solarbio). Initially, testicular samples were homogenized thoroughly in appropriate buffers to ensure uniformity, followed by centrifugation to eliminate cellular debris. Following a controlled incubation period to facilitate enzymatic reactions, the absorbance was meticulously measured at 450 nm, adhering strictly to standard operating procedures.
4.13
Sperm analysis
The cauda epididymis of adult males was dissected, and spermatozoa were squeezed out and suspended in 200 μL of human tubal fluid for 10 min at 37 °C. A 10 μL drop of solution was then loaded onto a hemocytometer, and the sperm count was evaluated as described previously [

w

4.14
Acridine orange (AO) assay
Sperm DNA fragmentation was assessed by the AO assay as described previously [
76
]. The principle of assay is that sperm nuclear DNA denatures when exposed to acid, resulting in a metachromatic shift of AO fluorescence from green (double-stranded DNA, dsDNA) to red (single-stranded DNA, ssDNA). Monomeric AO bound to native DNA (dsDNA) fluoresces green, whereas aggregated AO on denatured DNA (ssDNA) fluoresces red [
77
]. For AO staining, sperm were spread on poly-
l
-lysine-coated slides, allowed to dry, sequentially treated with solutions of 0.1 M HCl and 0.1 M NaOH, stained with freshly prepared AO solution for 5 min at room temperature, washed with distilled water, air-dried, and imaged. The ratio of ssDNA to dsDNA was considered the sperm DNA fragmentation index.

4.16
IP-LC‒MS/MS analysis
Testes were dissected from 8-week-old mice and lysed using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer (P0013C, Beyotime) supplemented with 1 mM phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride (PMSF). Testicular lysates were incubated with an anti-ASB1 rabbit antibody (Proteintech) overnight at 4 °C and then with Dynabeads A (Invitrogen) at 4 °C for 3 h. The precipitates were then pelleted, washed three times with RIPA buffer and boiled in 2 × SDS buffer for 5 min. The supernatant was resolved by SDS‒PAGE. The gel particles were cut into pieces approximately 1 mm
3
in size for further in-gel tryptic digestion. The peptides obtained after digestion were subjected to desalting using a StageTip (Thermo Scientific) and subsequently analyzed using an LTQ Orbitrap Velos mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) as described previously [
78
,
79
]. To identify proteins, we conducted a search of the tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) spectra against the UniProt protein database, applying a 1 % false discovery rate threshold for both peptides and proteins. To ensure accuracy, the experiments were performed in duplicate. The label-free quantification (LFQ) algorithm embedded in MaxQuant was employed to estimate the protein levels. Proteins that possessed LFQ values in the WT group but lacked LFQ values in the KO group were defined as ASB1 interactors.
4.17
Cell culture and treatment
HEK-293T cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and 1 % penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco) at 37 °C and 5 % CO

The expression constructs were generated via PCR cloning of the following cDNA sequences into the respective vectors: Flag-tagged ASB1 and ELOB into the pcDNA3.1 vector, HA-tagged Ub and mutant Ub into the pRK5 vector, and GFP-tagged SQOR and mutant SQOR into the pEGFP-N1 vector. Cells were transfected with the indicated plasmids and siRNAs using Lipofectamine 2000 reagent (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The cells were harvested at 48 h posttransfection for further analysis. All plasmids and siRNAs were used at a concentration of 1 μg/mL and 50 nM, respectively, unless specifically noted in the figure legends. The following siRNA target sequences were used: si-
ASB1
, 5’- GAAUCUAGUGAAGUGGGAA-3’; si-
ELOB

SQOR

4.18
Western blotting
Testicular tissue or HEK-293T cells were lysed using lysis buffer, and the protein concentration was measured using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime). The lysates were separated by SDS-PAGE and then transferred to 0.45-μm polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5 % nonfat milk for 2 h, followed by overnight incubation with the indicated primary antibodies (
Table S3
) at 4 °C. The next day, after being washed three times with Tris-buffered saline containing Tween (TBST), the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 2 h. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescent substrate (Beyotime) and quantified using the Image-Pro Plus software (version 6.0).
4.19
Co-IP assay
HEK-293T cells transfected with different expression vectors were harvested, resuspended in lysis buffer (Beyotime), and incubated with anti-Flag (Sigma) or anti-GFP (Proteintech) magnetic agarose beads overnight at 4 °C. The beads were washed in lysis buffer, boiled, and analyzed by Western blotting with the indicated antibodies. For endogenous co-IP, testicular lysates were incubated with the indicated antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The extracts were then incubated with Dynabeads A (Invitrogen) for 3 h at 4 °C, washed, boiled in SDS loading buffer, and analyzed by Western blotting.
4.20
In vitro ubiquitination assay
ASB1 immunoprecipitates from testicular lysates were mixed with a 50-μL reaction mixture containing 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.4), 5 mM MgCl
2
, 1 mM dithiothreitol, 2 mM ATP, 100 nM E1, 2 μM E2 (UbcH5a), 2.5 μM Ub and 10 μg His-tagged SQOR and incubated for 3 h at 37 °C. Then, the reaction mixtures were heated with the addition of 50 μL of SDS loading buffer at 95 °C for 10 min, cooled to room temperature and centrifuged. Finally, the products were subjected to immunoblotting.
4.21
In vivo ubiquitination assay
Testicular tissues or HEK-293T cells transfected with the indicated plasmids were lysed in SDS-denaturing buffer (50 mM Tris, pH 7.4; 150 mM NaCl; 1 % SDS) and boiled for 10 min. The cell lysates were then diluted 10-fold in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris, pH 7.4; 150 mM NaCl; 1 % NP-40; 0.5 mM EDTA). After centrifugation, the supernatants were immunoprecipitated with the indicated antibodies or beads overnight at 4 °C. The immunocomplexes were resolved by SDS‒PAGE and immunoblotted with the indicated antibodies.
4.22
Statistical analysis
The data are presented as means ± SD of at least three replicates. GraphPad Prism 8.0 software was used for the statistical analyses. Differences between two groups were analyzed using two-tailed unpaired Student’s
t
-test, and differences among multiple groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s or Dunnett’s
post hoc
test. A p value less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. The biological replicates used are described in the relevant figure legends.
