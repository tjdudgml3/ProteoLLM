Introduction
Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is a primary malignant tumor that accounts for more than 75 % of all primary liver cancers. A
meta
-analysis of 15,158 patients reported that HCC surveillance significantly improved early-stage HCC detection, which was also associated with significantly prolonged survival by providing multiple treatment options
[1]
,
[2]
. A surveillance method for the detection of early-stage HCC (eHCC), however, has yet to be devised. Currently, a comprehensive evaluation is performed for the diagnosis of HCC, including computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging, ultrasonography, hepatic function panels, and alpha-fetoprotein (AFP). AFP is not used solely as a circulating marker for HCC diagnosis because of its low sensitivity (39 %–64 %)
[3]
. As image-based diagnostic tests are less accessible, additional blood-based biomarkers are required for HCC screening.
Hepatocellular carcinoma is characterized by metabolism-associated gene alterations
[4]
. Therefore, several studies have examined endogenous metabolites as potential diagnostic markers for HCC
[5]
,
[6]
,
[7]
,
[8]
,
[9]
. However, few studies to date have identified metabolites for which the serum levels change progressively as the disease progresses in eHCC samples. Since chronic liver diseases such as liver cirrhosis (viral, alcoholic, and non-alcoholic) are the major drivers of HCC incidence
[10]
, correlation checks between metabolite-level changes and HCC development need to be well addressed. Moreover, integrative analyses of the genetic regulation of pathways associated with differentially expressed (DE) metabolites have not been identified in prior research.
Here, we comprehensively profiled and quantified metabolites by applying multiple liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS)-based platforms of metabolomics to the serum of patients with cirrhosis and eHCC, as well as a group of normal control (NC) with no clinical history of liver disease. The cohort comprised Asians, who are the population most vulnerable to HCC globally
[11]
. Through a
meta
-analysis of the transcript-level expression of very early-stage HCC derived from public sources, we sought evidence for disease-related alterations in the selected metabolites. Finally, cross-validation was used to assess the diagnostic potential of related metabolites, which was followed by an examination of the validation cohort.
Materials and methods
Transcriptomics datasets and
meta
-analysis
Human microarray data pertaining to very early-stage HCC tissue and its control were curated from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database with the search terms, “Carcinoma, Hepatocellular[MeSH Terms].” Very early-stage HCC included T1 of the AJCC TNM, stage A or lower of the BCLC system, grades I–II of the Edmondson–Steiner system, and good-moderate differentiation status. The NC group included individuals with no evidence of liver disease or paired non-cancerous adjacent tissues. When a dataset contained multiple HCC patient data, non-very early-stage HCC data were excluded from the analysis. Consequently, seven Affymetrix-based datasets (
GSE6222
,
GSE62232
,
GSE6764
,
GSE14520
,
GSE99807
,
GSE101685
, and
GSE19665
) and three Illumina-based datasets (
GSE76427
,
GSE89377
, and
GSE36376
) were included and analyzed using NetworkAnalyst
[12]
. A
meta
-analysis of the preprocessed data was used to derive the combined effect size (ES) for each gene as a pooled effect estimate. ES was defined as the log2[fold change] (Log2FC). When the absolute value of combined ES was greater than 1.000 and the −log[false discovery rate] (−logFDR) was greater than 2.000, the gene was found to be significantly over- or under-expressed. The detailed methods for the
meta
-analysis are presented in the
Supporting Information
.
Clinical serum samples and metabolomics analysis
The training set, based on an ongoing study to identify HCC biomarkers, included 34 patients with eHCC and 47 patients with cirrhosis diagnosed between February 2014 and June 2017 at Seoul National University Hospital (Seoul, Korea). Additionally, it included 37 healthy controls who underwent medical checkups at the Seoul National University Hospital Healthcare System Gangnam Center (Seoul, Korea) between April 2014 and May 2014. The validation set comprised 50 eHCC patients and 59 cirrhotic patients from an independent study evaluating the proteomic profiling of HCC between September 2011 and November 2017. The diagnosis of HCC is mainly based on the noninvasive criteria of the international
[13]
,
[14]
. Cirrhosis was diagnosed histologically, clinically, or based on typical radiological findings
[15]
. The control group was defined by subjects with no clinical or imaging evidence of liver disease. Serum samples were acquired after overnight fasting and stored at −80 ℃ until extraction.
Comprehensive metabolite profiling was conducted on the extracted serum using C18 reversed-phase liquid chromatography (RPLC) and amide hydrophilic interaction chromatography (aHILIC), followed by mass spectrometry analysis focusing on the (+) and (−) ESI polarities. The identified metabolites were classified according to the confidence levels defined by the Metabolomics Standards Initiative
[16]
. After metabolite identification, the DE metabolites among the three groups were characterized using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Furthermore, we employed the nonparametric Jonckheere–Terpstra (J-T) test using SPSS Statistics 25 to determine the presence of an ordinal response trend among the three progressive disease groups. Detailed methods, from sample preparation to data analysis, are presented in the
Supporting Information
.
Pathway mapping
Pathway mapping was conducted using metabolomic data within MetaboAnalyst 5.0
[17]
. Metabolites classified as environmental contaminants or xenobiotics were excluded from the analysis. Additional emphasis was placed on pathways containing multiple metabolites that exhibited an ordinal trend as the disease progressed to determine the relevance of systemic metabolic alterations. The pathway framework was based on the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes library. An integrated interpretation was performed by adding the associated genes from the pooled effect estimates of the
meta
-analysis into the selected pathways. A hypergeometric test was adopted for the enrichment method. Proteome and phosphoproteome data retrieved from the Proteomics Data Commons (PDC000198 and PDC000199) served as supporting evidence for the pooled effect estimates of the
meta
-analysis
[18]
,
[19]
.
Analysis of a public metabolomics dataset
A metabolomics dataset of a prospective cohort study (Study ID: MTBLS8764) was downloaded from an open-source repository (MetaboLights), which compared the serum metabolic enrichment characteristics of the two cirrhotic patient groups: one group with 165 cirrhotic patients who did not develop to HCC (Control), and the other group with 38 patients who developed to HCC (Case). The most recent data was analyzed when there were multiple data for each patient acquired at different time points. Prior to the statistical analysis, the intensity was normalized by the median value of each data followed by log-transformation.
Random Forest algorithm for group classification
The clinical dataset was divided into two sets: a training set with 80 % of the data, and a test set with the remaining 20 %. The training set was employed for model development by utilizing the quantitative values of metabolites from selected pathways, whereas the test set served as an independent dataset for model evaluation. The Random Forest algorithm was used for classification, applying the caret package version 6.0–94 in R
[20]
. The model was trained with a tuneLength parameter set to 20, allowing for the evaluation of 20 different hyperparameter configurations. A k-fold cross-validation procedure was performed on the training set. Specifically, repeated k-fold cross-validation with 10 folds and 5 repetitions was implemented. Confusion matrices and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves are presented to assess the model’s performance and to illustrate predictability. These analyses were implemented with the caret and pROC (version 1.18.5) packages, respectively. For the further evaluation of the model’s performance, all data was used to train a model and then applied to an independent cohort of fifty-nine cirrhotic and fifty eHCC patients. Prior to the evaluation, data was pre-processed with ComBat function from the sva package version 3.50.0, to remove the batch effect between the analyses.
Statistical analysis
The gene expression and metabolomics data were respectively analyzed using NetworkAnalyst 3.0 and MetaboAnalyst 5.0. Significant features were selected based on FDR (adjusted p-value) derived from the post-hoc analysis of each parametric test. GraphPad Prism 8.0 was employed to conduct univariate analyses of the population's characteristics, which included Chi-square test, Mann-Whitney
U
test, unpaired
t
-test, Kruskal-Wallis test, and one-way ANOVA. The significance levels of individual tests were established at 0.05, and a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The AFP level was analyzed by both parametric and nonparametric tests to explicitly reveal the positive skewness of the data distribution.

Table S1

meta

Integrated analysis of the pooled estimates of gene expression and the metabolites exhibiting an ordinal trend revealed multiple disease-associated metabolic pathways
The population numbers in the serum metabolomics for NC, cirrhosis, and eHCC were 37, 47, and 34, respectively. Their characteristics are summarized in
Table 2
. There were no statistically significant differences in age, sex, or total bilirubin concentration among the three groups. The albumin concentration was highest in NC, followed by cirrhosis and eHCC (p < 0.001). Patients with eHCC typically exhibit elevated AFP levels. However, nearly 80 % of patients with eHCC exhibited a moderate increase in AFP levels that did not exceed 20 ng/mL. This resulted in two distinct statistical analyses of AFP with contrasting outcomes: one-way ANOVA, a parametric analysis, reported statistically insignificant differences, whereas the Kruskal-Wallis test, a nonparametric analysis that assesses the features by ranking, reported statistically significant differences. Four LC–MS untargeted metabolomics platforms, C18 and aHILIC, each with positive and negative ionization modes, identified 418 circulating chemicals in 118 participants, of which 287 were unique after duplicate removal (
Fig. S2
A). A total list of the identified compounds is provided in
Table S3
; 152 compounds were identified by aHILIC with a positive ionization field (aHILIC–pos), 113 by aHILIC with a negative ionization field (aHILIC–neg), 96 by C18 with a positive ionization field (C18–pos), and 57 in C18 with a negative ionization field (C18–neg). Hypoxanthine, glutamine, phenylalanine, citrulline, tryptophan, phenylacetylglutamine, warfarin, glycocholate, and bilirubin were identified using all four analytical platforms, whereas 189 metabolites were detected using only one platform (
Fig. 2
A). Next, we retrieved 218 endogenous metabolites from the 287 uniquely identified compounds, of which 138 were significantly different between NC, cirrhosis, and eHCC (
Fig. S2
B). These DE metabolites were subjected to trend analysis to identify those for which the concentrations progressively increased in response to the disease status. There were 56 metabolites with increasing trends (NC < cirrhosis < eHCC;
Table 3
), whereas 38 exhibited decreasing trends (NC > cirrhosis > eHCC;
Table 4
). The metabolites identified with increasing and decreasing trends were classified according to their chemical structures (
Fig. S3
). Purine, pyrimidine, mono- and disaccharides, and amino acid classes were enriched as the disease progressed, in addition to bile acid classes, which have been the subject of numerous prior investigations
[21]
,
[22]
,
[23]
. In contrast, organic dicarboxylic acids, cholines, and others were identified as metabolite classes with decreasing trends. Interestingly, multiple dicarboxylic acids, including suberate, 6-carboxyhexanoate, sebacate, adipate, azelate, 2-methylmaleate, L-glutamate, and glutarate were highly correlated with the serum albumin levels (all p < 0.0001;
Table S4
). This phenomenon is in line with a previous report that the Sudlow binding site I of human serum albumin is likely to accommodate dicarboxylic acids in plasma
[24]
. Therefore, we presumed that the serum levels of these metabolites did not adequately reflect disease-induced systemic metabolic changes and omitted them from the subsequent pathway mapping and biomarker selection processes. We sought to ascertain whether any metabolic differences resulted from etiologies. Given that 58 % of the patients in the study exhibited liver disease caused by HBV, while the remaining patients acquired liver disease through other causes, subgroup analyses were conducted by stratifying the participants into HBV and non-HBV groups. The metabolites that exhibited significant increasing or decreasing trends remained consistent in both groups, indicating that the DE metabolites were not significantly influenced by the underlying causes (
Fig. S4
). To determine whether systemic alterations in metabolites are indicative of reprogrammed metabolism in cancer, we performed pathway analysis of the DE metabolites that exhibited significant trends. The pathways involving multiple DE metabolites with this trend were then prioritized, and the expression of their associated genes was monitored by matching the pooled effect estimates of the genes (
Fig. 2
B-D). As a result, our attention was directed towards three pathways: bile acid biosynthesis, phenylalanine and tyrosine metabolism, and butanoate metabolism. (
Fig. 2
E–G). The pooled estimates from the Affymetrix and Illumina
meta
-analyses indicated that most of the gene regulation in the pathways occurred in the same direction. In addition, multiple associated (phospho-)proteins retrieved from the HCC proteomics dataset in the Proteomics Data Commons were significantly over- or under-expressed, corresponding to the expression of their coding genes (
Table S5
).

Regarding bile acid biosynthesis, the detected serum bile acids gradually increased as liver status deteriorated. Cholesterol 7α-hydroxylase (CYP7A1) was elevated in both the Affymetrix and Illumina’s pooled estimates, and it was previously associated with a lower survival rate in multiple studies
[25]
,
[26]
,
[27]
. In contrast, enzymes involved in downstream reactions were consistently under-expressed in eHCC. Glycohyodeoxycholate and glycodeoxycholate, which are considered secondary bile acids and produced by colonic bacteria via multi-step 7α-dehydroxylation, followed the enrichment patterns of their precursor primary bile acids.
Although no clear direction of enzymatic regulation was observed, an attenuated tendency was observed in the downstream metabolism from phenylpyruvate – phenylalanine – tyrosine to dopamine production. Meanwhile, the metabolism in the direction of phenylalanine – phenylpyruvate – 3-(2-hydroxyphenyl)propanoate was expected to increase as the associated gene expression increased.
Genes related to butanoate metabolism, which constitute the former part of the
de novo
biosynthesis of cholesterol, were highly enriched in the eHCC group, especially
HMGCR
, a gene encoding HMG-CoA reductase, which is a rate-limiting enzyme
[28]
. The expression of the gene encoding HMG-CoA lyase (
HMGCL
), on the other hand, was significantly downregulated, indicating that the flow of metabolism to mevalonate could be expected to increase.
Prior to the evaluation of the discrimination performance of the eleven selected metabolites from the three pathways, the generalizability of the metabolic enrichment characteristics was assessed by comparing them to an independent dataset from an open-source repository. Nine of the eleven metabolites were identified after the data processing. With the exception of homovanillate and glycodeoxycholate, the metabolite expression patterns between case and control groups were consistent with those of our results (
Fig. S5
). Overall, consistent patterns of gene regulation in the selected pathways were identified, and it was estimated that the trend of the associated DE metabolites could be correlated with the onset of HCC.
A metabolite panel comprising the selected metabolites enhanced an AFP-based HCC discrimination machine-learning model
Next, we examined the diagnostic potential of selected metabolites from these three pathways by testing their discriminatory performance. When AFP was solely utilized as a marker for the discrimination of the three groups (NC, cirrhosis, and eHCC), poor performance was observed, with less than 50 % accuracy and particularly low sensitivity (
Fig. 3
A). The model was then trained using a combination of AFP and the metabolite panel and it outperformed the AFP-trained model in every possible manner (
Fig. 3
B). Notably, the model did not experience significant improvement when all the metabolites that were significant in the trend analysis were used as the training variables (
Fig. S6
). Therefore, we presupposed that the eleven metabolites comprising the metabolite panel were, in fact, important factors regarding model training. Variable importance was scored for the combined AFP and metabolite panel model to determine the features that significantly contributed to the model’s performance. Acetoacetate was the most important feature of the model, followed by AFP and the other metabolites (
Fig. 3
C). Therefore, we generated a model using only acetoacetate. The model trained with quantitative information of acetoacetate performed better than the AFP-based model; when combined with AFP, a modest improvement was observed (
Fig. S7
). Since acetoacetate was negatively correlated with progressive disease status (
Fig. 2
C), glycodeoxycholic acid, a positively correlated metabolite with one of the highest variable importance scores, was tested for model construction. Similar to acetoacetate, the model performance did not reach that of the metabolite panel-based model, although it performed better than AFP alone (
Fig. S8
A). A consistent result was observed when two primary bile acids, glycochenodeoxycholate and glycocholate, were used for training (
Fig. S8
B).

As mentioned earlier, high AFP levels strongly correlate with HCC; however, low sensitivity results in a high probability of false-negative results regarding HCC diagnosis when only AFP was used as a marker. We anticipated that the metabolite panel would enhance the prediction of AFP-negative patients. Therefore, the model was trained without patients’ data who have AFP levels > 20 ng/mL, which is considered a strict sensitivity cutoff value for HCC surveillance
[14]
,
[29]
. No significant difference in performance was observed after stratification of the patients with eHCC by AFP levels < 20 ng/mL (
Fig. 3
D). Although marginally enhanced, the accuracy of the model was not significantly different from that of the model trained using all of the eHCC patient data (
Fig. 3
E). In addition, we examined whether the metabolite panel could distinguish phenotypically modest participants by stratifying Child–Pugh class A. The performance of the model constructed using the metabolite panel surpassed that of the AFP-based model (
Fig. S9
). Finally, the performances of the two models trained by AFP alone or AFP and the metabolite panel were evaluated by generating ROC curves for binary group comparisons: eHCC vs. NC and eHCC vs. cirrhosis. In terms of discrimination between NC and eHCC, the AFP-based model exhibited a modest discrimination ability, whereas addition of the metabolite panel markedly enhanced the performance (
Fig. 3
F). The improvement in model performance was more pronounced when the cirrhosis and eHCC groups were compared. The AFP and metabolite panel-based model exhibited excellent performance, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.926, which was considerably higher than that of the AFP-based model (0.556) (
Fig. 3
G).
There was a previous study that identified metabolite diagnostic markers for HCC through an analysis of early-stage HCC samples
[30]
. Hence, we attempted to compare the performance of the model, which was trained using the metabolite markers reported in this reference. The authors identified canavaninosuccinate, phosphatidylcholines (PCs), lyso PC (LPC) 16:0, phenylalanine, oleamide, and glycochenodeoxycholate as potential diagnostic markers for HCC. Given that phenylalanine, glycochenodeoxycholate, and three LPCs were identified in our dataset, the model was constructed using these metabolites in addition to AFP level. We noted that the overall trend of metabolites was not as significant as those of our panel, with the exception of glycochenodeoxycholate that is also included in our metabolite panel (
Fig. S10
A). In spite of a slight enhancement in the model's performance, the model did not demonstrate competent prediction performance, with an accuracy of 68.2 % (
Fig. S10
B). Likewise, ROC curves revealed that the model was insufficient to rival the performance of our metabolite panel (
Fig. S10
C).

). Consistent with the prior result, the AFP and metabolite panel-based model outperformed the AFP-based model, with an AUC of 0.865 and 0.724, respectively (
Fig. 4

Fig. 4
B). Here, the AUC of the AFP and metabolite panel-based model was 0.811, while the AUC of the AFP-based model was 0.623.

Discussion
The present study sheds new light on the metabolic signature of HCC development by employing comprehensive metabolomics and a
meta
-analysis-based inspection of associated genes. We observed marked differences in several metabolites and identified a cluster of metabolites for which the serum levels changed correspondingly following HCC progression from NC to eHCC. These metabolites exhibit potential as diagnostic markers. Elevated or at times upsurged AFP levels are highly correlated with liver diseases, including HCC, providing a piece of valuable information for diagnosis. However, aside from patients with this distinct phenotype, the AFP level is uninformative for patient differentiation owing to an ambiguous increment. The oddly different results between parametric and nonparametric statistical analyses of AFP in the current study (
Table 2
) reflect the fact that liver disease progression indeed raises AFP levels, but to an insufficient degree. This unequivocally persuaded us that additional circulating markers are required to enhance HCC detection.
HCC progression involves the accumulation of genetic mutations that alter the expression of metabolism-regulating proteins
[31]
,
[32]
. Moreover, cancer metabolic reprogramming for adaptation to nutrient-depleted microenvironmental conditions, chemoresistance, and immune evasion has been recently highlighted
[33]
,
[34]
,
[35]
,
[36]
. A recent study reported a significantly different metabolic landscape, including amino acid, pyruvate, and glutamine metabolism, when comparing serum metabolites of eHCC (BCLC stage 0/A) and advanced-stage HCC (BCLC stages B and C)
[37]
. Therefore, to accurately assess the metabolic signature of eHCC, it is crucial to curate early-stage datasets to identify specific markers. Importantly, our study focused on eHCC and revealed that multiple pathways and DE metabolites correlated with disease progression based on an unprecedented level of metabolic profiling and information on associated gene expression derived from a
meta
-analysis. Gradual systemic metabolic changes were observed in the event of HCC, and these changes correlated with associated genetic alterations. Taken together, we presume that metabolic reprogramming in the herein focused pathways begins before neoplastic transformation and persists after the formation of a malignant tumor. Therefore, we believe that this study provides valuable insights into the physiological changes in HCC and potential diagnostic markers.
There was disagreement between the concentration of systemic DE metabolites and the associated gene expression. For example, tyrosine levels gradually increased following disease progression, whereas a distinct attenuated tendency was observed for the genes involved in the sequential metabolism from phenylalanine to dopamine (
Fig. 2
F). First, it could be attributed to the limitations of systemic metabolite profiling. However, another potential explanation is that reversed gene expression is induced as a feedback mechanism to control metabolic levels. Genes associated with the bile acid biosynthetic pathway were consistently downregulated in cancers, except for
CYP7A1
, which encodes an enzyme involved in the first step of bile acid metabolism from cholesterol. Hydroxylation of cholesterol by CYP7A1 is considered the rate-determining reaction in bile acid biosynthesis
[38]
. In addition, HCC is associated with elevated bile acid serum levels, and this has been reported to be a risk factor for HCC in patients with HBV
[8]
,
[39]
. Therefore, low expression of downstream genes in bile acid biosynthesis may not substantially affect the synthesis rate of bile acids and may be a consequence of regulatory effect against high
CYP7A1
expression. Moreover, the difference in metabolic enrichment is a snapshot of a particular metabolic state and, therefore, cannot provide information on the flux changes in metabolic processes.
In our machine learning models, acetoacetate had the greatest impact on model training among the selected metabolites. Numerous studies have shown that HCC is characterized by elevated cholesterol biosynthesis
[25]
,
[27]
,
[40]
, which is associated with a poor prognosis. A negative correlation between disease progression and acetoacetate levels may be attributable to this regulation. Presumably, acetoacetate can either be consumed for HMG-CoA synthesis or its production can be reduced as a result of the downregulated HMG-CoA removal by HMG-CoA lyase, which is an alternative fate for HMG-CoA other than entering the mevalonate pathway. This corresponded to the gene regulation tendencies of the pooled effect estimates from the Affymetrix- and Illumina-based
meta
-analysis of the current study (
Fig. 2
G). Furthermore, it has been documented that HCC inhibits associated metabolic pathways that secrete acetoacetate, which in turn stimulates macrophage recruitment mediated by β-1,3-N-acetylgalactosaminyltransferase II, ultimately promoting the development of solid tumors
[41]
.
In addition to acetoacetate, 3-hydrobutanoate exhibited a decreasing trend, suggesting that the reduction of ketone bodies may occur as HCC develops. It has been reported that nutrition-deprived HCC cells rewire their metabolic pathways and utilize ketone bodies as a source of nutrition, in contrast to normal adult liver cells
[42]
. Here, nutrition-limited condition activates mTORC2-AKT-SP1 signaling pathway, and this eventually induces 3-oxoacid CoA-transferase 1 (OXCT1), which controls the rate-limiting reaction in ketolysis. As shown in
Fig. 2
G, the pooled effect estimates of
OXCT1
and
AACS
, a gene that encodes for acetoacetyl-CoA synthetase, were consistently overexpressed in very early-stage HCC, indicating that the enhanced ketolytic pathway may be responsible for the decreasing tendency of ketone bodies. 3-hydroxybutanoate can also directly engage in epigenetic modification that is associated with the proliferation of HCC. Cui
et al.
reported that 3-hydroxybutanoate increases the acetylation of histone H3K9, which makes HCC cells susceptible to ferroptosis induced by erastin and sorafenib
[43]
. Therefore, the reduced ketogenesis, which corresponds with the reduced
HMGCL
expression in the current study, depletes 3-hydroxybutanoate and may promote HCC progression.
As elevated metabolite markers, bile acids have also demonstrated their value in improving the model performance in group discrimination. Alcohol- and virus-associated HCC have been associated with highly enriched circulating bile acids
[44]
. Another study reported that glycocholate and glycochenodeoxycholate increased by more than 10 and 12 times, respectively, in the HCC group compared to the controls
[45]
. Multiple gene regulatory pathways governing the accumulation of bile acids, including bile acid expression and transport, are associated with hepatic damages and hepatocarcinogenesis
[46]
,
[47]
. Secondary bile acids are produced by the gut microbiome, by 7 alpha-dehydroxylation of primary bile acids. This process is specifically catalyzed by
Clostridium
species, and high levels of secondary bile acids are associated with immune evasion, leading to HCC development
[48]
,
[49]
. However, metabolites that are considerably associated with microbial metabolism may be weakly influenced by host genetic factors and thus may not accurately reflect disease-induced phenotypes
[50]
. Nevertheless, a model trained using primary bile acids maintained a higher level of predictability, emphasizing that bile acids should still be considered as potential diagnostic markers for HCC (
Fig. S8
B).
Multiple metabolites involved in phenylalanine and tyrosine metabolism were correlated with HCC development in the current study. In a previous study, a deregulated phenylpyruvate level, for which the variable importance score was among the highest, was characterized in an HCC xenograft mouse model
[51]
. In a follow-up study of hereditary tyrosinemia type 1 patients with a tyrosine catabolism disorder, the patients had a high risk of developing HCC
[52]
. Moreover, gene signatures related to tyrosine metabolism have been correlated with the prognosis of patients with HCC
[53]
. Tyrosine can be converted to acetoacetate through a series of enzymatic reactions and this involves five enzymes, which are encoded by
TAT
,
HPD
,
HGD
,
GSTZ1
, and
FAH
[54]
. Notably, the pooled effect estimates of these five genes were all under-expressed in our in silico analysis (
Table S1
). As previously discussed, reduced ketogenesis may promote HCC progression. Therefore, attenuated tyrosine-to-acetoacetate ketogenic pathway might be associated with the development of HCC, which is demonstrated by the elevated tyrosine level.
The current study is limited by the fact that serum metabolites cannot fully reflect the metabolic reprogramming that occurs within a tumor. However, this does not negate the fact that a panel of serum metabolites could significantly enhance the accuracy of HCC surveillance. Therefore, the combined use of AFP and metabolite panels in HCC diagnosis should be considered, as our model validated the potential for enhanced discriminatory capabilities.
Conclusions
In this study, we comprehensively profiled a metabolic signature associated with the onset of HCC by employing multiple metabolomic platforms in three sequential groups: NC, cirrhosis, and eHCC. Meta-analysis of HCC transcriptomics datasets from very early-stage cancer revealed multiple DE genes, and distinct genetic regulation was observed for pathways involving multiple DE metabolites with gradual alterations. When the Random Forest algorithm-based model was trained by the panel using these metabolites, the classification performance was significantly enhanced compared with when AFP was used alone. Finally, this study highlights the significance of metabolite markers in the diagnosis of eHCC.
