Introduction
Cutting propagation has the advantage of maintaining the excellent characteristics of plants, and is the most commonly used way of propagation of plant excellent germplasm resources at present. The successful formation of adventitious root (AR) is crucial for the propagation process and involves comprehensive changes in morphology, physiology, and gene expression [
1
]. Recent studies have investigated these changes from anatomical [
2
], physiological [
3
], and molecular perspectives [
4
] to understand the underlying mechanisms of AR formation. Anatomical analysis provides insights into the timing, location, and process of AR development, forming the basis for determining periods, sampling, and indexing [
5
]. Specifically, AR in tree peony predominantly arises from the vascular cambium and, to a lesser extent, from callus tissues [
6
]. Physiological and biochemical responses, such as variations in endogenous hormone levels and enzyme activities, are intimately linked to AR formation in plants [
7
]. For instance, auxin, known for its pivotal role in various root organogenesis types, accumulates specifically at AR formation sites, acting as a master regulator of the process [
8
]. Moreover, AR formation is influenced by a network of hormonal pathways, with phenolic compounds, sugars, polyamines, and mineral nutrients playing unique and essential regulatory roles [
9
]. The influence of abscisic acid (ABA) on AR development, for example, is dose-dependent, with glucose modulating ABA levels by affecting enzyme activities and gene expression, subsequently impacting AR formation [
10
]. Ethylene serves as an enhancer of AR development [
11
], while the catabolism of polyamines and their by-product, hydrogen peroxide (H
2
O
2
), promote AR formation [
12
], with H
2
O
2
acting as a signaling molecule in the process [
13
].
All plant life activities, including the formation of AR, are governed by the spatial and temporal-specific expression of genes. Critical among these for AR development are the auxin signaling-responsive genes, including the
AUXIN RESPONSEFACTOR
(
ARF
),
LATERAL ORGAN BOUNDARIES-DOMAIN
(
LOB
) gene families [
14
]. Additionally, a multitude of genes have been identified as regulators of AR across various plants, such as the
WUSCHEL-related homeobox
(
MdWOX15, JrWOX16
) [
15
,
16
],
LATERAL ORGAN BOUNDARIES DOMAINs
(
MdLBD16a
) [
17
],
high-affinity K
+
(
MdHAK
) [
18
],
Auxin efflux carrier
(
CsPIN
) [
19
]. These genes do not function in isolation but interact synergistically. For example, the WOX-ARF modules exhibit a functional specialization in initiating different root types, wherein the WOX11-ARF6/8 complex triggers AR formation through the activation of
LBD16
[
20
]. Similarly,
LkWOX4
is involved in AR initiation and development, acting through the 3-Indoleacetic acid (IAA), jasmonic acid (JA), and abscisic acid (ABA) signaling pathways [
21
]. Moreover,
PIN3
plays a crucial role in AR regulation by mediating auxin transport [
22
]. These discoveries have significantly advanced our understanding of the intricate regulatory mechanisms underlying plant AR occurrence.
Cinnamomum parthenoxylon
is a valuable tree species native to southern China, renowned for its substantial essential oil content [
23
,
24
]. Its extracts find wide applications in the food [
25
,
26
], spices [
27
,
28
], and medical sectors [
29
–
31
]. The breeding of improved varieties predominantly occurs within individual specimens, as there is significant variation in the content and composition of the leaf essential oil among different individuals [
32
]. Propagation through cuttings represents the primary method of breeding for this species. However, the challenges associated with rooting cuttings stand as a major barrier to its cultivation and widespread adoption. Understanding the regulatory mechanisms behind AR formation is crucial for the plant’s survival and propagation, yet studies focused on this process in
C. parthenoxylon
are notably absent. In our research, to accurately identify regulatory factors, we simulated natural rooting conditions by using untreated cuttings and monitored the changes in critical substances during the rooting process. Additionally, we conducted a thorough analysis of transcriptome data through GO and KEGG to pinpoint the principal pathways and genes involved in rooting. This approach provides a foundational understanding for optimizing breeding and propagation conditions for
C. parthenoxylon
.
Materials and methods
Plant materials
Healthy and dormant branches were harvested from a three-year-old mother tree preserved at the
C. parthenoxylon
gene pool of the Jiangxi Provincial Academy of Forestry (27°53′N, 116°47′E). The cuttings, each with one leaf measuring 18 cm in length, were immersed in water for 30 min. For the analysis of AR formation, the stem bases of in vitro shoots, approximately 0.5 cm in length, were sampled at 0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25 and 30 days post-initiation. A portion of these samples was fixed in FAA fixative (70% ethanol: acetic acid: formaldehyde = 90:5:5) for subsequent observation of anatomical structures. The remaining samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and, after thorough mixing of materials collected at each time point, were stored at -80℃. This preparation was undertaken for the subsequent determination of physiological indices and transcriptome sequencing.
Morphological and anatomical observation
The anatomical structure was examined using the conventional paraffin sectioning technique. The samples were subjected to washing, gradient alcohol dehydration, xylene clarification, immersion in soft paraffin, embedding, and sectioning. Then, the paraffin-embedded sections were stretched and affixed onto slides. Staining was then performed using Safranin-O and Fast Green. Following staining, the paraffin sections were sealed with neutral balsam mounting medium. Observations and photographic documentation of the AR formation process were conducted using a Leica DFC 500 optical microscope. Based on the anatomical observations, three distinct stages of AR formation post-cutting were identified: Callus induction stage, AR primordia induction stage, and AR growth stage. The corresponding sampling time points for these stages were designated as CP0, CP1, CP2, and CP3, respectively, (CP0-CP1) Callus induction stage. (CP1-CP2) AR primordia induction stage. (CP2-CP3) AR growth stage.
Determination of endogenous hormone content
Root samples collected at the aforementioned four-time points were analyzed to quantify the contents of soluble sugar and soluble protein using anthrone colorimetry and Coomassie Brilliant Blue methods, respectively. Enzymatic activities of POD, PPO, and IAAO were assessed through colorimetric assays. Furthermore, the levels of IAA, IBA, GA, ABA, and ZR were quantified using high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS/MS). The experiment was conducted with six replicates to ensure statistical robustness and reliability of the data.
RNA-seq and quality control
The time points CP0, CP1, CP2, and CP3, representative of different stages of AR formation, were selected for RNA-seq. RNA extraction was conducted using an R6827 Plant RNA Kit (Guangzhou, China). Following quality verification of the RNA samples, mRNA was isolated and fragmented, after which double-stranded cDNA was synthesized through reverse transcription. Subsequently, sequencing libraries were prepared involving steps such as 5′ terminal repair, 3′ terminal adenylation, adapter ligation, and enrichment. Post-validation of the library quality, high-throughput sequencing was performed using the DNBSEQ-T7 platform. The raw data was processed with FASTP (version 0.21.0) to generate clean data for analysis [
33
]. Three biological replicates were performed.
Transcriptome assembly and functional annotation
Unigenes were derived through sequence assembly using the Trinity software (version 2.11.0) [
34
]. The functional annotation of unigenes was carried out using Diamond blastp (version 2.0.6.144), referencing multiple databases, including Nr, Pfam, GO, KEGG, and COG/KOG. DEGs between various rooting time points were identified using DESeq (version 1.26.0) [
35
], adopting an FDR < 0.05 and |log
2
FC|>1 as the selection criteria. The ClusterProfiler software (version 3.14.3) [
36
] was employed to examine the clustering patterns of DEGs, followed by functional enrichment analysis for each identified clustering pattern.
Validation of RNA-Seq by qRT-PCR
The 16 DEGs related to rooting selected were validated by real-time PCR. The specific primers for these genes were designed by using Primer premier 5, then were handed over to Ruibiotech (Beijing, China) for primer synthesis (Tab.
S1
). Actin was used as an internal reference gene. The relative expression of the DEGs was quantified using the 2
−ΔΔCT
method [
37
]. Three biological replicates were performed.
Statistical analysis
A one-way analysis of variance (
P
< 0.05) and Least Significant Difference were conducted via SPSS software (version 26). Images were produced using GraphPad Prism (version 9.0).

Analysis of sequencing and assembly result
Through high-throughput sequencing and meticulous data filtering, each sample yielded over 40 million clean reads. The ATGC content distribution showed a balanced ratio between A and T, and C and G, indicative of successful sequencing. Both Q20 and Q30 base percentages exceeded 97.56% and 92.14%, respectively, demonstrating high-quality sequencing data. The assembly process resulted in 75,027 unigenes with N50 values of 1275 for transcripts and 1816 for unigenes, reflecting good assembly integrity and continuity. High correlation coefficients (> 0.9) among biological replicates of the same time point underscored the reliability of the sample processing (Fig.
S1
).

For comprehensive functional insights, unigene functions were annotated using seven databases, with 25,467 unigenes annotated under an E-value threshold of < 0.00001. Uniprot (32.78%), Pfam (26.26%), and GO (24.26%) were the most referenced databases (Tab.

Discussion
AR formation is a vital process in plants, facilitating critical stages of their life cycle, specifically the vegetative and reproductive phases. This process encompasses root morphogenesis along with numerous physiological activities. It has been observed that the rooting capacity is closely related to the site of root origin [
41
]. Studies demonstrate an inverse relationship between rooting efficiency and callus formation [
42
], noting that roots emerging from callus tissue often exhibit poor connectivity with the stem’s vascular system, leading to decreased survival rates of cuttings [
6
]. However, in this study, AR formation in
C. parthenoxylon
is initiated from the callus, potentially acting as a barrier to effective rooting.
The rooting process is energetically demanding, primarily relying on carbohydrates, particularly when rooting is protracted. Soluble sugars at the cutting base are pivotal for root initiation [
43
,
44
]. A previous study revealed a negative correlation between non-structural carbohydrate levels in
Corylus avellana
cuttings and their rooting percentages [
45
]. Similar to observations in
Olea europaea
[
43
], soluble sugar levels in this research also diminished swiftly during callus formation. Energy-intensive activities like callus cell formation and division lead to significant expression of genes associated with kinase activity and carbohydrate metabolism within the GO framework, alongside upregulation of genes linked to sucrose degradation in the starch and sucrose metabolism pathway during callus induction stage. Furthermore, cell differentiation during AR formation requires abundant soluble proteins, which could explain the observed decline during the development of both callus and AR primordia in this study, paralleling findings from
Picea asperata
cuttings [
46
]. In the process of rooting, the activity of oxidoreductase changed dramatically, and a large number of genes related to oxidoreductase activity were differentially expressed. Oxidative enzymes play a critical role in the AR development of
C. parthenoxylon
. Enzymes such as POD, PPO, and IAAO are closely associated with AR formation, while SOD is implicated in regulating H
2
O
2
levels, mediated by hormonal activity [
47
], which is known to promote rooting [
12
]. This study revealed a unique trend in SOD activity, exhibiting a down-up-down pattern, and genes encoding SOD also showed the similar expression trend, which suggested a divergent role from SOD in rooting processes. POD activity and related gene expression decreased significantly during the formation of root primordia, but increased significantly during the growth of adventitious roots, indicating that POD was negative to the formation of root primordia, but conducive to the growth of roots. It was found that high activity POD could promote lignin synthesis, and thus affecting the differentiation of callus root primordia and promoting the elongation of root primordia [
48
,
49
]. Additionally, changes in PPO activity indicate its potential role in inhibiting cellular differentiation. IAAO levels initially rise during callus induction stage before subsequently decreasing, a trend consistent with previous studies involving smoke exposure [
50
].
Hormones, particularly auxins, play a pivotal role in regulating the formation of AR. When a plant sustains an injury, the injury site can develop multiple auxin biosynthesis sources [
51
]. This auxin is then transported to the procambium, where it regulates
WOX11
expression, impacting callus formation [
52
]. TAA and YUC are important enzymes in auxin biosynthesis [
14
]. In this study,
CpTAA
and
CpYUC
expression were the highest in CP0, levels of the auxins IAA and IBA increased rapidly during callus induction stage. Although high levels of exogenous auxin can facilitate the cell fate transition to callus, they inhibit subsequent differentiation [
53
], which aligns with the observed rapid decrease in auxin levels during AR primordia induction stage in this study. Furthermore, gene expression related to GA is inversely related to the ability of AR formation [
54
]. However, GA levels gradually increased in this study, which may indicate that GA directly reduces flavonoid biosynthesis, thereby enhancing rooting [
55
]. Additionally, the
CpGA
3OX
involved in GA biosynthesis upregulated in the rooting process, which had a similar tendency with GA content. Concurrently, numerous DEGs were identified within the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway. ZR has been noted to adversely affect adventitious rooting [
56
], however, the content of ZR increaseed during callus and AR primordia formation, suggesting that high ZR levels may promote rooting in
C. parthenoxylon
, which is consistent with the research result in tree peony [
6
]. The ABA typically inhibits AR formation in
Arabidopsis
[
57
], but enhances it in cucumber [
10
]. In our research, ABA exhibited an N-shaped trend, indicating variable impacts at different rooting stages. The roles of different hormones in rooting are complex and often opposing, hence the ratio of promotive versus inhibitive hormones, such as IAA/ABA [
58
] or IAA/ZR [
59
], is commonly used to assess rooting capability. The value of IAA/ZR decreased gradually before AR primordia formation, indicated it may be beneficial to AR formation, which is opposite in
Eucommia ulmoides
[
60
]. In this study, the change trend of IAA/GA value was similar to IAA/ZR, decreased gradually from cutting to formation of AR primordia, which is similar to the research results in tree peony [
6
]. It has been shown that the ratio (IAA + GA + ZR)/ABA can serve as an indicator of rooting ability [
61
], and in the initial two stages of AR formation during cutting, an increasing trend of (IAA + IBA + GA + ZR)/ABA was observed, which corroborates findings in
Larix kaempferi
[
61
]. Intriguingly, the same hormone can have different functions across the three rooting stages, implying that their effects diverge between the formation of callus, root primordium, and AR elongation. This discrepancy likely stems from the varying involvement of hormones in cellular differentiation, division, and growth.
The AR formation process is governed by intricate and interlinked regulatory networks. Among these, the plant hormone signal transduction and the MAPK signaling pathways play pivotal roles. Following the detachment of explants from the parent plant, wounded parts accumulate significant amounts of auxin, which is essential for inducing rooting. The transduction of auxin signals is crucial for responding to this hormone. The TIR1 functions to ubiquitinate and degrade Aux/IAA proteins [
62
], which act as negative regulators of AR formation [
63
,
64
], thereby modulating the activity of ARF proteins [
65
]. These ARFs are integral to AR development, with studies indicating that ARF6/8 can activate
LBD16
, triggering the initiation of AR primordia [
20
]. Furthermore, GH3 is involved in regulating endogenous auxin homeostasis [
66
], where
CsGH3
overexpression can inhibit root growth and development [
67
]. However, GH3-like proteins have been found to positively correlate with the number of ARs [
68
]. Additionally, SAUR15 is capable of phosphorylating the cell surface receptor-like kinase BRASSINOSTEROID-INSENSITIVE 1 (BRI1) [
69
], enhancing the expression of auxin biosynthesis genes and thereby promoting AR formation. The signal transduction of other hormones also plays a critical role in this context. For instance, the B-ARR cytokinin mutants exhibit reduced sensitivity to cytokinin, which impacts callus induction and subsequent rooting. In this study,
CpARF
expression was up-regulated, whereas
CpGH3
and
CpTIR1
were down-regulated across the three stages of AR formation. The gene expression changes encoding CpAUX/IAA and CpSAUR are inconsistent, suggesting they might influence AR through a stacking effect or be regulated by other upstream factors. Additionally, numerous DEGs enriched in the ethylene pathway are up-regulated, indicating a significant involvement of ethylene in AR formation. This aligns with research in cucumber, where auxin-induced AR formation has been shown to depend on ethylene [
70
]. MYC2, a bHLH transcription factor, is a key component of the hormone regulatory network with jasmonic acid (JA) as the core, and is involved in the regulation of a large number of plant development processes [
71
].
CpMYC2
was upregulated in the AR primordia formation in this study, indicating it plays a positive regulatory role in this process. The MAPK cascades are crucial signaling modules situated downstream of sensors/receptors, designed to perceive both endogenous and exogenous stimuli and then transmit these signals to the cell [
72
]. This study identified numerous DEGs enriched in this pathway, some of which are associated with AR formation. The genesis of ARs coincides with the moment cuttings are separated from the plant, introducing exogenous stimuli, specifically wounding. Previous research has demonstrated that wounding is a primary signal that initiates the regenerative process [
73
], influencing auxin levels to promote cell fate transition [
74
], and ultimately impacting AR development. In this signaling pathway, the disruption of CaM can reduce auxin responsiveness, consequently hindering AR formation through the mediation of Ca
2+
signaling [
75
]. The RESPIRATORY BURST OXIDASE HOMOLOG PROTEIN D (RBOHD), a type of NADPH oxidase, facilitates the propagation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) involved in AR induction [
76
]. Notably, the accumulation of significant ROS levels in the epidermal cells at nodal positions can trigger apoptosis of these cells, thereby reducing the mechanical resistance against the emerging root primordia [
40
]. Recent studies have revealed that both ethylene and reactive ROS pathways are swiftly activated in response to wounding, serving as signals that promote AR regeneration [
77
]. Ethylene, acting as a rapid response signal to damage [
77
], can enhance auxin biosynthesis and facilitate its basipetal transport, thereby concentrating auxin locally [
78
]. Concurrently, auxin may boost the expression of ethylene biosynthesis genes, such as
CsACS6
[
70
]. The ROS component, H
2
O
2
, is recognized as a significant influencer of AR formation, where its presence can increase the number and weight of ARs [
79
]. Wounding induces the activation of MAPK signaling cascades [
80
], with MAPKs contributing to plant hormone biosynthesis and signaling [
81
]. Inhibition of the MAPK cascade has been shown to diminish AR formation and growth [
79
], with MPK3/6 within this pathway modulating the expression of
ABI4
, which in turn suppresses the initiation and development of ARs [
40
]. In this research, genes related to ethylene, including
CpACS6
and two genes encoding RBOHD, were found to be upregulated.
Numerous studies have demonstrated that the formation of AR involves multiple cell fate transitions and developmental processes, with distinct regulatory mechanisms governing each transition [
52
,
53
]. In
Arabidopsis
and rice, the
WOX11
and
WOX5
genes serve as markers for founder cells and callus cells, respectively [
82
]. In this study, we observed differential expression of
CpWOX
genes at various stages of AR development. Additionally, we noted an upregulation in a gene encoding PP2C post the formation of root primordia, which may suppress the expression of
ABI4
[
40
]. In summary, significant changes were observed during the AR formation in cuttings, encompassing morphological, tissue-related, physiological, and transcriptomic alterations. These changes indicate active participation in this developmental process. Plant hormone signal transduction and MAPK pathways, as critical conduits involved in AR development, were found to be enriched with a plethora of DEGs. Following plant injury, the ethylene and ROS pathways within the MAPK cascade promptly respond to wounding by modulating gene expression to produce substantial quantities of ethylene and reactive oxygen species. These act as signals to enhance auxin synthesis and local accumulation, thereby stimulating cell metabolism and gene expression, promoting cell fate transformation, and initiating the AR formation process. This phase involves significant contributions from nutrients and oxidoreductases. In concert with wound signals, plant hormones regulate and facilitate AR formation. Moreover, other plant hormones interact with auxin while playing unique roles in this process. Furthermore, a significant enrichment of DEGs in the phenylpropanoid metabolism pathway suggests its potential supplementary role in AR formation. By simulating natural conditions, this study aimed to understand the plants’ ability to adapt to environmental changes and to uncover the self-regulation mechanisms employed by plants during AR formation post-injury. Analyzing the changes in critical substances and examining patterns and impacts of these changes offer insights and references for optimizing rooting conditions.
Conclusions
In this study, the ARs of
C. parthenoxylon
were found to originate from callus tissue, with significant changes in the content of solute sugars, proteins, endogenous hormones, and activities of related enzymes observed during the rooting process. This indicates their substantial involvement in this developmental phase. The formation of the root primordium represents a critical juncture, necessitating distinct conditions before and after its development. Through GO analysis and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of 3438 common DEGs identified across three stages of AR formation, we discovered that in addition to auxin, ethylene and plant wound signaling pathways played significant roles in the cutting of
C. parthenoxylon
. Additionally, the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway was implicated in rooting in a unique manner. This research identified fourteen DEGs as candidate genes through PPI network analyses, warranting further functional validation. This study provides a foundation for improving the treatment methods for
C. parthenoxylon
cuttings.
