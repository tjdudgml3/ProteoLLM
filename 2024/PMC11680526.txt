Introduction
Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) ranks among both the most common and most deadly types of lung cancer, manifesting with worldwide incidence and mortality increasing at unacceptably high rates [
1
,
2
]. LUAD creates a considerable risk to both the health and economic well-being of patients and allied healthcare systems, leading to countless loss of life in the hundreds of thousands annually [
3
]. The cost of treatment is often substantial enough to create neurosis and family socioeconomic risk from being affected by the disease. Currently, surgery, radiation, and chemotherapy are conventional methods used to treat cancer, for which LUAD also unequivocally benefits. Unfortunately, these conventional treatments have limited efficacy for advanced LUAD with toxicity complications previously burdensome [
4
,
5
]. The need for novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets to improve outcomes, and provide effective alternatives to conventional treatment strategies is urgently apparent.
The dysregulation of keratin genes has demonstrated aberrant activation in multiple cancer types, and in particular, keratin 18 (KRT18) [
6
,
7
]. KRT18 expression has been shown to correlate with aggressiveness in multiple cancer types, and lead to an adverse outcome after treatment, pointing to potential KRT18 as a tumor associated biomarker in cancer. Furthermore, the association between KRT18 expression and tumorigenesis in LUAD, and subsequently KRT18 may in fact correlate with patient outcomes [
8
,
9
]. Therefore, pursuing the potential role of KRT18 in LUAD may lead to novel insights into available biological characteristics of the disease, and newline to learn more of LUAD pathophysiology and clinical manifestation ability.
The present study utilizes a powerful and multifaceted bioinformatics approach to comprehensively analyze the expression pattern of KRT18 in LUAD, based on publicly accessible databases including The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO). This approach will enable the integration of clinical and pathological characteristics, gene expression, immune infiltration characteristics and profiling and functional enrichment processes in KRT18 expression. By applying bioinformatics and these integrated analyses, we aim to dissect the relationship between KRT18 expression, clinical-pathological features, patient outcomes and tumor immune microenvironments, an approach that could substantially aid the identification of KRT18 as a putative early-diagnosis and targeted therapy biomarker in LUAD.
In addition, the capacity of KRT18 to modulate the immune-response in the tumor microenvironments may provide additional specificity related to therapeutic strategies including immunotherapy. As such, the relationship between KRT18 expression and immune-cell populations such as T-lymphocytes, dendritic cells, among others, is a vital area of investigation that could help further the understanding of the immune complex in LUAD. Prior studies have shown that keratin genes could affect the tumor immune evasive mechanisms that may impact patients and their responses related to immunotherapies [
10
].
In summary, this research endeavors to address the gap in understanding related to KRT18 expression in lung adenocarcinoma by utilizing a comprehensive bioinformatics strategy to explore and assess expression patterns and clinical ramifications. Findings from this study will provide a crucial preliminary step to future studies investigating KRT18 as a biomarker and potential therapeutic target for lung adenocarcinoma patients. Furthermore, the current study can establish a basis for developing personalized treatment strategies for individuals with LUAD that encompass initial diagnosis, prognostication, and an array of comprehensive therapeutic target options [
11
–
13
].
Methods
Data source and processing from TCGA database
RNA-seq data from 33 tumor projects in the TCGA database (
https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov
) were downloaded and organized using the STAR pipeline, extracting data in TPM format, and analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank sum test [
14
]. A total of 598 RNA-seq samples were obtained from TCGA-LUAD, with 59 adjacent normal samples, and clinical data was available for 522 samples, including 598 RNA-seq samples with clinical information, with 23 RNA-seq samples originating from the same patient. Additionally, we downloaded two GEO datasets,
GSE31547
and
GSE40791
, for validation.
HPA database
The Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database (
https://www.proteinatlas.org/
) offers comprehensive data regarding the localization and expression levels of proteins across a range of human normal tissues, tumor tissues, cell lines, and blood cells, which are illustrated through immunohistochemical staining images [
15
,
16
].
CPTAC database
The corrected Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) database CPTAC3_Lung_Adeno_Carcinoma_Proteome.tmt10 dataset contains mass spectrometry proteomics data, which were converted to unitless Z-Score values using (x–μ)/σ [
17
]. The Wilcoxon Rank Sum Tests were employed to assess the statistical variations in expression levels between tumor tissues and normal tissues within the LUAD dataset.
Clinical pathological feature correlation analysis and diagnostic, prognostic analysis
Clinical data were sourced from the TCGA-LUAD database, with the exclusion of normal samples and those lacking clinical information. The data underwent processing through a log2 transformation, specifically log2(value + 1). Correlation statistical analysis was executed employing the Wilcoxon rank sum test, while data visualization was accomplished utilizing the ggplot2 package [
18
]. Furthermore, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was carried out using the pROC package, and Kaplan–Meier (K-M) survival analysis was conducted through Cox regression analysis [
19
,
20
].
Functional enrichment analysis
LUAD data were extracted from the TCGA database, and Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to identify genes associated with KRT18 expression. Enrichment analysis was performed using these correlated genes, including Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses [
21
,
22
]. Additionally, single-gene differential analysis was conducted using MSigDB Collections gene sets, and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) results were visualized using the ggplot2 package [
23
,
24
].
Immune infiltration analysis
RNA-seq data from the TCGA-LUAD project were downloaded and organized from the TCGA database, extracting TPM format data and clinical data, excluding normal samples, with data processed using log2(value + 1). Based on the ssGSEA algorithm provided in the R package GSVA [1.46.0], immune infiltration was calculated using markers for 24 immune cell types provided in the Immunity article, and the estimate [1.0.13] was used to calculate stromal and immune scores [
25
–
27
].
Immune genes, immune subtypes, and molecular typing
The TISIDB platform (
http://cis.hku.hk/TISIDB/download.php
) encompasses a range of immunomodulators and chemokines, which include genes that promote immune responses, genes that suppress immune activity, chemokines, and human leukocyte antigens [
28
]. To assess the differential expression of immune-related molecules between groups with high and low KRT18 expression, Wilcoxon tests were employed. The average expression levels of each gene within both groups were depicted in a heatmap for visualization. The research titled "The Immune Landscape of Cancer" undertook a comprehensive immune genomic analysis involving over 10,000 tumor samples spanning 33 distinct cancer types within the TCGA database [
29
]. In studies that transcended tumor categories, six immune subtypes were delineated: C1 (tissue healing subtype), C2 (IFN-γ dominant subtype), C3 (inflammatory subtype), C4 (lymphocyte exhaustion subtype), C5 (immune silent subtype), and C6 (TGF-β dominant subtype). Lung adenocarcinoma samples were stratified into high and low expression groups based on the median KRT18 expression, and the distribution of each subtype within these groups was calculated, with statistical significance evaluated through the chi-square test. Furthermore, the expression disparities of KRT18 across various molecular subtypes in the TCGA-LUAD cohort (sourced from the UCSC Xena database) were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis Rank Sum Test [
30
].
Gene alteration analysis
The cBioPortal database (accessible at
http://www.cbioportal.org/
) serves as a widely utilized online resource for the analysis and visualization of gene mutations [
31
]. In our study, we performed analyses of gene alterations alongside survival assessments utilizing this platform. To investigate the relationship between copy number variation scores and gene expression levels, we employed scatter plot analysis in conjunction with Spearman rank correlation coefficients. Scatter plots facilitate a visual depiction of the association between two variables, enabling us to discern the presence of either linear or nonlinear relationships. The copy number profiles were determined experimentally through whole-genome microarray techniques at the TCGA Genomic Characterization Center. Following this, the TCGA FIREHOSE pipeline utilized the GISTIC2 method to produce estimates of gene-level copy numbers. The GISTIC2 methodology further classified these estimates into categories of −2, −1, 0, 1, or 2, which correspond to homozygous deletion, single copy deletion, diploid normal copy, low copy number amplification, and high copy number amplification, respectively. We conducted a comparative analysis of gene expression variations across the five distinct copy number categories, employing the Kruskal–Wallis test to assess statistical significance.
Single-cell analysis and potential targeted drug screening
TISCH2 (
http://tisch.comp-genomics.org/
) serves as a specialized database for single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) that emphasizes the tumor immune microenvironment [
32
]. In our study, we performed a comprehensive analysis at the single-cell level across multiple datasets, utilizing this database. To identify potential therapeutic strategies capable of mitigating the tumor-promoting effects associated with specific genes, we executed a Connectivity Map (cMAP) analysis. The CMAP_gene_signatures.RData file encompasses 1,288 features related to compounds. We developed a gene-related signature by selecting the 150 genes that exhibited the most significant upregulation and the 150 genes that displayed the most significant downregulation, based on a comparative analysis of patients categorized by high and low gene expression levels in tumors. Employing the optimal feature matching technique known as XSum (eXtreme Sum), we assessed the gene-related features against the cMAP gene features to derive similarity scores for the 1,288 compounds. Compounds that yielded lower similarity scores are posited to have the potential to inhibit gene-mediated oncogenic processes, and the analytical approach adhered to methodologies established in prior research publications [
33
,
34
].
Experimental validation
In this study, we utilized human lung cancer cell lines A549 and H1299 provided by the Chinese Academy of Sciences Cell Bank to investigate the role of KRT18. We detected KRT18 expression using quantitative PCR (q-PCR) and assessed the effects of KRT18 knockdown on cell proliferation and migration capabilities through CCK-8 and scratch assays. Cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum under conditions of 37 ℃ and 5% CO
2
. KRT18 knockdown was achieved through siRNA transfection, and mRNA levels were confirmed using q-PCR. Cell proliferation was measured using the CCK-8 assay, while cell migration was evaluated with the scratch assay. These methods allowed us to assess the role of KRT18 in lung cancer cell functions and provide a foundation for future research.

Analysis of the correlation between KRT18 expression and clinical pathology, as well as its significance in diagnosis and prognosis
In order to investigate the relationship between KRT18 expression and various clinical pathological characteristics, we evaluated KRT18 expression in the context of T, N, and M staging, as well as pathological staging and initial treatment outcomes. Analysis of data from the TCGA database revealed that KRT18 expression levels were elevated in patients diagnosed with advanced cancer; however, no significant differences were observed concerning M staging. Furthermore, our findings indicated that KRT18 expression was significantly downregulated in comparison to cases of disease progression (PD) during initial treatment outcomes (Fig.
2
A–E). Subsequently, we conducted a more in-depth analysis to determine the implications of KRT18 expression for diagnosis and prognosis. As illustrated in Fig.
2
F, the area under the ROC curve (AUC) was calculated to be 0.846, signifying a substantial diagnostic value. By categorizing patients based on the median expression level of KRT18 into high and low expression groups, we discovered that individuals in the high expression cohort exhibited reduced survival rates across overall survival (OS), disease-specific survival (DSS), and progression-free interval (PFI), thereby indicating a poorer prognosis (Fig.
2
G–I).

Immune infiltration analysis
In addition, we explored the relationship between immune cell populations and KRT18 gene expression levels through correlation analysis. The findings indicated a significant association between different immune cell populations, such as T helper cells, B cells, and macrophages, and the expression levels of KRT18. Notably, dendritic cells (DC), Th1 cells, B cells, and T cells exhibited a negative correlation with KRT18 expression, whereas Th2 cells and NK CD56bright cells demonstrated a positive correlation with KRT18 expression levels (Fig.
4
A). Furthermore, KRT18 expression levels showed significant correlations with Stromal Score, ESTIMATE score, and immune score, indicating that KRT18 may play an important role in the tumor microenvironment (Fig.
4
B). The evaluation of immune cell enrichment in both high and low expression cohorts of KRT18 was conducted utilizing the Mann–Whitney U test, also known as the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. The results showed that under high KRT18 expression conditions, the enrichment scores of B cells, DC cells, and T cells were reduced (Fig.
4
C, D). The findings indicate that the expression levels of KRT18 could potentially affect both the composition and functionality of immune cells within the tumor microenvironment.

Experimental validation of KRT18 in lung cancer cells
In a series of meticulously designed cell experiments, the small interfering RNA targeting KRT18, referred to as siKRT18, was employed to effectively inhibit the expression of KRT18 in two specific lung cancer cell lines, A549 and H1299. This approach aimed to assess the potential impact of KRT18 on various functions associated with lung cancer cells. The results obtained from q-PCR analysis confirmed a significant suppression of KRT18 expression by siKRT18 in these lung cancer cell lines, as illustrated in Fig.
8

8
B, C. Following this, a scratch wound healing assay was performed to evaluate the influence of KRT18 on the migratory behavior of lung cancer cells. The findings from this assay revealed that the downregulation of KRT18 expression markedly diminished the migratory potential of these cells, as shown in Fig.
8

Discussion
LUAD is a common subtype of lung cancer with high incidence and mortality rates, making it a significant public health issue around the world. This disease not only exerts a notable strain on healthcare systems globally, but also burdens patients and their families financially owing to the high costs required for treatment modalities such as surgery, chemotherapy, and radiation therapy. Even with advances in therapeutic modalities, the prognosis for advanced LUAD remains poor, necessitating further development of biomarkers and therapeutic targets to improve both management and outcomes for patients with LUAD [
35
,
36
].
In this research, we examine the expression of KRT18 as a prospective biomarker in LUAD. Previous studies suggest that KRT18 is dysregulated in many different cancers, where elevations in expression levels imply more advanced disease and poorer prognoses [
10
,
37
]. In this study, we demonstrate a substantial correlation between KRT18 expression and various clinical-pathological characteristics in LUAD, along with its associated interactions in the immune microenvironment and prognostic importance. Further discourse will center on these findings in an attempt to propose KRT18 as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target for the management of LUAD [
38
]. Additionally, the mutation status of the KRT18 gene has emerged as a significant factor influencing both the expression of KRT18 and the progression of lung adenocarcinoma. Mutations in KRT18 can lead to alterations in the protein structure, potentially affecting its stability and function [
39
]. This disruption may result in aberrant expression levels of KRT18, which has been implicated in various cellular processes, including apoptosis, cell proliferation, and differentiation [
40
]. The relationship between KRT18 mutations and its expression levels is critical, as it may serve as a biomarker for tumor behavior and patient prognosis.
The examination of KRT18 expression in LUAD has provided valuable information on its potential for use as a biomarker and target for therapy. The upregulation of KRT18 in tumor tissues versus adjacent non-tumor tissues substantiates its significance given the data from both the TCGA and GEO datasets. This increase in KRT18 expression is consistent with previous reports identifying KRT18 as an oncogene in several malignancies and suggest a possible mechanism of tumor progression via the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) process [
41
]. Also, the significant correlations between KRT18 and poor outcome measures solidify its potential to improve diagnostic and prognostic delineation in LUAD. Additionally, while KRT18 demonstrated differential correlations with immune populations in two independently identified cohorts, similarly identified cohorts strengthen the argument for KRT18 as a legitimate biomarker that could facilitate treatment of LUAD patients variable with personalized therapy to improve clinical outcomes [
42
].
The immune contexture surrounding KRT18 expression plays a significant role in dictating the tumor's response to immune challenges. Our findings reveal an inverse relationship between KRT18 levels and various immune cell populations, including dendritic cells (DCs), T helper 1 (Th1) cells, and macrophages. This inverse correlation suggests that elevated KRT18 expression might foster an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, thereby facilitating tumor evasion from immune surveillance [
43
]. Conversely, the positive association observed between KRT18 and Th2 cells, as well as NK CD56 bright cells, warrants further investigation into the dualistic role of KRT18 in modulating the immune response to the tumor. This bidirectional relationship could potentially elucidate the distinctive function of KRT18 in orchestrating immune cell recruitment and shaping the immune landscape, which may have implications for the success of immunotherapeutic strategies. Gaining a deeper understanding of these immune microenvironmental interactions is essential for the development of strategies aimed at manipulating the immune microenvironment to enhance the effectiveness of immunotherapies in LUAD. Moreover, it is crucial for the design of personalized treatment regimens tailored to the specific immunological profiles of LUAD patients [
44
].
Survival analysis allows us to further substantiate the prognostic power of KRT18 in LUAD. The AUC from the ROC analysis attests to its powerful prognostic capability that is crucial for detecting LUAD patients who require early intervention. We have also found that high expression of KRT18 is associated with decreased overall survival, disease specific survival, and progression free interval, bolstering its potential prognostic application in the clinical setting [
45
]. Its relationship to adverse outcomes strengthens the case for its routine evaluation in LUAD patients, and its relation to negative prognosis opens avenues for KRT18-directed therapy to ameliorate these adverse prognostic markers. Further adding KRT18 expression data to other accepted prognostic factors will allow for a more complete model to stratify risk and develop personalized approaches to therapy [
46
].
Functional enrichment analysis of KRT18 expression underscores the biological pathways linked to KRT18, enabling us to demonstrate its connection to pathways related to immune response and lymphocyte differentiation. The positive association with monocyte differentiation indicates that KRT18 may influence myeloid cells and their interactions within the tumor microenvironment [
47
]. The significance of monocytes becomes evident when considering the emerging consensus regarding how tumor-immune cell interactions modify cancer biology and therapeutic responses. Moreover, the negative correlation in pathways related to neurodegenerative diseases suggests that KRT18 has ramifications extending beyond oncogenic activity, venturing into new biological realms [
48
]. The functional pathways identified in our enrichment analysis offer compelling evidence and a foundation for future investigations aimed at clarifying the mechanistic role of KRT18 in LUAD pathogenesis and positioning KRT18 as a potential therapeutic target.
The discovery of the compound NU.1025 through the cMAP analysis as a candidate to reverse molecular changes induced by KRT18 represents an exciting avenue for therapeutic development. The ability of NU.1025 to inhibited KRT18 expression in different lung cancer cell lines suggests it may serve as a suitable therapeutic targeting KRT18-mediated oncogenic effect [
49
]. This discovery is congruent with emerging approaches in targeting specific pathways associated with cancer cells by repurposing existing therapeutic drugs, which may expedite research findings to practice. Moreover, in addition to identifying KRT18 as a therapeutic target, identifying other compounds that can influence KRT18 expression may illuminate new strategies to enhance treatment regimens mentioned in the previous discussion in LUAD. Important opportunities for future research may be focused on delineating the exact mechanisms NU.1025 may mediate its effects and possible synergistic effects with current treatments modalities [
50
].
There are several limitations in this work. First, this work is highly reliant on secondary analyses performed with publicly available databases and the biases introduced by the data in doing analyses. Admittedly, the analysis were performed using a relatively large sample size; however, the issue of external geographic and ethnic diversity in the sample size is a concern in generalizability of our current findings. Moreover, while various bioinformatics approaches were conducted in this work to visualize gene expression and explore the possible biologic role of KRT18, the current work lacks strong in-vivo experimental validation. In addition, the lack of complete experimental data conducted from human specimens along the spectrum of care makes our conclusions inconclusive on the functional role KRT18 in lung adenocarcinoma.
In summary, this research offers crucial insights into KRT18 as a prospective biomarker and therapeutic target for lung adenocarcinoma. The correlation between KRT18 expression and clinical parameters, along with its relationship to immune cell infiltration, underscores its importance in the tumor microenvironment. This study paves the way for forthcoming investigations focused on unraveling the biological mechanisms underlying KRT18’s role in lung adenocarcinoma and its potential implications for future personalized treatment strategies. Ongoing research into KRT18’s functional significance, especially within the clinical context, will facilitate the acquisition of essential information to more precisely delineate the utility of KRT18 in targeting specific patients who may enhance their outcomes and gain from therapeutic interventions for this challenging malignancy.
