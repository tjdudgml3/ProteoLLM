Introduction
Eukaryotic transcription is precisely regulated by intricate interactions between transcription factors and cofactors (
1
,
2
). Recruitment of different combinations of transcription factors and cofactors to enhancer and promoter regions selectively regulates expression of target genes (
3
,
4
). Interactions between transcription cofactors stabilize their association with DNA-binding transcription factors and chromatin and also allow the recruitment of additional transcription cofactors that synergistically affect gene expression. Understanding these intricate networks of transcription cofactor interactions is essential for elucidating the molecular basis of transcriptional regulation, cellular differentiation, development, and disease pathogenesis (
2
). Among their diverse functions, many transcription cofactors have histone-modifying enzymatic activity. Such post-translational histone modifications play pivotal roles in eukaryotic transcriptional regulation by altering chromatin structure and providing binding platforms for diverse transcription factors and cofactors (
5–7
). In addition to histones, accumulating evidence has found that many histone-modifying enzymes also target non-histone proteins, including transcription cofactors, providing additional mechanisms for transcriptional regulation (
8–11
).
In mammalian cells, active-transcription–coupled histone H3 lysine 4 (H3K4) methylation marks are produced by six lysine methyltransferase 2 (KMT2) family proteins: MLL1/KMT2A, MLL2/KMT2B, MLL3/KMT2C, MLL4/KMT2D, SET1A/KMT2F and SET1B/KMT2G. Each of these KMT2 proteins forms a multimeric complex with several WRAD (
W
DR5,
R
bBP5,
A
SH2L and
D
PY30) subunits common to all complexes, as well as subunits unique to each complex (
12
). Among KMT2 family proteins, MLL2 (mixed-lineage leukemia 2) is responsible for H3K4 methylation on bivalent promoters in embryonic stem cells and a specific subset of genes linked to developmental regulation (
13
). Mutation and dysregulation of MLL2 have been implicated in various solid cancers (
14
,
15
), highlighting the importance of this KMT in maintaining cellular function and genomic integrity. MLL2, like its paralog, MLL1, undergoes proteolysis by taspase 1, and the resulting N-terminal (MLL2N) and C-terminal (MLL2C) parts of MLL2 non-covalently associate with each other (
16
). The SET-domain–containing MLL2C associates with WRAD subunits and is minimally sufficient to methylate H3K4 (
17
). MLL2N and its associated subunits, menin and HCF1/2, are thought to play roles in chromatin recruitment and interaction with other proteins. However, their detailed functions remain incompletely characterized (
18
). Although the two paralogs, MLL2 and MLL1, have similar domain organizations and compositions of associating subunits, they differentially associate with transcriptional machinery and target genes (
13
). This suggests that specific interactions of MLL2 and MLL1 with distinct proteins might account for their differential target gene recruitment and transcriptional regulation. In this context, several proteins have been reported to interact with the MLL2 complex and play roles in recruiting the complex to target genes (
19–21
) and enhancing its H3K4 methylation and transcriptional coactivator activities (
22
).
PRMT1, one of nine members of the PRMT (protein arginine methyltransferase) family, is a major type I methyltransferase responsible for more than 90% of overall arginine methylation in mammalian cells (
23
). PRMT1 was first identified as the mediator of histone H4 arginine 3 (H4R3) asymmetric dimethylation, which is associated with active transcription (
24
,
25
). In addition to H4R3 methylation, PRMT1 also methylates a large number of non-histone proteins with various cellular functions, including transcriptional and translational regulation, RNA processing, DNA-damage response, and signal transduction (
26
). PRMT1-mediated arginine methylation is known to regulate protein–RNA and protein–protein interactions that lead to changes in the function and stability of proteins (
27
,
28
). Because of the prominent roles of PRMT1 in many cellular processes, PRMT1 dysregulation has been implicated in numerous diseases, prompting extensive efforts to investigate PRMT1 as a therapeutic target (
26
,
29
,
30
).
Here, using biochemically defined systems and cell-based analyses, we demonstrate that PRMT1 directly interacts with and methylates multiple arginine residues in the N-terminal region of MLL2. We also provide mechanistic insight into the roles of PRMT1-mediated arginine methylation in the stabilization and transcriptional coactivator function of MLL2. Our study reveals a novel mechanism for controlling KMT2 family protein stability through arginine methylation and identifies a cooperative function of MLL2 and PRMT1 in gene expression.
Materials and methods
Plasmids and protein purification
For transfection-mediated expression in mammalian cells, cDNA encoding human MLL2 was subcloned into pCAGIPuro (Addgene) with an N-terminal FLAG tag; cDNAs encoding human PRMT family proteins and ubiquitin were subcloned into pcDNA3 (Invitrogen), with or without an N-terminal HA tag. For purification of His-PRMT1, cDNA was subcloned into pET28 (Novagen), expressed in
Escherichia coli
, and purified on Ni-NTA agarose (Qiagen) as previously described (
31
). For baculovirus-mediated purification of MLL2 fragments, cDNAs were subcloned into pFastBac1 (Gibco-Invitrogen) with an N-terminal FLAG tag, expressed in Sf9 cells, and purified on M2 agarose (Sigma-Aldrich) as previously described (
31
). Baculovirus-mediated reconstitution and purification of the MLL2
Core
complex, consisting of a FLAG-tagged MLL2 fragment (amino acid residues 2412–2715) and untagged WRAD subunits, was previously described (
17
). For purification of the MLL2 complex, FLAG-MLL2 293T cells were washed once with phosphate-buffered saline and then with 5 packed-cell volumes (PCVs) of hypotonic buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl [pH 7.3], 10 mM KCl and 1.5 mM MgCl
2
) after which they were resuspended in 2 PCVs of hypotonic buffer and incubated on ice for 10 min. Swollen cells were disrupted with a Dounce homogenizer (pestle B, 15 strokes) and centrifuged at 5200 × g for 15 min. Pellets were resuspended in extraction buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl [pH 7.9], 300 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl
2
, 1 mM EGTA, 0.5% Triton X-100, 1 mM PMSF, 2 mM DTT and protease inhibitor cocktail [Roche]), minced with a Dounce homogenizer (pestle B, 3 rounds of 15 strokes at 10-min intervals) on ice, and then centrifuged at 37 000 × g for 15 min. The resulting clarified nuclear extracts were incubated with M2 agarose for 4 h. After extensive washing with wash buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl [pH 7.9], 200 mM NaCl, 2 mM MgCl
2
, 0.2 mM EDTA, 15% glycerol, 0.1% NP40, 1 mM PMSF and 1 mM DTT), the complex was eluted with wash buffer containing 0.25 mg/ml FLAG peptide.
Cell lines and RNA interference
FLAG-MLL2 and FLAG-PRMT1 cell lines were generated by transfecting 293T cells with pCAGIPuro plasmids containing FLAG-MLL2 and FLAG-PRMT1 cDNAs, respectively, and selecting stable clones by growing in medium containing 2 μg/ml puromycin (Sigma-Aldrich). For MLL2-knockout (KO) cell lines, two guide RNAs targeting different sites of the
KMT2B
locus (5′-CAG AGC TAC CCG CAC TCT TT-3′ and 5′-TTC GGA GAG ACG GGG CAC GG-3′) were subcloned into pSpCas9(BB)-2A-GFP (Addgene) and pSpCas9-(BB)-RFP (
32
), respectively. Plasmids were co-transfected into 293T cells, and single cells positive for both GFP and RFP were isolated by fluorescence-activated cell sorting. KO was verified by immunoblot analysis and genomic DNA sequencing of polymerase chain reaction (PCR) products generated with primers bracketing guide RNA target sites. For short hairpin RNA (shRNA)-mediated knockdown (KD), PRMT1-targeting DNA fragments (5′-CCG GCA GTA CAA AGA CTA CAA-3′) and non-targeting fragments (5′-CAA CAA GAT GAA GAG CAC CAA-3′) were subcloned into pLKO.1-TRC (Addgene). Plasmids were co-transfected into 293T cells together with pCMV-VSV-G (Addgene) and psPAX2 (Addgene), and viruses were harvested after 72 h. Cells were infected with lentiviral particles and selected with 2 μg/ml puromycin 48 h after transduction.
Immunoprecipitation and liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry analysis for identification of MLL2-interacting proteins
Control and FLAG-MLL2 293T cells were resuspended in hypotonic buffer (10 mM HEPES [pH 7.5], 10 mM KCl, 1.5 mM MgCl
2
and 0.2 mM PMSF). Swollen cells were disrupted with a Dounce homogenizer (pestle B, 15 strokes) and centrifuged at 5000 × g for 15 min. Pellets were resuspended in low-salt buffer (20 mM HEPES [pH 7.5], 20 mM KCl, 1.5 mM MgCl
2
, 0.2 mM EDTA and 0.2 mM PMSF) and minced with a Dounce homogenizer (pestle B, 6 strokes). After slowly adding high-salt buffer (20 mM HEPES [pH 7.5], 1.2 M KCl, 1.5 mM MgCl
2
, 0.2 mM EDTA and 0.2 mM PMSF), samples were centrifuged at 20 000 × g for 30 min. Supernatants were adjusted to 150 mM KCl by adding low-salt buffer. The resulting nuclear extracts were immunoprecipitated with M2 agarose, and beads were washed five times with wash buffer (20 mM HEPES [pH 7.5], 150 mM KCl, 1.5 mM MgCl
2
and 0.2 mM EDTA) and then twice with mass spectrometry (MS) buffer (100 mM Tris–Cl [pH 8.5]). Bound proteins were eluted with MS buffer containing 10 M urea, and then eluted samples were diluted to 8 M urea by adding MS buffer. Proteins were reduced by adding Tris(2-carboxyethyl)-phosphine to a final concentration of 3 mM, and cysteine residues were alkylated by adding 2-chloroacetamide to a final concentration of 11 mM. Proteins were digested with Lys-C-endoprotease (Wako) by incubating at 37°C for 4 h. Samples were adjusted to 2 M urea and further digested with trypsin (Promega) at 37°C for 16 h. Digestion was stopped by adding trifluoroacetic acid to a final concentration of 1%. Digested peptides were desalted on a reversed-phase C
18
StageTips column (
33
). Elutes were dried in a vacuum concentrator and resuspended in 0.1% formic acid. Subsequent liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analyses were performed using an EASY-nLC 1000 coupled to an Orbitrap Eclipse Tribrid mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) equipped with a custom electrospray ionization source. Digested peptides were separated on a 150-mm reversed-phase analytical column (internal diameter, 75 μm) packed with C18 AQ resin (3 μm, 10 nm) (Bonna-Agela Technologies) using a 5–95% acetonitrile gradient at a flow rate of 350 nl/min. Full-scan survey mass spectra were collected in the Orbitrap (400–1600
m/z
) utilizing a standardized automated gain control target of 400 000 ions with a resolution of 120 000. Tandem mass spectra (MS/MS) were acquired using an automated gain control target of 50 000 ions with a resolution of 15 000. MS peaks were generated from MS raw files using MaxQuant (version 1.6.8.0). The Andromeda peptide search engine in MaxQuant was used to match MS peaks against the UniProt human database (June 2020; 20 368 entries). Search parameters were LysC digestion, trypsin digestion, fixed carboxyamidomethyl modifications of cysteine, variable oxidation of methionine, variable acetylation of protein N-termini and variable carbamylation of peptide N-termini. Two missed cleavages were allowed, and peptide mass tolerances were 4.5 and 20 ppm for precursor and fragment ions, respectively. Protein inference and quantitation were performed using MaxQuant with a 1% false discovery rate (FDR) threshold for both peptides and proteins. MaxLFQ (MaxQuant label-free quantitation) intensities were used to infer the abundances of identified proteins (
34
). Two biological replicates were analyzed, and each biological replicate was measured in triplicate by LC-MS. Perseus v1.6.7.0 (
35
) software was used for preparing volcano plots. All identified MLL2-interacting proteins are listed in
Supplementary Table S1
.
Co-immunoprecipitation assays
For co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) of endogenous proteins, cells were lysed in lysis buffer (20 mM Tris–Cl [pH 7.9], 200 mM KCl, 0.2 mM EDTA, 20% glycerol, 0.1% NP-40 and 1 mM PMSF), and cell extracts were incubated with antibody at 4°C overnight. After addition of Protein A
/

For Co-IP involving FLAG-tagged proteins, cells were transfected with combinations of expression plasmids, as indicated. After 48 h, cells were lysed in lysis buffer and cell extracts were incubated with M2 agarose at 4°C for 3 h. Beads were extensively washed with lysis buffer, and bound proteins were monitored by immunoblotting. Intracellular MLL2 ubiquitylation assays followed the same Co-IP experimental procedures as used for FLAG-tagged proteins, and the ubiquitylation status of MLL2 was monitored by immunoblotting with an anti-HA antibody.

Results
Identification of PRMT1 as a novel MLL2-interacting protein
Multiple transcription factors and cofactors act collectively through direct physical interactions to precisely regulate expression of target genes. In this context, we sought to identify novel interacting protein(s) of the human MLL2 complex. To this end, we immunoprecipitated nuclear extracts of 293T cells expressing FLAG-MLL2 from a chromosome-integrated gene (FLAG-MLL2 293T cell line) using an anti-FLAG antibody, and identified interacting proteins by LC-MS analysis (
Supplementary Table S1
). In addition to identifying WRAD subunits (WDR5, RbBP5, ASH2L and DPY30) common to all KMT2 complexes and MLL2-specific subunits (menin, HCF1 and HCF2) (
13
,
18
), which validated our screening approach, our immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry (IP-MS) analysis also identified PRMT1, a protein arginine methyltransferase, as a potential candidate MLL2-interacting protein (Figure
1A
,
Supplementary Figure S1A
). In a confirmation of intracellular interactions, we found that anti-FLAG antibody co-immunoprecipitated endogenous PRMT1 from the FLAG-MLL2 293T cell line (Figure
1B
, left). A reciprocal approach using a FLAG-PRMT1 293T cell line verified the interaction between PRMT1 and the MLL2 complex (Figure
1B
, right). In a further analysis, Co-IP assays using an anti-MLL2 antibody also confirmed physical interaction between endogenous MLL2 and PRMT1 in cells (
Supplementary Figure S1B
).

MLL2 and MLL1 have a similar domain composition and organization. Also, all KMT2 family proteins contain a highly conserved C-terminal SET domain, around which common WRAD subunits are associated (
13
,
18
). Therefore, we questioned whether the binding of PRMT1 is specific to MLL2 or is general to KMT2 family proteins. Co-IP experiments with ectopically expressed HA-PRMT1 and the FLAG-KMT2 proteins, MLL1, MLL2, SET1A and SET1B (from full-length expression plasmids available in our laboratory), revealed that the interaction of PRMT1 with MLL2 is selective (Figure
1C
). This result suggests that PRMT1 binds to a region unique to MLL2 rather than to common features of KMT2 complexes, such as the SET domain or WRAD subunits (see below). Nine PRMT family proteins, each with a distinct enzymatic profile, target proteins and cellular localization, are expressed in mammalian cells (
29
,
41
). In a test of interactions between MLL2 and PRMT family proteins, MLL2 was found to bind to PRMT1, as expected, but interestingly was also found to interact with PRMT5 and PRMT6 (Figure
1D
). Despite MLL2 interactions with multiple PRMT family proteins, we focused on PRMT1 in this study because it was one of the topmost proteins in the interactome identified by our LC-MS analysis and is the major arginine methyltransferase in mammalian cells (see ‘Discussion’ section).
The MLL2 complex directly interacts with PRMT1 through the N-terminal 1–300 amino acid region of MLL2
An
in vitro

Supplementary Figure S2A
) and PRMT1 (
Supplementary Figure S2B
) revealed direct protein interactions between the MLL2 complex and PRMT1 (Figure

). To determine which regions of MLL2 are responsible for interacting with PRMT1, we generated plasmids expressing FLAG-MLL2 fragments (Figure

PRMT1 methylates multiple arginine residues in the N-terminal 1–300 amino acid region of MLL2
Having established a direct interaction between MLL2 and PRMT1, and given the known protein methyltransferase activities of the MLL2 complex and PRMT1, we investigated the possibility that the MLL2 complex and PRMT1 can methylate each other. To test this, we performed
in vitro
methylation assays employing purified MLL2 complex and PRMT1 (
Supplementary Figure S2
) in the presence of
3
H-labeled SAM, and monitored protein methylation by fluorography. These assays showed multiple methylation products in the presence of PRMT1, revealing a band pattern consistent with intact and degraded forms of FLAG-MLL2 in the purified MLL2 complex (
Supplementary Figure S2A
), suggesting PRMT1-mediated methylation of MLL2 (Figure
3A
). However, no methylation signal was observed around the position corresponding to purified PRMT1, suggesting that PRMT1 is not methylated by the MLL2 complex. The inability of the MLL2 complex to methylate PRMT1 is supported by the above observation that PRMT1 binds to the N-terminal region of MLL2 (Figure
2
), which is distal from the catalytic SET domain located at the C-terminus of MLL2. To test whether PRMT1 methylates MLL2 in cells, we co-transfected MLL2-KO 293T cells (
Supplementary Figure S3A
) with FLAG-MLL2 and HA-PRMT1 expression plasmids, immunoprecipitated cell lysates with an anti-FLAG antibody and monitored methylation of MLL2 by immunoblotting with an antibody that recognizes asymmetric dimethylarginine (ADMA). This analysis showed that MLL2 was methylated by PRMT1, as evidenced by an increased ADMA signal at the N-terminal part of MLL2 (MLL2N; resulting from taspase 1 cleavage) (
43
) when PRMT1 is co-expressed (Figure
3B
, anti-ADMA immunoblot, lane 11 versus lane 10). PRMT1-mediated methylation of MLL2 is further supported by the observation that catalytically dead mutant PRMT1
G98R
(
44
) did not increase methylation of MLL2N, although it retained the ability to bind MLL2 (lane 12). The absence of protein methylation activity of PRMT1
G98R
was confirmed with an
in vitro
histone methylation assay (
Supplementary Figure S3B
) using purified PRMT1
G98R
(
Supplementary Figure S2B
).

) and found that PRMT1 can specifically methylate the 1–300 amino acid region of MLL2 (hereafter, MLL2 1–300) (Figure
3C
). Consistent with this, testing for intracellular methylation by expressing FLAG-MLL2 fragments and HA-PRMT1 in 293T cells showed that an anti-ADMA antibody exclusively recognized MLL2 1–300 among anti-FLAG–immunoprecipitated MLL2 fragments (Figure
3D
). A more detailed analysis of PRMT1-mediated methylation of MLL2 1–300 in cells showed that methylation was detectable even in the absence of PRMT1 overexpression, but became more prominent when PRMT1 was co-expressed (
Supplementary Figure S3C
). We also found that methylation of MLL2 1–300 disappeared upon knockdown (KD) of endogenous PRMT1 using short hairpin RNA targeting PRMT1 (shPRMT1) (
Supplementary Figure S3D
). Collectively, these results demonstrate that PRMT1 methylates the N-terminal 1–300 amino acid region of MLL2, where it directly binds.
Next, we sought to determine which arginine residue(s) in MLL2 are methylated by PRMT1. It has been reported that PRMT1 preferentially methylates arginine residues within repeats of RGG and RG sequences (RGG and RG motifs, respectively) often found in the intrinsically disordered region, but also methylates certain arginine residues that deviate from this consensus (
45
,
46
). The MLL2 1–300 region is highly enriched with arginine residues, containing a total of 53 arginines - six of which (residues 33, 49, 281, 287, 290 and 297) are found in RGG motifs (Figure
3E
). We initially purified mutant MLL2 1–300 proteins in which these six arginines were individually replaced with alanine and then tested them in
in vitro
methylation assays. However, none of these mutant proteins exhibited a noticeable reduction in methylation level compared with that of wild-type (WT) MLL2 1–300 (data not shown). Therefore, we prepared a mutant protein in which all six arginines in RGG motifs were replaced with alanines (
Supplementary Figure S3E
) and tested the resulting mutant in an
in vitro
methylation assay. Mutation of these six residues (MLL2 1–300
AGG
) substantially reduced PRMT1-mediated methylation relative to WT MLL2 (Figure
3F
, lane 7 versus lane 6) but did not completely eliminate it. Accordingly, we prepared another mutant protein (MLL2 1–300
AGG/AG
) in which additional alanine substitutions were introduced at arginines in seven RG motifs, including those clustered in the 90–100 amino acid region (residues 91, 93, 95, 97 and 99) and those surrounded by C-terminal RGG motifs (residues 293 and 295) (
Supplementary Figure S3E
). We found that PRMT1-mediated methylation was further reduced with the MLL2 1–300
AGG/AG
mutant, both in a cell-free system (Figure
3F
, lane 8 versus lanes 6 and 7) and in cells (
Supplementary Figure S3F
). The decreased methylation of MLL2-1–300
AGG
and MLL2 1–300
AGG/AG
was not attributable to defects in their interaction with PRMT1, since both retained the ability to bind to PRMT1 (
Supplementary Figure S3G
).
The significant reduction, but incomplete abrogation, of MLL2 1–300
AGG/AG
methylation raises the possibility that additional arginine residues are methylated by PRMT1 or that compensatory methylation might occur in cases where PRMT1-preferred methylation sites are mutated (
47
). In this context, we found that the N-terminal 1–300 amino acid region of MLL2 is highly intrinsically disordered (
Supplementary Figure S3H
), potentially allowing a large number of arginine residues to become accessible to, and methylated by, the catalytic site of PRMT1. Notwithstanding these possibilities, our findings collectively suggest that PRMT1 methylates multiple arginine residues in the N-terminal 1–300 amino acid region of MLL2. Thus, despite being unable to identify all arginine residues methylated by PRMT1, the significantly reduced methylation in MLL2 1–300
AGG/AG
allowed us to employ this protein as a methylation-defective mutant in subsequent analyses.
PRMT1-mediated arginine methylation stabilizes MLL2
PRMT1-mediated protein methylation has been implicated in various cellular functions (
26
,
27
,
41
,
48
). Among these, we investigated whether PRMT1 regulates the stability of MLL2, as PRMT1 depletion was consistently associated with decreased expression of MLL2 1–300 (
Supplementary Figure S3D
). To test this, we first established a FLAG-MLL2 1–300/MLL2-KO 293T cell line by integrating a FLAG-MLL2 1–300 expression plasmid into the chromosome of the MLL2-KO 293T cell line, thereby avoiding potential interfering effects of endogenous MLL2 on the stability of ectopically introduced FLAG-MLL2 1–300. In a test of protein stability, a cycloheximide (CHX) chase assay demonstrated a gradual decrease in FLAG-MLL2 1–300 protein levels over time upon depletion of endogenous PRMT1 (Figure
4A
). A similar analysis using a cell line expressing FLAG-tagged full-length MLL2 in an MLL2-KO background (FLAG-MLL2/MLL2-KO 293T cell line) revealed reduced levels of MLL2N in the absence of PRMT1 (Figure
4B
). Notably, the levels of MLL2C (C-terminal part of MLL2) were also found to be decreased to a lesser extent compared to MLL2N. To separately assess PRMT1-dependent control of MLL2N and MLL2C stability, we performed CHX chase assays using cell lines individually expressing FLAG-MLL2N (encoding MLL2 amino acids 1–2062) or FLAG-MLL2C (encoding MLL2 amino acids 2063–2715) (
43
). Because these cell lines were established in an MLL2-KO background, MLL2N and MLL2C are present alone without their associating cellular counterparts. These experiments showed that CHX treatment decreased MLL2N levels in the absence of PRMT1 (
Supplementary Figure S4A
) but did not alter MLL2C levels (
Supplementary Figure S4B
). These findings suggest that PRMT1 primarily affects the stability of MLL2N, and that the slight reduction in MLL2C levels induced by CHX treatment observed in Figure
4B
is likely attributable to its association with destabilized MLL2N.

Next, to examine whether PRMT1-mediated arginine methylation affects the stability of MLL2, we ectopically expressed methylation-defective MLL2 1–300
AGG
or MLL2 1–300
AGG/AG
in MLL2-KO 293T cells and performed CHX chase assays. Notably, the initial protein levels of these mutants were lower than those of WT protein (Figure
4C
, lanes 4 and 7 versus lane 1), reflecting their intrinsic instabilities. Upon CHX treatment, mutant proteins levels were reduced more rapidly compared with WT protein (Figure
4C
). Furthermore, a similar assay showed a slight, but noticeable, reduction in full-length MLL2
AGG
and MLL2
AGG/AG
mutant proteins compared with WT upon CHX treatment (Figure
4D
). The lesser reduction in full-length MLL2 compared with MLL2 1–300 suggests that additional mechanisms may contribute to stabilizing full-length MLL2.
For several proteins, it has been reported that methylation of arginine residues protects against ubiquitin-dependent degradation (
49
). Accordingly, we examined whether PRMT1-mediated methylation regulates the ubiquitylation of MLL2. To this end, we co-transfected 293T cells with FLAG-MLL2 1–300, PRMT1 and HA-ubiquitin expression plasmids, immunoprecipitated cell lysates with an anti-FLAG antibody, and monitored ubiquitylation of MLL2 by immunoblotting with an anti-HA antibody. The results showed that MLL2 1–300 was poly-ubiquitylated in cells (Figure
4E
, lane 11) and that its ubiquitylation level was significantly decreased by co-expression of PRMT1 (lane 12 versus lane 11). This suggests that PRMT1-mediated methylation suppresses ubiquitylation of MLL2. In a similar assay using methylation-defective mutants, we found that MLL2 1–300
AGG
and MLL2 1–300
AGG/AG
were significantly more ubiquitylated compared with WT (
Supplementary Figure S4C
). We further found that treatment with MG132, a potent proteasome inhibitor, restored the decreased MLL2 1–300 protein levels observed in the absence of PRMT1 (Figure
4F
). Collectively, these findings indicate that PRMT1-mediated arginine methylation stabilizes MLL2 by protecting it from ubiquitin-dependent degradation.
MLL2 and PRMT1 cooperatively stimulate expression of a chromosomal reporter gene
Given the well-known transcriptional coactivator functions of MLL2 and PRMT1 (
13
,
18
,
26
,
41
) and their direct physical interaction, demonstrated above, we tested whether MLL2 and PRMT1 act together to regulate gene expression. To this end, we generated a Gal4-Luc/MLL2-KO 293T cell line by knocking out chromosomal MLL2 genes in Gal4-Luc 293T cells, a cell line containing a chromosomally integrated luciferase reporter gene with upstream Gal4-binding sites that was utilized previously for demonstrating the coactivator function of MLL2 (
22
). Deleting chromosomal MLL2 genes prevents endogenous MLL2 protein from influencing transcriptional regulation. In a luciferase assay using an established cell line, PRMT1 and MLL2 alone significantly increased transcription by 6.4- and 15.2-fold, respectively (Figure
5A
, lanes 3 and 4 versus lane 2) in an activator (Gal4-VP16)-dependent manner (
Supplementary Figure S5A
). Notably, co-expression of PRMT1 and MLL2 further enhanced transcription (32.2-fold), producing a larger than additive increase in transcription (lane 7 versus lanes 3 and 4), indicating their cooperative function in transcription. This cooperative effect does not appear to be attributable to mutual stimulation of protein methyltransferase activity toward histones because purified PRMT1 and MLL2 complex did not affect each other's histone methyltransferase activity
in vitro
(
Supplementary Figure S5B
). Consistent with this, we found that increased levels of H3K4 and H4R3 methylation around the transcription start site of the chromosomally integrated luciferase reporter gene, resulting from the expression of MLL2 and PRMT1, respectively, were not significantly affected by co-expression of PRMT1 and MLL2, respectively (
Supplementary Figure S5C
). To define the region within MLL2 responsible for its coactivator function, we tested the effects of MLL2N (MLL2 1–2062) and MLL2C (MLL2 2063–2715) fragments on transcription. We found that MLL2N enhanced transcription to a level equivalent to full-length MLL2 (Figure
5A
, lanes 5 and 4), but MLL2C did not (lane 6). Importantly, these results indicate that the transcriptional coactivator function of MLL2 is mainly attributable to MLL2N and not to MLL2C, which contains a catalytic SET domain and is minimally sufficient to methylate histone H3K4 together with associating WRAD subunits (
17
). In addition, we found that MLL2N further enhanced transcription when PRMT1 was co-expressed (lane 8 versus lane 5). In a related observation, we found that MLL2 1–300 (the minimal PRMT1-binding region), whether expressed alone or together with PRMT1, did not increase transcription (data not shown), suggesting that the region responsible for interaction with PRMT1 (MLL2 1–300) is not sufficient for cooperative function with PRMT1, and that additional regions within MLL2 301–2062 are necessary (see ‘Discussion’ section).

MLL2 and PRMT1 co-regulate genes related to cell membrane and extracellular matrix functions
Given the physical interaction and cooperative transcriptional activity of MLL2 and PRMT1, we performed RNA-seq analyses using MLL2-KO and PRMT1-KD (shPRMT1-treated) 293T cells to identify target genes co-regulated by MLL2 and PRMT1. This analysis showed that 440 genes in the MLL2-KO group and 1284 genes in the PRMT1-KD group were downregulated more than 2-fold compared with control cells; of these,192 genes were common to both groups (Figure
6A
). A gene ontology (GO) analysis revealed that these shared genes are primarily involved in biological processes related to cell membrane and extracellular matrix (ECM) functions, such as ECM organization, collagen fibril organization and transmembrane transport (Figure
6B
). Snapshot images of RNA-seq analyses showed decreased transcription of
ITGA4
(encoding a fibronectin receptor),
COL2A1
(alpha-1 chain of type II collagen),
SLC7A3
(amino acid membrane transporter),
SLC2A3
(glucose transmembrane transporter) and
ALKAL1
(receptor tyrosine kinase ligand) – representative top-ranked co-downregulated genes – in MLL2-KO and PRMT1-KD cells (
Supplementary Figure S6
). A quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analysis confirmed these results, demonstrating that expression of these genes, which was reduced in MLL2-KO and PRMT1-KD cells, was further reduced in double KO/KD (MLL2-KO/PRMT1-KD) cells (Figure
6C
), suggesting collaborative functions of MLL2 and PRMT1 in regulating the expression of these genes. Finally, given previous reports of the involvement of the above representative genes in cell movement (
50–54

). Collectively, our results demonstrate that MLL2 and PRMT1 co-regulate target genes related to cell membrane and ECM functions and that their depletion impairs cell migration and invasion.

Discussion
Post-translational modifications of histones play critical roles in many cellular processes, including transcription (
5–7
). Numerous studies have reported that histone-modifying enzymes also modify non-histone proteins, including transcription factors and cofactors (
9
). This enables a dynamic and nuanced response to cellular signals, allowing for the precise control of gene expression necessary for proper cellular function, development and adaptation to environmental changes. Our IP-MS analysis, performed as part of our attempt to better understand transcriptional regulation by the H3K4 methyltransferase, MLL2, identified PRMT1, a versatile protein arginine methyltransferase, as a novel MLL2-interacting protein. PRMT1 was found to directly bind and methylate MLL2 on multiple N-terminal arginine residues in RGG/RG motifs, which are the preferred methylation sites of PRMT1 often found in intrinsically disordered regions. Our study further demonstrated that PRMT1-mediated arginine methylation stabilizes MLL2 and potentiates its coactivator activity as well as its cooperative function with PRMT1 in regulating target gene expression. Based on the previously established functions of protein arginine methylation (
27
,
28
), we also propose that PRMT1-mediated arginine methylation contributes to MLL2-dependent transcription by regulating interactions of MLL2 with proteins and non-coding RNAs that additionally participate in transcriptional control (Figure
7
).
Figure 7.
Schematic model of the cooperative function of MLL2 and PRMT1 in gene expression. (
A
) PRMT1 directly binds to the intrinsically disordered N-terminal region of MLL2 (depicted by a curved line) and methylates multiple arginine residues within RGG/RG motifs. This arginine methylation may allow for the binding of additional (i) proteins that prevent ubiquitylation and proteasome-dependent degradation of MLL2N, resulting in stabilization of the MLL2 complex. Although not detailed in this study, methylated arginine may also recruit several (ii) non-coding RNAs and stabilized MLL2N and MLL2N-bound PRMT1 may recruit additional (iii) transcriptional cofactors. All these factors could cooperatively stimulate transcription. (
B
) In the absence of PRMT1 or presence of mutations in arginine residues in the N-terminal region of MLL2 (not depicted), MLL2N is poly-ubiquitylated and undergoes proteasome-dependent degradation. This can also lead to partial destabilization of MLL2C (as indicated by a dotted line). Destabilized MLL2 and defective recruitment of transcription cofactors and non-coding RNAs impair transcription.
Regarding the regulation of MLL2 interactions with proteins through PRMT1-mediated methylation, we found that methylation-defective full-length MLL2
AGG/AG
(
Supplementary Figure S7A
) and MLL2 1–300
AGG/AG
(
Supplementary Figure S7B
) retained their ability to interact with complex subunits, including menin which binds to the N-terminus of MLL2. Consistent with this, the depletion of endogenous PRMT1 did not affect the interaction of MLL2 with menin (
Supplementary Figure S7C
). These results suggest that PRMT1-mediated methylation does not affect the interactions of MLL2 with its complex subunits.
Protein stability control is crucial to protein function, and protein degradation pathways are actively studied as potential therapeutic targets (
55–57
). Among KMT2 family proteins, MLL1 and MLL4 are reported to exhibit regulation of stability and function by proteasome-dependent degradation pathways. MLL1 is phosphorylated by ATR kinase in response to genotoxic stress during S phase, disrupting its interaction and degradation by the SCF
Skp2
complex, an E3 ubiquitin ligase (
58
). Stabilized MLL1 methylates H3K4 at late replication origin and delays DNA replication, suggesting that MLL1 protein stability regulates cell-cycle progression. In addition, binding of phosphorylated MLL4 to the SCF
FBXW7
E3 ubiquitin ligase was shown to trigger degradation of MLL4, which promoted proliferation of mature B-cell neoplasms (
59
). In addition to phosphorylation as a mechanism for controlling protein stability, as demonstrated for MLL1 and MLL4, our study adds a new example of protein stability control of KMT2 family members, demonstrating regulation of MLL2 stability through arginine methylation (Figure
4
). In contrast to phosphorylation, which can promote or inhibit protein degradation in a context-dependent manner, arginine methylation predominantly increases protein stability (
49
). Mechanistically, arginine methylation itself (
60
,
61
), arginine methylation-dependent phosphorylation (
62–64
), and methylated-arginine-bound proteins and non-coding RNAs can inhibit poly-ubiquitylation by E3 ubiquitin ligases and subsequent proteasome-mediated degradation. Therefore, it would be interesting to determine whether an E3 ubiquitin ligase degrades MLL2 in an arginine-methylation-dependent manner. Notable in this context, our IP-MS analysis found several E3 ubiquitin ligases that are potential candidates responsible for the degradation of MLL2 (
Supplementary Table S1
).
Our reporter gene expression analysis demonstrated that PRMT1-mediated arginine methylation of MLL2 in its N-terminal 1–300 amino acid region enhances MLL2 transcriptional coactivator activity and cooperative activity with PRMT1 (Figure
5
). Arginine methylation can affect the transcriptional activity of MLL2 by increasing its stability, as shown in this study, and possibly by recruiting additional transcription cofactors and/or non-coding RNAs (Figure
7
), an intriguing idea that warrants further study. It is worth noting that MLL2N, encoding amino acids 1–2062, exhibited transcription activity equivalent to that of full-length MLL2, whereas MLL2 1–300 alone did not promote transcription (data not shown). This implies that the MLL2 region encompassing amino acids 301–2062 is primarily responsible for the transcriptional coactivator function observed in our analysis. The fact that MLL2 301–2062 lacks the domain responsible for enzymatic activity but contains several domains responsible for interaction with DNA and protein suggests that this region plays a role in transcriptional coactivator function by tethering MLL2 to chromatin and recruiting additional transcription cofactors to MLL2. In contrast to the causal role of MLL2-dependent H3K4 methylation in transcription, for which there is direct evidence (
22
), support for the transcriptional coactivator activity of MLL2C (encoding 2063–2715 amino acids) based on our reporter analysis is limited. Although MLL2C and associated WRAD subunits are minimally sufficient to methylate H3K4
in vitro
(
17
), the lack of the N-terminal part responsible for chromatin recruitment and interaction with other transcription cofactors necessary for proper function of MLL2 could account for the limited coactivator activity of MLL2C. In relation to this, our reporter assay found that catalytically dead mutant MLL2
Y2604A
exhibited a transcriptional coactivator activity almost equivalent to that of WT MLL2 (
Supplementary Figure S8A
), further supporting that the transcriptional coactivator function of MLL2 is mainly attributable to MLL2N and not to MLL2C. The absence of H3K4 methylation activity of MLL2
Y2604A
(
18
) was confirmed with an
in vitro
histone methylation assay using purified MLL2
Core
complex (
Supplementary Figure S8B
and
C
) containing minimal components required for H3K4 methylation activity (
17
).
The cooperative function of MLL2 and PRMT1 raises the question of whether these two proteins co-localize on the promoter or enhancer regions of target genes. We attempted to answer this by performing chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) analysis but failed to detect PRMT1-enriched genomic locations (data not shown). We think this failure is likely attributable to the unsuitability of commercially available anti-PRMT1 antibodies for detecting endogenous PRMT1 in ChIP analyses, as other researchers have also reported (
65
).
Interestingly, our intracellular protein interaction analysis found that, in addition to PRMT1, PRMT5 and PRMT6 interact with MLL2 (Figure
1D
). Consistent with this, our IP-MS analysis also identified PRMT5 (but not PRMT6) as an MLL2-interacting protein (
Supplementary Table S1
). These observations raise the possibility that other PRMT family members might also be able to methylate MLL2 and participate in MLL2-related functions. Because all three of these PRMT family proteins are known to preferentially methylate arginines within RGG/RG motifs (
28
), it is possible that they may regulate the functions of MLL2 in a similar manner. However, unlike the type I methyltransferases, PRMT1 and PRMT6, which have very similar domain structures and protein size, PRMT5 is a type II methyltransferase that generates symmetric dimethylarginine and contains a unique triose-phosphate isomerase-barrel domain at its extended N-terminus (
29
). This raises the possibility that PRMT5 may interact with and regulate MLL2 functions differently from PRMT1. Future detailed analyses of the impacts of individual PRMT family proteins on MLL2 functions are warranted. In relation to the methylation of KMT2 family complexes by PRMT family proteins, a previous study reported that PRMT1 and PRMT5 can methylate the arginine 296 residue within ASH2L (
66
). However, the specific function of this ASH2L methylation has not been demonstrated. Since ASH2L is a common subunit of all KMT2 family complexes, the effects of PRMT1 and PRMT5-mediated ASH2L methylation would not be specific to the MLL2 complex.
Mutation and dysregulation of MLL2 and PRMT1 have been implicated in various cancers (
15
,
29
,
30
). Our study found that MLL2 and PRMT1 co-regulate targets involved in cell migration and invasion, which are elevated in cancer cells (Figure
6
). In addition to providing mechanistic insight into MLL2- and PRMT1-regulated gene expression, our study raises the possibility of targeting MLL2 and PRMT1 interactions as a potential therapeutic strategy.
