Introduction
Powdery mildew is a common disease of turfgrass, which can infect a variety of grasses, such as
Cynodon dactylon
,
Poa pratensis
,
Festuca filiformis
,
Agrostis stolonifera
and
Dactylis glomerata
. Among these grasses, the incidence of infection in
P. pratensis
,
F. filiformis
and
C. dactylon
is the most serious [
1
].
Blumeria graminis
is the pathogen that causes powdery mildew in grass and can infect the whole aboveground organs of grass, with leaves and leaf sheaths being the most affected. When plants are infected with powdery mildew, a spiderweb-like, white powdery mold layer will appear on the site of the disease, which starts as a dot, then converges into a sheet, and finally covers all the leaves; this layer of mold seriously affects the photosynthesis of the plant, increases the respiratory intensity and the transpiration rate of the plant, and ultimately leads to the death of the plant by drying [
2
].
P. pratensis
is one of the main cool-season turfgrasses used in urban lawn establishment, and powdery mildew is a common and major disease of
P. pratensis
.
P. pratensis
powdery mildew usually occurs in shaded areas of lawns and in seed fields used for seed production [
3
–
5
]. The occurrence of
P. pratensis
powdery mildew is more favorable under conditions of poor air circulation, high relative humidity, low light intensity, and temperatures ranging from 16 ~ 22 ℃ [
6
]. Currently, fungicide spraying, rational fertilization and moderate pruning are the main methods used to control
P. pratensis
powdery mildew.
However, similar to spraying fungicides on other plants, spraying fungicides for a long time will increase the resistance of powdery mildew to fungicides in
P. pratensis
, and some systemic fungicides will also cause morphological changes in
P. pratensis
[
7
].
The selection of
P. pratensis
varieties resistant to powdery mildew has been slow due to the effects of fusionless reproduction and the phenomenon of ploidy level variation, and there are currently fewer
P. pratensis
varieties with higher levels of resistance to powdery mildew, with most varieties originating from ecotypes. Ecotype breeding is a common method used by
P. pratensis
breeders. Czembor et al. [
8
] screened 444 ecotypes of
P. pratensis
collected from nine habitats in Poland, including meadows, wet meadows, peat meadows, pastures, agricultural fields, forests, waterfronts, ditches, and roadsides, for powdery mildew resistance and found that the average powdery mildew resistance score for these ecotypes was 4.3, that 20 were more resistant, and that the habitat with the highest percentage of highly resistant
P. pratensis
was wetlands. These ecotypes of
P. pratensis
can be used to improve disease resistance in commercial
P. pratensis
. Tissue culture can lead to the production of somatic mutants and is a rapid method of increasing plant resistance, and can circumvent some of the problems that arise during conventional breeding of
P. pratensis
. Msikita et al. [
5
] evaluated seven
P. pratensis
varieties propagated using tissue culture techniques for powdery mildew resistance and found that regenerated plants obtained from tissue culture differed from those obtained from seed propagation in terms of resistance to powdery mildew. Further, 10 resistant tissue culture regenerated plants were selected for nutrient propagation, and the results showed that six of the regenerated plants were sustainably resistant. Recently, Sun et al. [
9
] found in their research that with the increase in the amount of nitrogen fertilizer applied, the incidence degree of powdery mildew in the nitrogen-sensitive
P. pratensis
variety “Balin” was more severe than that in the nitrogen-tolerant
P. pratensis
variety “Bluemoon”. Moreover, the
PpGS1.1
gene encoding glutamine synthetase is a potential central gene for powdery mildew resistance, and the nitrogen metabolism pathway participates in the plant-pathogen interaction process through the regulation of the
PpGS1.1
gene. Zhang et al. [
10
] found through RNA-seq technology that the expressions of differential genes such as
HXK
,
INV
,
GS
,
AGpase
and
β-amylase
in the “starch and sucrose metabolism” pathway of
P. pratensis
were closely related to powdery mildew resistance. Overall, current research on powdery mildew of
P. pratensis
mainly focuses on the identification of pathogens, the screening of resistant varieties, and the prevention and control of the disease. However, there are relatively fewer studies on the mechanism of
P. pratensis
’s resistance to powdery mildew and on the infection process of the powdery mildew fungus in
P. pratensis
. The relevant theories remain to be further explored and improved.
Proteomics is the study of all the characteristic information of proteins expressed by a certain organism at a specific time and space, which includes the expression level of proteins, posttranslational modifications, and the interaction patterns between proteins and other molecules, etc., revealing certain biological processes or disease resistance-related molecular mechanisms at the protein level [
11
,
12
]. Metabolomics is another emerging discipline after genomics, transcriptomics and proteomics and can be categorized into targeted metabolomics and nontargeted metabolomics according to different research purposes. Currently, metabolomics technology has gradually become an important tool in plant pathology, especially in plant‒pathogen fungal interactions. Through metabolomics technology, a series of metabolites related to disease resistance can be identified by analyzing the changes and differences in metabolite profiles of plants after invasion by pathogenic bacteria. These metabolites can not only aid in the study of plant disease resistance mechanisms and the identification of plant resistance but can also be used to effectively identify functional genes involved in plant metabolic processes through the combination of various omics and genetic approaches, allowing for the screening of resistant varieties and the breeding of disease-resistant plants [
13
,
14
]. Therefore, proteomics and metabolomics technologies provide a basis for selecting excellent disease-resistant breeding parents and also offer technical support for cultivating new lines of disease-resistant transgenic plants, which is of great significance to breeding work.
In this study, we first analyzed the disease incidence of different disease-resistant
P. pratensis
varieties and then discussed the changes in their in vivo redox states. iTRAQ proteomics and nontargeted metabolomics were utilized to determine the mechanism underlying the response of
P. pratensis
to powdery mildew infestation, and the proteomics and metabolomics results were subsequently validated via qRT-PCR. This study enhances our understanding of the mechanism of powdery mildew resistance in
P. pratensis
and provides data for the selection of
P. pratensis
powdery mildew-resistant varieties as well as the mining and utilization of related disease resistance genes.
Materials and methods
Plant growth and pathogen inoculation
In the previous research work, we took the disease index (DI) as the grading standard (DI = 0, immune; 0 < DI ≤ 5.0, highly resistant; 5.0 < DI ≤ 15.0, moderately resistant; 15.0 < DI ≤ 25.0, moderately susceptible; 25.0 < DI ≤ 50.0, highly susceptible; DI > 50.0, extremely susceptible) and screened out the highly resistant
P. pratensis
variety ‘BlackJack’ and the extremely susceptible variety ‘EverGlade’, using them as the experimental materials [
15
]. Seeds of these two
P. pratensis
varieties were purchased from the Beijing Clover Grass Technology Development Centre (Beijing, China), and their specimens were kept in the herbarium of this center. Seeds were sterilized with 20% NaClO and sown in seedling pots with a diameter of 10 cm and a height of 12 cm, in which the substrate was autoclaved nutrient soil (nutrient soil: vermiculite = 2:1). After sowing, the seedling pots were placed in an artificial incubator with a day and night temperature of 25 ± 1 ℃, a light duration of 16 h during the day/8 h at night, a light intensity of 5000 lx, and a relative humidity of 50 ~ 70%. When the
P. pratensis
seedlings grew to approximately 10 cm, they were thinned to 50 plants per bowl for the next experiment.
P. pratensis
infested with powdery mildew were identified on the lawn. Then, a brush was used to gently brush the powdery mildew fungus from the
P. pratensis
blades into sterile water to make the mother liquor. The mother liquor was diluted to a spore suspension of 1 × 10
6
spores·mL
− 1
using the hemocyte counting plate counting method, and 0.1% Tween-20 was added for the inoculation test. At the time of inoculation, the configured spore suspension was uniformly sprayed on
P. pratensis
leaves, and the spraying was stopped after the suspension was sprayed onto all of the leaves and the suspension naturally dripped down. Approximately 50 mL of suspension was sprayed per seedling pot, and the relative humidity of the culture environment was maintained at approximately 60% during the onset of the disease. Three independent experimental replicates were performed, each consisting of three independent biological replicates.
Disease assessment
The incidence rate and disease index were calculated at 9 days post inoculation (dpi).
P. pratensis
powdery mildew is graded based on a 9-point scale as follows: Grade 0 indicates no symptoms; for Grade 1, there are a small number of powdery mildew spots on less than 5% of the entire leaf area; in Grade 3, the lesion layer is relatively thin and the area accounts for 6-10% of the whole leaf area; in Grade 5, the lesion layer is relatively thick and the area accounts for 11-20% of the whole leaf area; in Grade 7, the lesion layer is very thick and the area accounts for 21-40% of the whole leaf area; for Grade 9, the lesion layer is very thick and covers more than 40% of the whole leaf area. These grading standards are applied in three independent replicate experiments with at least twenty plants per line [
16
]. The formulas for incidence rate and disease index are as follows:

Physiological measurements
The physiological parameters were determined by collecting 0.5 g of leaves at 9 dpi. The relative water content was determined according to the method of Salisbury et al. [
17
]. The relative electrolyte conductivity (REC) was determined according to Lutts et al. [
18
]. The chlorophyll content was determined according to Sumanta et al. [
19
]. The malondialdehyde (MDA) content was measured by the thiobarbituric acid method [
20
].The superoxide radical (O
2
·−
) generation rate was determined by hydroxylamine oxidation [
21
]. The level of hydrogen peroxide (H
2
O
2
) was measured according to Willekens et al. [
22
]. Phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) and cinnamyl alcohol dehydrogenase (CAD) activities were determined using assay kits produced by Suzhou Comin Biotechnology Co.Ltd. (Suzhou, China). Cinnamoyl-CoA reductase (CCR) activity was determined using an ELISA kit produced by Shanghai Lianmai Biotechnology Co.Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Peroxidase (POD) activity was determined by guaiacol method [
23
].The total lignin content was measured according to Barnes et al. [
24
].
Protein extraction, digestion and iTRAQ labeling
Leaf samples (1.0 g) were weighed, and proteins were extracted by SDT (4%(w/v) SDS, 100 mM Tris/HCl pH 7.6, 0.1 M DTT) lysis, followed by protein quantification via the BCA method [
25
,
26
]. One hundred micrograms of protein from each sample were removed and were trypsinized using the FASP method [
25
], followed by desalting of the digested peptides using a C18 cartridge (Waters, Milford, USA). The desalted peptides were lyophilized, dissolved in 40 µL of dissolution buffer, and then quantified by measuring the OD
280
value of the filtrate with a visible ultraviolet spectrophotometer. One hundred micrograms of peptide were removed from each sample, and the peptides were labeled using an iTRAQ labeling kit (AB SCIEX, Boston, USA).
Strong cation exchange (SCX) and LC‒MS/MS analysis
The mixed labeled peptides were graded using an AKTA Purifier 100 liquid chromatograph (General Electric, Boston, USA). The lyophilized mixed peptide fragments were reconstituted with 2 mL of buffer A (pH 3.0, 10 mM KH
2
PO
4
, 25% ACN). The samples were injected from the injector to the column for separation, and gradient elution was performed at a flow rate of 1 mL·min
− 1
: 0% buffer B (pH 3.0, 10 mM KH
2
PO
4
, 25% ACN, 0.5 M KCl) for 25 min with a gradient of 0-10%; a gradient of 10-20% for 25-32 min; a gradient of 20-45% for 32-42 min; a gradient of 45-100% for 42-47 min; a gradient of 100% for 47-60 min; and after 60 min, the gradient was set to 0%. During the elution process, the absorbance at 214 nm was monitored, and the elution fractions were collected every 1 min, freeze-dried and desalted using a C
18
cartridge (Waters, Milford, USA).
The graded samples were separated using an Easy nLC (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, USA), a high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) liquid system with nanoliter flow rates. Buffer A was a 0.1% formic acid solution, and buffer B was a 0.1% formic acid acetonitrile solution (84% acetonitrile). The chromatographic column was equilibrated with 95% buffer A. The sample was injected into the injection column (Thermo Scientific Acclaim PepMap 100, 100 μm×2 cm, nanoViper C
18
) by an autosampler and separated by an analytical column (Thermo Scientific EASY column, 10 cm, ID75 µm, 3 μm, C
18
-A2) with the flow rate set at 300 nL·min
− 1
. The samples were separated by chromatography and analyzed by mass spectrometry using a Q-Exactive Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, USA).
Proteomic data analysis
The raw data obtained from mass spectrometry analysis were maintained as RAW files, and the RAW files were checked for library identification and quantitative analysis using Mascot (v 2.20) and Proteome Discoverer (v 1.40). The identified proteins were considered to be differentially abundant proteins (DAPs) when the fold change in expression was more than 1.2-fold (upregulation ≥ 1.2-fold or downregulation ≤ 0.83-fold) and the
P
value was < 0.05.
Blast2GO (
www.blast2go.com
) was utilized to perform BLAST comparison, GO entry extraction, GO annotation and InterProScan supplemental annotation on the target protein collection. KEGG pathway annotation was performed on the target protein collection using the KEGG Automatic Annotation Server (KAAS,
http://www.genome.jp/tools/kaas/
). The distribution of each GO classification and KEGG pathway in the target protein collection and the overall protein collection was evaluated by Fisher’s exact test, and this GO function or KEGG pathway was considered to be significantly enriched when the corrected
P
value was < 0.05.
Metabolite extraction
Leaf samples (100 mg) were added to a 2 mL centrifuge tube, and a 6 mm diameter grinding bead was added. A total of 400 µL of extraction solution (methanol: water = 4:1 (v: v)) containing 0.02 mg·mL
− 1
of an internal standard (L-2-chlorophenylalanine) was used for metabolite extraction. The samples were ground with a Wonbio-96c (Wanbo Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) frozen tissue grinder for 6 min (-10 °C, 50 Hz), followed by low-temperature ultrasonic extraction for 30 min (5 °C, 40 kHz). The samples were left at -20 °C for 30 min and centrifuged for 15 min (4 °C, 14000×g), after which the supernatant was transferred to an injection vial for LC‒MS/MS analysis. An equal amount of supernatant from each processed sample was mixed as a QC sample.
LC-MS output analysis and data processing
LC-MS/MS analysis of the samples was conducted on a Thermo UHPLC-Q-Exactive HF-X system (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, USA) equipped with an Accucore HILIC column (2.1 mm × 50 mm, 2.6 μm; Thermo Scientific, Waltham, USA) at Novogene Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). The mobile phases consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water: acetonitrile (95:5, v/v) (solvent A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile: isopropanol: water (47.5:47.5, v/v) (solvent B). The flow rate was 0.30 mL·min
− 1
, and the column temperature was 40 °C.
The mass spectrometric data were collected using a Thermo UHPLC-Q-Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source operating in positive mode and negative mode. The optimal mass spectrometry conditions were set as follows: source temperature, 320 °C; sheath gas flow rate, 35 arb; aux gas flow rate, 10 arb; ion-spray voltage floating (ISVF), -3200 V in negative mode and 3200 V in positive mode; normalized collision energy, 20-40-60 V rolling for MS/MS. The full MS resolution was 60,000, and the MS/MS resolution was 7500. Data acquisition was performed in Data Dependent Scans (DDS) mode. The detection was carried out over a mass range of 100-1500 m·z
− 1
.
Metabolomics data analysis
Pretreatment of the LC/MS raw data was performed by Progenesis QI (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA) software, and a three-dimensional data matrix in CSV format was exported. The information in this three-dimensional matrix included the sample information, metabolite name and mass spectral response intensity. The internal standard peaks, as well as any known false-positive peaks (including noise, column bleeds, and derivatized reagent peaks), were removed from the data matrix, and the abundant and pooled peaks were removed. At the same time, the metabolites were identified by searching databases, and the main databases used were HMDB (
http://www.hmdb.ca/
) and Metlin (
https://metlin.scripps.edu/
).
The data matrix after library searching was preprocessed as follows: at least 80% of the metabolic features detected in any set of samples were retained. After filtering, for specific samples with metabolite levels below the lower limit of quantification, the minimum metabolite value was estimated, and each metabolic signature was normalized to the sum. To reduce the errors caused by sample preparation and instrument instability, the response intensities of the sample mass spectrometry peaks were normalized using the sum normalization method to obtain the normalized data matrix. Moreover, the variables of the QC samples with a relative standard deviation (RSD) > 30% were excluded and log10 logarithmized to obtain the final data matrix for subsequent analysis.
Then, the R package ‘ropls’ (v 1.6.2) was used to perform principal component analysis (PCA), orthogonal least partial squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), and 7-cycle interactive validation to evaluate the stability of the model. The metabolites with VIP > 1 and p < 0.05 were determined to be significantly differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs) based on the variable importance in projection (VIP) obtained by the OPLS-DA model and the p value generated by the Student’s
t
test. The DEMs obtained from the screening were annotated by metabolic pathway annotation via the KEGG database (
http://www.genome.jp/kegg/
) to obtain the associated differentially abundant metabolite pathways.
Co-analysis of proteomics and metabolomics
The DAPs and DEMs were matched against the protein and metabolite information contained in the KEGG pathway database, and the DAPs and DEMs annotated to the same pathway were considered to have potential functional associations. Finally, the association information of the DAPs and DEMs was obtained in the form of a shared pathway. The metabolic pathway maps related to the DAPs and DEMs were exported to visualize their functional characteristics. Pearson correlation analysis was performed based on the abundance values of the DAPs and DEMs, and correlation cluster analysis was performed to plot the strongly correlated DAP-DEM relationship pairs in the form of a heatmap.
RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT‒PCR) validation
Total RNA was extracted from collected
P. pratensis
leaves using a plant total RNA extraction kit (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China). The extracted RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript™ II 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China). The cDNA obtained by reverse transcription was used as a template, and the
Actin
gene was used as an internal reference gene [
27
]. cDNA was analyzed by real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR using a LightCycler 96 real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR system (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Gene-specific primers were designed with Premier 6.0 software (Additional file 4: Table
S1
). The real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR reaction system was 20 µL, and the two-step amplification method was used. The reaction conditions were predenaturation at 94 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s and annealing at 60 °C for 30 s. The relative expression of each gene was calculated by the 2
−ΔΔCt
method [
28
]. The qRT-PCR analysis included three independent technical repeats with three biological replicates.
Results
Symptoms and physiological changes under powdery mildew infection
After inoculation with powdery mildew, two
P. pratensis
varieties with different disease resistance phenotypes showed significant differences in symptoms and physiological characteristics (Fig.
1
). The resistant
P. pratensis
variety ‘BlackJack’ was found to be infested with a small amount of powdery mildew at 5 dpi, and a powdery mildew layer formed on the infested area at 9 dpi, but the area was less than one-tenth of the leaf area, showing that this variety has strong resistance to the disease. However, at 5 dpi, the leaves of the susceptible
P. pratensis
variety ‘EverGlade’ already showed a large number of irregular greenish-yellow spots, which gradually enlarged to form a powdery mildew layer. At 9 dpi, the white powdery mildew faded and formed a yellowish brown to black closed capsular shell, and the leaves became locally necrotic and eventually wilted and died (Fig.
1
A). The incidence rate and disease index of the resistant variety ‘BlackJack’ were 2.33% and 1.23, respectively, while those of the susceptible variety ‘EverGlade’ were 83.00% and 62.93, respectively (Fig.
1
B and C).

Discussion
Most studies on powdery mildew in
P. pratensis
have focused on disease type, symptoms and integrated control methods, while relatively few studies have investigated the mechanisms of disease resistance. Therefore, elucidating the mechanisms of
P. pratensis
resistance to powdery mildew is critical for optimizing integrated management strategies for controlling powdery mildew infection in turf. The objective of this study was to determine the differences in proteomic and metabolomic responses between the resistant and susceptible varieties ‘BlackJack’ and ‘EverGlade’ after infestation with powdery mildew to analyze the mechanism of powdery mildew resistance in
P. pratensis
and to lay a theoretical foundation for the selection of
P. pratensis
varieties resistant to powdery mildew and for the identification and utilization of related disease resistance genes.
Induced structural resistance is a physically active resistance exhibited by a plant infested with a pathogen that causes changes in the morphology and structure of plant cells, subcells or tissues; it can cause a defense response in the host plant, limiting the infestation of the pathogen to individual cells or localized tissues [
29
]. When plants are infested with pathogenic bacteria, the cell wall, as the first line of defense against infestation, is damaged, which induces the plant to repair the cell wall. In this process, lignin deposition in the cell wall, intercellular layer and cytoplasm accelerates, leading to thickening and lignification of the cell wall, which enables the plant cell wall to resist the mechanical pressure of the pathogen infestation and, at the same time, cuts off the nutrient uptake pathway of the pathogen to inhibit its growth [
30
]. We measured the lignin content of two different resistant
P. pratensis
varieties before and after powdery mildew infection and found that before powdery mildew infection, the lignin content of ‘BlackJack’ was significantly greater than that of ‘EverGlade’. After powdery mildew infection, the lignin content in ‘BlackJack’ showed an increasing trend, while the lignin content in ‘EverGlade’ showed a decreasing trend (Fig.
8
D). Cécile et al. [
31
] concluded that the disease resistance of plant varieties is positively correlated with the lignin content in leaf tissues and that the lignin content and the degree of lignification can be used as key indicators for evaluating the strength of plant disease resistance. The differences in the lignin content of the two different resistant
P. pratensis
varieties prior to powdery mildew infestation may be related to the genetic characteristics of the varieties and may also determine initial disease resistance.
The phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway is an important step in lignin synthesis [
32
,
33
]. Our results showed that the accumulation of proteins associated with phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, including PAL (EC: 4.3.1.25), CCR (EC: 1.2.1.44), CAD (EC: 1.1.1.195) and POD (EC: 1.11.1.7), changed after powdery mildew infestation of two different resistant
P. pratensis
cultivars (Fig.
8
A). We measured the activities of these enzymes and found that after powdery mildew infestation, the activities of all four enzymes increased in ‘BlackJack’, whereas only CAD activity showed an increasing trend in ‘EverGlade’ (Fig.
8
C). PAL is the first key rate-limiting enzyme in the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway and is involved in the synthesis of phytoalexins, lignins and phenolics, as well as being closely associated with different forms of exciton-induced disease resistance [
34
]. Li et al. [
35
] analyzed key enzymes of the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway in
Lycium
inoculated with
Fusarium oxysporum
and reported that PAL activity was greater in disease-resistant varieties than in disease-susceptible varieties and that PAL activity was positively correlated with the disease resistance of
Lycium
. Notably, in our study, PAL activity was significantly increased in ‘BlackJack’ than in ‘EverGlade’, indicating that PAL activity is also positively correlated with
P. pratensis
resistance to powdery mildew. Lignin is produced by the dehydrogenation and polymerization of monomers of three alcohols (coniferyl alcohol, p-coumaryl alcohol and sinapyl alcohol), which form three corresponding lignin monomers, guaiactl lignin (G-lignin), 5-hydroxy-guaiacyl lignin (H-lignin), and syringyl lignin (S-lignin), which require the involvement of CCR for their synthesis [
36
]. Kawasaki et al. [
37
] reported that upregulated expression of the
CCR
gene in rice results in increased synthesis of lignin monomers, which reduces rice injury caused by pathogenic bacteria. The
AtCCR2
gene in
Arabidopsis
has a similar effect [
38
]. Similarly, in our study, we also found that after inoculation with powdery mildew, the accumulation of the CCR protein and its enzyme activity increased in ‘BlackJack’, while the opposite result was observed in ‘EverGlade’ (Fig.
8
A and C), which may be directly related to the fact that ‘BlackJack’ showed stronger resistance to powdery mildew.
CAD is one of the key enzymes in the lignin synthesis pathway and deserves our in-depth study. It acts in the final step of lignin monomer biosynthesis, which requires the catalytic reduction of different cinnamaldehydes to the corresponding cinnamyl alcohols using Zn
2+
and NADH as coenzymes [
39
]. Inhibition or inactivation of CAD activity can lead to a decrease in the lignin content in
Medicago truncatula
[
40
]. In this study, after inoculation with powdery mildew, we found that the protein accumulation and enzyme activities of CAD significantly increased in both
P. pratensis
varieties (Fig.
8
A and C), suggesting that CAD plays an important role in the response of
P. pratensis
to powdery mildew infestation. The same conclusions were reached in studies on
Arabidopsis
[
41
] and
Triticum aestivum
[
42
]. Notably, a common DEM obtained from the proteomics and metabolomics coanalysis, namely, coniferin, was downregulated in both
P. pratensis
varieties (Fig.
8
A). Coniferin produces coniferyl alcohol under the action of coniferin beta-glucosidase (EC: 3.2.1.126), and coniferyl alcohol undergoes monomer dehydrogenation polymerization to produce G-lignin (Fig.
8
A). Thus, the downregulation of coniferin accumulation in both
P. pratensis
varieties after inoculation with powdery mildew may be closely related to the massive synthesis of lignin.
Conclusions
In this study, we utilized iTRAQ proteomics and nontargeted metabolomics to elucidate the response mechanism of
P. pratensis
to powdery mildew infestation. The shared KEGG pathways of the DAPs and DEMs were comprehensively analyzed, and a total of four KEGG pathways were ultimately identified. Among these pathways, the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway, which is enriched in both
P. pratensis
varieties, was selected for further analysis. Further analysis revealed that lignin biosynthesis plays an important role in the response of
P. pratensis
to powdery mildew infestation. This study enhances our understanding of the mechanism of powdery mildew resistance in
P. pratensis
and provides data for the selection of
P. pratensis
powdery mildew-resistant varieties as well as the mining and utilization of related disease resistance genes.
