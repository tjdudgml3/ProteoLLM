Promyelocytic leukemia (PML) is the main scaffold protein of PML‐nuclear bodies (PML NBs); subnuclear domains that orchestrate cell survival pathways in response to apoptosis, DNA damage, viral infection, and senescence [
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]. The PML gene encodes at least six nuclear isoforms in humans cells that share a RING‐B‐box coiled‐coil (RBBC) motif and a variable C‐terminal region [
5
,
6
]. These PML isoforms homo‐oligomerize
via
RBBC and SUMO‐SUMO interaction motif (SIM) intermolecular interactions to form the nuclear domain known as a PML NB [
7
] that then recruits numerous client proteins (> 150)
via
SUMO or SIM interactions, including transcription factors, chromatin remodeling factors, kinases, phosphatases and components of the SUMO‐modifying machinery itself [
8
,
9
]. In addition to PML NBs, six non‐canonical PML substructures have been identified in the nuclei of cells exposed to stress stimuli, such as senescence, oncogenic transformation, nuclear envelope damage, excess fatty acids and accretion of misfolded proteins (reviewed in [
10
]). These novel PML‐containing subdomains contain a different repertoire of client proteins than found in canonical PML NBs, which presumably ascribes new cell function(s) to these domains. One of these atypical PML substructures are Lipid‐Associated PML Structures (LAPS), which are comprised of a nuclear lipid droplet (nLD) surrounded by PML, CTP:phosphocholine cytidylyltransferase α (CCTα), Lipin1, and other lipid metabolic enzymes and proteins [
11
,
12
]. LAPS are formed during exposure of cells to excess fatty acids but their role in the cellular response to this external stress is not fully understood.
Eukaryotic cells utilize fatty acids as an energy source and for the synthesis of membranes but when demands are met, excess is stored as triacylglycerol (TAG) in cytosolic lipid droplets (cLD). In contrast, nLDs are less abundant than cLDs, smaller in size, and restricted to specific cell types. nLDs were first identified in mouse and human hepatocytes [
13
,
14
], and later in Huh7 hepatoma cells [
12
], U2OS osteosarcoma cells [
11
,
12
], yeast [
15
] and
Caenorhabditis elegans
[
16
]. In Huh7 cells, nLDs are derived from lipid droplets in the lumen of the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) that are the precursors for assembly of very low‐density lipoproteins. Under conditions of ER stress and fatty acid overload, ER luminal lipid droplets enter the type I nucleoplasmic reticulum and break through into the nucleoplasm at sites on the membrane demarcated by the PMLII isoform [
17
]. During its egress into the nucleoplasm the nLD associates with PMLII to form LAPS. An alternate mechanism identified in U2OS cells that do not secrete lipoproteins involves assembly of nLDs and LAPS on the inner nuclear membrane (INM) by a mechanism akin to cLD assembly and budding from the ER but independent of seipin [
18
]. In addition to PML, nascent nLDs also associate with lipid biosynthetic enzymes, including diacylglycerol acyltransferases 2, Lipin1, and glycerol phosphate acyltransferases [
11
,
12
,
17
,
18
], indicating they arise by localized TAG synthesis on the INM. Results with PML knockout (KO) U2OS cells showed that LAPS account for 60% of total nLDs and are depleted of canonical PML NB‐associated proteins, such as SUMO, DAXX and SP100 [
11
]. These results suggest that an initial step in LAPS formation involves deSUMOylating and disruption of PML NBs and loss of other client proteins, and the subsequent recruitment of PML to the surface of nLDs.
Previously, we examined the interaction of a limited subset of known PML‐interacting proteins with PML NB and LAPS by microscopy imaging [
11
]. To provide an unbiased and more comprehensive comparison, we employed APEX2‐mediated proximity‐dependent biotinylation of naive and oleate‐treated U2OS cells to characterize the interactome of PMLI and II. Since PMLI and II associate with PML‐NBs, LAPS and are free in the nucleoplasm, antibody‐based imaging using proximity ligation assay (PLA) was used to identify the preferred PML substructure for select interactors. Quantitative mass spectrometry of streptavidin‐affinity purified peptides revealed 64 high‐confidence PMLI‐ and PMLII‐interacting proteins, notably two networks of ESCRT and COPII vesicle transport proteins that were differentially enriched in control or oleate‐treated conditions. PLA revealed that the COPII proteins SEC23B, SEC24A and USO1 preferentially interacted with PML NBs, identifying a hitherto unknown nuclear function for these well‐known COPII coat and adaptor proteins in the ER‐Golgi secretory pathway.

Identification of biotinylated proteins by quantitative mass spectrometry
Sample preparation and analysis was conducted as previously described [
24
]. Proteins bound to NeutrAvidin‐agarose were subject to five washes with 20 m
m
NH
4
CHO
3
, reduced with 10 m
m
DTT for 30 min and alkylated with 15 m
m
chloroacetamide for 1 h. On‐bead digestion with trypsin (1 μg overnight) was stopped with 1% formic acid. Eluted peptides were collected, and the beads were treated with 1% formic acid and 60% acetonitrile to release residual peptides. Pooled eluates were dried, resuspended in 0.1% trifluoroacetate (TFA) and peptides were desalted using 100 μL pipet tips containing a C18 resin (Thermo‐Fisher Scientific, Burlington, ON, Canada). Peptides were dried, resuspended in 1% formic acid and 500 ng was injected into an HPLC (nanoElute, Bruker Daltonics, Burlington, ON, Canada) equipped with a trap column (Acclaim PepMap100 C18, 0.3 mm id × 5 mm, Dionex Corporation, Sunnyvale, CA, USA) and analytical C18 column (1.9 μm beads, 75 μm × 25 cm, PepSep, Bruker Daltonics, Burlington, ON, Canada). Peptides were separated over 2 h using a linear gradient of 5–37% acetonitrile in 0.1% TFA at a flow rate of 400 nL·min
−1
while being injected into a TimsTOF Pro ion mobility mass spectrometer equipped with a Captive Spray nano electrospray source (Bruker Daltonics). The target intensity was set to 20 000, with an intensity threshold of 2500. Samples processing was acquired using diaPASEF mode. Briefly, for each single TIMS (100 ms) in diaPASEF mode, we used 1 mobility window consisting of 27 mass steps (m/z between 114 to 1414 with a mass width of 50 Da) per cycle (1.27 s duty cycle) and collision energy of 42.0 eV. These steps cover the diagonal scan line for +2 and +3 charged peptides in the m/z‐ion mobility plane.
The raw files were analyzed using
dia‐nn
software (version 1.8.1) and the Uniprot human proteome database (21/03/2020, 75 776 entries). FASTA digest for library‐free search/library generation was enabled, as well as Deep learning‐based spectra, retention time and ion mobility prediction. The settings used for the analysis were: 1 miscleavage was allowed; fixed modifications were carbamidomethylation on cysteine and N‐terminal methionine excision; enzyme was trypsin; peptide length between 7 and 30 amino acids; precursor charge range was between 2 and 4; a mass tolerance of 20 ppm was fixed for both precursor ions and fragment ions; precursor false discovery rate was set to 5%; match between runs, unrelated runs and MaxLFQ options were also enabled.
The average peptide count data was filtered using the CRAPome repository [
25
] with a threshold score of 0.2. to remove common mass spectrometry contaminants. The residual proteins from the APEX2‐PMLI and ‐PMLII data sets were then compared against the APEX2‐NLS protein data sets using the default settings of the program SAINTexpress using a score threshold of 0.2 [
26
]. Dot plots of the resulting proteins were made using ProHits‐viz with a Bayesian false discovery rate threshold of 0.05 [
27
] and interaction networks analyzed using
string
v11 [
28

Results
APEX2
‐
PMLI
and
APEX2
‐
PMLII
localize to
PML NBs
and
LAPS
The interactome of PML NBs and LAPS was investigated by biotin proximity labeling of these structures in U2OS cells using APEX2 fused to the N‐terminus of PMLI and PMLII (referred to hereafter as APEX2‐PMLI and APEX2‐PMLII, Fig.
1A
). We chose APEX2 in part due to its smaller mass compared to biotin ligases, rapid catalysis for better temporal resolution of bait‐protein interactions, and synthesis of a short‐lived biotin‐phenol radical that reacts with electron‐rich tyrosine residues [
31
,
32
]. Biotin ligases that modify lysine residues were not used for this application since PML and many of its client proteins are frequently modified by SUMOylation of this amino acid. To control for non‐specific nuclear biotinylation, APEX2 with a C‐terminal NLS (referred to hereafter as APEX2‐NLS) was expressed in cells. All three constructs have an internal HA tag to visualize expression by confocal microscopy and immunoblotting in transiently transfected U2OS cells, which are ideal for this study because they express abundant PML NBs and LAPS [
11
,
12
]. APEX2‐PMLI, and APEX2‐PML‐II and APEX2‐NLS were expressed in U2OS cells at the expected molecular mass of 125 kDa when probed with an HA antibody (Fig.
1B
). Confocal immunofluorescence was used to determine if APEX2‐PMLI and ‐PMLII associated with PML NBs and LAPS (Fig.
1C,D
). In transiently transfected U2OS cells both APEX2‐PMLI and APEX2‐PMLII were localized to nuclear puncta corresponding to PML NBs that were positive for HA and PML (Fig.
1C,D
). Treatment of these cells with oleate for 24 h resulted in the appearance of BODIPY493/503‐positive nLDs that had the HA‐tagged fusion proteins on their surface (Fig.
1C,D
) indicating that both APEX2‐PML probes were incorporated into LAPS. The nucleus also contains a pool of soluble, non‐SUMOylated PML that can assemble into PML NBs under stress conditions [
33
]. HA and PML diffusely stained the nucleus in control and oleate‐treated cells indicating that soluble APEX2‐PML1 and II interactions will be detected (Fig.
1C,D
).

The
APEX2

PML NBs
and

Having established that APEX2‐PML proteins localized to nuclear bodies and LAPS similarly to their endogenous counterparts, APEX2‐based proximity biotinylation was used to establish the PMLI and II interactomes in control and oleate‐treated cells. U2OS cells expressing APEX2‐PMLI, APEX2‐PMLII, or APEX2‐NLS were treated with or without 500 μ
m
oleate for 24 h, followed by 30 and 1 min incubations with biotin‐phenol and H
2
O
2
, respectively. Prior to quantitative mass spectrometry analysis, the expression of fusion proteins and efficiency of the biotinylation reaction was confirmed in cell lysates by immunoblotting with a HA antibody and a streptavidin probe (see Fig.
2C
). High confidence APEX2‐PMLI and ‐PMLII interactors were identified using SAINT score analysis and filtered for non‐specific interactions against the APEX2‐NLS and CRAPome [
25
] data sets. A total of 64 proteins interacted with both PML isoforms in control and oleate‐treated cells and were sorted into a dot plot to illustrate the isoform‐ and condition‐specific interactions (Fig.
3A
). Although many interacting proteins were shared between the two PML isoforms, more high confidence interactions were identified for APEX2‐PMLI. Approximately 75% of the interactors are nominally cytoplasmic or localized to cytoplasmic organelles, with the remainder designated as nuclear‐localized (Fig.
3B
). Well‐known PML NB and LAPS interactors identified by other methodologies, such as co‐immunofluorescence, were not represented in our set suggesting that biotin proximity labelling targets a unique subset of interactions in these PML structures; a result we speculate may arise if biotinylation of some interacting proteins is occluded by post translational modifications or association with the PML structures themselves. To facilitate identification of common networks, proteins that interacted with both PMLI and PMLII in the presence (Fig.
3B
) and absence of oleate (Fig.
3C
) were pooled and analyzed using STRING (disconnected proteins were excluded from the network based on confidence using all active interaction sources). The resulting networks highlighted two interconnected clusters: (a) Endosomal sorting complexes required for transport (ESCRT) proteins such as TSG101, STAM and STAM2 and (b) COPII vesicular coat proteins and tethers such as SEC23B, SEC24A, SEC24B, SEC23IP and USO1. These interaction networks were evident with both PMLI and PMLII and under control and oleate‐treated conditions but were more robust for APEX2‐PMLI in oleate‐treated cells.

Interaction of
COPII
transport proteins with
PML NBs
and
LAPS
An intriguing feature of the PML interactome was the association of ESCRT and COPII transport proteins with PMLI and II, and the enrichment of some interactors (i.e., SEC24B, USO1, TSG101) in oleate‐treated cells (Fig.
3
). The reported nuclear localization and functions of TSG101 [
34
], SEC23B [
35
] and USO1 [
36
] prompted a detailed validation of PML‐specific interactions. We chose to analyze the COPII network by assessing the localization of SEC23B, SEC24A and USO1 with PML NBs and LAPS using a combination of confocal immunofluorescence and PLA.
Consistent with their role in COPII vesicle transport between the ER and Golgi apparatus [
37
], SEC23B and SEC24A were primarily localized to the perinuclear compartment of control and oleate‐treated cells (Fig.
4A,B
). However, SEC23B‐ and SEC24A‐positive puncta were also evident in the nuclei of cells cultured with or without oleate. Magnified ROIs showed partial co‐localization of SEC23B and SEC24A with PML NBs and LAPS as well as nLDs devoid of PML. Due to the resolution limits of confocal microscopy, the interaction of SEC23B and SEC24A with PML was evaluated by PLA. This technique involves primary antibody pairs to suspected interacting proteins, secondary antibodies with DNA tags that ligate when targets are within 40 nm, and a rolling circle DNA amplification step to enhance sensitivity [
38
]. Using this technique, we validated the method using proteins known to interact with PML NBs and LAPS. First, a polyclonal antibody against DAXX, a canonical PML NB‐associated protein that is depleted from LAPS [
11
], when combined with a PML monoclonal antibody produced numerous PLA puncta in nuclei of U2OS cells (Fig.
5A
) that were strongly reduced by oleate treatment for 24 h (Fig.
5B,C
). Secondly, a polyclonal antibody against CCTα, which associates with nLDs and LAPS in oleate‐treated U2OS cells [
11
], was used in combination with a PML antibody. Unexpectedly, the combination of CCTα and PML antibodies produced numerous PLA puncta in untreated U2OS cells (Fig.
5D
), indicating that CCTα also associates with PML NBs. However, oleate treatment caused the appearance of intense PLA signals on LAPS but an overall reduction in puncta due to the coalescence of PML and CCTα onto LAPS (Fig.
5E,F
). PML KO U2OS cells exhibited a near complete absence of CCTα‐PML puncta confirming the specificity of PLA to detect PML‐specific interactions (Fig.
5F
).

To confirm that PML was required for nuclear localization of USO1, and to identify the PML isoform(s) responsible, USO1 nuclear localization was quantified in PML KO cells transiently expressing GFP‐PMLI and/or GFP‐PMLII. Discussion
In response to cellular stressors, PML NBs reorganize into non‐canonical structures whose functions are poorly understood due in part to a lack of information on their repertoire of interacting proteins. LAPS are one of these alternate PML subdomains formed when PML and PML‐client proteins are recruited to the surface of nLDs that form in response to cellular stress induced by excess fatty acids. LAPS are relatively depleted of several PML NB client proteins such as SP100 and DAXX [
11
] but are enriched in enzymes involved in lipid synthesis and storage [
12
,
18
], indicating a dramatic shift in PML function to cope with excess fatty acids. To characterize the shifting landscape of PML interactors during fatty acid stress, we conducted an unbiased APEX2 proximity analysis of PMLI and PMLII interactors in cells treated with oleate to promote LAPS formation compared to untreated cells harboring only PML NBs. Previously, chromatin interactions with PML NBs were assessed using an APEX2 knock‐in approach at the
PML
locus [
42
]. However, this study did not examine the proximity interactome of PML‐interacting proteins. Thus, our study is the first to employ APEX2 to specifically examine PML protein interactions, and how the proximity interactome of PML changes upon fatty acid stress. We chose to overexpress APEX2‐PMLI and II in a wild‐type rather than a PML‐null U2OS cells because (a) PML NBs AND LAPS are not composed of single PML isoforms and (b) LAPS formation is dependent on PMLII. Thus APEX2‐PMLI and II would be integrated into biologically relevant PML structures in wild‐type cells. However, overexpression has the potential for non‐specific interactions due to mis‐localization PML NBs, LAPS or soluble PML. To mitigate this issue, several PML interactions within a prominent node (Fig.
3
) were validated using PLA at endogenous expression levels. Cross referencing our APEX2‐PML interactome to reported PML protein interactions in the BioGrid data base [
43
] revealed common interacting partners from two studies. The first identified LPP, PPP1R13L, STAM, SEC24B, TNKS1BP1, IGBP1 and ERCC6L in a screen employing a split SUMO‐TurboID using PML as a substrate [
8
]. The second study using affinity capture and western blotting identified CSNK1D as PML‐interacting protein [
44
]. This provides confidence in our approach, which extends these finding to a unique set of APEX2‐PML interactors identified here that reflects the choice of experimental system (i.e., cells and treatments) as well as features of the APEX2 probe.
A novel finding coming from this study was the identification of two nodes of PML‐interacting proteins involved in cytoplasmic COPII and ESCRT vesicle trafficking. ESCRT complexes in endosomes recruit ubiquitinated proteins into multivesicular bodies that are degraded upon fusion with primary lysosomes [
45
]. ESCRT‐1 proteins TSG101, ESCRT‐0 proteins STAM, STAM2 and HGS, and the ESCRT‐associated phosphatase PTPN23 and NEDD4 ligase were identified in both PMLI and PMLII interaction sets. STAM was independently identified in a split SUMO‐TurboID screen [
8
]. In conjunction with VPS13D, TSG101 remodels the phospholipid monolayer of cLDs to assist in the removal of fatty acids to the mitochondria for oxidation during glucose starvation [
46
]. TSG101 is also localized to the nucleus [
47
] where it could have a related function on nLDs or LAPS.
Non‐canonical functions of COPII vesicle transport proteins SEC23B, SEC24A, SEC24B, SEC23IP, TRAPPC3 and USO1 are also inferred from the APEX2‐PML interactome. SEC24B was also identified as a PML interactor in a split SUMO‐TurboID screen [
8
]. Immunofluorescence microscopy and PLA confirmed the interaction of the COPII coat proteins SEC23B and SEC24A, as well as the Golgi tethering factor USO1, with PML NBs. Similar to DAXX (Fig.
5A,B
), all three COPII transport proteins had diminished PLA puncta in oleate‐treated cells suggesting they dissociate from PML NBs during or after PML association with LAPS. The nuclear localization of USO1 was also reduced in PML KO cells, but this effect was less significant under oleate‐treated conditions. Collectively, the COPII proteins behave like the PML NB client proteins SUMO, DAXX and SP‐100 that have limited association with LAPS [
11
]. The reduced association of SEC23A, SEC24B and USO1 with LAPS detected by PLA is at odds with in the APEX2‐based biotinylation screen that showed similar (SEC24A) or enhanced (SEC23B and USO1) interaction in cell exposed to oleate (Fig.
3A
). An explanation for this discrepancy could be the different pools of PML interactors detected by the two methods. In addition to PML‐NBs and LAPS, proximity labelling will also capture the interactions of soluble PMLI and II in the nucleoplasm of control and oleate‐treated cells (Fig.
2A
). In contrast, the PLA signals were almost exclusively associated with strong immunofluorescence staining of PML NBs or LAPS. Thus, oleate treatment could generate a pool of soluble PML isoforms whose protein interactions are not readily detected by PLA. Since PML exists in different monomeric and polymeric forms, the interactome provided by proximity biotinylation must be validated and refined by immunofluorescence or PLA to define the preferred structural interactions. PLA is also not without technical challenges, however. For example, PLA using SEC23B and SEC24A antibodies required methanol fixation, which could disrupt PML NBs and LAPS and account for the reduced PLA signal for SEC23B and SEC24A (Fig.
6
; 2–3 puncta per cell) compared to USO1, CCTα or DAXX antibodies (Figs
6
and
7
, 7–15 puncta per cell). We also noted that the incubation conditions required for PLA greatly diminished the ability to detect cLDs, nLDs and LAPS with lipophilic dyes.
Studies on SEC23B and USO1 suggest some intriguing connections to nuclear PML NB function. SEC23B is specifically localized to the nucleoli where it is involved in the ER stress response to proteosome inhibition and transcription of ribosomal protein genes [
35
,
48
]. This observation raises the possibility that SEC23B and possibly other COPII proteins that associate preferentially with PML NBs could mediate an ER stress response induced by fatty acid overload. Nuclear‐localized USO1 has been identified as a potential RNA binding protein that is dysregulated in B‐cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia and multiple myeloma [
36
,
49
,
50
]. Intriguingly, caspase cleavage of USO1 generates a C‐terminal fragment that is SUMOylated and translocates to the nucleus where it enhances the apoptotic response in cells [
51
]. However, we were unable to reproduce either SUMOylation or caspase cleavage of USO1 in apoptotic U2OS cells (results not shown). While USO1 and SEC23B have non‐canonical roles in the nucleus that could involve PML, the presence of multiple COPII coats and a tether in the PML interactome also indicate that a related nuclear vesicle transport pathway could be interacting with PML structures. The proximity of PMLI and II to nuclear COPII vesicles could explain the large number of interactions. PMLI and II had interactions with a similar set of COPII proteins suggesting that the conserved N‐terminal region shared by all PML isoforms is involved. However, the specific requirement of PMLII for nuclear localization of USO1 shows that its unique C‐terminus encoded by exon 7b, which interacts with nuclear membranes [
12
] and nLDs [
52
], could also be involved.
Our PML proximity interactome study is the first to examine the changing protein associations of PML structures under cell stress. Specifically, we employed APEX2‐PML constructs to interrogate the interactome of PMLI and II in the context of fatty acid stress. This approach revealed non‐conical nuclear functions for cytoplasmic vesicle transport proteins including SEC23B, SEC24A and USO1. Orthogonal validation of these three COPII‐associated proteins interactions using PLA indicated a preferred interaction with PML NBs. As such SEC23B, SEC24A and USO1 likely have as yet to be elucidated ‘moon‐lighting’ function(s) in the nucleus. These results illustrate the power of combining proteomic analyses with cell stress to identify novel conditional nuclear subdomain interactions.
