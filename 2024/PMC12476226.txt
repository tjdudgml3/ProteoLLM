INTRODUCTION
Little is known about the role of glucocorticoids (GC) in the regulation of the human testicular function. We recently found that human testicular peritubular cells (HTPCs) are a dominant site of expression of the glucocorticoid receptor (GR) encoded by
NR3C1
.
1
,
2
,
3
These cells can be isolated and studied in vitro and are an appropriate and unique cellular model for the human testis.
4
They enable us, among others, to study actions of GCand we previously found that the synthetic GC dexamethansone (Dex) has a strong influence on the phenotype of cultured HTPCs and caused drastic changes of their proteome and secretome.
1
,
2
,
3
GC are major stress signals and also act as a timing signal for peripheral circadian oscillators.
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
Via GC action, peripheral clocks in the different organs of the body are synchronized with the central clock, which resides in the hypothalamus.
The mammalian circadian clock machinery is made up of a conservative transcriptional/translational feedback loop comprising four core clock genes and proteins, which regulate daily rhythmicity within a negative feedback loop: CLOCK (circadian locomotor output cycles kaput), BMAL1 (brain and muscle ARNT‐like protein 1), PERIOD (PER1‐3), and CRYPTOCHROME (CRY1+2).
11
,
12
Herein, CLOCK and BMAL1 encode helix‐to‐helix transcription factors, which form heterodimers to drive the transcription of
PER1
,
PER2
, and
PER3
together with
CRY1
and
CRY
,
13
via binding to E‐box regulatory elements. Once these molecules are translated, they build heterodimers in the cytoplasm to interact with other proteins, and after a time lag, return into the nucleus to abolish the transcriptional activity of CLOCK/BMAL1. Moreover, these 4 core proteins are able to crosstalk with clock‐controlled output genes to adapt physiological needs in a tissue‐ and cell‐specific manner.
14
In addition, mammalian
TIMELESS
gene is a less‐known circadian clock element, which also interacts with CRY and PER.
15
Expression and regulation of clock genes by GC were shown to occur in cultured cells, as well.
16
Yet, whether this is the case also in isolated testicular cells and in the testis, is not well known. Evidence exists for clock gene expression in testicular Leydig cells,
17
which express the GR.
We tested whether HTPCs express clock genes and whether Dex may regulate clock genes of HTPCs. We further examined expression of GR in the testis of a nonhuman primate (NHP) species, performed organotypic incubations, complemented by studies with immortalized testicular peritubular cells, to examine whether regulation of clock genes by Dex can also occur in the testis.

MATERIAL AND METHODS
2.1
Cells
HTPCs were obtained by the explant culture from small testicular fragments of men (29–55 years), who underwent testicular sperm extraction (TESE). Typical genetic causes for male infertility were ruled out. We followed all guidelines and legal regulations and written informed consent from all patients was obtained (Local Ethics Committee of Technical University of Munich, Faculty of Medicine; project 491/18S‐KK). The isolation and culture procedures of HTPCs, as well as an in‐depth characterization of cells were previously published.
18
,
19
The density of cells varied between 1.0–1.5 × 10
5
cells per 60 mm dish—depending on the duration of the cell culture experiment. When cells reached 70% confluence, experiments were started.
Generation, characterization, and culture of immortalized
Callithrix jacchus
(
Cj
) TPCs (iMKTPCs) were also described in detail previously.
20
Briefly, frozen stocks of iMKTPs were used, 2.5 × 10
5
cells per 60 mm dish were seeded, grown to 70% confluence and then serum starved overnight before exposure to Dex.
2.2
Experiment 1: gene expression patterns of circadian clock genes in human testicular peritubular cells for 24 h time series
To examine a 24 h (circadian) expression profile of clock genes, fresh Dulbeccos Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) containing 10% fetal calf serum (FCS) was added to cells from four individual donors and left overnight. On the next day, at 09:00 a.m. (Experiment time = 0 h), samples from all four individual donors were harvested at 4 h intervals for up to 24 h. To evaluate mRNA abundance of circadian clock genes, each cDNA sample at the indicated time points was run in duplicate in quantitative real‐time PCR (qPCR) measurements.
2.3
Experiment 2: effect of dexamethasone on circadian clock gene mRNA expression in human testicular peritubular cells for 48 h
The effect of Dex on clock gene expression was investigated in cells of three individual patients, which were also used in experiment 1. For monitoring Dex effects, medium was changed (Gibco, Paisley, UK), followed by a starvation period in the serum‐free medium (DMEM without FCS) overnight. This was done in order to synchronize the cell cycle. On the next day, at 09:00 a.m., this medium was replaced with Dex‐supplemented medium (DMEM without FCS, supplemented with 1 µM Dex dissolved in ethanol, final concentration of 0.04%). Control conditions included a solvent control (0.04% ethanol in DMEM without FCS). Cells from all three individual donors were harvested each at
t
= 0 (prior to 1 µM Dex treatment) and at the following time points: 4, 8, 24, 36, and 48 h after the beginning of Dex exposure. The qPCR measurements from all patients at the indicated time points were performed in duplicates.
2.4
Experiment 3: organotypic incubations
Common marmoset monkeys (
Cj
) were housed at the self‐sustaining marmoset monkey colony of the German Primate Center (Deutsches Primatenzentrum; DPZ, Göttingen). The animals used for this study were 3.5 years old males, that is, young adult, sexually mature healthy animals.
21
Testes were obtained from three animals euthanized for scientific purposes unrelated to this study or castrated for colony management purposes. Euthanasia and/or castration were performed by experienced veterinarians following all legal guidelines. All testes were washed 3× in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) supplemented with penicillin–streptomycin and the Tunica albuginea was removed. Each testis was cut to equally sized fragments (approximately 50 mg each). Each tissue fragment was incubated individually in a humidified incubator (37°C, 5% CO
2
in air) in culture medium (DMEM/F12 + GlutaMAX (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) + 1× penicillin–streptomycin (PAN Biotech, Aidenbach, Germany) supplemented with 1 µM Dex or with ethanol (solvent control), diluted to concentration equivalent to the treatment, for 6 h (5 treated and 5 negative control fragments per incubation period). At the end of the incubation, each fragment was placed in a 2 mL tube, snap‐frozen immediately in liquid nitrogen and shipped on dry ice to Munich. For qPCR and mass spectrometry analyses, one testicular tissue fragment / animal / treatment was randomly selected, cryopulverized, equally distributed and then subjected to either qPCR studies or mass spectrometry analyses. In order to optimize qPCR measurements, an additional fragment was used, while the remaining fragments were used for experiments not related to this study.
2.5
Experiment 4: effect of dexamethasone on BMAL1 and PER1 expression in iMKTPCs
This experiment included three biological replicates (passages 25–27) for 6 h Dex‐treatment of iMKTPCs including corresponding controls. As described for experiment 2, medium
20
was changed followed by a starvation period. On the next day, medium was replaced with Dex‐supplemented medium (medium without FCS, supplemented with 1 µM Dex dissolved in ethanol, final concentration of 0.04%). For control purposes medium contained the solvent (0.04% ethanol in medium without FCS). After 6 h, cells from all biological replicates were harvested and processed for qPCR measurements.
2.6
Isolation of RNA, reverse transcription (RT‐PCR) and quantitative real‐time PCR
For qPCR studies, total RNA from cultured HTPCs, iMKTPCs, and testicular fragments was isolated using QIAGEN RNeasy Mini‐Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Between 360 and 1000 ng RNA were reverse transcribed using SuperScriptTM II (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Adequate final dilutions corresponding to 2 ng per qPCR‐reaction were prepared and finally applied to quantitative RT‐PCR reactions using the Luna Universal qPCR Master Mix (New England Biolabs, Frankfurt am Main, Germany). Primers (see Table
S1

http://primer3.wi.mit.edu
; Accessed on March 2023). Runs were performed in duplicate on the LightCycler 96 System (Roche Diagnostics, Penzberg, Germany) and qPCR expression values were calculated using the ddcq method.
22
In experiment 1, the gene expression value was normalized to the mean value across all time points and to the expression of the housekeeping gene
GAPDH
. In experiment 2, target gene values were normalized to the first time point (
t
= 0) prior to the addition of 1 µM Dex. In experiments 2 and 3, results were normalized to the housekeeping gene
GAPDH
, in experiment 4 the housekeeping gene
PPIA
was shown to be the most stably expressed housekeeping gene and was therefore used for normalization.

p
< 0.001) and reached its maximum at 8 h (
p
< 0.001) resulting in an 18‐fold rise. Thereafter,

DISCUSSION
Dex is frequently used in clinical practice for a number of medical conditions, yet somewhat surprisingly, possible testicular consequences of Dex treatment in man are not well known.
27
We recently provided insights in GR‐mediated, Dex‐induced changes in human testicular cells, which encompass phenotypic and proteomic changes,
1
,
2
,
3
by using a highly adequate human cellular model, HTPCs. These cells retain the great majority of their in situ characteristics
4
and allow for functional studies.
We first studied expression of eight clock genes and then tested, whether they may be expressed in a circadian pattern in HTPCs, employing qPCR. Expression was readily detected and under standard culture conditions we found that the transcript levels of
BMAL1
and
Timeless
showed significant circadian oscillations. For
BMAL1
,   a key element of the clock gene machinery, this is in accordance with earlier reports of cultured cells.
10
,
16
,
28
A role for
TIMELESS
in circadian rhythmicity remains controversial and may depend on the cellular model or tissue.
29
,
30
,
31
,
32
,
33
In HTPCs, the significant rhythmic expression suggests that
TIMELESS
is a clock gene of relevance. Intriguingly,
BMAL1
expression fluctuated in an opposite manner compared to
PER3
, suggesting that both genes are relevant components in the circadian expression pattern in HTPCs.
The other clock components did not show statistically significant cyclicity in expression levels in our study, which involved four primary, patient‐derived HTPCs, that is, cells, with some degree of heterogeneity. The cells were cultured in the presence of serum, which may affect the amplitudes or rhythmic expression of some genes. In general, metabolic states, the cellular redox state may influence clock gene expression
14
,
34
and clock components may also be governed by yet other mechanisms, for example posttranslational modifications,
35
,
36
and intracellular signaling pathways.
37
These points were, however, not at the center of our work, and will require further studies.
We rather asked the question, whether Dex is able to alter clock gene expression in HTPCs. It is well known that endogenous GC, released from the adrenal gland, acts as a timing signal for many peripheral circadian oscillators.
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
In this way, peripheral clocks are synchronized with the central clock in the hypothalamus.
As the presence of serum in the culture medium, among others, may interfere with clock genes,
38
,
39
,
40
experiments aiming to examine Dex action, were performed in the absence of FCS. We found that Dex significantly elevated the transcript levels of
PER1, PER2, BMAL1
and
CRY2
. Further
PER2
levels remained elevated over 48 h over solvent controls. Changes in abundance of transcript levels of the other clock genes examined were not significantly affected.
That GC or Dex are regulators of clock genes in mouse peripheral organs and cells, e.g. primary mouse mesenchymal stem cells, mouse liver, and kidney,
41
,
42
,
43
,
44
and NIH3T3 cells,
45
as well as in cultured rat‐1 fibroblasts
46
was described. Dex also induced a rapid increase of
Per1
in bronchial epithelium and in peripheral blood mononuclear cells in vitro.
47
Yet that GC or Dex alters clock gene levels in peritubular cells of the testis was not known.
We next addressed the question, whether such a regulation in isolated cells may occur also in the testis of a NHP. In testis of
Cj
, data mining revealed strong GR expression by somatic cells (
https://apps.kaessmannlab.org/SpermEvol/
, Accessed February 21, 2024) and our immunohistochemical studies supported protein expression in testicular peritubular cells. We thus performed organotypic cultures of small fragments of
Cj
testes in medium without FCS. The short time point (6 h) was chosen to minimize potential culture artifacts. The results of subsequent qPCR analyses of samples from two animals showed strong responses of
PER1
and
BMAL1
, which were both elevated (2–3 times), yet in samples from a third animal no change at all was monitored.
These results imply that Dex can affect testicular clock genes in this systemic approach. Yet, as
Cj
is a species, in which 10–15 times higher cortisol level than in humans were described,
48
one may expect an overall down‐regulation of GR and therefore a generally blunted response. It may be possible that further, possibly stress‐related increases of cortisol in the third, non‐responsive animal, or other experimental conditions, may have downregulated the system even more.
Peritubular cells are dominant sites of GR expression in
Cj
testes, next to other somatic cells. These cells contribute only a small proportion of the overall testicular proteins. Bearing this in mind, it is not surprising that a proteomic analysis of potential Dex action, especially after 6 h, did not yield significant changes. If changes occurred in somatic cells, which express GR, they most likely are “diluted.”
To address this point, we studied TPCs from
Cj
. We previously characterized both primary and immortalized TPCs (iMKTPCs) from
Cj
,
20
,
25
which as we found retain the majority of the TPCs characteristics, resemble HTPCs and therefore are a highly translational model.
20
Furthermore, we found that primary TPCs respond to Dex by reduced cytokine expression, as documented earlier
2
and we now found that iMKTPCs also respond to Dex. When we added 1 µM Dex to these cells, we found that this treatment, as in organotypic incubations, significantly induced expression of
BMAL1
and
PER1
within 6 h. Hence, peritubular cells are involved in GC‐driven clock gene expression.
The results in HTPCs suggest rhythmically changing clock genes. Previous studies in mouse testis reported non‐rhythmical expression of clock genes, including
PER1
,
PER2
,
CRY1
, and
NPAS2
, and therefore concluded that the mouse testis lacks circadian rhythmicity. However, circadian clock genes are rhythmically expressed in extra‐testicular ducts and accessory organs of mice.
49
,
50
Since these reports appeared, evidence has accumulated indicating robust circadian clock genes and steroidogenic gene expression in mouse and goat Leydig cells.
51
,
52
,
53
A While we did not test endogenous cortisol for the ability to regulate clock genes, the clearcut actions of Dex, found in HTPCs, iMKTPCs and organotypic testes cultures, suggest a GC‐link between the central and a presumed testicular clock in primates.
Clearly, our study with human cells and NHP tissue and derived cells has additional limitations, with respect to sample size, observation time, and inter‐individual variability. Yet, in conclusion, we report that major circadian clock genes are expressed rhythmically in HTPCs. We show that Dex strongly affects key clock gene expression in HTPCs and iMKTPCs, as well as in testis tissue of an NHP. It is possible that this mode of action is involved in the manifold consequences of Dex on HTPCs,
1
,
2
,
3
which may manifest themselves later and hence may negatively influence the normal working of the male gonad. Thus, testicular consequences may occur, when Dex is used as medication and altered clock gene expression is likely a part of this.
At present time, it is not known whether, in particular, long‐term Dex medication may affect male fertility. Our results indicate that this topic has relevance for male health and therefore requires thorough investigation. The examination of the fertility status of men receiving GC‐treatment may be a first step.
