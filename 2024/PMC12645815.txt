Introduction
Parasites are divergent and highly specialized organisms that typically undergo complicated life cycles with sophisticated developmental programs to adapt to variant environments [
1
,
2
]. Many parasites possess sophisticated immune evasion strategies, including antigenic variation, which enable them to achieve persistent infection and still pose great challenges to global public health [
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
]. In‐depth explorations of parasite biology represent a crucial avenue for the development of effective control measures and strategies for disease intervention. Protein post‐translational modification (PTM) has emerged as a critical regulation that fine‐tunes protein properties and function, thereby regulating several key aspects of cellular biology and life processes [
7
,
8
,
9
]. Over the last 30 years, a large array of PTMs has been discovered [
10
]. Dynamic PTMs are mediated by specific enzymes to covalently bind or remove various chemical groups after protein synthesis, greatly increasing the complexity of organismal proteomes [
11
]. Several of the most studied types of PTM markers include acetylation, phosphorylation, and methylation, regulating a variety of critical cell activities, including gene expression, cell cycle, signal cascading, and energy metabolism [
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
].
With the availability of genome sequences and the tremendous progress of high‐throughput molecular analytic technologies, proteome‐wide surveys of PTMs in parasites have been achieved, including parasite‐specific markers. In addition to the investigation of PTMs on individual proteins, proteome‐wide explorations of interactions of various PTMs have highlighted the significance of PTM crosstalk [
16
,
17
]. PTMs in trypanosomatid parasites,
Toxoplasma gondii
, and
Plasmodium falciparum
has been more comprehensively studied (Figure
1
, Table
1
) [
17
,
18
,
19
].
Trypanosoma
parasites, the causing disease of sleeping sickness and Chagas disease, are life‐threatening pathogens with a worldwide distribution [
20
,
21
].
T. gondii
[
22
,
23
] and
P. falciparum
[
24
,
25
], the etiologic agent of zoonotic toxoplasmosis and the devastating disease malaria, are obligate intracellular apicomplexan parasites. PTMs have also been investigated to various degrees in other protozoa parasites and helminths, with findings indicating their involvement in numerous biological processes in these parasites [
26
].

In this review, we focus on PTMs that have been demonstrated to play important roles in parasite biology and how they orchestrate cellular processes, highlighting the epigenetic efforts of histone PTMs and their modulators.
2
Techniques Used in the Studies of PTMs
Most PTMs were first identified in histones, with the advent of improvements in proteomic and mass spectrometry (MS) methods, as well as sample preparation, enabling high‐throughput PTM‐omics identification, PTMs have been found to be widespread throughout the proteome [
34
,
35
,
36
]. Up‐to‐date, more than 400 PTMs have been identified with some exhibiting well‐defined functional roles across diverse life kingdoms. Most novel PTMs were detected and quantified employing robust MS‐based proteomics techniques (Figure
2
, Table
1
).
FIGURE 2
Flowchart illustrating the proteomic procedures for proteins and modified site identification. Parasite proteins were extracted and trypsinized. Peptides were subjected to various affinity enrichment strategies and separated by high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and analyzed by tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS). The figure created in BioRend. Zhang, N. (2024) https://BioRender.com/m15a830.
Traditionally, phosphorylation, the most distributed PTM, is analyzed by isotope labeling to phosphoproteins with radioactive signals that can be identified by protein separation, such as two‐dimensional gel electrophoresis or high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), to identify phosphorylated sites [
37
,
38
]. This approach, however, is time‐consuming and does not allow for high throughput analysis. Over recent years, there have been notable advancements in the enrichment methods of phosphorylated proteins [
19
,
39
]. These include the introduction of antibody‐based enrichment, immobilized metal affinity chromatography (IMAC), and metal oxide/hydroxide affinity chromatography. The combination of various enrichment methods and MS techniques has gradually become the main tool for studying protein phosphorylation.
Dissections of PTMs occurring on lysine residues, including acylations and ubiquitination, rely on the corresponding pan‐antibodies for enrichment [
40
]. Two non‐radioactive methods, biotin click [
41
] and lipid acyl biotin exchange (ABE) [
42
,
43
], have brought breakthroughs in palmitoylated protein identification, opening up more possibilities for the discovery of new palmitoylation sites in various cells and tissues.
Glycosylated proteins are identified mainly using MS‐based techniques combined with proteases as well as specific glycoside endonucleases [
44
]. The glycosylated proteins were initially identified through the observation of differences between their masses and that of the theoretical mass of the unmodified proteins. The modified sites were then defined by tandem MS, whereby the amino acid residue to which the glycosylation had occurred was determined. Hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography (HILIC) is a widely accepted technique for the separation of labeled
N
‐glycans, and the introduction of ultra‐high performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) has improved the separation process [
45
]. The enrichment of
O
‐glycosylated proteins is currently based on the use of modification‐specific antibodies [
19
].
3
Landscape of Proteome‐Wide PTM Identification in Parasites
This section focuses on studies of proteome‐wide PTM‐ome identification in variant parasites, and in‐depth functional investigations of key modified proteins and mediators are described in detail (Table
1
).
3.1
Protein Phosphorylation
Phosphorylation is one of the most common and pleiotropic regulatory mechanisms in cell biology, regulating a wide range of cellular functions including signal transduction, proliferation, and homeostasis [
46
]. Protein phosphorylation is regulated by kinases and phosphatases and can occur on a variety of amino acids, with serine (Ser, S) being the most abundant, followed by threonine (Thr, T), and the least common being tyrosine (Thy, Y).
In the bloodstream form (BSF)
T. brucei
, Nett et al. first revealed the presence of 491 phosphoproteins and 1204 phosphosites utilizing strong cation‐exchange (SCX) fractionation and TiO
2
chromatography [
47
], and then identified 34 phosphotyrosine‐containing proteins by using anti‐phosphotyrosine‐specific antibodies [
48
]. Further, SILAC (Superstable Isotope Labeling of Amino Acids in Cell Culture) labeling combined with SCX and TiO
2
enrichment quantified the phosphoproteome of two stages of
T. brucei
, BSF, and procyclin form (PCF), 8275 phosphosites were quantified out of 10,096 phosphorylation sites of 2551 proteins identified [
49
]. Another SILAC‐based quantitative analysis of
T. brucei
subjected to heat shock identified 193 heat shock‐responsive phosphorylation sites, a large number of which are enriched in RBPs related to thermotolerance [
50
]. In
T. cruzi
epimastigote, a total of 192 phosphosites on 119 phosphoproteins were identified by SCX fractionation and IMAC enrichment [
51
]. Employing TiO
2
chromatography, 753 phosphoproteins and 2572 phosphorylation sites were identified in
T. cruzi
[
52
]. Amastigogenesis and metacyclogenesis refer to the transition from trypomastigotes to amastigotes and from procyclic promastigotes to metacyclic promastigotes, respectively. A comprehensive quantitative proteomics and phosphoproteome of
T. cruzi
by in vitro mimicking amastigogenesis has identified a large number of membrane proteins, Ca
2+
transporters, and cAMP signaling pathways that are directly related to the amastigogenesis [
53
].
The phosphoproteome of
T. cruzi
was quantified during metacyclogenesis, revealing the presence of over 4000 protein groups and nearly 7400 phosphorylated sites [
54
]. Furthermore, SILAC was applied to further quantitatively analyze the PTMs at different experimental points along the nutrient stress stage for metacyclogenesis [
55
], and there were 4205 proteins and 4846 phosphosites identified, revealing that some regulated sites may trigger the occurrence of metacyclogenesis, such as fatty acid synthesis.
In
L. donovani
, phosphorylation plays a pivotal role in signal transduction pathways in mammalian hosts experiencing heat shock [
56
]. Morales et al. purified phosphoproteins in
L. donovani
by IMAC followed by MALDI‐MS and MS/MS, 221 phosphoproteins were identified [
57
]. They further analyzed the
L. donovani
dynamic phosphoproteome using a quantitative gel‐based approach, revealing that 38% of the proteins, including heat‐shock protein complexes and chaperone complexes specific to the infectious stage, showed significant stage‐specific differences [
37
]. Tsigankov et al. compared the phosphoproteins of
L. donovani
promastigotes and amastigotes by mimicking parasite differentiation and found 1614 phosphorylated sites of 627 proteins. The majority of the phosphosites are stage‐specific. They further revealed serine phosphorylation in a previously identified trypanosomatid‐specific “SF” motif was significantly enriched in amastigotes [
58
].
Phosphorylation in malaria parasites was initially studied only in the asexual stage of
P. falciparum
[
39
,
59
–
61
]. However, recent years have seen a shift toward a more comprehensive approach, with comparisons being drawn between multiple parasite stages [
18
,
62
]. Global kinomic and phospho‐proteomic analyses identified 1177 phosphosites on 650 proteins in
P. falciparum
[
60
]. Phosphorylation regulates a wide range of cellular activities, such as DNA synthesis, transcription, and metabolism, as well as key parasitic processes, including invasion and cell adhesion [
60
,
63
]. Another global quantitative phosphoproteomic analysis combining chemical and genetic tools identified 69 phosphorylated sites associated with protein kinase G (PKG) in
P. falciparum
[
64
]. They demonstrated that
Plasmodium
parasites utilize PKGs to regulate egress and invasion.
In
T. gondii
, iTRAQ‐based quantitative phosphoproteome of tachyzoites at the stages of “just invading” and “prior to egress” identified 42 phosphorylation sites in 38 phosphoproteins [
65
]. Phosphorylated proteins are significantly enriched on rhoptry neck proteins (RONs), rhoptry proteins (ROPs), and dense granule proteins (GRAs) and regulate the process in which parasites invade host cells [
39
]. One of the ways in which
T. gondii
regulates myosin‐driven processes is through calcium‐dependent phosphorylation of myosin A, a class XIVa family myosin motor protein [
66
]. Quantitative detection of calcium‐dependent phosphorylation demonstrates that the parasite motility is associated with calcium signaling and that the phosphorylation level of the parasite motility is likely modulated by multiple kinases [
67
]. Furthermore, by comparing the phosphoproteomes of tachyzoites and bradyzoites of
T. gondii
, many secreted proteins were found differentially phosphorylated [
68
].
Tyrosine phosphorylation in
Schistosoma mansoni
miracidia showed phosphorylation is correlated with the adaptation of the parasite to the host [
69
]. Reversible protein phosphorylation on the surface of the infective larvae of
Trichinella spiralis
catalyzed by ecto‐protein kinase and phosphatase, and was presumed to be involved in signal transduction of host cells [
70
]. A total of 3461 phosphorylation sites on 1758 phosphorylated proteins were identified in
Spirometra erinaceieuropaei
which caused sparganosis in humans [
71
]. The phosphorylation of specific proteins at different stages of the morphological transformation of
Trichomonas vaginalis
has been identified, elucidating the expression profile of phosphorylated proteins in the trophozoite, pseudocyst, and amoeboid stages [
72
].
3.2
Protein Methylation
Methylation (Met) is the process of catalytic transfer of methyl groups from reactive methyl compounds, such as S‐adenosylmethionine, to amino acid residues. Methylation occurs mainly on arginine and lysine, which is catalyzed by protein arginine methyltransferases (PRMTs) and lysine methyltransferases (KMTs), respectively [
73
].
Arginine methylation, which can undergo mono‐(Met1) and di‐methylation (Met2) is an important regulator of cellular and nuclear activity, mainly occurring in the cytoplasmic and nucleoproteins, and is associated with processes such as epigenetic and transcriptional regulation, RNA splicing and DNA damage response [
73
,
74
]. Lysine methylation, which can undergo Met1, Met2, and tri‐methylation (Met3), possesses an important role in the construction and maintenance of heterochromatin and euchromatin regions, and regulates protein function through two main mechanisms, interact with other PTMs and affect protein–protein interactions [
12
,
75
].
In
Trypanosoma
parasites, about 40 trimethylated proteins were identified in both
T. brucei
and
T. evansi
, the number is lower than any other kinds of PTMs [
19
]. However, arginine monomethylation accounts for approximately 5% of the
T. gondii
proteome, mainly occurring on DNA and RNA binding proteins, including Apetala 2 (AP2) transcription factors, suggesting that arginine methylation may play a role in the RNA biology and transcriptional regulation of the parasite [
76
]. Compared to a previous phosphoproteomics study, 90% of the arginine monomethylated proteins were also detected to be phosphorylated in
T. gondii
, which increases the possibility of interaction between monomethyl arginine (MMA) and phosphorylation [
76
]. In
P. falciparum
, lysine and arginine methylation has been shown to be widespread in proteins expressed at asexual blood stages [
77
,
78
].
3.3
Protein Acylations
3.3.1
Acetylation
Acetylation is one of the most prevalent PTMs, plays a central role in a myriad of variant biological processes [
79
,
80
]. The acetyl group is typically found in the
N
‐terminal regions of proteins, or the lysine
ε
‐amino group, where it functions to neutralize the positive charges [
13
,
81
]. Acetylation has been described in multiple parasites [
19
,
63
,
82
–
86
].
In
Trypanosoma
parasites, Moretti et al. analyzed different acetylation profiles in
T. cruzi
epimastigotes (EPI),
T. brucei
BSF, and
T. brucei
PCF [
85
].
T. brucei
BSF relies on glycolysis for energy supply in mammalian hosts, whereas PCF utilizes oxidative phosphorylation. These findings revealed a very distinct set of species‐specific acetylated proteins and that glycolytic enzymes have higher acetylation level ratios in PCF than in BSF parasites. Further, they found that higher levels of aldolase acetylation were observed in PCF cultured in the absence of glucose, which resulted in the inhibition of aldolase activity, reduction in substrate binding capacity, and alteration of the catalytic structure of the enzyme [
87
]. These findings confirm the role of acetylation in regulating enzyme activity in the glycolytic pathway in trypanosomes and highlight its importance for parasite metabolism. Our findings identified multiple histone hyperacetylated regions in both
T. brucei
and

More than 400 acetylated sites were identified in the proteins of intracellular tachyzoites of

There is an important correlation between the level of protein acetylation and parasite genotype‐associated virulence, as acetylation was predominantly identified in most virulent RH strain with 458 acetylated proteins, followed by PYS strain with 188 acetylated proteins, while only 115 acetylated proteins were detected in the PRU strain [

Acetylation of

The global comparative investigation of lysine acetylation in juvenile and adult

3.3.2
Crotonylation and 2‐Hydroxyisobutyrylation
Lysine crotonylation and 2‐hydroxyisobutyrylation are two widely distributed PTMs, which were first discovered on histone in 2011 and 2014, respectively [
34
,
35

By comparing ten PTMs in two species of trypanosomes, we found that crotonylation and 2‐hydroxyisobutyrylation were the most widespread PTM in trypanosomes [

3.3.3
Succinylation
Lysine succinylation has a greater impact on protein structure and function [

T. gondii

3.3.4
Malonylation
Malonyl‐CoA is a bridge between glycolysis and fatty acid synthesis, and malonylation is a novel acylation with malonyl‐CoA as the modification donor [

3.3.5
Palmitoylation
Palmitic acid is a long‐chain saturated fatty acid, an important component of various lipids that play an important role in cell membrane formation, signaling, and energy storage [
103

104

The main function of lipid‐based palmitoylation is to bind other soluble proteins on the cell membrane and to allow lipid‐modified proteins to be transported between different compartments of the cell. It is worth mentioning that palmitoylation is the only reversible lipid modification. Palmitoylation is mainly classified into
S
‐palmitoylation and
N
‐palmitoylation according to the linkage mode. ABE and metabolic labeling with a palmitic acid analog followed by click chemistry (MLCC) have been reported as two fundamentally distinct methods for the global purification of palmitoyl proteins [

106

Palmitoylation acts as a bioregulator in the parasite life cycle with an important role in parasite development, invasion, and drug resistance (Review in [
107
,
108

Plasmodium
parasites, palmitoylation enhances the hydrophobicity of proteins and contributes to their membrane binding and microtubule cytoskeleton integrity [
109
,
110

P. falciparum
schizonts, and dynamic palmitoylation is obviously necessary for the completion of its asexual life cycle and is essential for invasion and other virulence‐related events [
111

112

T. gondii
tachyzoites, mainly located in the cytoplasm and cell membrane, particularly affecting its motility and invasion [

A total of 363 palmitoylated proteins were identified in
T. vaginali

G. lamblia
[

3.3.7
Lactylation
Lactate, an important carbon‐containing metabolite of the cellular glycolytic pathway, plays an important role in the Warburg effect in tumor cells and a wide range of diseases [
36

122
,
123

Trypanosomes, the early branched protozoan parasites, have numerous cellular processes that are distinct from those of other eukaryotes including energy metabolic and gene expression regulation. Taking
T. brucei
as a model, to explore lactylated sites and their significance, it was found that lactylated proteins are widely involved in fundamental parasite biology, such as translation, carbohydrate metabolism, regulation of chromatin dynamics, DNA recombination, and repair [

It is worth noting that most of the enzymes in the glycolytic pathway, the only energy supply pathway of the pathogenic bloodstream stage, were lactylated, and the glycolysis rate was affected by the level of lactylation. In addition, both histones and gene regulators can undergo lactylation, which affects gene expression. In
T. gondii
, a total of 983 Kla sites on 523 lactylated proteins were identified in the tachyzoites, and a large number of these proteins are located in the nucleus and are thought to potentially regulate gene expression [

T. gondii

3.4
Protein Ubiquitination
Ubiquitin is a 76 amino acid polypeptide that binds to lysine residues to regulate cellular biological processes. The most important impact of ubiquitination is to achieve selective degradation of modified proteins. The protein to be degraded is recognized by ubiquitin ligases catalyzing the reaction by adding ubiquitin to the lysine residues of the target proteins, and which will be degraded by the proteasome. Ubiquitin is covalently bound to the target protein through a multi‐stage reaction of E1 (ubiquitin‐activating enzyme), E2 (ubiquitin‐binding enzyme), and E3 (ubiquitin ligase), forming a polyubiquitin chain that acts as a marker for protein hydrolase recognition, resulting in rapid breakdown of the target protein. The ubiquitin‐proteasome system can be used to control the lifespan of specific proteins, thereby regulating a variety of cellular functions such as cell division, DNA repair, immune defense, protein turnover, and intracellular transport.
In
P. falciparum
, ubiquitin proteome was detected in the parasites at all erythrocytic stages, implying that protein ubiquitination is closely associated with the parasite development [
128
]. We found that the erythrocyte α‐spectrin is selectively ubiquitylated after
P. falciparum
invasion, which was recognized and digested by the 26S proteasome, a process essential for intracellular development and egress of the parasite. This process is obviously regulated by the PI3K enzyme, which phosphorylates E3 ligase and the 26S proteasome subunit [
129
].
In
T. gondii
tachyzoites, almost 1000 ubiquitinated sites on more than 500 proteins have been identified, demonstrating its critical role in cell cycle transition, cell division, and host invasion [
130
]. Ubiquitination has also been detected on proteins such as cytoskeleton, intimal complex, and gliding‐associated protein (GAP) and may have an effect on the rearrangement of the cytoskeleton. Ubiquitin was also detected on the chromatin‐modifying proteins enzymes, GCN5‐b, and proteins interacting with GCN5‐b [
131
]. In addition, multiple ubiquitination sites were also found in the
Toxoplasma
histone and AP2 transcriptional regulators, which may have important regulatory effects on
Toxoplasma
transcription.
Furthermore, protein ubiquitination activity was observed in
Giardia intestinalis
trophozoites [
132
]. The findings revealed that during encystation and excystation, the expression of ubiquitin genes remained consistent, while there were variations in the transcriptional activity of the activating (E1), binding (E2), and ligase (E3) ubiquitin enzymes.
3.5
Ubiquitin‐Like Modifiers
Ubiquitin‐like modifiers (UBLs) encompass protein modifiers structurally and evolutionarily related to ubiquitin [
133
]. Small ubiquitin‐like modifier (SUMO) is involved in many cellular processes such as the regulation of the nuclear body [
134
]. SUMO is approximately 12 kDa and has a ubiquitin‐like structure, and its binding to the substrates also requires multi‐step reaction catalyzed by three enzymes [
134

Functions of SUMOylated proteins are primarily in the nucleus and are involved in events such as DNA replication and repair, chromatin structure, transcriptional regulation, cell cycle, mitosis, and signal transduction. In malaria parasites, SUMOylation plays a crucial role in the oxidative stress response and the interaction of two regulatory enzymes, E1 activating enzyme and E2 conjugating enzyme [
135
]. Additionally, SUMOylation regulates several other biological processes, such as gene expression, ubiquitination, and proteasomal pathways, in the malaria parasite [

Entamoeba histolytica
, protein SUMOylation is observed in pseudopodia, phagocytic channels, and surrounding the adhered and ingested erythrocytes [
137

3.6
Protein Glycosylation
Protein glycosylation is the process by which single sugar or glycans are covalent bound to residues of proteins [
138
]. The glycans in parasites are categorized into three distinct types: glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI), O‐linked glycans, and N‐linked glycans.
GPI is widely employed by eukaryotes for the anchoring of proteins to the cell surface, and is crucial for the development and survival of
P. falciparum
and trypanosomes [
139
,
140

Trypanosoma
parasites are exemplars in antigenic variation, where GPI‐anchored proteins shield trypanosomes from the host immune system, a process that evades clearance primarily through the expression of antigenically distinct variant surface glycoprotein (VSG) coats. Previous studies suggested that the immune evasion relies primarily on surface amino acid variation in VSG, but recent studies have shown that the variability of VSG includes surface PTM, such as glycosylation of VSG, in addition to amino acid sequence alterations [
142
,
143
]. An important contribution to protecting the barrier formation is the N‐glycan of VSG [
143
,
144
].
N‐glycosylation is the covalent attachment of sugar chains to asparagine residues of proteins, increasing their stability and function. In trypanosomes, N‐linked glycans reduce retarding intermolecular interactions, allowing membrane proteins to function properly even when densely packed [
145
]. N‐glycosylation of
T. brucei
proceeds in a specific manner by the action of different oligosaccharyltransferase (OST) activities [
146
]. Interfering with the pattern and accessibility of N‐glycans on VSGs by carbohydrate‐binding agent could be explored as novel avenues for the development of drugs to combat trypanosomiasis. In our PTM analysis of trypanosome flagellar proteins, we observed that N‐glycosylation exhibited a higher level in
T. brucei
than
T. evansi
, with two flagellar proteins occurring predominantly, paraflagellar rod component 16 and flagellar adhesion protein 2 [

]. Analysis of the
T. gondii
N‐glycosylated proteome revealed that N‐glycosylation is involved in gliding motility and other functions related to intracellular development, and through treatment experiments, it was found that N‐glycosylation may be involved in modifying key proteins for invasion [
147
]. In
P. falciparum
, N‐glycosylated proteins are present at lower abundance and are primarily associated with cellular localization and protein binding [
63
]. However, N‐glycosylation is pervasive in infected erythrocyte proteins, where it plays a crucial role in the complement response, the coagulation cascade, and immune reactions.
O‐glycosylation is a highly dynamic PTM regulated by ON and OFF enzymes (O‐GlcNAc transferase and O‐GlcNAcase) that can compete with phosphorylation [
148
,
149
]. O‐glycosylation involved in regulating signaling processes, affecting protein expression, degradation, and transport. The absence of O‐glycosylation on the mucin‐like domain of mucin‐like glycoproteins leads to a lack of the cyst wall rigidity in
T. gondii
, suggesting that O‐glycosylation may be involved in regulating the relevant structures of the cell wall [
150
]. In
P. falciparum
, the late trophozoites were found to contain 13 O‐glycosylated proteins, all of which are functionally important proteins involved in gene regulation, energy metabolism, stress response, and parasite survival [
149

].
3.7
Protein Nitrosylation
Protein S‐nitrosylation (SNO) is a key PTM on the cysteine thiols, induced by nitrosative stress [
151

The available evidence of SNO is identified in
P. falciparum
and

T. gondii
also play a role in several key biological functions, including protein translation, gene transcription, and the invasion and proliferation of the parasite within host cells [

].

The process of protein prenylation entails the covalent attachment of hydrophobic prenyl chains to cysteine residues of protein substrates In
P. falciparum

3.9
Protein Lipoxidation
Protein lipoxidation is a non‐enzymatic PTM in which reactive lipid species are covalently conjugated to the target proteins, which exhibits a marked elevation under oxidative stress [
157
]. In parasites, protein lipoxidation remains largely uninvestigated, but it is an emerging area of interest. The role of electrophilic lipid products, including 4‐hydroxynonenal (4‐HNE), malondialdehyde (MDA), and acrolein in malaria parasites and patients has been studied [
158

Malaria parasites digest erythrocyte hemoglobin during intraerythrocytic development and polymerize hemozoin (HZ), which comes into close contact with the membrane of the parasite's digestive vacuole, thereby causing lipid peroxidation. Isolated HZ catalyzes the peroxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids with subsequent degradation to 4‐HNE and other products. 4‐HNE‐protein conjugation was quantitatively analyzed in both
Plasmodium
‐infected erythrocytes [
158
] and in host cells [
159
,
160

PTM Mediators in Parasites
4.1
Kinases and Phosphatases
Phosphorylation is catalyzed by protein kinase, which transfers phosphoryl groups to specific amino acid residues of the substrate proteins, while its reverse process is removed by protein phosphatase. It is the opposite function of these two enzymes, and the energy consumption and generation involved, that makes phosphorylation the primary modality of regulation for many physiological activities [
162

However, a number of inhibitors of protein kinases and phosphatases have now been found to inhibit parasite activity and are promising candidates for antiparasitic drugs [

164

A total of 39 serine‐ and threonine‐specific phosphatases (STPs) have been identified in humans, while a greater number are present in kinetoplastid parasites. The trypanosome genome encodes for more than 150 protein kinases, but only approximately 80 protein phosphatases [
165
,
166

,
168

T. bruc

T. cruzi

P. falciparum

,

,
173

Plasmodium
and mammals. Protein kinases are frequently autophosphorylated on regulatory residues, including tyrosines in PfCLK3 activation loop, and have been shown PfCLK3 Y526 is essential for full kinase activity [

P. falciparum
PKG is a signaling hub that regulates protein production and parasite invasion [

5
PTMs in Epigenetic Regulation and Gene Expression
A majority of parasites have complicated life cycles with distinct morphological and developmental characteristics, which necessitates sophisticated regulatory mechanisms. Histones undergo a diverse array of covalent PTMs and along with the replacement of histone variants, which has been identified as a molecular link between genome architecture, DNA accessibility, and antigenic variation, comprise the “histone code” which is a crucial regulatory mechanism of gene expression in parasitic microorganisms [
207
,
208
]. PTMs have been observed to alter chromatin structure, thereby playing a pivotal role in epigenetic regulation of gene expression in protozoan parasites [
209
]. The protein components of chromatin and their covalent PTMs relay crucial information to the cell about the underlying DNA sequences instead of simply being packaging proteins for compacting DNA [
208
].
Histones are small, basic proteins that form an octamer with two copies of canonical histone proteins (H2A, H2B, H3, and H4), which then assemble together. Although the canonical histones are very well conserved in many parasites, including the Apicomplexa, the histones of trypanosomes are quite divergent, probably reflecting their unique mechanisms of gene regulation [
198
]. In addition to the canonical histone proteins, several variant histones are present in eukaryotes that may be replaced with the canonical core histone.
P. falciparum
lacking H2A.X, the eukaryotic H2A histone variants [
210
].
Histone PTMs can alter the nucleosomes conformation as well as specific binding sites of the transcription factor complex, thereby altering the ordered recruitment of the RNA polymerases, chromatin structures, and DNA accessibility (Figure
3
) [
209
,
211
]. The PTM state indicates whether chromatin is repressive or activated toward transcription. Modified histones can delineate the boundaries between promoter regions, gene coding regions, intergenic space, and DNA strand breaks. These marks can also be used to distinguish between active and silent genes. It is frequently observed that multiple PTMs act in concert to affect gene expression. We found that more PTMs occurred in histone tails than in globular domains, and variant histones are less conserved than core histones in trypanosomes [
19
]. Dimethylation and trimethylation on H3K76, regulated by DOT1A and DOT1B, were identified in both
T. brucei
,
T. evansi
, and
T. cruzi
, but H3K4me3, described as the most conserved histone modification in other eukaryotes, was consistently unidentified in trypanosomes [
28
]. In
P. falciparum
, the serine 121 of the canonical H2A is phosphorylated which is a maker of damaged DNA foci [
210
]. However, in
T. gondii
, TgH2A.X is associated with DNA damage [
212
]. Lysine acetylation of Histone 3 (H3KAc) of
T. vaginalis
is enriched in nucleosomes surrounding the transcription start site of the active gene, thereby regulating transcription and pathogenesis in this eukaryotic parasite [
213

Amoebae use actin‐rich membrane extensions called pseudopodia, whereas kinetoplastid are propelled by flagella. Host–Parasite Interactions
The majority of parasites exhibit intricate life cycles, undergoing a multitude of developmental stages and transformations in diverse host organisms. To continuously adapt to new environments and resist stress, parasites manipulate the host cells, for example, by down‐regulating immune pathways and reorganizing subcellular structure of host cells. PTMs of secreted protein, surface antigens, microneme family members, GRAs, and ROPs play a pivotal part in a number of essential processes for the parasites, including host cell attachment and invasion, gliding motility and adhesion, intracellular survival.
Parasites induce PTMs of proteins that are secreted into the host cell as well as native host proteins. In

Concluding Remarks
PTMs modulate a wide range of cellular functions, playing an important role in nearly every aspect of cell biology. This modulation can be induced or removed in response to changes in the cellular environment and state. As a result, PTMs are predicted to be key regulators of parasite developmental transitions, biology, and pathogenicity. Technically, the analysis of large datasets requires the implementation of rapid approaches for the interrogation of extensive gene cohorts, such as proteomics datasets for the identification of protein subcellular localization or the discovery of interaction partners within larger complexes. Recent studies have shown that 4‐D proteomics adds a fourth dimension, ion mobility which can improve the protein coverage, detection cycle, and data accuracy of the identified proteins. In the future, with the emergence of new technologies, it is expected that more PTM types can be identified, thus providing a comprehensive and dynamic spectrum of the parasite‐host PTM interactions, and laying the foundation for better analysis of parasite biology.
PTMs require a series of regulatory enzymes for positive and negative reversible regulation, including writers, erasers, and readers, which may be important targets for the development of antiparasitic drugs. These modifiers represent a promising avenue of research for the treatment of numerous human diseases, including cancer. Most studies on PTMs are still focused on bioinformatics analysis, but future studies should in‐depth analyze the biological functions of PTMs and validate the functions of important modified proteins, utilizing reverse genetics to validate the functions of crucial modified proteins and reveal the exact roles of PTMs in parasites. In addition, more multi‐omics approaches, including transcriptomics, proteomics, PTMs, and metabolomics, should be used to conduct studies on parasites and their interactions with host cells, a more comprehensive understanding of parasite biology, and defining the concrete drug targets. Furthermore, PTMs of transcription factors, RNA‐binding proteins, ribosomal proteins, and other relevant factors should be a primary focus, as these have been linked to the formation of macromolecular complexes, heterochromatin assembly, RNA characterization, translation efficiency, and ultimately, gene expression. As our understanding of the significance of parasite epigenetic gene regulation improves, this field of study will undoubtedly inform and enhance current antiparasitic drug development efforts.
