Background
The use of pesticides in agriculture poses a significant environmental and societal challenge in crop production. Grapevine (
Vitis vinifera
L.) is highly susceptible to a plethora of pathogens, making the viticulture industry one of the largest consumers of pesticides compared to other agricultural food crops.
Downy mildew (DM) is caused by the obligate biotrophic oomycete pathogen
Plasmopara
(
P.
)
viticola
(Berk. & M.A. Curtis; Berl & De Toni), which has a complex life cycle significantly dependent on environmental conditions and the host plant [
1
,
2
]. DM stands as one of the most devastating grapevine diseases worldwide, and its control requires high amounts of fungicides. About 7 to 15 applications of synthetic fungicides during each season [
3
] are used to ensure sustainable yields and quality within the wine industry. Therefore, there is a growing demand for ‘natural’ alternatives that can safeguard consumer and producer health, soil and environmental integrity and biodiversity. This requires continuous efforts within the industry and scientific community to explore organic alternatives. Current main sustainable approaches used in viticulture are the use of disease resistant cultivars and organic farming [
4
]. Both strategies have, however, several drawbacks such as the breakdown of resistances [
5
], the lack of consumer acceptance and the lowering of the wine quality of resistant cultivars. The critical point of organic farming is the high dependency on copper and sulphur-based fungicides that have lower efficiencies and rainfastness than synthetic ones. This increases the number of required treatments per season, in some very rainy years by 100%, questioning its sustainability.
Examples of organic alternatives range from essential oils (EOs) [
6
–
12
] and volatile organic compounds (VOCs) [
13
]; to chitosan [
14
], grape trunk and root extracts [
15
]. The EOs from the
Lamiaceae
family possess a unique chemical composition, including carnosoic acid, carnosol, rosmarinic acid, carvacrol, thymol, and p-cymene [
16
]. These components contribute to the antioxidant and fungicidal properties of EOs towards various crop pathogens and pests, proposing a potential solution for sustainable pest management [
9
]. Contact and volatile phases of EOs from some
Lamiaceae
plants, like the
Origanum vulgare
and
Thymus vulgaris
, exhibited inhibitory efficacy against the development of the fungus
Botrytis cinerea
, impacting early fungal developmental stages and causing alterations in hyphal morphology in a dose-dependent manner [
12
]. Oregano essential oil vapour (OEOV), used against
P. viticola
, demonstrated a 5-fold reduction in the pathogen’s sporulation when priming (P; 24 h pre-inoculation) or treating (T; just after inoculation) the leaf discs, in respect to the control. Moreover, a 20-fold reduction was observed when priming and treatment were applied together (PT). As the sporangia treated with OEOV did not show any impairing capacity for subsequent infection, the antimicrobial effect of OEOV at this
P. viticola
stage was ruled out [
7
]. This observation suggests an induction of the plant defence response capable of restricting pathogen development.
RNA-seq experiments have been employed to elucidate molecular mechanisms associated with the potential triggering of the grapevine defence system by OEOV [
7
,
8
]. Hormonal interactions, uncovering the involvement of jasmonic acid, ethylene, and salicylic acid biosynthesis, along with the corresponding signalling cascades have been shown to be involved. Furthermore, an up-regulation of genes linked to the phenylpropanoid pathway and pathogen recognition was observed [
7
,
8
].
Transcriptomic analyses have been widely used to unveil regulatory molecular networks. However, it presents limitations in capturing changes across all molecules within a cell, as it misses post-transcriptional regulations and molecule biosynthesis [
17
]. Proteomics analyses are also invaluable in assessing the impacts of pathogen interactions in grapevine [
18
,
19
]. However, proteome annotations often fall short of offering a comprehensive overview when compared to transcriptomic strategies. The integration of metabolomics to transcriptomics can further enhance the analysis, as the metabolome is more closely aligned with the actual phenotype, providing a more complete and insightful perspective [
20
] allowing to include small molecules (50–1500 Da) [
21
]. This type of dual omics approach has been used before to provide a comprehensive understanding of grapevine defence molecular responses induced by various treatments such as VOCs, plant extracts, organic compounds [
13
,
22
,
23
].
In this context, to complement the transcriptomic insights of the OEOV priming and treatment effects in grapevine leaf discs [
7
], we carried out untargeted metabolomics to capture a more comprehensive set of endophenotypic details. With this extension of the analysis, we aimed to incorporate metabolites and pathways that might not have been identified with transcriptomics alone. This approach aims to get a better understanding of the changes induced by priming and treating grapevine leaf discs against DM with OEOV, thereby shedding light on previously overlooked aspects of plant defence mechanisms against
P. viticola
.
Results
Untargeted metabolomics reveals clear shifts in the grapevine metabolome
To deepen and complement the insights obtained in the time series experiment of Vigneron et al., 2023, a metabolomic analysis was performed on the same samples used from the previously published transcriptomic study [
7
] (Fig.
4
A). These samples were inoculated with
P. viticola
spores and treated with OEOV as follows: (1) plant-priming (P; 24 h pre-inoculation), (2) direct treatment (T; just after inoculation), or (3) plant-priming and treatment combined (PT). Subsequently, metabolomic analysis was performed on the leaf discs at different time points (24 h post-inoculation (hpi), 48 hpi, and 72 hpi) to screen for differentially expressed metabolites (Supplementary Fig. S
1
).
Untargeted metabolomics was performed using LCMS/MS from ethanol extracts, profiling semipolar leaf metabolites, especially stress-responsive compounds (see
Methods
section). Processing and curation of 22,646 raw metabolomics signals yielded 5916 metabolomic features (removal of blanks, SN > 10, CV QC < 20%), of which 2620 were unknowns (or unknown features which means there is no match to MS1 or MS2), 3045 were proposed annotated metabolites (match to MS1 only, i.e., level 3 MS ID), hereafter designated as putative metabolites, and 251 matched identified metabolites (match to MS1 and MS2, i.e., level 2 MS ID). Of the 5000 most significant features (interquartile range) standardised to the exact dry weight (DW) of the sample and then normalised prior to statistical analyses (median normalisation, cube root transformation, Pareto scaling), 2447 and 2067 were statistically significant (ANOVA with adjusted FDR correction) at
P
< 0.05 and
P
< 0.01, respectively. This indicated a good robustness of the metabolomic dataset with a clear phytochemical diversity shift.
To determine the time point at which metabolome orchestration was highest, primarily a binary Principal Component Analysis (PCA) of metabolome features between non-inoculated (C) and inoculated control (Ci) (no OEOV application) samples at 24, 48, and 72 hpi was performed (Fig.
1
A). PC1 vs. PC2 clearly separated the two conditions at 48 and 72 hpi, with an overall variance of 65 and 62.3%, respectively.

Interestingly, separation by metabolites of C and Ci was not possible at 24 hpi (total variance of 77.9%). To obtain quantitative information on the metabolomic shifts induced by DM infection, volcano plots were generated showing the statistical (
P
< 0.05, FDR-corrected) vs. biological significance (fold change, FC > 1.5) of the modulated metabolites between C and Ci conditions at each time point (Fig.
1
B). In response to inoculation, no metabolic marker showed differential accumulation at 24 hpi, while 160 accumulated and 319 decreased at 48 hpi, and 1 accumulated and 2 decreased at 72 hpi. This indicated that the major shifts in global metabolic profiles occurred at 48 hpi, the time point at which further chemometric analyses were performed.
The combination of priming and treatment induces the greatest metabolomic changes
The PCA at 48 hpi revealed that all conditions impacted metabolome profiles along PC1 (63.8%), with an overall variance of PC1 vs. PC2 of 75.1% (Fig.
2
A). To examine metabolic markers at 48 hpi, Volcano plots (
P
< 0.05, FDR corrected, FC > 1.5) for binary comparisons between the Ci condition and the treated conditions (P, T, and PT) were used to display increased or depleted metabolic markers in response to each condition (Fig.
2
B). Based on these differentially accumulated metabolic markers, Venn diagrams were constructed to visualise the specific and overlapping markers of each condition (Fig.
2
C). Interestingly, 525 metabolic markers were commonly accumulated in all conditions, and 291 were depleted. Fourteen metabolic markers specifically accumulated in response to P, 1 to T, and 48 to PT, while 77 markers specifically depleted in response to P, 13 to T, and 314 to PT (Fig.
2
C). These results suggested that all conditions (priming, treatment, and combined priming and treatment with OEOV) shared a large part of their metabolic response, but priming against DM seemed to be more critical than treating the already inoculated leaf discs. Nevertheless, PT treatment appeared instigating the most drastic shift in the plant metabolome.

Metabolomic analysis of grapevine leaf discs treated with OEOV
General metabolic alterations at 48 hpi across various treatments can be visualised in a comprehensive manner with a heatmap encompassing all metabolic markers (Fig.
3
A). Here, metabolites were grouped through hierarchical clustering based on pairwise Pearson correlation. A consistent pattern of metabolic depletion induced by OEOV was observed when compared to the control (Fig.
3
A).

To gain a better insight into the metabolism, putative metabolic markers were annotated based on Classy Fire [
24
] (Supplementary Table S
1
). Among the 525 accumulated metabolic markers common to all treatments, the most frequently found metabolites were potential alkaloids, including harmala alkaloids, benzenoids such as anilides, benzenesulfonamides, derivatives of benzoic acids, fluorenes, and phenols. Additionally, prevalent markers included lignan glycosides, lipids such as fatty acyls, glycerolipids, sesquiterpenoids, terpene glycosides, and saccharolipids. Organoheterocyclic compounds such as ketones, benzodiazepines, piperidines, and quinolines, and phenylpropanoids like cinnamic acids, coumarins, flavonoids, isoflavonoids, chalcones and tannins were also notable. Out of these 525 markers, 206 compounds remained unknown. In contrast, of the 291 depleted metabolic markers common to all treatments, the most abundant depleted markers belong to the phenylpropanoid pathway such as 2-arylbenzofuran flavonoids, cinnamic acids, coumarins, flavonoids, isoflavonoids, and stilbenes; but also, benzenoids such as derivatives of benzene and phenols; and organoheterocyclic compounds such as azoles, benzopyrans, quinolines, and derivatives. Seventy-one depleted metabolic markers remained unknown.
Under the P condition, the metabolic responses were less diverse. The most frequently depleted markers were lipids, amino acids, terpenes, and phenols, including cinnamic acids, coumarins, flavonoids, and bioflavonoids. Very few metabolic markers accumulated during the priming condition, but among these markers, some harmala alkaloids, lignans, fatty acyls, prenol lipids, and some phenols, including isoflavonoids and stilbenes, were identified.
The metabolic markers specific to the T condition were very few and mostly depleted. In between, some phenols, lignan glycosides, glycerolipids, coumarins, and flavonoids were found. The PT condition showed a large number of depleted metabolites such as benzenoids, organoheterocyclic compounds, and some phenols such as coumarins, flavonoids, phenylpropanoic acid, isoflavonoids, and stilbenes. Nevertheless, some benzenoids, prenolipids, organoheterocyclic compounds, and some phenols showed accumulation.
Overall, the metabolomics analysis revealed a dynamic readjustment of both primary (i.e., lipids, amino acids) and secondary (i.e., phenols, terpenes) metabolic pathways. Intriguingly, it appears that most of the metabolic readjustment is shared among all treatments. However, among the treatment-specific changes, PT exhibits the most pronounced metabolic shift, followed by P and then T (Fig. 3B).
Metabolomics as a complementary analysis to transcriptomics
To find the crosstalk between the present metabolomic analysis with the previously performed transcriptomics, the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) at 24, 48 and 72 hpi, that were differentially expressed across all treatments (P, T, and PT) were selected. Then these DEGs were filtered based on their putative roles in metabolic pathways (Supplementary Fig. S
2
). The remaining DEGs selected were categorised in order to comprehend additional events in the cells that occur in response to OEOV. Genes related to cell cycle, molecular transporters, auxin, abscisic acid, ethylene hormone pathways, as well as other pathways involving pathogen recognition and heat shock response appeared (Supplementary Fig. S
3
).
Next, the accumulated or depleted metabolic markers common to all three treatments (P, T, and PT) at 48 hpi were analysed. The highest matched modulation of transcript and metabolite was found at 24 hpi for transcriptomic data. The difference of time points between dataset could be explained by numerous factors including the slower pace of metabolic changes; since at 24 hpi, no metabolic shifts could be perceived (Fig.
1
A, B).
Comparison of the selected DEGs related to metabolic pathways and associated metabolites revealed correlation for the glutathione metabolism, stilbene metabolism, flavonoid metabolism, terpene indole alkaloid synthesis, carbohydrate metabolism, lipid metabolism, and phenylpropanoid metabolism [
7
] (Fig.
4
C). The study focused mainly on secondary metabolites, as these compounds play major roles in plant defence.

In many cases, the pattern of expression was conserved between DEGs and metabolite markers. For example, probable and putative stilbenes metabolite markers were depleted congruently with the down-regulation of stilbene synthases genes (STS) (Fig.
4
B, D). Similarly, glutathione S-transferases (GSTU), which are dependent on glutathione, were up-regulated (Fig.
4
B), and putative L-glutathione metabolite was consistently depleted across the three treatments (Fig.
4
D). A similar trend was observed with phosphofructokinase and putative carbohydrates (Fig.
4
B, D).
The 4-coumarate-CoA ligase (4CL) is an important enzyme involved in the formation of intermediate products of the phenylpropanoid pathway. Different 4CLs metabolise different compounds and in the transcriptomic analysis, two 4CLs appeared: one up-regulated and one down-regulated (Fig.
4
B). Examining the metabolites, putative phenylpropanols were both accumulating and depleting (Fig.
4
D), suggesting a more general shift in the metabolic pathway.
In addition, the anthocyanidin 3-O glucosyltransferase and a presumable chalcone synthase (CHS), both related to the flavonoid metabolism, appeared up and down-regulated respectively in the transcriptomic analysis. Putative and probable flavonoid metabolites were mainly found depleted (Fig.
4
B, D). As for desacetoxyvindoline 4-hydroxylase (involved in terpene alkaloid synthesis), no related secondary metabolites were found (Fig.
4
B). Overall, these results show that in some cases, a coherent match between transcript and metabolite dynamics is conceivable, but not always.
Finally, the remaining non-DEGs-related secondary metabolites that were significantly accumulated or depleted after OEOV were examined. Putative lignans, terpenes, and alkaloids mainly appeared accumulated, while putative benzenoids exhibited both depletion and accumulation, providing other potential candidates for understanding the mechanisms of OEOV action (Supplementary Fig. S
4
).
Discussion
In the present research, the effects of OEOV priming and/or treatment on grapevine leaves infected with DM were studied at a metabolomic level. The data revealed that the time window for metabolite accumulation occurs at 48 hpi. Additionally, the OEOV causes most drastic shift in the plant’s metabolome when used as PT. However, priming alone was shown to be more effective in shifting the metabolome than only treating the plants. Here, the metabolomic results confirm previously described pathways found in transcriptomic analyses [
7
,
8
] and reveal some new metabolites that might also play a role in the protection granted by OEOV to
V. vinifera
L. cv. Chasselas plants against
P. viticola
infection.
Carbohydrate accumulation
This study reported an up-regulation of DEGs related to carbohydrate metabolism and a significant increase in putative carbohydrates (Fig.
4
B-D). Here, probable D-psicose, a specific carbohydrate that has shown promise as a priming agent in rice [
25
], was found accumulated. Therefore, it would be intriguing to explore its role and potential in
V. vinifera
.
In general, elevated carbohydrate levels have been previously described to occur after
P. viticola
inoculation [
26
]. There is currently several hypothesis surrounding this type of event; for example, it might be caused by the pathogen hijacking the plant’s metabolism to feed itself or might be associated with a reallocation of carbon resources of the plant (Fig.
5
), providing a pool of precursors for the biosynthesis of phenylpropanoids and flavonoids [
26
,
27
].

Phenylpropanoid pathway readjustment
Phenylpropanoids, a family of secondary metabolites, play a crucial role in various plant responses to both biotic and abiotic stresses [
28
]. Derived from phenylalanine in the shikimate pathway, it produces cinnamic acid, initiating a directed metabolic flux that branches into the production of various phenylpropanoid-derived compounds [
29
]. Distinct 4-coumarate-CoA ligases (4CL) catalyze the conversion of their preferred phenylpropanoid compounds to CoA [
30
,
31
]. In the case of grapevines, 4CL gene was up-regulated after OEOV priming and/or treatment, while 4CL2 was down-regulated [
7
] (Fig.
4
B). Interestingly, some 4CL substrates, such as probable coumaric acid glycoside, caffeic acids and caffeic acid glycoside appear mainly depleted (Supplementary Tables S
1
and S
2
), indicating a readjustment of phenylpropanoid metabolism.
Several products and sub-products derived from the phenylpropanoid pathway undergo significant changes after vines are either primed and/or treated with OEOV. Stilbenes have been documented to accumulate in infected cells of artificially mildew-infected leaf discs in various cultivars; therefore, stilbenes were suggested to play a role in plant defence against pathogens [
32
,
33
]. The RNA-seq data of Vigneron et al. obtained from the same biological samples as the present metabolomic data (Fig.
4
A), show a down-regulation of STS transcripts (Fig.
4
B) which matched the depletion of probable stilbene metabolites such as Ɛ-viniferin and astringin (Fig.
4
D). In another study featuring non-targeted metabolomics, stilbenes showed no difference in DM resistant or susceptible Vitis genotypes at a constitutive level [
34
] and in the resistant Caucasian
V. vinifera
L. cv. Mgaloblishvili [
35
]. Therefore, stilbene synthesis/regulation may not be crucial for certain form of resistance toward DM. Furthermore, it is important to note that the study of Maia et al., 2020 was conducted on whole vines as opposed to our study which could be the reason for the observed differences. Similarly, the study of Toffolatti et al., 2018 which was conducted as well on leaf discs of resistant vine varieties, presented several differences in its defence mechanisms that could also explain the differences we observe in the phenylpropanoid pathway.
Another sub product affected in the phenylpropanoid pathway is chalcone, a derivative of coumaroyl-CoA and three malonyl Co-A molecules, catalysed by CHS [
36
]. Congruent with the transcriptomic data indicating down-regulation of a presumably CHS (Fig.
4
B) after OEOV treatment, the metabolomic data reveals mainly a depletion of probable and putative chalcone-related metabolites (Fig.
4
D) including flavonoid and associated glycoside derivatives [
37
]. On one hand, flavonoids and associated derivatives are known antioxidant and phytoalexins that have been documented to accumulate following infection [
38
]. Further investigation should be done to understand the depletion of flavonoid observed in our study. Tannins, on the other hand, are a diverse group of polyphenolic compounds found in various plant tissues and, have been described to protect grapevine against fungal pathogens. Tannins work by interacting with proteins and other macromolecules, inhibiting enzyme activity, chelating essential metals, inducing protein precipitation and disrupting cell membrane integrity [
39
].
The metabolomic analysis also revealed an accumulation of putative lignans, indicating their production along the phenylpropanoid pathway [
40
,
41
]. Lignans are known to be important in plant defences, showing toxicity to fungal pathogens by inhibiting essential enzymes and altering transduction pathways, thereby delaying their growth, and inducing plant defence-related genes [
42
–
44
]. Lignans were also shown to be deposited to strengthen plant secondary cell walls, thus preventing the pathogen spread [
41
,
45
,
46
]. Since both putative lignans and putative tannins appear to be significantly accumulated after OEOV priming and/or treatment, this suggests that they could be part of a pathogen defence response, hindering infection by
P. viticola
(Fig.
5
).
Terpenes and their signalling cues
Terpenes and terpenoids, characterised by their lipophilic nature and low molecular weight, possess the capability to disrupt cell membranes, leading to cell death or inhibition of sporulation of fungi and other pathogens [
10
].
Terpenes are recognised for their antimicrobial properties by damaging the pathogen’s cell membrane and influencing its morphology [
8
,
47
]. The metabolomic profiling revealed the presence of 2 probable terpene glycosides and 23 putative sesquiterpenes and monoterpenes following 48 hpi of priming and/or treatment, suggesting that they might be an important part of the response produced by OEOV.
Sesquiterpenes and monoterpenes accumulation after
P. viticola
inoculation was previously reported in the resistant American hybrids, SO4 and Kober 5BB that are usually used as rootstocks, but not in Pinot noir [
48
]. Additionally, the
P. viticola
resistant grapevine variety Mgaloblishvili, was shown to produce terpenes, and four of them (farnesene, nerolidol, ocimene and valencene) have been reported to be implicated in the reduction of the severity of the disease by affecting the pathogen sporulation, making its reproductive structures appear short and sterile [
49
]. The study, nevertheless, proposed that volatile organic compounds like terpenes are likely to work in a blend rather than alone to establish resistance [
49
]. This suggests that terpenes may constitute an inherent grapevine response to
P. viticola
(Fig.
5
).
Volatile terpenes can be produced by plants as a response to insects and pathogens [
50
,
51
]. Monoterpenes and sesquiterpenes are well-known communication molecules in plant–insect interactions and plant-to-plant signalling cues. Upon being perceived, specific volatile terpenes can sensitise distinct signalling pathways in receiver plant cells, which in turn trigger plant innate immune responses, influencing each other through hormonal crosstalk modulating SA and JA signalling [
52
]. It would be interesting to study if the oregano essential oil simulates a plant-to-plant signalling cue via terpenes, which triggers and immune response against
P. viticola
in
V. vinifera
L. cv. Chasselas plants.
Alkaloids production
The metabolomic data highlighted a significant accumulation of putative harmala alkaloids, particularly at 48 hpi. Among the 10 putative metabolites, 6 were annotated as putative ketones. In Arabidopsis, toxic ketones have been associated with stress responses and have been identified as constituents of cuticular wax [
53
–
55
]. In grapevines, aldehydes, ketones, and alcohols have been found in leaves and their cuticular wax [
56
,
57
]. Future studies could potentially explore whether OEOV affects cuticular wax formation and composition, the production of ketones in grapevine under stress conditions, and how this might affect
P. viticola
infection.
Oxidative stress response
Transcriptomic data [
7
] revealed an up-regulation of respiratory burst (RBOHs) (Supplementary Fig. 2), which is primarily responsible for ROS production in response to stress [
58
–
61
]. Additionally, other DEGs related to ROS in the transcriptomic data, including
GSTU8
,
GSTU7
, and
GSTU22
, appeared up-regulated (Fig.
4
A). These GSTUs are glutathione-dependent enzymes with diverse cellular functions in plants, including detoxification of electrophilic xenobiotics and peroxides in response to oxidative stress [
62
–
64
]. Consistently, a depletion of the putative L-glutathione metabolite was found after P, PT and T (Fig.
4
B). In recent studies in
Camellia sinensis
, the
CsGSTU8
expression has be characterised for scavenging for excess ROS under drought conditions [
65
]. GSTU7, on the other hand, has been characterised in
Arabidopsis thaliana
for reducing oxidative damage caused by the herbicides [
66
,
67
]. These results suggest that during the interaction with
P. viticola
, OEOV may induce an additional plant stress, triggering an oxidative stress response (Fig.
5
).
Response against pathogens
Pathogen-related 4 (PR-4), chitinase A, and acidic endochitinase ChiB1 demonstrated up-regulation following the priming and/or treatment with OEOV (Supplementary Fig. S
3
, Fig.
5
). PR-4 has been shown to have an antifungal activity through its chitinase, RNase or DNase function [
68
–
71
]. In grapevines, the identified
VvPR4b
encodes a chitinase II-like protein, and knockout lines exhibited more extensive and rapid
P. viticola
colonization on leaf discs [
72
]. How chitinases affect
P. viticola
cell wall integrity remains thus to be elucidated.
The multi-layered plant immune response encompasses various modules from pathogen recognition to biosynthesis of phytoalexin which are metabolite potentially toxic to the pathogen. Two probable benzenoids metabolites, 6-pentyl-2 H-pyran-2-one and barbatic acid were found to accumulate. These metabolites exhibit antimicrobial properties in other pathosystems [
73
]. However, their role in
V. vinifera
and
P. viticola
interaction is currently unknown. Understanding whether these probable metabolites are phytoalexins could lead to the development of new strategies for managing
P. viticola
in grapevines.
Conclusion
Focusing on leaf discs rather than whole plant leaves, this study compares transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses to provide insights into the molecular responses of
V. vinifera
L. cv. Chasselas to OEOV treatment against DM. While leaf discs are a useful controlled system for understanding transcriptional and metabolomic responses, they do not fully replicate the complexity of whole plants in natural field environments. Yet, leaf discs are a good way to understand and select treatments to use later in the field without huge economic implications. Because whole plants face more complex environmental and biological interactions that could impact treatment efficacy, future studies should be made to validate the OEOV application under field conditions, exploring optimal dosages, impact in the plant´s health, application frequency, and efficacy in different weather conditions and soil health.
The findings in this study uncover a complex interaction among metabolic pathways, particularly highlighting the phenylpropanoid pathway, oxidative stress regulation, and the biosynthesis of organic compounds such as terpenes and potential phytoalexins. The results suggest that OEOV has the potential to induce natural defence mechanisms in grapevines, whether applied as a priming agent or a direct treatment. Of particular interest is the confirmed priming effect, offering growers a proactive approach to disease management that can be applied before infection, reducing the need for spraying during less optimal weather conditions such as rainy periods and wet soils. Future research may explore the feasibility of preventive priming applications using microcapsules or volatile dispensers.
In summary, this study provides novel insights into the mechanisms underlying grapevine defence priming induced by OEOV, highlighting newly identified metabolites. These findings lay the groundwork for further exploration of OEOV applications as alternatives to synthetic pesticides, enhancing our understanding of plant-pathogen interactions.
Methods
All the methods were performed in accordance with relevant institutional guidelines and regulations.
Plants, inoculation and treatment
Experimental methods are described in Vigneron et al. [
7
]. Briefly, the 2nd to 5
th
leaves from the apex of
V. vinifera
L. cv. Chasselas plants were cut to create leaf discs (Ø 1.1 cm) with a cork borer and placed in a Petri dish with wet paper filter. Leaf discs were produced the same day when leaves were collected.
For all leaf disc were taken from
Vitis vinifera
cv. Chasselas plants. For each condition, a total of 15 leaf discs (3 Petri dishes with 5 discs each) were pooled for one replicate, three replicates per condition were used. The lignified shoots with buds to produce cv. Chasselas (VIVC variety number 2473) plant cuttings were obtained from certified vineyards of the Agroscope clonal selection program (no voucher specimen of this material has been deposited in a publicly available herbarium; appropriate permission was obtained from the land owner to enter and pick the plant wood). The formal identification of the plant material used in our study was performed by Agroscope and we had permission to collect cv. Chasselas.

Metabolomics
Metabolomic analyses were commonly conducted to evaluate global phytochemical diversity in response to stresses [
74
,
75
]. Robotised high-throughput ethanol extractions were employed to obtain semi-polar metabolites from 10 mg of freeze-dried ground leaves, as previously described [
75
]. Untargeted metabolic profiling by UHPLC-high resolution mass spectrometry (LCMS) was performed as described previously [
74
,
76
] using an Ultimate 3000 ultra-high-pressure liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system coupled to an LTQ-Orbitrap Elite mass spectrometer interfaced with an electrospray (ESI) ionisation source (ThermoScientific, Bremen, Germany). Reverse-phase chromatographic separation, negative ionisation, and MS settings were detailed in a previous study [
76
]. The LCMS analytical sequence included 950 sample runs (610 unique biological samples, 167 technical duplicates, and 2 technical triplicates), 70 biological standards and 120 extraction blanks (prepared without plant material and used to rule out potential contaminants detected by untargeted metabolomics). Blanks were extraction blanks containing the exact same solvent proportion as the extracted samples, but without biological material (see Luna et al. [
75
]).
All sample runs were randomised with a satisfactory level of biological replication (
n
= 3). In addition, a Quality Control (QC) sample was prepared by mixing 20 µL from each sample and biological standard. QC samples were injected every 12 samples (108 in total) and used for untargeted metabolomics for (i) the correction of signal drift during long batches and (ii) the calculation of coefficients of variation for each metabolomic feature so only the most robust ones are retained for chemometrics [
77
]. Briefly, MS1 full scan acquisitions at high resolution (240k) were performed on 6 QC samples for exact mass measurements, and all samples were subjected to MS2 Data Dependent Analysis (DDA, 30k resolution) to generate fragmentation information for further annotation [
76
].
Raw LCMS data were processed using MS-DIAL v 4.8 [
78
], yielding 22,646 RT-m/z features. After data curation (blank check, SN > 10, CV QC < 20%), we retained 5916 final features for further chemometrics, of which 251 matched with MS1 and MS2 (Level 2 ID), 3045 suggested features matched with MS1 only (Level 3 ID), and 2620 unknowns (no match). MS-DIAL annotation of metabolic features was performed using a MetaboHUB in-house natural product database generated by Dr. Guillaume Marti (AgroMIx, Toulouse, France). Thus, putative annotation of differentially expressed metabolites resulted from MS-DIAL screening of the MS1 detected exact HR m/z and MS2 fragmentation patterns against multiple online databases [
78
]. Besides, InChiKeys of annotated features were used in ClassyFire batch to provide an automated structural ontology of chemical entities [
24
]. A second annotation based on the putative SMILES was done using Reaxys. Retention time correction was also applied in MS-DIAL, and metabolomic signals were normalised by the QC samples injected every 12 samples [
24
]. Retention time correction was also applied in MS-DIAL, and metabolomic signals were normalised by the QC samples injected every 12 samples.
Prior to multivariate statistics, the curated dataset was normalised using MetaboAnalyst v 5.0 [
79
] by median normalisation, cube root transformation, and Pareto scaling, resulting in a curated dataset for multivariate statistics of the top 5000 metabolomic features based on interquartile range filter in MetaboAnalyst. Multivariate statistical analyses of metabolomics data were performed in MetaboAnalyst v 5. Missing values were imputed by default according to MSDIAL processing which uses 1/10 of the minimal intensity. Extraction blanks, standards, and QC samples were discarded for clarity of visualisation.
