1.
Introduction
Maackia amurensis
lectins have been used in research for over 60 years [
1
,
2
]. In particular,
M, amurensis
seed lectin (MASL) has been used as an agent that binds to α2–3-linked sialic acids [
3
,
4
]. These glycosylation modifications are often found on extracellular protein receptors associated with viral infection, inflammation, and cancer [
5
,
6
]. Consequentially, MASL can target these receptors to inhibit viral infection, arthritis development, and cancer progression [
7
]. For example, MASL inhibits SARS-CoV-2 spike binding to the ACE2 receptor in cell culture [
8
]. MASL also targets the podoplanin (PDPN) receptor to inhibit osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis progression in mice [
9
,
10
], and suppress psoriatic epidermal hyperproliferation in skin cultures [
11
]. In addition, MASL targets the PDPN receptor to inhibit melanoma cell motility and viability in culture and tumor progression in mice [
12
]. MASL also targets the PDPN receptor to inhibit oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) cell growth and motility [
13
,
14
] and is being assessed as an oral cancer treatment in an ongoing Phase 1 human clinical trial (
NCT04188665
).
M
.
amurensis
lectin can also target receptors to inhibit non-small cell lung cancer [
15
,
16
] and childhood acute lymphoblastic leukemia [
17
] cell viability.
Vendors supply
M
.
amurensis
lectins by several different names, and the nomenclature is not clearly defined. These can be classified into two major groups. One group called MAL (
M. amurensis
lectin) also includes MAM (
M. amurensis
mitogenic), MAL1, MAA (
M. amurensis
agglutinin), and MAA1. The other group called MAH (
M. amurensis
hemagglutinating) also includes MAL2, MAA, and MAA2. Notably, MAA has been included in both groups, some names including MAL and MAL1, MAA and MAA1, MAH and MAL2, and MAA and MAA2 are sometimes used interchangeably. In addition, some vendors supply MAA or MAL as potentially mixed lectins without distinction as described by Geisler and Jarvis [
4
].
We used SDS-PAGE to compare MASL with other
M. amurensis
lectins. We found that MASL fits in the MAL lectin group which is formed by subunits of 27–36 kD that dimerize on nonreduced gels, while MAH lectins are formed by subunits of 25–28 kD that do not dimerize. Direct protein sequencing indicates that all MASL isoforms contain the same primary amino acid sequence which includes a cysteine that apparently drives their dimerization, and that different glycosylation events are associated with each molecular weight isoform. We also produced and sequenced a soluble human PDPN receptor and confirmed that it contains several extracellular α2–3-linked sialic acid moieties, and found that it shows potential to act as a ligand trap to inhibit PDPN mediated OSCC cell growth and motility. In addition, we characterize very strong binding kinetics between MASL and PDPN. Moreover, this study confirms that human OSCC cells express the PDPN receptor in situ and in culture, that PDPN is glycosylated with sialic acids, and that MASL can inhibit the growth and motility of these cells in culture.

Methods and materials
2.1.
Lectin SDS-PAGE analysis
MASL (Sentrimed), MAA (EY Laboratories #L-7801-1), MAL1 (Vector Labs #L1310-5), MAL2 (Vector Labs #L-1260-2), MAM (CosmoBio #JCM-J110) were resolved on 12% SDS-PAGE gels (20 μg/lane) in loading buffer (2% SDS, 10% glycerol, and 0.05% bromophenol blue in 62.5 mM Tris-HCl pH6.8) with (reducing) or without (nonreducing) 10% β-mercaptoethanol, and stained with Coomassie Brilliant blue R 250 (Sigma-Aldrich #CI42660).
2.2.
MASL LC-MS/MS analysis
LC-MS/MS was analyzed as described [
18
–
20
] with the following modifications. 20 μg MASL (Sentrimed) was resolved by SDS-PAGE, stained with Coomassie, and bands were excised from the gels. Protein was reduced with 10 mM DTT for 30 min at 60 °C, alkylated with 20 mM iodoacetamide for 45min at room temperature in the dark, and digested overnight with 0.2 μg of either trypsin (37 °C), chymotrypsin (20 °C) or Asp-N (37 °C) (Pierce MS Grade, ThermoFisher). Peptides were extracted twice with 5% formic acid, once with 60% acetonitrile, and dried under vacuum. Half of the chymotryptic peptides were further digested with PNGaseF. LC-MS/MS was conducted using a nano LC (Dionex Ultimate 3000 RLSCnano System, ThermoFisher) interfaced with an Eclipse Tribrid mass spectrometer (ThermoFisher). Each sample (~25% of digests) was loaded onto a fused silica trap column (Acclaim PepMap 100, 75umx2cm, ThermoFisher). After washing for 5 min at 5 μl/min with 0.1% TFA, the trap column was brought in-line with an analytical column (Nanoease MZ peptide BEH C18, 130A, 1.7 μm, 75umx250mm, Waters) for LC-MS/MS. Peptides were fractionated at 300 nL/min using a segmented linear gradient of 4–15% B in 5min (where A: 0.2% formic acid, and B: 0.16% formic acid, 80% acetonitrile), 15–50% B in 50min, and 50–90% B in 15min. Solution B is then returned to 4% for 5 min before the next run. The scan sequence began with an MS1 spectrum (Orbitrap analysis, resolution 120,000, scan range from M/Z 275–1500, automatic gain control target 1E6, maximum injection time 100 ms). The top N (3 s) duty cycle scheme was used to determine the number of MS/MS scans performed for each cycle. Precursor ions of charges 1–7 were selected for MS/MS and a dynamic exclusion of 60sec was used to avoid repeat sampling. Precursor ions were isolated in the quadrupole with an isolation window of 1.2
m
/
z
, automatic gain control target 1E5, and fragmented with higher-energy collisional dissociation with a normalized collision energy of 30%. Fragments were scanned in Orbitrap with resolution of 15,000. MS/MS scan ranges were determined by the charge state of the parent ion but a lower limit was set to 110
m
/
z
. Peak list MASCOT Generic Format (MGF) files were generated by Thermo Proteome Discoverer (v. 2.4) and searched against a database including relevant MASL and variant sequences plus a fasta database composed of common lab contaminants (CRAP) using an in-house installation of GPM Fury (X!Tandem Alanine). Mudpit searches were conducted using all mgf files from LC-MS/MS analysis of the each sample digested with different proteases. Parameters for the initial search were as follows: parent mass error ±7 ppm, fragment mass error ±20 ppm, and fixed modification of carbamidomethylation on cysteine and variable modifications of methionine monooxidation and asparagine deamindation. Variable modifications during three rounds of refinement were as follows: 1st round - monooxidation at methionine and tryptophan, 2nd round - deamination at glutamine and asparagine, 3rd round - dioxidation at methionine and tryptophan with protease specificity set to non-specific. Minimum acceptable peptide and protein expectation scores were set at 10
−2
and 10
−4
, respectively. Results were confirmed and analyzed by manual inspection. Evidence for O-linked glycosylation sites was not seen and N-linked sites were confirmed by PNGase F digestion.
2.3.
Soluble human PDPN (shPDPN) production and analysis
A sequence encoding a protein consisting of a 19 amino acid signal peptide from mouse Ig heavy chain V region BCL1 followed by the entire extracellular region containing amino acids 23–131 of human PDPN terminating in a HIS tag was engineered into pcDNA3.1(+) and transfected into FreeStyle HEK293F cells (ThermoFisher #R790–07). Resulting recombinant soluble human PDPN (shPDPN) was purified on Ni Sepharose after tangential diafiltration of the medium, followed by exchange to PBS on Superdex 200 and analysis by SDS-PAGE. For mass spectroscopy analysis, shPDPN (0.5 μg/μl in 25 mM ammonium bicarbonate, pH 8) was digested with either trypsin (0.01 μg/μl) or GluC (0.01 μg/μl) (Sequencing Grade; Promega) for 16 h at 37 °C. The digestion reaction was stopped by adding trifluoroacetic acid to 1%. Peptides mixtures (10 μl) were injected for LC-MS/MS analysis on an Ultimate 3000 RSLCnano system in-line connected to a Q Exactive HF Biopharma mass spectrometer (Thermo). Trapping was performed at 20 μl/min for 2 min in loading solvent A (0.1% trifluoroacetic in water) on a 5 mm trapping column (Thermo scientific, 300 μm internal diameter (I. D.), 5 μm beads). The peptides were separated on a 250 mm Aurora Ultimate, 1.7 μm C18, 75 μm inner diameter (IonOpticks) kept at a constant temperature of 45 °C. Peptides were eluted by a non-linear gradient starting at 0.5% solvent B reaching 26% solvent B (0.1% trifluoroacetic in acetonitrile) in 30 min, 44% solvent B in 38 min, followed by a 7-min wash at 56% solvent B and re-equilibration with solvent A at a flow rate of 300 nl/min. The mass spectrometer was operated in data-dependent mode, automatically switching between MS and MS/MS acquisition for the 12 most abundant ion peaks per MS spectrum. Full-scan MS spectra (375–1500
m
/
z
) were acquired at a resolution of 60,000 in the Orbitrap analyzer after accumulation to a target value of 3,000,000. The 12 most intense ions above a threshold value of 15,000 were isolated with a width of 1.5
m
/
z
for fragmentation at a normalized collision energy of 28% after filling the trap at a target value of 100,000 for maximum 120 ms. MS/MS spectra (200–2000
m
/
z
) were acquired at a resolution of 15,000 in the Orbitrap analyzer. The polydimethylcyclosiloxane background ion at 445.120028 Da was used for internal calibration (lock mass) and QCloud was used to control instrument longitudinal performance during the project. Data analysis was performed with BioPharma Finder software (Thermo Fisher Scientific), using the Peptide Mapping tool. Resulting MS/MS spectra were analyzed with BioPharma Finder 3.0 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and mapped onto the appropriate protein sequence with trypsin or Glu-C selected as proteases. For peptide identification, the following parameters were used: maximum peptide mass of 7000 Da, mass accuracy of 5 ppm and a minimum confidence of 0.80. Deamidation of asparagine and glutamine, and oxidation of methionine and tryptophan were set as variable modifications. The search for glycosylation modifications was enabled (human specific) and the maximum number of variable modifications per peptide was set at 2.
2.4.
MASL-shPDPN binding analysis
MASL was suspended in deionized water (10 mg/ml), injected on a pre-equilibrated 120 ml Superdex 200 column (XK 16/60 Cytiva), and eluted to obtain a tetrameric fraction that was screened on SEC-MALLS and quantitated by Little Lunatic (Unchained Biolabs) and verified by SDS-PAGE. Kinetic binding was evaluated on a Sartorius Octet R8 equipped with Octet NTA Biosensors (Sartorius 18–5101). Briefly, 500 nM shPDPN was loaded on NTA biosensors with N-(3-dimethylamino-propyl)-N-ethylcarbodiimide (EDC, Sigma 03449) and N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS Sigma 130672). Ethanolamine (Sigma E-9508) was then used to inactivate unused active ester groups. shPDPN coated biosensors were then immersed in wells containing a 0–200 nM dilution series of the MASL analyte for 10 min followed by 30 min of dissociation time in kinetic buffer (PBS with 0.01% BSA, 0.002% Tween 20, and 0.005% NaN3). A PDPN coated biosensor was also immersed in kinetic buffer to compensate for potential dissociation of the PDPN ligand. Bound shPDPN was then tested against the MASL dilution series in kinetic buffer with a baseline at 60 s, loading at 360 s, activation at 60 s, quenching at 60 s, second baseline at 300 s, association at 600 s, and dissociation at 1800 s, all at 1000 rpm. A separate experiment was conducted without PDPN loading to verify the absence of non-specific binding. Sensorgrams were collected using the high-sensitivity 8-channel detection mode on an Octet R8 instrument and analysis was performed using Octet Analysis Studio (12.2.2.26) and Octet BLI Discovery (12.2.2.20) software as instructed by the manufacturer (Sartorius).
2.5.
Cell migration and viability assays
Human protocols and informed consent were approved by the University Institutional Review Board (study ID Pro20140000009) and USFDA (IND #118210). Sen1bs human OSCC cells were obtained from an oral cancer patient enrolled in a clinical trial (#
NCT04188665

13

14

For migration assays, cell monolayers were scratched and visualized immediately before and 24 h after treatment with 0 nM, 770 nM, 1440 nM, and 2280 nM MASL, or with 0 nM, 770 nM, 1440 nM, and 2280 nM shPDPN from 24 h to 48 h after treatment, and migration was quantitated as the number of cells that entered 123 × 123 μm squares placed along the center of the wound as described [
13
,
14

18

13

2.6.
Immunohistochemistry
Surgical specimens were fixed in 10% formalin in PBS, paraffin embedded, sectioned (4 μm), and processed for hematoxylin/eosin staining and immunohistochemistry with D2–40 monoclonal antibody (D2–40 Agilent M361901–2) to detect human PDPN as described [
14
,
21

2.7.
Western blotting
Western blotting was performed as described [
13
,
22
]. Confluent Sen1bs cells were treated 0 nM, 770 nM, 1540 nM, and 3080 nM MASL or shPDPN for 24 h or 48 h, respectively, washed with PBS, transferred to microcentrifuge tubes, pelleted, aspirated, and frozen at −80 °C before they were lysed in buffer (2% SDS, 10% glycerol, 10 mM EDTA, 50 nM DTT, 50 mM NaF, 1 mM Na3VO4, and 1 mM PMSF in 62.5 mM Tris pH 6.8), sonicated, and clarified by centrifugation. Protein (10 μg/lane) was resolved by SDS-PAGE, transferred to Immobilon-P membranes (Millipore #1PVH00010), and incubated with antisera specific for PDPN (D2–40 Agilent M361901–2) or GAPDH (Cell Signaling #2118). Primary antibodies were recognized by appropriate secondary anti-IgG antibodies conjugated to horseradish peroxidase including mouse (JacksonImmunoResearch #115-035-003), and rabbit (Proteintech #SA00001–2) and detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (Thermo Scientific 32209). Gels were stained with Coomassie and membranes were stained with India ink to verify equal loading and transfer after blotting.
3.
Results
3.1.
MASL consists of differentially glycosylated isoforms that form dimers
A variety of
M. amurensis
lectins are being used without clearly defined nomenclature or molecular characterization [
4
]. To address this confusion, we used SDS-PAGE to resolve MASL and other
M. amurensis
lectins including MAA, MAL1, MAL2, and MAM. Reducing gels resolved MASL, MAL1, and MAM as two major bands at 36 kD and 33 kD, and two minor bands at 28 kD and 27 kD. In contrast to these three lectins, reducing gels resolved MAA and MAL2 into smaller subunits with 2 major bands at 28 kD and 27 kD as shown in
Fig. 1a
.
Results from reducing SDS-PAGE indicate that all of the M. amurensis lectins examined here contain subunits that migrate at 28 kD and 27 kD. We employed nonreducing SDS-PAGE to examine the ability of these subunits to dimerize. MASL subunits formed dimers that migrated at 72 kD, which are likely mediated by a cysteine residue in the carboxy region of the protein as previously reported [
12
]. MAL1 and MAM subunits also produced these dimers on nonreducing gels. However, MAA and MAL2 subunits did not form dimers in these gels. Thus, these lectins represented two distinct groups with MASL, MAL1, and MAM forming dimers, while the group containing MAA and MAL2 do not form dimers on nonreducing gels as shown in
Fig. 1a
.
We sequenced MASL to verify its identity. Subunits resolved at 27 kD, 28 kD, 33 kD, and 36 kD by reducing SDS-PAGE described above and shown in
Fig. 1a
and
b
. Each band was excised and examined by LC-MS/MS. All four MASL isoforms contain the same primary amino acid sequence. This resulting sequence was aligned with previously reported sequences as shown in
Fig. 2
. This sequence contains a cysteine at residue 272 similar to that reported by Ochoa-Alvarez et al. [
12
] and Yamamoto et al. [
23
], as opposed to a serine in this position reported by Van Damme et al. [
24
]. This cysteine residue is consistent with driving dimer formation seen in nonreducing gels seen in
Fig. 1a
. The MASL sequence defined here also contains threonine, serine, and glutamate at residues 223, 225, and 253 instead of serine, isoleucine, and tryptophan reported by Ochoa-Alvarez et al. [
12
] and Van Damme et al. [
24
]. Glycosylation sites were also found on asparagines at residues 90 and 132 as reported by Yamamoto et al. [
23
] in all four MASL isoforms. Asparagine was glycosylated at residue 208 on the 33 kD and 28 kD isoforms, and residue 220 on the 36 kD, 33 kD, and 28 kD isoforms as reported by Yamamoto et al. [
23
]. In addition to these sites, glycosylation sites were also found on asparagine 68 on the 28 kD and 33 kD isoforms, and asparagine 194 on the 36 kD and 33 kD isoforms as shown in
Fig. 2
.
3.2.
The extracellular PDPN region contains many sialic acid moieties and interacts with MASL
The PDPN receptor has been identified as a biologically relevant MASL target. Sialic acids on PDPN are presumed to be relevant for MASL binding [
9
,
10
,
12
–
14
,
25
]. We produced soluble human PDPN (shPDPN) in human HEK293F cells to verify sialic acid modification of this receptor. This protein migrated between 18 kD and 36 kD, with a mean molecular weight of 24.6 kD instead of its expected 12 kD size by SDS-PAGE as shown in
Fig. 1c
. We sequenced this protein by LC-MS/MS to find that PDPN was glycosylated at 26 amino acids as shown in
Fig. 3
. These glycosylation events were exclusively on serine or threonine residues. Seventeen of these 26 modifications contained sialic acid, which include glycosylation of threonine at residues 34 and 52 as previously reported [
21
,
26
–
28
].
MASL can effectively bind to α2–3-linked sialic acid residues found on the PDPN receptor [
3
,
4
,
7
]. We analyzed MASL and PDPN binding kinetics to characterize this interaction. Combined with size exclusion chromatography coupled to a multi-angle laser light scattering detector, these data indicate that MASL subunits form a tetramer that binds 2 PDPN molecules with dynamics shown in
Table 1
. Association rate constants indicate that MASL initially binds to PDPN with a Kon1 of 1.6 × 10
4
/ms followed by a secondary Kon2 of 8.5 × 10
2
/ms. Dissociation rate constants indicate that MASL dissociates from PDPN with Koff1 and Koff2 values of 1.6 × 10
−4
/s and 2.5 × 10
−3
/s, respectively. These data indicate that the equilibrium dissociation constants for KD1 and KD2 values are calculated to be 1.0 × 10
−8
M and 2.9 × 10
−6
M, respectively. These values indicate a strong affinity between MASL and PDPN consistent with the use of MASL as a potential PDPN blocker on OSCC cells.
3.3.
MASL and shPDPN can inhibit human OSCC cell growth and migration
MASL can target PDPN to inhibit human OSCC cell growth and motility [
13
,
14
,
25
]. Robust PDPN expression was found on human OSCC cells by IHC of patient biopsy as shown in
Fig. 4a
. These patient cells adapted to cell culture and Western blotting of cells adapted to culture as shown in
4b
. MASL has been reported to decrease PDPN expression in OSCC cells [
13
]. However, in contrast to these previous results from OSCC cells obtained from a different oral cancer patient [
13

Fig. 4b

MASL has been found to inhibit OSCC cell motility and viability [

14

Fig. 5

Fig. 6
.
Soluble receptors can act as “ligand traps” to inhibit oncogenic signaling [
29
–
32
]. We, therefore, examined the potential of shPDPN to inhibit oncogenic PDPN signaling in the OSCC cells used in this study. shPDPN inhibited cell motility and viability in a dosage dependent manner consistent with its potential to act as a PDPN ligand trap. shPDPN decreased cell motility by 36 ± 6.5%, 63 ± 12%, and 39 ± 1.4% at 770 nM, 1540 nM, and 3080 nM compared to untreated controls, respectively (mean ± SEM, n = 2) as shown in
Fig. 5
shPDPN also decreased cell viability by 47 ± 2.5%, 61 ± 1.5%, and 70 ± 3.0% at 770 nM, 1540 nM, and 3080 nM compared to untreated controls, respectively as shown in
Fig. 6
.
4.
Discussion
M
.
amurensis
lectins are used as valuable research and botanical agents [
7
]. These lectins have been classified into two major groups including MAL (
M. amurensis
lectin) and MAH (
M. amurensis
hemagglutinating). However, their nomenclature and composition are not clearly defined [
4
]. Here we report that these lectins can be classified into two groups based on the ability of their subunits to dimerize. One group forms dimers in nonreducing conditions, while the other group does not.
M
.
amurensis
seed lectin (MASL) forms dimers, presumably mediated by a cysteine residue in the carboxyl region of the protein. MAA and MAL2 lectins examined here did not form dimers and, therefore, might not contain this cysteine residue. We propose that MASL, MAL1, and MAM studied here are members of the historically named MAL lectin group, while MAL2 and MAA are members of the MAH group [
4
]. MAL and MAH amino acid sequences have been reported to be over 86% homologous with each other based on previous cDNA sequencing results [
33
,
34
]. Differences between these lectins might arise from their fractionation process, or MAH isoforms including MAL2 and MAA examined here might represent MAL degradation products that have lost their carboxyl terminal region cysteine residue. Further sequencing of these lectins could be employed to confirm or refute this possibility.
The MASL product used here has been consistent for over 10 years as evidenced by sequencing of its constituent isoforms [
12
]. This study also confirmed previously reported glycosylation events on asparagine residues at positions 90, 132, 208 and 220 [
23
]. In addition, results from this study identified novel glycosylation sites on asparagine residues at positions 68 and 194.
MASL can bind to the PDPN receptor which has emerged as a potent mediator of cancer and inflammation. This transmembrane mucin like receptor is often expressed at the invasive front of cancers including OSCC [
21
,
22
,
25
,
35
–
37
]. In addition to MASL, this study also examined the sequence and glycosylation events on recombinant soluble human PDPN (shPDPN) produced by human HEK293F cells. This study confirmed previous reports of sialic acid modifications of threonine residues at positions 34 and 52 [
21
,
26
–
28
] and found 15 other sialic acid modified threonine residues throughout the PDPN receptor. MASL can target sialic acid moieties on this receptor to ameliorate cancer progression [
12
–
14
], arthritic inflammation [
9
,
10
], and psoriatic epidermal hyperproliferation [
11
] and is being assessed as an oral cancer treatment in an ongoing Phase 1 human clinical trial (
NCT04188665
). In this study we report MASL-PDPN binding kinetics for the first time that we know of. The initial KD value of 1.0 × 10
−8
M indicates that MASL has a strong affinity for PDPN consistent with its potential use as a medicinal agent.
We utilized a signal peptide cleaved from the final protein during secretion by signal peptidase activity to produce a soluble human (shPDPN) protein. This construct contains the entire extracellular region of the receptor with 17 threonine residues glycosylated with sialic acid glycosylation events produced by human cells. Results from this study indicate that this shPDPN construct has the potential to serve as decoy receptor or ligand trap to inhibit PDPN signaling as evidenced by its ability to inhibit OSCC cell growth and motility.
Results from this study also confirm that MASL also can inhibit human oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) cell growth and motility as previously reported [
13
,
14
]. In contrast to reports that MASL treatment decreases PDPN expression by human OSCC cells [
13
], MASL did not decrease PDPN expression in cells obtained from the patient examined in this study. These data indicate that effects of MASL on PDPN expression might depend on specific cells and patient tumor characteristics. Taken together, results from this study define the sequence of MASL, its interactions with PDPN, and effects on human OSCC cell growth and motility.
