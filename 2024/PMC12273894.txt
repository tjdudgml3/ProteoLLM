1
INTRODUCTION
Glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) are linear polysaccharides that generally contain several sulphate groups in different positions [
1
,
2
]. They have diverse biological roles as the structure (e.g. epimerization of glucuronic acid and sulphation positions) and length of the GAG chain are critical in binding of proteins and subsequent signal transduction processes. These polysaccharide chains are made up of disaccharide repeating units of uronic acid and amino sugars. One of the most important classes of GAGs is heparan sulphate (HS) that consists of glucuronic or iduronic acid and
N
‐acetylglucosamine residues [
3
]. HS disaccharides can contain up to four sulphate groups [
4
,
5
].
Several sample preparation steps are required before the analytical characterization of GAGs. The workflow generally begins with enzymatic or chemical depolymerization of the polysaccharide chains into oligo‐ or disaccharides. This is most commonly followed by solid‐phase extraction (SPE) purification, solvent evaporation and subsequent freezing. The structural analysis of HS disaccharides can be performed by various analytical techniques [
6
]. One of the most widely used approaches is mass spectrometry (MS) or ultraviolet (UV) detection combined with separation techniques such as capillary electrophoresis or high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) [
7
]. For HPLC separation, a wide variety of methods are available, such as reversed‐phase chromatography with derivatization or ion‐pairing, size exclusion chromatography, graphitized carbon, hydrophilic interaction chromatography (HILIC) and HILIC‐weak anion exchange chromatography [
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
]. Because this procedure involves several sample handling steps such as transfer, solvent evaporation and storage, which may cause major sample loss, a thorough optimization of the workflow is necessary.
Stability and recovery of the HS disaccharides are mostly affected by solvent evaporation, freezing and storage. These processes are also strongly influenced by the types of the solvents the samples are dissolved in and the storage vessels. The analysis may be hindered by sample loss due to precipitation on different surfaces or chemical degradation during storage. Following the depolymerization of the HS chains, the purification of the samples is necessary. A widely used technique is SPE [
14
], which usually requires solvent exchange both before and after the purification to match conditions with further steps of analysis. In the most cases, the solvent exchange includes solvent evaporation and re‐constitution, which has a significant influence on the recovery of the samples. During HPLC–MS measurements, the samples are often stored in an autosampler at 4°C for shorter or longer periods before their analysis. The most commonly used injection solvents are water, acetonitrile (ACN), methanol (MeOH) and their mixtures with or without salt additives [
15
].
Our research group has previously investigated the effect of evaporation and storage on the stability and recovery of chondroitin sulphate (CS) disaccharides, another major class of GAGs [
16
]. We have found that the highest recovery values were observed when evaporating the samples from low volumes of aqueous solutions using a SpeedVac. CS disaccharides may decompose during storage in the autosampler; however, they are more stable in ACN ‐based solvents than in MeOH‐containing ones [
16
]. It has been observed that under neutral conditions at increased temperatures CS chains degrade into smaller fragments, monomers (glucuronic/iduronic acid or
N
‐acetylgalactosamine) and desulphated components [
17
]. HS disaccharides can contain more sulphate groups than CS and, therefore, may be more sensitive to various parameters during storage and sample preparation. Despite its relatively high potential to degrade, there is insufficient information in the literature on the stability and recovery of HS disaccharides. However, the decomposition of heparin, a GAG with a very similar structure to HS has been previously investigated. Heparin is produced by mast cells and is more densely sulphated and contains more iduronic acid residues compared to HS. Kozlowski et al. followed the acid hydrolysis of heparin at 40, 60 and 80°C. It was observed that heparin was resistant to glycosidic cleavage at pH 1–6 between 40 and 60°C; however, minor glycosidic cleavage was observed after 24 h at pH 1 at 80°C [
18
]. Furthermore, over 50°C under basic conditions (pH 9 or above), side reactions took place on the backbone of heparin, including 2‐
O
‐desulphation [
19
]. Jandik et al. also investigated the degradation pathways of heparin analysing sodium heparin, incubated under acidic and basic conditions at 30 and 60°C. Heparin showed a notable stability within the first 500 h, but between 1000 and 2000 h a rapid degradation occurred. The decomposition processes observed under these circumstances included the internal hydrolysis of glycosidic linkages and particularly the loss of
N
‐sulphate groups [
20
].
The variations and measurement errors introduced during the analytical workflow of GAGs are largely caused by the sample preparation methods, so it is crucial to study the stability and recovery of the target compounds under conditions encountered during sample preparation. In this work, an extensive study was performed to investigate the stability and recovery of Δ
4,5
‐unsaturated HS disaccharides under several sample preparation conditions. Even though manufacturers provide some practical considerations regarding the storage of HS disaccharides, these have not been previously investigated in detail. In particular, information on their recovery is largely missing, which may also significantly influence results of HS disaccharides obtained under various sample preparation workflows. Optimal conditions can facilitate the identification of future anomalies that may arise during measurements, which may allow more accurate analysis of biological samples.

2.2
Liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry
The HPLC–MS measurements were performed by Waters nano‐Acquity ultrahigh performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) system attached to Waters quadruple time of flight Premier mass spectrometer. The chromatographic separation of HS disaccharides was achieved on a self‐packed capillary column. For this, 250 µm i.d. fused silica capillaries were fritted using a commercial Frit Kit (Next Advance, Inc.). Briefly, a 3:1:1 mixture of potassium silicate (Kasil 1624, Kasil 1) and formamide was drawn in the capillary, which was stored in an oven at 80°C for 4 h. The column was prepared by unpacking a GlycanPac AXH‐1 1.9 µm analytical column (2.1 × 150 mm) (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and filling it into fritted silica capillary by a pressure injection cell until the chromatographic bed reached a 10 cm length. The HPLC eluents were the following: 10 mM ammonium formate in 75:25 v/v ACN:water (pH 4.4) (solvent a) and 65 mM ammonium formate in 75:25 v/v ACN:water (pH 4.4) (solvent b). The HS disaccharides were separated using the following gradient program. Isocratic hold on 6% B for 3.5 min, followed by a steep linear increase (0.5 min) to 70% B and a further shallow linear increase (2.5 min) to 95% B. This was followed by 3.5 min wash with 100% B eluent and an equilibration with 6% B eluent for 5 min. The HPLC–MS method used has been previously described in detail [
13
,
14
,
21
]. Samples were injected in solvent a. The instrument parameters were the following: capillary voltage: 1.9 kV; sampling cone voltage: 17 eV, extraction cone voltage: 5 V; source temperature: 80°C; desolvation temperature: 120°C; cone gas flow: 25 L/h and desolvation gas flow: 420 L/h. HS disaccharides were detected in negative ionization mode as [M–H]

2.3
Data analysis
The intensity values originated from MS measurements as area under the curve (AUC) of extracted ion chromatograms (EICs). The peaks in the EICs were integrated by the QuanLynx add‐in of the Waters MassLynx 4.2 software. The data visualization and statistical comparison were performed by using Microsoft Office Excel and Python 3.11.7 with Spyder 5.4.3. In the first step of the statistical analysis of the results, the initial conditions were tested using Shapiro–Wilks‐ (normality) and Levene's‐ (equality of variance) tests. Student's
t
‐test (equal variance), Welch's
t
‐test (unequal variances) and Wilcoxon rank sum test (non‐normal data) were used for two‐group comparisons. For multiple group comparisons, ANOVA (equal variances), Welch's ANOVA (unequal variances), Kruskal–Wallis
H
‐test (non‐normal data) and the corresponding post hoc tests (Tukey HSD, Games‐Howell, Dunn) were used. For modelling recovery at the different stages of sample preparation, relative intensity values were used. These were calculated by dividing the AUC corresponding to the analysed component by the corresponding peak area in the control sample. For the data illustration, the average of the relative intensity values of the five parallel samples was used. The data set was filtered using the median method except for the values, which were extreme outliers. Nine samples were found to be highly different from their respective replicate samples. In one case, two out of the five parallel samples showed significant differences from the others. Samples, which were larger or smaller than the mean of the parallel samples (without the outlier) by at least 2 standard deviations (SDs), were identified as outliers. These samples were disregarded from the further evaluation. The measurement data were uploaded to the GlycoPOST database [
22
] under the accession number GPST000390.

2.4.1
Effect of solvent type on evaporation
The effect of evaporation was investigated using a heated vacuum centrifuge (SpeedVac) at 55°C for 12.5 mM AMBIC, 20 mM Tris–HCl and water solvents. The evaporation temperature was chosen by considering the moderate sample decomposition and the drying speed. Evaporation from AMBIC and Tris–HCl solvents was investigated as enzymatic depolymerization is generally performed in the presence of these buffers. Samples were dissolved in 50 µL of the aforementioned solvents.
2.4.2
Effect of freezing
Pre‐dried samples were dissolved in 50 µL of water, then frozen and thawed repeatedly for 5 or 10 cycles. Freezing was carried out in a normal freezer (−20°C, freezing time: 1 h) or liquid nitrogen (−196°C, freezing time: 10 s). After freezing, samples were thawed in a block heater at 37°C (approximately 1 min), and then after the completion of all freezing cycles, they were dried down using a heated vacuum centrifuge.
2.4.3
Effect of storage
Effect of storage temperature
Samples were stored in 50 µL water for 48 h at the following temperatures: −18, 4, 20, 37 and 55°C.
Effect of pH
Pre‐dried samples were dissolved in 50 µL 1 mM hydrochloric acid (pH 3), water (pH 7) or 1 mM NaOH (pH 11). The samples were stored at 37°C for the following time points: 0, 6, 12 and 24 h.
Effect of digestion buffer
The pre‐dried disaccharide samples were dissolved in 50 µL of digestion buffer, 12.5 mM AMBIC or 20 mM Tris–HCl. The samples were incubated for 24 and 48 h at 37°C.
Effect of type of storage vessels on recovery of HS disaccharides
To investigate the effect of the type of storage vessel on the storage stability of HS disaccharides, pre‐dried samples were dissolved in 20 µL water or HPLC–MS injection solvent (10 mM ammonium formate in 75:25 v/v ACN:water). The examined containers were 0.5 mL plastic Eppendorf Safe‐Lock Tubes (Eppendorf Corporate) and borosilicate type 70 glass vial with glass insert (LAB‐EX Kft.). The samples were stored at 4°C for 24 h to simulate autosampler storage. After storage, all the samples were pipetted into plastic tubes for solvent evaporation. For these experiments, the control samples were mixed, evaporated to dryness and stored dry and frozen in plastic tubes. Control samples were dissolved in water or the injection solvents and dried in new plastic tubes prior to the measurement.
Effect of type of solvent on storage of HS disaccharides
To investigate the effect of solvents on the storage stability of samples, pre‐dried samples were dissolved in 20 µL of a specific solvent. The tested solvents were 90:10 v/v% ACN:water, 75:25 v/v% ACN:water, 50:50 v/v% ACN:water, 10 mM ammonium formate in 75:25 v/v% ACN:water, 75:25 v/v% MeOH:H2O and 10 mM ammonium formate in 75:25 v/v% MeOH:water. The solvents were stored at 4°C for 0, 6 and 12 h to simulate autosampler storage.
3
RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
HS chains are considered to be stable under neutral pH conditions for longer time periods. However, when depolymerization of the chains is performed for the subsequent analytical characterization of their disaccharide building units, several sample preparation steps are carried out, which may affect recovery and stability. Although being invaluable information for unbiased HS disaccharide analysis, these have not been systematically investigated to date. Our goal was to perform a comprehensive study to compare common parameters and methods for HS samples of biological origin in order to reduce the bias in results occurring from sample loss during the sample preparation process. A commonly used sample preparation workflow comprises HS extraction and digestion into disaccharides, derivatization, sample clean‐up and instrumental analysis (Figure
1
, left panel). This multi‐step workflow necessitates several intermediate sample handling steps influencing intensities observed during the measurements (Figure
1
, right panel). We investigated these steps in detail, including storage in buffers used for digestion, sample purification and injection, along with solvent evaporation techniques and freezing cycles for storage. Further sample handling steps affecting stability and recovery were also considered and are discussed in the following. We have made small sample size comparisons, and in many cases, we see trends that are not significant. However, in several cases, we identified statistically significant differences supported by statistical tests, which are shown in Table
S2
.

Throughout the text, we refer to dissociation or degradation of the HS disaccharides. Under dissociation, we refer to loss of sulphate groups in the ion source, easily identifiable in the HPLC–MS EICs. Meanwhile, degradation occurring during sample preparation and storage cannot be distinguished by chromatography and can only be indicated by intensity changes compared to the control sample.
3.1
Effect of solvent type on evaporation
First, we studied the effect of the type of solvent on the evaporation process using a heated vacuum centrifuge. Testing the evaporation of the samples is necessary, because solvent exchange is an inevitable step in a general workflow (Figure
1
) on several occasions. In addition, the best way to store samples is to keep them dry and frozen. The used solvents strongly influenced the recovery of the samples (Figure
2A
). We observed recovery values between 80% and 100% utilizing 12.5 mM AMBIC or water as a solvent for most HS disaccharides. Note that recovery values above 100% can be found for some disaccharides (AMBIC: D0S0, D2A6 and D0S6; water: D0S0 and D2A6), but these are within the SD of the technical replicates. SD values indicated that evaporation from water is more favourable than from AMBIC.

In the case of the evaporation from 20 mM Tris–HCl, the disaccharide content reduced drastically in the samples. Examining the EICs of the HS disaccharides, we found significant fronting of the peaks; however, the peak maximum is located at the same retention time as in the control samples (Figure

3.2
Effect of freezing
Because standard samples, which are generally used for analytical measurements, are most commonly stored frozen in large portions, they are thawed and refrozen several times. Consequently, it is crucial to examine the effect of the freezing method and the number of freezing cycles. Common freezing methods (−20°C freezer and −196°C liquid nitrogen) were investigated in 5 and 10 cycles. We could identify several differences between the two methods and cycle times (Figure
2C
). Storing the samples for five cycles, the disaccharide content was lower in the case of the −20°C freezer. The SD was not significantly different among the two methods except for D0S6 disaccharide, which had 4.5 times higher SD in liquid nitrogen. Comparing 10‐cycle freezing, the average recovery was higher in the freezer (80%–95%) as compared to liquid nitrogen (70%–90%), but the SD was twice as high. The results demonstrate that although higher sample loss occurs using liquid nitrogen freezing, the SDs were lower. This means more predictable results, which can be beneficial when biological samples are studied.
3.3
Effect of storage
The correct choice of storage parameters is essential to minimize the sample loss. Therefore, different parameters were investigated to identify the ideal ones for the storage of HS disaccharide samples, including storage temperature, pH, digestion buffer and storage in different solvents.
3.3.1
Effect of storage temperature
It is generally advised to choose the lowest possible temperature for storing samples to avoid their decomposition. However, in many cases, such as storage in an autosampler or during the sample preparation steps, the samples cannot be kept at low temperature. The effect of storage temperature was examined for aqueous solutions, which were stored for 48 h at −18, 4, 20, 37 and 55°C. Based on the results, it is feasible to store samples under or at 4°C for 48 h, because the recovery of the disaccharides at −18 and 4°C was between 85% and 95% (Figure
3A
). The SDs at 4°C were almost double of the values at −18°C. At 20°C, the recovery was between 80% and 100%, but the SD was four times higher than at 4°C. At elevated temperatures (37 and 55°C), the recovery reduced, whereas the SD mostly increased, especially for the D0S0 disaccharide. This leads to a decrease in the reliability of measurements and, subsequently, the potential loss of biological information. Our results confirmed that the stability of the HS disaccharides decreases with increasing temperature in aqueous solutions, but the samples can be stored for up to 48 h at 4°C, the temperature commonly set in the autosampler, allowing analysis of larger sample cohorts.

3.3.2
Effect of pH
HS disaccharides can contain several sulphate groups with negative charge, which can easily dissociate, and the pH of the solvent can further facilitate degradation. To examine the effect of different pH values on the stability of HS disaccharides, the samples were stored in 1 mM hydrochloric acid, MilliQ water and 1 mM sodium hydroxide solvents at 37°C for 0, 6, 12 and 24 h (Figure
3B
). The largest sample loss was observed at basic pH (pH 11), and recovery values were between 10% and 60% (Figure
3B
). At pH 3, the recovery was between 60% and 100%. In the acidic range, the recoveries of the samples were significantly higher than at basic pH, but the SD values were similar. In the case of neutral pH (pH 7), the recovery was between 75% and 100%. Several significant differences were identified between pH 3–11 and pH 7–11 (Table
S2
). Based on the observations, there are minor differences between the storage times as well, but those discrepancies are within the SD; therefore, the recovery of the samples was inherently affected by the pH at which they were stored. The results demonstrate that it is the most advantageous if the HS disaccharide samples are stored at neutral conditions and the samples can be stored for up to 24 h; however, for most disaccharides, storage at acidic pH is also feasible, but it is not preferred in the case of long‐term storage. Basic conditions should be avoided, because significant sample degradation occurs. This is corroborated by previous literature reports where HS chains have also been shown to degrade under basic conditions [
23
].
3.3.3
Effect of digestion buffer
It is also important to investigate the effect of the most commonly used digestion buffers on sample recovery because this can be considered storage for up to 48 h. Depolymerization of the HS chains is generally performed in AMBIC or in Tris–HCl buffers [
24
,
25
,
26
]. Stabilities of HS disaccharides were investigated during 24 and 48 h’ storage in AMBIC solution at 37°C (Figure
3C
). Recovery values between 70% and 100% were observed. Comparing the 24 h and 48 h storage, the disaccharide content and the SD were higher at 24 h, whereas after 48 h’ storage, the recovery was reduced, except in the case of D2A6 and D0A0 disaccharides, which increased (relative standard deviation: 26%).
For 20 mM Tris–HCl, the recovery of most disaccharides dropped extraordinarily after 24 and 48 h storage. In the digestion buffer storage experiment, we also observed the same phenomenon as previously mentioned in Section
3.1
during evaporation from Tris–HCl. Fronting chromatographic peaks (Figure
2B
) and a drastic reduction of disaccharide abundances were also detected. In order to investigate the phenomenon in detail, further measurements were performed. First, pure Tris–HCl buffer was injected, and then co‐injection was performed with 20 mM Tris–HCl and the HS disaccharides. During the pure Tris–HCl injection, we observed that Tris elutes in chloride adduct form ([Tris+Cl]
−
) from the chromatography column in dead‐time therefore is not responsible for fronting and does not cause ion suppression during the MS measurements. HS samples dissolved in 20 mM Tris–HCl buffer were measured by the HPLC–MS method before (Figure
S1A
) as well as after storage at 37°C for 24 h (Figure
S1B
). There were no visible changes in the chromatography after the storage. Finally, evaporation was performed with different volumes of 20 mM Tris–HCl buffers, 10 and 50 µL, respectively, and after evaporation, samples were dissolved in the injection solvent. The length of evaporation (and thus the length of the incubation of the molecules at 55°C) was 50 min for the 10 µL volume, whereas 140 min for the 50 µL samples. In the case of evaporation from 10 µL Tris–HCl buffer, the fronting was not observed (Figure
S1C
); however, for the evaporation from 50 µL, the fronting was repeatable (Figure
S1D
). The anomaly could not be observed when injecting HS disaccharides from Tris–HCl‐containing solutions, neither after evaporation from 10 µL, but only after evaporation from 50 µL (Figure
S1
). This suggests that a chemical modification between Tris and the HS disaccharides takes place at 55°C after a longer period of time, which is not in connection with the storage under the conditions of enzymatic digestion. We hypothesize that a covalent modification may have occurred between the HS disaccharides and Tris–HCl at elevated temperatures during longer periods of solvent evaporation. This result is a hydrophobic modification on a given subset of the molecules, thus causing the long frontings of the peaks. However, the product is too labile to be observed under the applied ESI conditions; we only detected the original HS disaccharide mass channels. This hypothesis is supported by the fact that previous papers have reported that Tris–HCl formed imine at elevated temperature with sterane compounds and carbohydrates [
27
,
28
]. In solution, the ring shapes of sugars are dynamically in equilibrium with the linear forms. During this formation change, the sugar rings can be transformed into open chain forms, and the glycosidic hydroxyl group is transformed into an oxo group of an aldehyde, thus allowing imine formation. However, the products of this reaction could not be identified by our mass spectrometer system, presumably due to the complete fragmentation in the ion source. Due to this phenomenon, when the in‐solution digestion of HS chains is performed, we suggest the use of AMBIC buffers.
3.3.4
Effect of type of storage vessels on storage of HS disaccharides
Because charged molecules can irreversibly adsorb on the surface of the containers’ wall, it was important to investigate the recovery between storing HS disaccharide samples in commonly used plastic tubes and glass vial with insert (Figure
3D
). Storing samples in water or the HPLC–MS injection solvent was also compared for both storage vessels. Higher recovery values were observed in plastic tubes for aqueous solutions. Examining the glass vial, using water as solvent showed higher recovery values for the samples with smaller SD values except for D2S6. For the D0S6 and D2S6 disaccharides, the recovery was significantly higher in water than in the injection solvent in the case of glass vials (Figure
3D
). These are due to the fact that HS disaccharides are significantly more soluble in water. Taking the storage vessels into account, the plastic tubes used for the aqueous solutions showed favourable recoveries for the non‐ and less‐ sulphated disaccharides (D0A0, D0A6 and D0S0), whereas for the doubly and triply sulphated disaccharides, recoveries were slightly better in the glass vial. In the case of the tested injection solvent, recovery values were higher in plastic tubes for all disaccharides, whereas the SD was lower, except for D0S0 and D2S6. Based on these results, it is preferable to use plastic tubes for sample storage, which is better if the samples are stored in water, as it reduces the rate of sample precipitation.
3.3.5
Effect of type of solvent on storage of HS disaccharides
For the separation and analysis of GAG disaccharides, the type of chromatographic system determines the applicable eluents and injection solvents. In addition to the requirements of the system, the potential degradation of the samples must also be considered that mentioned earlier. The most commonly used injection solvents for disaccharide analysis are water, ACN, MeOH and their mixtures with or without buffer salts. Crucial steps include performing heated vacuum evaporation of Tris‐HCl‐containing solutions and storing the HS disaccharide samples under alkaline conditions; these should be avoided when establishing a workflow. Other intermediate steps of sample preparation result in minor sample loss; however, some practical considerations should be followed to maximize the reliability of HS disaccharide analysis. First, frequent thawing and freezing of the samples are not recommended, but if necessary, it is better to use liquid nitrogen. Second, HS disaccharide samples are safe to store in neutral‐pH aqueous solutions for up to 24 h at room temperature and even up to 48 h at 4°C. Third, when using organic solvent‐based injection solvents, incorporating ammonium formate salt in both ACN and MeOH‐based solvents increases the stability of the multiply sulphated HS disaccharides. Finally, sample loss due to precipitation can be reduced by dissolving HS disaccharides in water and storing them in plastic tubes.
The presented results contribute to our understanding of possible anomalies of the HS disaccharide analysis workflow and enable development of more reliable sample handling workflows for the future investigation of HS disaccharides from biological samples.
