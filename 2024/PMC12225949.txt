Introduction
Bacterial diseases caused by members of the
Xanthomonas spp
. have a significant impact on crop yields and quality worldwide
[1]
. The
Xanthomonas spp
. comprises phytopathogenic bacteria that are distributed worldwide are responsible for diseases in several important cash crops
[2]
,
[3]
. It is reported that they can cause about 350 different plant diseases
[4]
. Among them, the notorious diseases include rice bacterial leaf blight (
BLB
) caused by
Xanthomonas oryzae pv. Oryzae
(
Xoo
), citrus canker (
CC
) caused by
Xanthomonas axonopodis pv. Citri
(
Xac
), mango bacterial black spot (
BBS
) caused by
Xanthomonas campestris pv. Mangiferae indicae
(
Xcm
), angular leaf spot of strawberry (
ALS
) caused by
Xanthomonas fragariae
(
Xf
) and so on. These pathogens have become obstacles in agricultural production all over the world because of the rapid reproduction, strong transmission and drug-resistance
[5]
. Pesticides play a pivotal role in the management of these diseases. At present, traditional chemical synthetic pesticides are mainly used to reduce and control the harmful degree of these plant diseases. However, long-term irrational use not only leads to the development of resistance of pathogenic bacteria, but also harms human health and the environment
[6]
. Therefore, it is a sustainable alternative strategy to combat these diseases through investigating new antibacterial drugs with low toxicity, good environmental compatibility and unique mechanism of action.
Plant-derived green pesticides have the merits of high efficiency, low toxicity, high selectivity, and low resistance to plant diseases and insect pests
[7]
. With the increasing issues of environment degradation, plant-derived green pesticides have drawn more and more attention from the world pesticide community, which provides development opportunities and huge market space for the development of plant-derived green pesticides. Therefore, it is crucial to find antibacterial active ingredients from plants and subsequently modify them into potent precursors for innovative pesticides.
Formononetin (FMN, 7-hydroxy-4′-methoxy isoflavones), as part of the group of isoflavones, is widely found in legumes such as Glycyrrhiza uralensis Fisch, Astragalus membranaceus, Trifolium pretense, and Pueraria lobata
[8]
. Modern pharmacological studies have shown that formononetin and its derivatives have antibacterial
[9]
,
[10]
, antiviral
[11]
,
[12]
, antitumor
[13]
,
[14]
, antioxidant
[15]
, anti-inflammatory and other biological activities
[16]
,
[17]
,
[18]
, which are widely used in the clinic with remarkable efficacy. Yet, relatively little studies have been reported about formononetin and its derivatives in the field of pesticides.
Piperidine is an organic heterocyclic amine commonly found in natural alkaloids, such as the broad-spectrum anticonvulsant piperine, the antimalarial febrifugine and the antibiotic cycloheximide (
Figure S1
). Piperidine is widely used as a building block and reagent in synthesizing organic compounds, which is a vital fundament in the production of drugs
[19]
. Its derivatives have a broad spectrum of pharmacological activities, such as antibacterial
[20]
,
[21]
,
[22]
, antiviral
[23]
, anticancer
[24]
,
[25]
, insecticidal
[26]
and so on, which makes piperidine a favored dominant skeleton in drug synthesis.
To sum up, the natural product formononetin was used as the lead compound in this study, and the flexible alkane chain was used as a bridge to introduce 1-boc-4-methylaminopiperidine to the hydroxyl group of formononetin. Then, 40 formononetin derivatives containing benzyl piperidine were obtained by modification of various substituted benzyl chloride. Although the derivatives demonstrate tremendous antibacterial potential, the precise mechanism underlying their antibacterial activity remains unclear to our current knowledge. In this study, SEM, virulence factors, defense enzyme activities assay, proteomics and metabolomics to elucidate the potential mechanism of action of formononetin derivatives containing benzyl piperidine.
Materials and methods
Instruments and chemicals
The nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra of target compounds were characterized using a JEOL ECX500 500 MHz NMR instrument (JEOL, Japan). High resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) was acquired on a Thermo Scientific Q Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). SEM images were observed using an Olympus BX53 fluorescence microscope (Olympus Ltd, Japan) and a Nova Nano SEM 450 instrument (FEI Company, USA). The melting points of target compounds were taken on an X-4B microscope melting point apparatus (Shenguang, Shanghai, China). LC-MS/MS (EASY-nLC 1200 UPLC system and Orbitrap Exploris 480 with a nano-electrospray ion source, ThermoFisher Scientific, USA) was used to obtain proteomic data. Metabolomics is based on a high flux LC-MC system (Triple TOF 6600 + SCIEX, Foster City, CA, USA) and LC-30A ultra-high liquid chromate spectrometer (Shimadzu, Japan). The raw material formononetin with 98 % purity, various substituted benzyl chloride and 1-boc-4-methylaminopiperidine, etc. were obtained from Shanghai Tansoole Company (Shanghai, China) without further purification. The reagents used were obtained from Tianjin Zhiyuan Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Tianjin, China).

Intermediate

The combined extraction liquid was dried using anhydrous Na

Synthesis of intermediate c
The intermediate

Subsequently, the boc group was eliminated by dropwise addition of a solution of 37 % HCl. The reaction was monitored using TLC and allowed to proceed for a duration of 1–2 h. After completion, a significant amount of methanol was eliminated through rotary distillation under reduced pressure. Then, the resulting mixture was transferred into 100 mL of water and subjected to three extractions with 20 mL ethyl acetate to eliminate certain impurities. After adding a suitable 5 % NaHCO
3

Enzyme activity test
The compound
H32

TC and ZT were employed as positive controls. The as-prepared agents were evenly sprayed onto the surface of the pot rice plants until the water droplets reached the point of almost falling. After 24 h, 2 to 3 cm sections were excised from the top of the rice leaves, and the wound leaves were immersed in the bacterial solution for 5 to 10 s. Rice samples were collected on days 1, 3, 5, and 7 and stored at −80 °C in a refrigerator for later use. The enzyme activities of catalase (CAT), peroxidase (POD), and superoxide dismutase (SOD) were quantified in accordance with the instructions provided by the kit (Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China).
Label-Free quantitative proteomics test
Proteomics is essentially a large-scale study of the characteristics of proteins under varying conditions, encompassing protein expression levels, post-translational modifications, protein–protein interactions, and other factors
[35]
. It aims to achieve a comprehensive and holistic understanding of disease mechanisms, cellular metabolism, and other processes at the protein level
[36]
. The experimental procedure and analysis of label-free quantitative proteomics are based on previously reported methods with slight modifications
[37]
,
[38]
. The protein samples utilized in this study were 1 % DMSO-treated
Xoo
protein and
H32
-treated
Xoo
protein, which were subsequently extracted and digested. In brief,
Xoo
was cultured in NB medium at 28–30 °C and 200 r/min in a shaker until reaching an optical density (OD
595
) of 0.6–0.8. Subsequently, the sterilized medium was employed to adjust its OD
595
to 0.1, and the bacterial solution was then distributed into sterile 100 mL conical bottles with each bottle containing 50 mL. The control group was supplemented with 1 % DMSO, while the treatment group received a DMSO solution of
H32
. The treatment group was exposed to final concentrations of 0.5 × EC
50
(0.035 μg/mL), EC
50
(0.07 μg/mL), 2 × EC
50
(0.14 μg/mL), 5 × EC
50
(0.35 μg/mL) and 10 × EC
50
(0.7 μg/mL). Each concentration was tested in triplicate. The control and treatment groups were incubated together in a shaker at 28-30℃ and 200 r/min until the optical density (OD
595
) reached 0.6–0.8 in the control group, while an OD
595
of 0.3–0.4 was considered optimal for the treatment group. The samples of the control group and the optimal treatment group were subsequently centrifuged at 4 °C and 8000 r/min. After discarding the supernatant, the collected bacteria were cleaned 3 times with PBS buffer. Subsequently, the samples of each group were added with 4 times the volume of cracking buffer (8 mol/L urea, 1 % protease inhibitor), respectively, for ultrasonic cracking. After that, the supernatant was collected, and the protein concentration was determined with BCA kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The sample was slowly added to the final concentration of 20 % (m/v) TCA to precipitate protein, then vortexed to mix and incubated for 2 h at 4 °C. The precipitate was collected by centrifugation at 4500 g for 5 min at 4 °C. The precipitated protein was washed with pre-cooled acetone for 3 times and dried for 1 min. The protein sample was then redissolved in 200 mmol/L TEAB and ultrasonically dispersed. Trypsin was added at 1:50 trypsin-to-protein mass ratio for the first digestion overnight. The sample was reduced with 5 mmol/L dithiothreitol for 30 min at 56 °C and alkylated with 11 mmol/L iodoacetamide for 15 min at room temperature in darkness. Finally, the peptides were desalted by Strata X SPE column. Subsequently, the peptides were dissolved by liquid chromatography mobile phase A and separated by EASY-nLC1200 ultra-high performance liquid phase system, then injected into NSI ion source for ionization, and finally analyzed by Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometry.
Untargeted metabolomics test
Metabolites are the basis of an organism's phenotype and can help us understand biological processes and their mechanisms more intuitively and effectively
[39]
. Based on qualitative and quantitative analysis of metabolites, metabolites can be utilized to investigate metabolic pathways or networks, enabling research on diverse macro phenotypic manifestations in individuals as well as the reaction mechanisms induced by various diseases, drugs, and pathogens
[40]
. In this study, a series of cutting-edge technologies such as metabolite extraction, liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry tandem analysis and bioinformation analysis were organically combined to conduct untargeted metabolomics studies on the samples. The collection of bacteria is the same as proteomics. The samples, which had been stored at −80 °C refrigerator, were thawed on ice. The thawed samples were homogenized by a grinder (30 Hz) for 20 s. A 400 μL solution (Methanol: Water = 4:1, v/v) containing internal standard was added into 40 mg of the sample and vortexed for 3 min. The samples were placed in liquid nitrogen for 5 min and on the dry ice for 5 min, and then thawed on ice and vortexed for 2 min. This freeze–thaw circle was repeated three times in total. The samples were centrifuged at 12,000 r/min for 10 min (4 °C). Afterward, the supernatant (300 μL) was collected and stored at −20 °C for 30 min. Finally, the samples underwent another centrifugation step at 12,000 r/mim for 3 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatant (200 μL) was transferred for LC-MS analysis.
qRT-PCR verification experiment
The bacterial collection method employed in this experiment is identical to the one utilized in proteomics. The total RNA extraction procedure was followed to extract total RNA from the collected bacteria of both the control group and the drug treatment group, respectively. The cDNA was then synthesized by reverse transcription according to the instructions for cDNA synthesis using the cDNA Kit. The amplification was ultimately accomplished through qRT-PCR following specific protocols. Meanwhile, the 16S gene was used as an internal reference control gene. Relative expressions of each transcript were calculated using the 2
−ΔΔCt
method.

Analysis of the effect of H32 on the growth rate of
Xoo
The bacterial growth curve reflects the population growth of unicellular microorganisms in liquid culture under certain environmental conditions
[41]
. According to the different growth rates, the growth curve could be divided into delayed phase, logarithmic phase, stable phase and decline phase
[42]
. As shown
Fig. 3

, the growth trend of
Xoo
treated with
H32
remained consistent in the logarithmic growth phase and the stationary phase in the range of 0 to 1.56 μg/mL compared with the control group. The growth rate of
Xoo
decreased as the concentration of
H32
increased, indicating a negative correlation between the growth rate and drug concentration. Furthermore, when the concentration of
H32
approached 3.125 μg/mL, the growth of
Xoo
almost stopped, displaying that
H32
had a significant inhibitory effect on the growth of
Xoo
.

Effect of H32 on
Xoo
virulence factors
Biofilm formation and EPS production
.
Biofilms are important virulence factors of some pathogenic microorganisms and can enhance the virulence of pathogens
[43]
. The results in
Fig. 3
B
showed that the ability of pathogen biofilm formation was decreased in the drug treatment group compared with the control group. As shown in
Fig. 3
B
, the absorbance of crystal violet at 570 nm gradually decreases with the increase of
H32
concentration. This observation suggests indicating that
H32
exhibits inhibitory effects on pathogenic biofilm formation, consequently reducing pathogen resistance to drug stress and ultimately leading to a decline or elimination of pathogen infection and colonization in the host. During the establishment of microcolonies and biofilm formation, bacteria secrete EPS to protect themselves from adverse environmental stresses. Bacterial EPS is the main substrate required for biofilm formation, and it also helps bacteria resist the defense response of host plants. At the same time, it may interfere with antibiotics, which is more conducive to the infection of pathogens. As shown in
Fig. 3
C, H32
affected EPS production in a concentration-dependent manner. These results suggested that
H32
could limit biofilm formation by reducing EPS production, thus reducing the resistance of bacteria to host plant defense responses and further inhibiting their ability to infect host plants.
Effect of H32 on the extracellular enzymes ofXoo
The plant cell wall is a cytoprotective barrier. Plant pathogens can secrete a large number of cell wall-degrading enzymes through quorum sensing to degrade plant cell walls and obtain nutrients required for growth, facilitating bacterial colonization and infection of host plants
[44]
. In this study, the extracellular cellulase and amylase of
Xoo
were studied, and the results are shown in
Fig. 3
D and E
. Among them, the effect of
H32
on cellulase is shown in
Fig. 3
D
. On the cellulose plate stained with 1 % congo red, a clear hydrolysis circle could be observed in the control group, with an average diameter of 21.6 mm. In the
H32
treatment group, a significant decrease in the diameter of the hydrolysis circle was observed at concentrations of 10, 100, and 200 μg/mL. Specifically, the measurements were 18.4, 15.3 and 13.9 mm, corresponding to inhibition rates of 14.6, 29.1 and 35.8 %. The effect of
H32
on amylase was demonstrated in
Fig. 3
E
, where a visibly transparent hydrolysis zone could be observed on the starch solid medium treated with I
2
/KI, exhibiting an average diameter of 18.8 mm in the control group. The hydrolysis circle of
H32
treatment group exhibited a significant reduction at concentrations of 10, 100 and 200 μg/mL, with corresponding diameters measuring 14.8, 11.9 and 8.4 mm respectively. Moreover, the inhibition rates were 37.2, 63.9 and 96.5 % for these respective concentrations. These results suggested
H32
could affect the colonization and invasion of the pathogen by affecting the secretion of extracellular cellulase and amylase, thereby reducing the pathogenicity of the pathogen, which is consistent with the results of
H32
on
Xoo
biofilm formation and EPS production.
Effect of H32 on the motility ofXoo
Bacterial motility is usually regulated by complex networks and closely related to virulence
[45]
. Motility can promote the adhesion and invasion of bacteria to host cells, which is closely related to biofilm formation and bacterial virulence
[46]
. The effect of
H32
on the swarming motility of
Xoo
colonies was tested and compared on 0.6 % agar plates
(
Figure S2
). Bacteria
Xoo
spread around from the inoculation point on semi-solid medium in the control group, forming large bacterial circles with an average diameter of 28.8 mm. The bacterial circles spreading outward in the control group treated with
H32
at concentrations of 0.125, 0.25, 0.5 and 1.0 μg/mL exhibited significant reductions, displaying average diameters of 22.0, 13.7, 6.3 and 0 mm respectively. Correspondingly, the inhibition rates were 23.5, 52.3, 79.1 and 100 %. These results suggested that
H32
could reduce the virulence of the pathogen and inhibit its colonization and infection of the host by limiting the motility of

Metabolomics analysis
Metabolomics is the culmination of the transcriptome and proteome, forming a highly interconnected system that directly reflects the overall phenotypic traits of an organism
[48]
. To gain further insights into the metabolic response and adaptive mechanism of bacteria in the presence of
H32
, as well as to elucidate the role of bacterial metabolism in the antibacterial process mediated by
H32
, untargeted metabolomics was employed in this study to facilitate the identification of potential metabolic targets, thereby enhancing our comprehension of the interaction between
H32
and
Xoo
bacteria. Furthermore, through integration with proteomics analysis, we revealed the antibacterial mechanism of
H32
. Firstly, the metabolites were extracted from the collected bacterial samples and subjected to LC-MS analysis. TIC overlap, comprehensive correlation analysis and CV filtering were used to ensure the validity of the data. Secondly, multiple factor analysis (FC), hypothesis testing (p value analysis), principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least squares (OPLS-DA) were used to analyze the obtained high-quality data (
Fig. 5
A and 5B
). Finally, the screening threshold of VIP > 1 and p < 0.05 between the treatment and control groups was set to highlight metabolites with significant differences and the up-regulated and down-regulated data of these metabolites. In total, a total of 3028 metabolites were identified in the control and
H32
-treated groups in this study, of which 1206 were differential metabolites, 317 were up-regulated and 889 were down-regulated (
Fig. 5
C
). It could be observed from the differential metabolites bar chart (
Figure S3
) that the proportion of significantly down-regulated metabolites (18 metabolites) in the top 20 metabolites was much higher than that of up-regulated metabolites (2 metabolites). In addition, the volcano plot (
Fig. 5
D
) and differential metabolites clustering heat map (
Fig. 5
E
) revealed that the number of up-regulated metabolites is significantly less than the number of down-regulated metabolites, showing the resistance effect mediated by differential metabolites. The interaction of metabolites in bacteria gives rise to diverse metabolic pathways. The differential metabolites were annotated using the KEGG database, and subsequently classified based on KEGG pathway type. Enrichment analysis was then performed on the results of the differential metabolites, resulting in 97 enriched pathways. The bubble map in
Fig. 5
F
illustrates the top 20 enriched KEGG pathways, primarily encompassing acid biosynthesis, cofactor biosynthesis, microbial metabolism in different environments, valine, leucine and isoleucine biosynthesis, etc. Most of these pathways are involved in the synthesis and metabolism of amino acids, which play a crucial role in the physiological and biochemical activities of bacteria, involving the synthesis and transformation of carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, nucleic acids, nucleotides and other substances. These substances are the effective components of synthetic bacteria and are of great significance to reconstruct bacterial metabolic system and ensure the normal operation of bacterial body function. A considerable number of metabolites in the enriched KEGG metabolic pathway display downregulated, indicating that
H32
impedes amino acid synthesis, leading to a decrease in energy and nutrition necessary for bacterial life activities. Consequently, these findings suggested that
H32
could disrupt normal physiological and biochemical processes, inhibit bacterial proliferation, and eventually reduce bacterial virulence or death.

Combined analysis of proteomics and metabolomics
To further elucidate the antibacterial mechanism of
H32
and identify its possible targets, we performed co-annotation and enrichment analysis of proteomics and metabolomics data. The results revealed that 37 pathways exhibited differential expression in both proteins and metabolites (
Fig. 5
G
). Among them, one pathway (
Fig. 5
H
) was significantly enriched by both proteomics and metabolomics, involving map 00290 (Valine, leucine and isoleucine biosynthesis). Valine, leucine and isoleucine are three essential branching-chain amino acids (BCAAs) in bacteria, representing important nutrients in bacterial physiology. Their roles range from supporting protein synthesis to signaling and fine-tuning adaptation to amino acid starvation. In some pathogenic bacteria, adaptation to amino acid starvation involves the induction of virulence gene expression, so that branched-chain amino acids support not only proliferation during infection but also evasion of host defenses. A total of six proteins and seven metabolites were enriched in this pathway, of which five proteins were significantly downregulated, including A0A0K0GPQ2, A0A0K0GPP0, A0A0K0GPL2,
B2SNG5
,
B2SNG9
, and their corresponding regulatory genes were PXO_02608, ilvB, PXO_02607, ilvC, leuA, respectively. In addition, one protein (A0A0GFN6) was significantly upregulated, and its regulatory gene was PXO_03634. Seven metabolites, identified as MW0107960, MW0010027, MW0104000,
MW010525
, MEDL00575, MW0015534, MEDN1093, were all significantly downregulated. These results suggested that
H32
might affect the normal physiological activities of the bacterium
Xoo
by targeting the map 00290 pathway and affecting the synthesis of these three branched-chain amino acids, leading to the reduction of virulence and even death of the pathogen, and reducing its pathogenicity to the host. Therefore,
H32
can be used as a potential antibacterial agent against
Xanthomonas
, which provides a new idea for the development of new pesticides.

Conclusion
In summary, 40 formononetin derivatives containing benzyl piperidine were successfully synthesized, and the antibacterial activities and possible mechanisms of action were intensely elucidated. Among them,
H32
was the most effective against
Xoo
with EC
50
of 0.07 μg/mL, and
H6
was the most effective against
Xac
with EC
50
of 0.24 μg/mL. Furthermore, the efficacy of
H32
against
BLB
and
H6
against
CC
was validated through
in vivo
pot experiments. The SEM and virulence factors assay demonstrated that these highly active compounds not only disrupted the bacterial cell wall, resulting in leakage of cellular contents, but also exerted an influence on extracellular enzymes activities and motility ability of pathogen, resulting in the virulence of pathogenic bacteria to weaken or disappear. Consequently, they effectively limit biofilm formation, reduce virulence, and alleviate colonization and infection of host cells. The defense enzymes assay showed that
H32
could activate the activities of defense enzymes and improve the resistance of rice to disease. The combined proteomics and metabolomics results showed that
H32
could affect the normal physiological and biochemical activities of bacteria by regulating a variety of pathways. More specifically, the co-enrichment results of proteomics and metabolomics indicated that
H32
could inhibit the synthesis of branched-chain amino acids necessary for bacterial proliferation, leaving bacteria in a state of starvation of amino acid deficiency, thus inhibiting bacterial division and making them less pathogenic or dying. These results indicated that formononetin derivatives containing benzyl piperidine could be used as a potential and effective inhibitor against
Xanthomonas spp
. In addition, this study provides theoretical support and technical parameters for the development of formononetin as a lead compound of green pesticide.
