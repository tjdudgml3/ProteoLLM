INTRODUCTION
Plants have developed sophisticated physiological, molecular and morphological adaptations to mitigate the harmful effects of environmental extremes such as extended drought and high salinity (Backhaus et al.,
2014
; Osakabe et al.,
2014
). In addition to these inherent responses, plant resilience can be enhanced by the vast community of associated microorganisms—the plant microbiome. These microorganisms positively model the plant–soil feedback and extend the metabolic capabilities of their host (Angulo et al.,
2022
; de Vries et al.,
2018
; Petipas et al.,
2021
; Timm et al.,
2018
; Vandenkoornhuyse et al.,
2015
). For instance, xerophytic and halophytic plants inhabiting arid lands recruit plant growth‐promoting (PGP) microorganisms that enhance their tolerance to the environmental stresses typical of such ecosystems by modifying root system architecture and/or stimulating the production/accumulation of osmoprotectants and exopolysaccharides, among others (Alsharif et al.,
2020
; Cherif et al.,
2015
; Daffonchio et al.,
2015
; Köberl et al.,
2011
; Marasco et al.,
2012
,
2016
; Soussi et al.,
2016
).
The impact of microorganisms on plant health and productivity largely depends on their compositional and functional diversity within the community, as well as the nature of their interactions with the plant host (Durán et al.,
2018
; Hassani et al.,
2018
; Marasco et al.,
2021
). Among these beneficial microorganisms, members of the
Pseudomonas
genus—a recurrent and abundant group in the soil, plant root and rhizosphere—stand out for their PGP properties (Cherif et al.,
2015
; Ferjani et al.,
2015
; Lundberg et al.,
2012
; Mercado‐Blanco & Bakker,
2007
; Mosqueira et al.,
2019
). Yet, pseudomonads are ubiquitously distributed across various soil types, including saline, sodic and semi‐arid/arid soils (Chatterjee et al.,
2017
; Egamberdiyeva,
2005
; Mosqueira et al.,
2019
; Zerrouk et al.,
2016
). These bacteria efficiently colonize plant roots by forming biofilms on the rhizoplane (Barahona et al.,
2010
,
2016
; Noirot‐Gros et al.,
2018
; Rieusset et al.,
2020
) and/or entering root tissues as endophytes (Cherif et al.,
2015
; Liu et al.,
2017
; Mosqueira et al.,
2019
). Their capacity to interact with a broad range of host plants, including
Arabidopsis thaliana
, grapevine, soybean, cotton, rice, potato, date palm and ornamental plants, has made them a model system for studying plant–microbe interactions (Cherif et al.,
2015
; Kuklinsky‐Sobral et al.,
2004
; Mosqueira et al.,
2019
; Ruzzi & Aroca,
2015
; Surette et al.,
2003
; Vega et al.,
2005
).
Species like
P. putida
,
P. fluorescens
and
P. brassicacearum
(among others) significantly influence plant responses by inducing morphological, physiological and biochemical changes (Chu et al.,
2019
,
2020
; Etesami & Glick,
2020
; Li et al.,
2022
), acting as biostimulators, biofertilizers and biocontrollers (Ashajyothi et al.,
2020
; Bashan et al.,
2004
; Bashan & Holguin,
1998
; Etesami & Glick,
2020
; Glick,
2012
; Molina et al.,
2020
; Ortiz‐Castro et al.,
2020
; Zamioudis et al.,
2013
). A distinguishing feature of many
Pseudomonas
strains is their ability to produce phytohormone‐like molecules, such as indole‐3‐acetic acid (IAA), one of the most common and well‐studied auxins (Chu et al.,
2020
; Kong et al.,
2017
; Ortiz‐Castro et al.,
2011
; Persello‐Cartieaux et al.,
2001
; Sukumar et al.,
2013
; Wang et al.,
2005
; Zamioudis et al.,
2013
; Zerrouk et al.,
2016
). IAA particularly plays a critical role in plant growth and development, regulating key processes such as cell elongation, division and differentiation. Such processes are essential for controlling and modulating root development and architecture (Chu et al.,
2020
; Li et al.,
2022
; Ortiz‐Castro et al.,
2020
; Yasmin et al.,
2020
; Zamioudis et al.,
2013
). For instance, applying exogenous IAA or IAA‐related metabolites modifies root branching patterns, stimulating root hair elongation, lateral root formation and gravitropism (De Smet et al.,
2007
; Spaepen et al.,
2007
); all these traits are relevant in anchoring the plant to the soil and enhancing root exploration capacity for water and nutrients, especially under abiotic stress conditions like drought and salinity (Chu et al.,
2019
; Etesami & Glick,
2020
; Glick,
2012
; Ngumbi & Kloepper,
2016
).
Auxin homeostasis in plants is regulated by both its synthesis and transport mechanisms (Roychoudhry & Kepinski,
2022
). The establishment of an auxin gradient within plant tissues, as well as auxin maxima in meristems, are mediated by polar auxin transport, which involves proteins such as the PIN efflux carriers and AUX1 (and LAX) influx carriers (Grieneisen et al.,
2007
). These gradients regulate cell division and differentiation, helping to define and guide root growth. It is generally agreed that IAA‐producing bacteria—such as
Pseudomonas
spp.—can modify endogenous homeostasis/production of auxin and stimulate auxin‐inducible gene expression in plants, modulating root architecture, growth patterns and stress tolerance (Etesami & Glick,
2024
; Pantoja‐Guerra et al.,
2023
). However, several other bacterial‐derived molecules can influence auxin signalling by directly modulating auxin biosynthesis, transport and degradation or by interacting with other plant hormones that cross‐talk with the auxin pathway, such us ACC‐deaminase, quorum‐sensing signals, volatile organic compounds and other hormones (Glick,
2014
; Ortiz‐Castro et al.,
2011
; Schaller et al.,
2014
; Zhang et al.,
2007
). This fine‐tuned chemical communication between plants and bacteria highlights the complexity of their interactions (Etesami & Glick,
2024
).
Although the beneficial effects of PGP pseudomonads have often been linked to their IAA production, how these bacteria influence auxin signalling and transport and how these changes further contribute to plant growth is still overlooked. In a previous report, we demonstrated that two beneficial IAA‐producing
Pseudomonas
(strains E102 and E141), isolated from the root tissues of date palm (
Phoenix dactylifera
cv. Deglet Nour) in a Saharan oasis, promote host growth under extended drought (Cherif et al.,
2015
). These strains exhibited multiple PGP traits in vitro, dominated the root endosphere of date palms across heterogeneous desert oasis agroecosystems, and were able to colonize multiple date palm cultivars (Cherif et al.,
2015
; Mosqueira et al.,
2019
). In the present study, we further investigate how these bacteria affect plant auxin transport and response in
Arabidopsis
and protect plants under abiotic stress, specifically salinity.
Our results show that
Arabidopsis
plantlets exposed to strains E102 and E141 exhibit a root phenotype similar to those treated with IAA, characterized by a remodelled root architecture with elongated root hairs and an increased number of lateral roots. This bacterial‐mediated phenotype is associated with increased levels of the plant auxin‐responsive promoter (DR5) and alterations in IAA transport, including the mislocalization of the auxin efflux carrier PIN2. While IAA‐defective mutants of both E102 and E141 strains showed a reduced capacity to increase auxin levels, as well as a diminished ability to induce auxin‐mediated growth phenotype, that is, root hair elongation, the treatment with free‐cells supernatants or dead/non‐active cells completely abolished it. Importantly, we demonstrate that an active auxin signalling pathway is required for these bacteria to induce modifications in root system architecture, leading to adaptive advantages and enhanced plant growth under stress conditions.
EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES
Growth conditions and genome sequencing of
Pseudomonas
strains
E102
and
E141
The bacterial strains E102 and E141 (Cherif et al.,
2015
) were inoculated in Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB; Sigma) and incubated overnight at 30°C. Genomic DNA was extracted from fresh culture using the Qiagen Genomic‐tip 100/G kit (Qiagen) following the bacterial sample preparation and lysis protocol found in the Qiagen Genomic DNA Handbook. DNA was checked by agarose gel electrophoresis and quantified using a Qubit 3.0 fluorometer. Approximately 20 μg of DNA was sent to the KAUST Bioscience Core Lab facilities for sequencing. Genome sequencing was performed using a PacBio Single Molecule, Real‐Time (SMRT) Sequencing System. The HGAP software package (Pacific Biosciences) was used for genome assembly and quality assessment. Automatic genome annotation was conducted using Prokka (Seemann,
2014
), which predicted coding sequences (CDS), tRNAs, rRNAs and other non‐coding RNAs. Genome overviews were created by Circos to reveal the annotation (Cumsille et al.,
2023
). A multi‐locus sequence analysis (MLSA) and a phylogenetic tree, which included the alignment of 120 concatenated single‐copy conserved marker genes of bacterial genomes, were conducted de novo using the GTDB‐Tk software on the E102 and E141 isolates and close related strains within the
Pseudomonas
genus (Chaumeil et al.,
2023
).
Functional annotation was conducted using the EggNog database (Huerta‐Cepas et al.,
2016
). We employed the PGPT‐Pred and PIFAR‐Pred tools from the plant‐associated bacteria web resource (PLaBAse) to predict a broader range of bacterial PGP traits (PGPT) and interaction factors determining the plant–bacteria association, respectively, by analysing the genomic CDS using blastp+hmmer (strict) algorithm. The databases were accessed via the web platform (
https://plabase.cs.uni‐tuebingen.de/pb/plabase.php
). Analysis of genes involved in specific PGP activities—based on previously published data (Andrés‐Barrao et al.,
2017
; Boch et al.,
1996
; Bruto et al.,
2014
; Saha et al.,
2016
; Shariati et al.,
2017
)—was also conducted using P‐BLAST (
e
‐value < 10
−6
) against the UniProt database to evaluate the bacterial potential to synthesize osmolytes, aminocyclopropane‐1‐carboxylate (ACC) deaminase, IAA and exopolysaccharide (EPS). Finally, biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) associated with secondary metabolite production in E102 and E141 genomes were predicted and analysed using the antiSMASH (antibiotics & Secondary Metabolite Analysis SHell) web server (Blin et al.,
2023
). The genomes of strains E102 and E141 were deposited at NCBI under accession numbers CP084025 and CP084024, respectively.

Determination of
IAA
concentration
Indole‐3‐acetic acid (IAA) produced by E102 and E141 was quantified using ultrahigh‐performance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC‐MSMS). The bacterial cultures of E102 and E141 were grown in TSB for 72 h in the pre‐set and absent of 0.5 mg/mL filtered
l
‐tryptophan at 30°C with 100 rpm shaking. TSB medium non‐inoculated was used as control. The cultures and control medium were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 10 min to collect the supernatant, which was further filtered by a 0.2 μm filter to remove any particulates that could clog the UPLC column. The obtained solutions were diluted 10 times before being injected into the Vanquish Flex UPLC system coupled to a TQS Altis Triple Quadrupole mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific). Details of chromatographic separation were reported in Method
S2
. A calibration curve of pure, commercial IAA (Sigma‐Aldrich) was generated. Three replicates for the strains were prepared for the experimental measurement.
IAA
biosynthesis mutant construction
Based on the genome sequences of
Pseudomonas
spp. strains E102 and E141, mutants of the IAA biosynthesis genes
iaaM
(tryptophan 2‐monooxygenase),
aldA
(aldehyde dehydrogenase) and
trpC

S1

iaaM

aldA

trpC

The synthesized fragments were then cloned in a carrier vector pUC57(Am
R
) and sequentially excised with the following couple of restriction enzymes, BamHI and KpnI, PstI and KpnI, or EcoRI and PstI, and cloned in the corresponding site in pEX19Gm (Hoang et al.,
1998

Clones with a chromosomal insertion of the pEX19Gm plasmids were selected on TS agar plates supplemented with 40 μg/mL gentamycin (Gm) and 100 μg/mL nitrofurantoin (Nf). Plasmid excision from the chromosome was subsequently selected on TS agar plates supplemented with 15% (w/v) sucrose. The mutants were verified by PCR using primers specific to the genomic DNA sequences upstream and downstream of the targeted genes (Table
S1
). PCR products were evaluated on agarose gel and deletions were confirmed (Figure
S1
). IAA production was finally quantified, as described above. Regardless of the inactivation of three genes, IAA biosynthesis mutant △
iaaM‐aldA‐trpC
of strains E102 and E141 retained their ability to produce IAA, even if to a reduced extent (Figure
S2
), suggesting that other IAA synthesis pathways can be activated in these

Effect of bacterial treatments on
Arabidopsis
development and growth
An in vivo screening was further conducted to evaluate the effect of strains E102 and E141 (and IAA biosynthesis mutant △
iaaM‐aldA‐trpC
of the two strains) using
Arabidopsis
Col‐0 under different stress conditions, that is, the absence and presence of salt stress. Surface sterilization of the seeds was performed by soaking
Arabidopsis
seeds (Col‐0) in 70% ethanol for 2 min, followed by 1% hypochlorite solution for 20 min and rinsing 5–6 times with sterile distilled water. Seeds were resuspended in a 0.1% agarose solution and placed at 4°C for 3 days to break dormancy. Individual seeds were plated on ½ MS solid medium (0.8% agar) supplemented with 1% sucrose for germination. Plates were placed vertically in a growth chamber at 23°C, 55% relative humidity and 16/8 h light/dark cycle. Four‐days after germination, seedlings were transferred to new plates with ½ MS solid medium (without sucrose) to evaluate bacteria treatment effects under no (0 mM NaCl), mild (50 mM NaCl) and severe (100 mM NaCl) salinity (Claeys et al.,
2014
). Bacterial strains were grown overnight in the TSB medium, following the conditions described above. Cell concentration was assessed, and the correct number of bacterial cells were inoculated in the selected medium just before solidification (i.e. 10
7
cells/plate or increasing concentration 0, 10
1
, 10
3
, 10
5
and 10
7
bacterial cells/plate). Three plates were prepared for each treatment, with seven plants per plate and the experiment was repeated three times to ensure reproducibility. The square plates were incubated in the growth chamber under the abovementioned conditions. Three‐days after the treatment (d.a.t.), plates were visualized at the Nikon stereomicroscope SMZ‐25 and the obtained images were used to get the number of root hairs developed in the root hair emergence zone (~500 μm above the tip). Each square plate was scanned at 7 d.a.t., and the images were analysed using ImageJ software to obtain the number of lateral roots and root length. Further scanning was performed at 14 d.a.t. before measuring the fresh shoots and root biomasses using an analytical balance. A total of 10–15 plants were measured for each treatment. Post hoc Tukey's multiple comparison tests were used to compare the effect of the treatments. Spearman correlation was applied to evaluate the relationship among the root architecture traits (root hairs, lateral root and root length) and the bacterial concentration to which plants were exposed.

RESULTS
Phylogenomic analysis and metabolic potential of
Pseudomonas
strains
E102
and
E141
The genomes of E102 and E141 strains were sequenced (Figure
S3
and Table
S3
). Strain E102 has a genome size of 5.7 Mbp with G + C content of 60.6 mol% and 5080 coding DNA sequence (CDS), while strain E141 has a genome with ~400,000 bp more nucleotides but a similar G + C content (60.3 mol%). Both genomes were contained within a single contig. Strain E012 and E141 show 4364 and 4676 annotated protein‐coding genes, respectively; both have a total of 16 rRNA genes and 68 tRNA (Table
S3
). The E102 and E141 genomes share 4012 genes, while they have 830 and 1136 specific genes, respectively (Figure
S4
and Data
S2
). Phylogenetic position in a neighbour‐joining phylogenomic tree constructed from a multi‐locus sequence analysis (MLSA) based on 107 single‐copy essential genes (Figure
S5
) affiliates strain E102 to
Pseudomonas frederiksbergensis
and strain E141 to
Pseudomonas rhizophila
(previously identified as
P. brassicacearum
based on 16S rRNA gene analysis in Cherif et al.,
2015
). Further genome analyses were conducted to assess the metabolic potential of the two strains. They encode for multiple PGP traits, with genes annotated by PGPT‐Pred mainly categorized in plant system colonization, bio‐fertilization, stress control (biocontrol), phytohormones and plant‐signal production (Figure
S6
and Data
S3
). Specifically, we detected in both strains the genes involved in tryptophan‐dependent IAA synthesis, including
iaaM
and
iaaH
of the indole‐3‐acetamide pathway and
aldA
of the indole‐3‐pyruvate pathway, as well as genes (
trpA
,
trpB
,
trpC
and
trpD
) related to the production of tryptophan (Table
1
and Data
S3
), the primary precursor in microbial IAA biosynthesis. The ACC deaminase enzyme was only found in the E141 genome (Table
1
); this enzyme catalyses the breakdown of ACC, which is the immediate precursor of ethylene, a plant hormone involved in growth regulation, stress responses and senescence (Glick,
2014
). Among biofertilization activities, both strains encode for nitrogen fixation, siderophore production and/or transport, phosphate solubilization via organic acid production (gluconic acid and 2‐ketogluconic acid) and/or phosphonate degradation, and potassium solubilization (Table
1
, Figures
S6
and
S7
and Data
S3
). The two strains are also equipped to cope with salinity and drought stress, typically present in desert oases. Specifically, they encode genes involved in synthesizing and transporting of compatible solutes and osmoprotectants, such as glycine‐betaine, proline and trehalose (Table
1
and Figure
S6
).

In
Arabidopsis
, the development of root hairs is organized in a specific pattern in the epidermis where two types of cells can be distinguished: hair cells and non‐hair cells (Schiefelbein et al.,
2014
). These cells are regulated by a specific network where CAPRICE (CPC) and TRYPTICHON (TRY) genes promote differentiation of hair‐forming cells, and WEREWOLF (WER) blocks root hair formation in the non‐hair cells (Schiefelbein et al.,
2014
). To test whether the observed increase of RH results from a rapid hair elongation or an alteration of the root hair distribution pattern leading to root hair emergence from the non‐hair cell rows, we evaluated the effect of bacteria on the non‐hair/hair cells pattern (N‐cells/H‐cells) by detecting the presence of the transcription factor
WEREWOLF
in the N‐cells (Bernhardt et al.,
2003
; Galway et al.,
1994
; Lee & Schiefelbein,
1999
; Masucci et al.,
1996
). Using the
pWEREWOLF::CFP
reporter (Hassan et al.,
2010
), we found that the bacterial treatment induced RH elongation without influencing their distribution pattern (i.e. absence of RH in N‐cell lines; reporter line in Figure
2D
). These results indicate that E102 and E141 strains do not stimulate ectopic RH formation but rather their elongation. Since the bacterial treatment increased the elongation of RH, we then checked whether treatments with bacteria could bypass the RH regulatory network. We used the
cpc‐try
double mutant devoid of RH (Schellmann et al.,
2002
) and found that the bacteria do not induce RH formation (Figure
S11
and Table
S4
). Similarly, since bacteria were in part recruited/associated in/with the root cap region (Figure
1
), we evaluated whether alteration of the root cap, in terms of layers and size, can compromize the effect of the bacteria on the roots. In
Arabidopsis
, root cap development is regulated by two sets of transcription factors, FEZ and SOMBRERO (Willemsen et al.,
2008
): while
fez
mutant knockout possesses fewer root cap layers,
sombrero
(
smb
) displays additional root cap layers. By testing both
fez
and
smb
(Figure
S11
and Table
S4
), we found that E102 and E141 strains have a reduced RH elongation in
fez
mutants (i.e. fewer cap layers) when compared to wild type (E102

vs. E102
WT
:
p
= 0.0002 and E14
fez
vs. E141
WT
:
p
= 0.0008), while the RH elongation measured in the treated
smb

vs. E102

:
p
= 0.25 and E14

:

Bacteria promotion of root hair elongation is mediated by auxin
The root hair phenotype mediated by E102 and E141 treatments closely resembled those induced by exogenous IAA, the hormone that regulates many aspects of plant growth and development, including root hair elongation, root zonation and lateral root formation (De Smet et al.,
2007
; Dello Ioio et al.,
2007
; Fukaki et al.,
2005
). Both E102 and E141 strains were shown to produce IAA when cultured in the presence of tryptophan (L‐Trp), whereas IAA production was drastically reduced in its absence (Figure
S2
). It is important to note that treatments with IAA‐defective mutants of E102 and E141 resulted in a significant reduction in RH elongation (Figure
S12
and Table
S5
). Application of cell‐free supernatants from E102 and E141 cultures further diminished the RH elongation, which was completely absent when plants were treated with dead cells of either E102 or E141 (Figure
S13
). These results suggest that the direct interaction between live, actively colonizing bacteria and plant roots is crucial for inducing the root hair elongation phenotype rather than exposure of the roots to bacterial‐derived molecules/signals, such as IAA. Furthermore, these findings indicate a potential relationship between IAA produced/released by the actively colonizing bacteria and the observed changes in RH phenotype in treated plants.

Given the increased
DR5
activity, we next examined whether this bacterial effect depends on a functional auxin signalling pathway. The auxin signalling cascade requires auxin binding to the transport inhibitor response (TIR) receptor and the transcriptional repressors Aux/IAA (Lavy & Estelle,
2016
). Auxin binding leads to ubiquitination and degradation of Aux/IAA proteins, which modulates the activity of the auxin response factor (ARF), thereby regulating auxin‐mediated growth responses (Wang & Estelle,
2014
). To test it, we used the
axr3‐1
mutant (
axr3‐1‐DR5::GFP
), which harbours a semi‐dominant mutation in domain II in the AXR3/IAA17 gene, impairing auxin response, that is, insensitive to IAA (Knox et al.,
2003
; Leyser et al.,
1996
; Rouse,
1998
; Swarup et al.,
2005
). The treatment of the
axr3‐1
mutant with the E102 and E141 did not result in any detectable change in auxin distribution or root hair elongation, showing phenotypes comparable to those of the mock plants (Figure
4I–M
). These results indicate that the E102 and E141 bacterial strains require an intact, functional auxin signalling pathway to exert their promoting effects. However, when WT
Arabidopsis
plants were treated with L‐Kyr and Yucasin—two inhibitors of IAA biosynthesis—the bacteria partially induced the auxin‐dependent response (Figure
4N–P
) with signal intensities that did not reach those obtained without inhibitors. The ability of bacteria to elicit an auxin response, even when the plant's IAA production is inhibited, suggests that bacterial IAA can partially compensate for the plant's deficiency and be transported throughout the root tissues.
Bacteria use and modulate the polar auxin transport to change auxin distribution
Since the E102 and E141 strains displayed similar effects on the root system and auxin distribution, we focused only on the E102 strain to conduct a detailed analysis of the bacteria‐mediated effect on auxin transport. The spatial distribution of auxin in the root is mediated by polar auxin transport (PAT), with the influx carriers of the AUX/LAX family pumping auxin into the cells (Swarup et al.,
2001
,
2008
) and the PIN‐FORMED (PIN) family of efflux carriers transporting auxin out of the cells (Gälweiler et al.,
1998
; Müller et al.,
1998
; Friml et al.,
2003
). To determine whether the E102 bacterial strain can influence PAT in the roots, we first monitored the expression of the reporter line
pAUX1::AUX1‐YFP
(Table
S2
). In plants treated with bacterial cells, we observed a reduction in
AUX1
expression in the root meristem (Figure
5A,B
). However, it was compensated by an increment of expression in the vasculature tissues and in the zone of emerging lateral primordia within the differentiation zone (see arrows in Figure
5A,B
). Next, we analysed the localization and expression of PIN proteins using
pPIN1::PIN1‐GFP
,
pPIN2::PIN2‐GFP
and
pPIN7::PIN7‐GFP
transgenic lines (Table
S2
). We observed a decrease in the expression of all three PIN proteins in the root meristem (Figure
5C,D
and Figure
S16
). Along with this decrease in expression, we observed a change in the
PIN2
localization in the epidermis, from being uniform at the plasma membrane (mock in Figure
5E
) to becoming non‐polar and localized in intracellular compartments (Figure
5F
). Since PIN proteins have been shown to cycle between the plasma membrane and the endosomes (Geldner et al.,
2001
; Tanaka et al.,
2013
), we used the endocytic tracer FM4‐64 (Bolte et al.,
2004
) to assess whether it colocalizes with
PIN2
protein upon treatment with the bacteria. In the E102‐treated roots, only a fraction of
PIN2
colocalized with the FM4‐64, indicating that the effect of the bacterium on
PIN2
internalization does not exclusively rely on endocytosis (Figure
5G,H
). It indicates that strain E102 modifies PIN protein localization by modulating their expression and intracellular cycling, polar auxin transport and auxin distribution.

DISCUSSION
Overall, our results showed that the presence of the pseudomonads E102 and E141 strains significantly rearranges the root system architecture of
Arabidopsis
, including the promotion of root hair elongation and lateral root formation, by modifying auxin signalling and transport in the plant (Figure
7
). These modifications confer adaptive advantages in response to abiotic stresses, such as salinity and drought, in different plant species, that is,
Arabidopsis
and lettuce (this study) and date palm (Cherif et al.,
2015
). These findings are reminiscent of the previously described function of other
Pseudomonas
strains isolated from arid environments that induce beneficial effects on the plant hosts under drought conditions (Rolli et al.,
2015
). Our data also indicate that they might represent a conserved mechanism used by desert‐plants' microorganisms to exert their beneficial role to a given host (e.g., promote the root system). It has been shown that PGP microbial communities at the root‐soil interface and within the root tissues of plants exposed to drought and salinity stresses have the potential to improve plant resilience by modulating hormone homeostasis (Alwutayd et al.,
2023
; de Zélicourt et al.,
2018
; Eida et al.,
2018
; Mayak et al.,
2004
; Papadopoulou et al.,
2023
; Schmitz et al.,
2022
; Yang et al.,
2009
). Such increase in plant fitness mediated by the desert microbiome opens promising new avenues for establishing/supporting agriculture in arid (or even conventional) environments, which are experiencing increasing water‐stress related extremes because of the ongoing climate change.

Pseudomonas

These morphological changes are accompanied by alterations in PIN2 localization, as PIN2 protein becomes more internalized in vesicles alongside increased levels of the auxin response signal, as shown by the synthetic auxin‐responsive promoter

Plant root systems modulate their architecture to cope with the changes in their environment, favouring soil anchorage, water and nutrient uptake (López‐Bucio et al.,
2003
; Nibau et al.,
2008
). The overall changes induced by microorganisms in the root architecture (here, a higher number of secondary roots and enhanced root hair elongation) suggest that the mechanism used to induce plant resistance under abiotic stress increases the effective root surface available/involved in water and nutrient uptake (Tachibana & Ohta,
1983
). As most soils have an uneven distribution of resources (Wang et al.,
2020
), the spatial distribution of the root system, including root elongation and branching, is strategic to efficiently explore, reach and uptake the available nutrients and water (Lynch,
1995
,
2007
), especially under drought and salinity stresses when the plant needs to reduce the cytotoxicity caused by the accumulation of ions (e.g. Na
+
) to sustain the metabolism and, ultimately, the survival (Isayenkov & Maathuis,
2019
; Koevoets et al.,
2016
; Morton et al.,
2018
). At the same time, a rise in root branching determines an increase in the available root exudates in the rhizosphere, essential for the successful colonization of the rhizosphere and rhizoplane by edaphic microorganisms (Herms et al.,
2022
; Vives‐Peris et al.,
2020
). Interestingly, the E102 and E141 strains greatly colonize the root, with a preference for root hairs and root cap, as previously observed for other pseudomonads (Tovi et al.,
2019
). These preferential colonization sites are possibly related to differences in the available resources; for instance, root deposits and exudates are mainly abundant near the root tip and in the maturation zone, where numerous root hairs are present (Badri & Vivanco,
2009
; Dennis et al.,
2010
; Sasse et al.,
2018
; Walker et al.,
2003
). In addition, the metabolic capabilities of bacteria, such as motility, biofilm formation and exopolysaccharide release, also determine their capacity and preferential sites during colonization (Badri & Vivanco,
2009
). In the case of E102 and E141 strains, the functional genomic comparison and in vitro tests revealed many similarities; they have a relatively high number of rRNA genes, along with several genes involved in carbohydrate metabolism and they both produce EPS, depicting typical traits of plant‐associated microorganisms, which are able to respond fast to the changes in nutrient availability, favouring their positive selection and biofilm formation (Azimi et al.,
2016
; Klappenbach et al.,
2000
; Niazi et al.,
2014
; Noirot‐Gros et al.,
2018
; Shrestha et al.,
2007
; Trujillo et al.,
2014
). Interestingly, the root modifications observed here were only in plants colonized by living bacterial cells and bacterial culture‐filtered supernatant but not in plants exposed to dead bacterial cells. These results support the assumption that, for certain bacteria, contact with the plant (or the metabolites they release) is necessary to exert PGP services.
Many aspects of plant root growth and development, including RH elongation and LR development (Overvoorde et al.,
2010
), are triggered and regulated by auxin, a class of plant hormones of which the IAA is the most studied. Considering that auxin is essential in many aspects of plant development, it is not surprising that it is one of the primary targets for some bacteria, including members of the
Pseudomonas
genus (Jiménez‐Vázquez et al.,
2020
; Ng et al.,
2015
; Ortiz‐Castro et al.,
2020
; Sukumar et al.,
2013
; Uzma et al.,
2022
; Wang et al.,
2015
; Zamioudis et al.,
2013
). In many instances, the inoculation of plants with PGP bacteria induces changes in root architecture, mainly by stimulating root hair elongation, lateral root formation and shortening the root length (Spaepen et al.,
2007
; Spaepen & Vanderleyden,
2011
), as we also observed in our study. These modifications were attributed to the activation of the plant auxin response (Knox et al.,
2003
; Pitts et al.,
1998
). The modifications induced by E102 and E141 were impaired in
axr3‐1
mutant, which has a reduced auxin sensitivity, that is, auxin‐resistant phenotypes (Leyser et al.,
1996
). The absence of the
AXR3
gene, encoding an AUX/IAA protein belonging to the auxin transcriptional repressor family, impedes the IAA transduction and, consequently, the normal auxin signalling. This result indicates that our PGP bacteria need a functional auxin response pathway to trigger the plant response, as previously observed for other PGP microorganisms, such as
Pseudomonas
,
Bacillus
,
Phyllobacterium
and
Thricoderma
(Contesto et al.,
2010
; Contreras‐Cornejo et al.,
2009
; Persello‐Cartieaux et al.,
2001
; Wang et al.,
2015
).
In addition to auxin signalling, the bacteria also affected auxin transport and distribution by reducing PIN protein levels and their plasma membrane polarity, as well as by promoting their internalization in vesicles (Figure
7
). It has already been described that PIN proteins can cycle between the plasma membrane and the endosome; while PIN internalization is usually observed when plants are treated with auxin transport inhibitors (Geldner et al.,
2001
; Paciorek et al.,
2005
), auxin inhibits endocytosis and promotes PIN2 localization in the plasma membrane (Paciorek et al.,
2005
). However, in the presence of the two pseudomonads used in this study, we observed an increase in auxin and a decrease in its transport caused by PIN2 internalization. The latter is most likely mediated by the continuous recycling of PIN2, which might be subjected to both endocytosis and exocytosis. It is known that the control of PIN polarity is a highly complex process, where posttranslational modification plays a key role (Löfke et al.,
2013
). The observed internalization of PIN2 suggests two plausible scenarios: (i) the bacteria might excrete a substance that increases auxin levels and, as a compensatory mechanism, the PIN trafficking increases, leading to an overall increased PIN internalization (Figure
7
); for instance, this alteration could be mediated by the modulation of the ADP‐ribosylation factors and guanine nucleotide exchange factors (ARF‐GEFs) (Geldner et al.,
2001
) and the ARF‐GTPase‐activating proteins that favour PIN recycling from the plasma membrane and/or (ii) the bacterial signalling molecule might have a positive effect on auxin by increasing its levels and a negative effect on polar auxin transport. However, plant secretory peptides have been shown to alter polar auxin transport and membrane PIN2 localization (Whitford et al.,
2012
), thus, it is plausible to suppose that bacteria can induce PIN posttranslational modifications, including phosphorylation important for maintaining PIN polarity or ubiquitination that promote PIN‐mediated endocytosis (Löfke et al.,
2013
). The advances in single‐cell technologies and increased sensitivity in metabolomics and proteomics will help identify the signalling molecules and provide a comprehensive understanding of the molecular mechanisms by which the bacteria modulate auxin transport and homeostasis.
CONCLUSIONS
Our findings present a mechanistic model illustrating how
Pseudomonas
bacteria can influence the root developmental programme to promote growth and enhance the adaptation of plants under salinity stress. The two tested bacteria disrupt the polar auxin transport, leading to modifications in the post‐embryonic root developmental program, characterized by increased root hair elongation and enhanced branching of the root system. Notably, the bacteria modifications depend on the plant's auxin machinery and confer an adaptive advantage to the plant exclusively under stress conditions, such as mild and severe salinity. Overall, the results obtained help understand the responses of plants treated with PGP bacteria, considering spatiotemporal developmental patterns, as well as physiological, morphological and genetic responses. It allows us to understand better one of the possible ways in which bacteria can interact with plants and the mechanisms that regulate microbe‐mediate adaptation.
