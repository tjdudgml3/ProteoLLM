Introduction
Diabetes mellitus affects 29.3 million adults with pre-diabetes in an additional 115.9 million (
Martin, 2024
). Painful diabetic neuropathy (PDN) is a disabling, intractable, and common syndrome occurring in approximately 25% of diabetics (
American Diabetes, 2011
,
Dyck, 1991
,
Spallone et al., 2012
,
Abbott et al., 2011
). The associated neuropathic pain significantly impacts the quality of life for patients (
daCosta DiBonaventura et al., 2011
). Despite the high prevalence and impact, current therapies for PDN have limited effects in treating pain (
Bril, 2011
,
Quilici, 2009
,
Callaghan et al., 2012
,
Finnerup, 2015
), fail to remediate the damage to nerves, and have side effects associated with their systemic administration (
Finnerup, 2015
,
Attal and Bouhassira, 2021
,
Shinu, 2022
). Therefore, there is an urgent need for better tolerated and more effective therapies for PDN.
PDN is characterized by neuropathic pain associated with dorsal root ganglion (DRG) nociceptor hyperexcitability and the degeneration with loss or retraction of the cutaneous DRG neuron axons that innervate the skin (small fiber neuropathy) (
Lauria and Devigili, 2007
,
Sommer and Lauria, 2007
). A critical barrier to developing effective treatments for PDN is the lack of understanding of the molecular mechanisms leading to neuropathic pain and small fiber neuropathy.
Keratinocytes are the most abundant epidermal cell type. Recent studies have discovered a new role for keratinocytes in mediating innocuous and noxious touch and thermal sensation in healthy skin (
Stucky and Mikesell, 2021
). Keratinocytes detect touch stimuli in the skin and transmit mechanical information related to pressure and brushing (
Mikesell, 2022
,
Moehring, 2018
). Optogenetic inhibition of keratinocytes
in vivo
inhibits the responses to noxious mechanical and thermal stimuli (
Moehring, 2018
,
Sadler et al., 2020
). There is also evidence that keratinocytes may contribute to persistent neuropathic pain. A study involving the transplantation of human keratinocytes into rodents with transected nerves showed increased excitability of DRG neurons and chronic pain
in vivo
(
Radtke et al., 2010
). However, the specific role of keratinocytes in PDN has not been widely investigated.
The skin is a highly complex biological system. Along with nociceptive DRG neurons, various other neuronal subpopulations terminate in the skin, both in the dermis and the epidermis (
Abbott et al., 2011
). Keratinocytes are closely juxtaposed to cutaneous nerve terminals, suggesting that there may be bidirectional communication. Interestingly, in rodents and human skin, cutaneous nerve terminals in the epidermis form synapse-like contacts and also tunnel through keratinocytes, where they form connexin 43-positive gap junctions, enabling direct cellular communication (
Talagas, 2020
,
Erbacher, 2024
). However, the functional implications of such observations remain unclear. One such ubiquitous mode of intercellular communication recently garnering more appreciation in the skin is mediated by extracellular vesicles.
Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are composed of lipids, proteins and nucleic acids (
van Niel et al., 2018
,
Mathieu et al., 2019
,
Gurung et al., 2021
,
Kalluri and LeBleu, 2020
) and were initially posited to be involved in removing cellular waste (
Pan et al., 1985
). However, a substantial body of research now suggests a wider role in intercellular communication. EVs are released from most cell types and have been linked to several neurogenerative diseases (
Rastogi, 2021
,
Fan et al., 2022
) and the progression of different cancers (
Dai, 2020
). Keratinocyte-derived extracellular vesicles (KDEVs) have demonstrated their ability to modulate melanocyte pigmentation (
Lo Cicero, 2015
), regulate dermal fibroblast gene expression (
Nasiri et al., 2020
), mediate crosstalk with macrophages in cutaneous wound healing (
Zhou, 2020
), and play a crucial role in dermal immune responses in psoriasis (
Jiang, 2019
,
Kotzerke, 2013
). Additionally, EVs derived from mesenchymal stem cells can directly alter the excitability of DRG nociceptors in mice (
Ai, 2023
). Exploration of the role of EVs in diabetes, however, has primarily focused on adipose tissue and inter-organ communication (
Ashrafizadeh et al., 2022
,
Sun, 2018
,
He, 2020
). Recent studies have unveiled a potential role for EVs in impaired wound healing associated with diabetes (
Wei, 2020
). Yet, research on the effects of EVs on diabetic neuropathy is limited and has been conducted using EVs isolated from mesenchymal (
Fan, 2020
,
Ahmed and Al-Massri, 2024
) or Schwann cells (
Jia, 2018
). Notably, EVs isolated from mesenchymal stromal cells have shown promise in ameliorating peripheral neuropathy in a mouse model of diabetes (
Fan, 2020
). Conversely, EVs derived from high glucose-stimulated Schwann cells have been found to promote the development of diabetic neuropathy in mice (
Jia, 2018
). A rigorous and comprehensive investigation of KDEVs and their role in PDN represents an important, yet understudied frontier in pain and peripheral neuropathy research.
Using size exclusion chromatography, we obtained enriched KDEVs from mice and performed an unbiased molecular cargo characterization with proteomic analysis and small RNA sequencing. We found significantly altered protein and microRNA content of KDEVs from high-fat diet (HFD)-induced PDN compared to regular diet (RD) control mice. Altered pathways were involved in axon guidance and synaptic transmission. Additionally, using an
in vivo
conditional EV reporter mouse line, we demonstrated that epidermal-originating GFP-tagged keratinocyte-derived extracellular vesicles are retrogradely trafficked into the DRG neuron cell body.
In this study, we provide the first comprehensive isolation and molecular characterization of KDEV protein and microRNA cargo in both RD and HFD mice. Our findings suggest a novel communication pathway between neurons and keratinocytes involving KDEVs, which could have implications for PDN.
Results
Keratinocytes release a diverse population of extracellular vesicles.
To study keratinocyte-derived extracellular vesicles (KDEVs), we fractionated cell-conditioned medium (CCM) from cultured adult mouse primary keratinocytes (
Moehring, 2018
,
Sadler et al., 2020
) using a qEV 35-nm size-exclusion chromatography column (
Böing, 2014
) (
Fig. 1
A). We observed an increasing total protein concentration with each successive fraction (
Fig. 1
B; silver stain) and immunoblotted for known EV markers. Fractions 2 and 3 were enriched with the EV-associated cargo markers Alix, Tsg101, and Syntenin-1 as well as the transmembrane tetraspanins CD63 and CD81 (
Arya et al., 2024
,
Mathieu et al., 2019
,
Gurung et al., 2021
,
Kalluri and LeBleu, 2020
). These fractions lacked Gm130 and Calnexin, which are Golgi-associated proteins (
Théry, 2018
) (
Fig. 1
B). Thus, pooled fractions 2 and 3, representing our highly enriched KDEV fractions with minimal free-floating protein contamination, were used for all subsequent analyses (
Wang, 2024
).

To investigate the role of KDEVs in painful diabetic neuropathy, we employed the clinically relevant and well-established HFD model of PDN (
George, 2021
,
George, 2024
,
Jayaraj, 2018
,
Menichella, 2014
), developed by feeding mice a high-fat diet for ten weeks, leading to obesity, glucose intolerance and mechanical allodynia with small-fiber degeneration (
George, 2024
,
Jayaraj, 2018
,
Menichella, 2014
). Cultured keratinocytes from the RD and HFD mice were grown to 90% confluency in low-calcium, EV-depleted medium for scratch assays. The HFD keratinocytes had impaired wound healing during the 3 days of observation (Supplemental Fig. 1A-B), consistent with what has been shown in scratch assays with high glucose exposure (
Dam, 2017
) and what is observed in diabetic patients (
Nowak et al., 2021
). Fraction 2/3 KDEVs had sizes within the range of small EVs of 59.2 ± 14.5nm (RD;
Fig. 1
C) and 70 ± 26.3nm (HFD; Supplemental Fig. 2B) by dynamic light scattering (DLS) and concentration peaks at 95 ± 6.1nm (RD;
Fig. 1
D) and 86 ± 8.7nm (HFD; Supplemental Fig. 2C) by nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA). Tunable resistive pulse sensing (TRPS) determined that the mean size average for RD KDEVs was 97.7 ± 4.5nm with a concentration peak of 72.3 ± 4.5nm (
Fig. 1
E). KDEVs from both RD and HFD were visualized with negative staining transmission (
Fig. 1
F) and cryo-electron microscopy (
Fig. 1
G), which revealed the expected crescent morphology and intact vesicular structure.
Keratinocytes release soluble factors that encourage DRG neuron axonal growth.
Given that loss of cutaneous innervation is reported in PDN (
Lauria and Devigili, 2007
,
Sommer and Lauria, 2007
,
Jayaraj, 2018
), we next sought to investigate the role of KDEVs on DRG axonal growth. We employed a microfluidics co-culture system with primary adult mouse DRG neurons in one chamber and adult mouse keratinocytes in the other (
Fig. 2
A). This setup allowed for cell medium exchange only through the microchannels between the chambers. Interestingly, we found that DRG neuronal axons crossed the microchannels separating the two chambers at a higher rate when grown in a co-culture with keratinocytes compared to when grown alone, indicating that keratinocytes release soluble growth factors that promote axonal growth (
Fig. 2
B-C).

To test whether mouse KDEVs are functional, we labeled them with DiR, which is a carbocyanine DiOC18(7) lipophilic fluorescent dye that non-covalently inserts into lipid membranes. We then applied these fluorescently labeled KDEVs to primary DRG neuron cultures and observed a robust uptake of these KDEVs by both the cell bodies and neurites of the neurons (
Fig. 2
D). To better model physiological relevance, we then cultured DRG neurons in one chamber of our microfluidic system and allowed their neurites to occupy all the microchannels, thus preventing the free flow of medium between chambers. We then added DiR-labeled KDEVs to the empty chamber to test whether they could be transported through the neurites to the DRG neuron cell body. Indeed, DiR-labeled KDEVs were readily detected in the DRG neuron cell bodies 16 h post application, suggesting the
in vitro
retrograde transport of DiR-labeled KDEVs through the neurites (
Frühbeis, 2020
) (
Fig. 2
E).
Keratinocyte-derived extracellular vesicles alter their protein cargo in painful diabetic neuropathy.
We characterized the KDEV protein content in the context of PDN using a proteomic approach (
Graykowski et al., 2020
). The analysis was performed on the pooled fractions 2/3 using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) for both RD and HFD KDEVs (
Fig. 3
A). A gene ontology enrichment analysis on proteins detected in pooled fractions 2/3 for both groups clustered in EV categories, suggesting a robust small EV enrichment for both RD and HFD KDEVs (
Fig. 3
B). Importantly, we found similar quantities based on the number of spectral counts of the canonical EV-associated proteins Alix and Syntenin-1 in both samples with decreased detected levels of Tsg101 in HFD KDEVs (
Fig. 3
C). Fractions 2/3-enriched proteins also significantly clustered in the GO enrichment term ‘Axon Development,’ (
Fig. 3
C; Insert), suggesting neuron-keratinocyte communication through KDEVs. Moreover, we identified 90 differentially expressed EV-associated proteins (EAPs; FC ≥ 1.5, p < 0.05) between RD and HFD KDEVs, with biological replicates clustering by group (
Fig. 3
D-E). The differentially expressed REACTOME pathway with ‘MAPK Family Signaling Cascades’ included both Mapk1 and Mapk3 (
Fig. 3
G left; adjusted p-value ≤ 0.05), with Mapk1 found in fraction 2/3 (
Fig. 3
F). Furthermore, a gene ontology enrichment analysis on the EAPs revealed ‘Wound Healing’ as differentially expressed in HFD KDEVs (3G middle; adjusted p-value ≤ 0.05), consistent with the known impairment in diabetic patients (
Greenhalgh, 2003
) and as shown in our 2D primary HFD keratinocyte scratch assays (Supplemental Fig. 1A-B). Notably, several annexins were differentially expressed, with annexin VII in fraction 2/3 of our immunoblots (
Fig. 3
F); annexins play a role in ESCRT-III mediated plasma membrane repair (
Häger and Nylandsted, 2019
,
Creutz, 1992
,
Sønder, 2019
,
Williams et al., 2023
) and wound healing (
Häger and Nylandsted, 2019
). Another differentially expressed pathway was ‘Neurotransmitter Secretion’ (
Fig. 3
G right; adjusted p-value ≤ 0.05), consistent with the physical localization of DRG nerve afferents, which tunnel through the keratinocyte cytoplasm to form synapse-like contacts (
Talagas, 2020
,
Erbacher, 2024
).

The microRNA cargo of keratinocyte-derived extracellular vesicles is altered in painful diabetic neuropathy.
EVs contain gene-modifying RNAs, with microRNAs being the most abundant RNA species (
Böing, 2014
). Several microRNAs have been associated with pain in diabetic neuropathy (
Fan et al., 2020
), but the KDEV microRNA content has not yet been identified in the context of PDN. Hence, we next investigated the microRNA cargo of RD and HFD KDEVs using an unbiased small-library RNA sequencing approach.
The top ten most abundant microRNAs identified, including their variants, accounted for 74.8% of the total small RNA sequenced for both groups (
Fig. 4
A). Additionally, the top two hits, the let-7 and miR-23 families, accounted for almost 40% of the total RNA sequenced for both groups. By cross-referencing the miRDB, TargetScan v7, and DIANA-microT databases (
Fig. 2
B), the predicted protein targets of these small RNAs were used to run a KEGG enrichment analysis for let-7 and miR-23, with both revealing ‘Axon Guidance’ as a predicted target pathway (
Fig. 4
C). Between the RD and HFD KDEVs, there were 35 significant differentially expressed microRNAs that clustered by group (
Fig. 4
D) and 33 with a fold change (FC) ≥ 1.5 (
Fig. 4
E). By cross-referencing the same three databases, the predicted target proteins from each microRNA were run through a gene ontology enrichment analysis with three producing significant target pathways (
Fig. 4
F; Supplemental Fig. 3F-G). Both miR-684, which has been implicated in multiple sclerosis (
Ibrahim et al., 2021
), and miR24-3p, which has been studied in the context of cancers (
Mukherjee et al., 2022
,
Cui, 2020
) along with its regulation of proliferation related to annexin-6 activity (
Lin, 2022
), revealed predicted target axon-guidance related pathways (
Fig. 4
G). Given that both the proteomic and small RNA sequencing datasets revealed predicted target pathways involved in axon guidance and that painful diabetic neuropathy is accompanied by peripheral nerve degeneration (
Jayaraj, 2018
), we next designed an
in vivo
animal model to investigate the interaction between KDEVs and DRG neuron afferent fibers.

.
Fraction 2/3 from our fractionated keratinocyte CCM consistently immunoblotted for the transmembrane tetraspanins CD63 and CD81 (
Gurung et al., 2021
) (
Fig. 1
B), in addition to the luminal EV markers Alix, Syntenin-1, and Tsg101. We crossed the commercially available CD63-emGFP fl/fl mouse line (JacksonLaboratory Strain#:036865) with K14-Cre mice to generate a K14-CD63-emGFP EV reporter mouse model (
Fig. 5
A) to label EVs originating from basal layer keratinocytes and their progeny. We observed robust GFP expression throughout the keratinocyte progeny, with K14 staining co-localizing in basal layer keratinocytes, confirming the limitation of expression to the epidermis (
Fig. 5
B). Furthermore, we immunoblotted whole epidermis cell lysate and detected both the membrane-bound CD63-GFP fusion protein and soluble GFP (
Fig. 5
C), presumably due to endogenous protein recycling. We next cultured keratinocytes from this EV-reporter mouse. As expected, we observed a strong GFP signal along the outer membrane of these keratinocytes (
Fig. 5
D). We next immunoblotted all ten fractions obtained from CCM of primary keratinocyte cultures for GFP and detected a strong GFP signal, both the fusion and soluble forms, in fractions 2/3, which corresponded to our KDEV enriched fractions (
Fig. 5
E). Furthermore, isolated GFP-labeled KDEVs were functionally internalized by DRG neurons with GFP detected in both the neuron cell bodies and neurites (Supplemental Fig. 4A). Given the gene ontology enrichment pathways highlighted from our proteomic and small RNA sequencing experiments, we next turned our attention to the DRG neurons.

Discussion
We isolated and characterized keratinocyte-derived extracellular vesicles (KDEVs) morphologically and molecularly using an unbiased proteomic and small RNA sequencing approach. Our research revealed that KDEVs alter their cargo in a mouse model of painful diabetic neuropathy (PDN), and we identified several gene ontology enrichment pathways that were differentially expressed. In both “omic” datasets, neuron-keratinocyte pathways were enriched both under normal physiology and in PDN. Additionally, we created a K14-CD63-emGFP extracellular vesicle (EV)-reporter mouse line and observed a GFP signal in the DRG neurons and neurites, suggesting that keratinocyte-derived GFP-tagged EVs are retrogradely trafficked from epidermal keratinocytes into the DRG neurons of mice.
These findings suggest a direct communication pathway between the epidermis and the peripheral nervous system, which is altered in our HFD mouse model of PDN. While there are limitations in studying EVs from
in vitro
cell cultures (
Jeppesen et al., 2023
), we first demonstrated that primary HFD keratinocytes maintained an impaired wound healing capacity through a scratch assay (
Supplementary Fig. 1
A-B), consistent with what has been shown in scratch assays with high glucose exposure (
Dam, 2017
) and what is observed in diabetic patients (
Nowak et al., 2021
). In our microfluidic paradigm, the presence of keratinocytes enhanced neurite outgrowth, indicating release of a soluble factor by keratinocytes that encourages neurite growth (
Fig. 2
A-C). Keratinocytes release a heterogenous population of EVs, which we visualized with EM (
Fig. 1
F-G); interestingly, different subpopulations of EVs possess distinct molecular properties (
Willms, 2016
), possibly explaining the differences observed in Tsg101 expression between RD and HFD KDEVs (
Fig. 2
C).
Proteomic analysis of our KDEVs revealed that they contained several catenin and plexin isoforms (3C; Insert). The canonical WNT-signaling pathway plays a significant role in axon development in the central (
Herrera, 2023
,
Huang, 2022
) and peripheral (
Duraikannu et al., 2018
) nervous systems and has been implicated in Schwann cell-axon communication (
Lewallen, 2011
). Plexins, the surface receptors for semaphorins, are involved in neuron axonal growth and guidance, as well as a host of other functions (
Alto and Terman, 2017
,
Janssen, 2010
,
Takamatsu and Kumanogoh, 2012
,
Li, 2022
). Semaphorin 4C-Plexin-b2 signaling has been reported to be markedly increased in states of persistent pain in mice, and downregulation of this pathway led to the impairment of inflammatory hypersensitivity through RhoA-ROCK-dependent mechanisms (
Paldy, 2017
). Additionally, Sema3A and Plexin A were reported to be dysregulated in the spinal cord of a HFD model of PDN (
Li et al., 2020
). To our knowledge, this is the first report of these proteins being present as KDEV cargo. Additionally, notch1, which was recently reported to facilitate neuron-to-neuron communication through EVs in the hippocampus of mice (
Wang, 2024
), was detected in our KDEVs for all three biological replicates of both diet groups (3C; Insert), suggesting that this mechanism of internalization may also apply to keratinocyte-neuron terminal endings in the skin. Notch signaling is known to play a prominent role in developing neurons, including in axon guidance (
Kopan and Ilagan, 2009
,
Zhang et al., 2023
). Pathogenic variants in the Notch ligand Jagged1 were implicated in the development of peripheral neuropathy in two independent families and confirmed in a mouse model (
Sullivan, 2020
). Thus, Notch signaling may represent an unexplored, novel communication pathway between keratinocytes and DRG neuron terminal nerve endings.
The KDEVs from both groups contain differentially expressed synaptotagmin, which is an essential component of the presynaptic vesicle release complex that facilitates vesicle fusion with the plasma membrane (
Wolfes and Dean, 2020
,
Ullah et al., 2021
), along with synaptophysin and synapsin-2, both of which are involved in regulating vesicle docking to the inner plasma membrane (
Thiel, 1993
,
Cesca et al., 2010
,
Park, 2021
) (3G; Right Panel). These proteins have all been previously reported in keratinocytes (
Talagas, 2023
). However, it is still unclear why these proteins are present as KDEV cargo and what their function in keratinocyte-to-neuron communication might be. Further experiments are required to validate all these cargo proteins, both in mice and human KDEVs, and to investigate their mechanism of action on nerve terminals.
Our small RNA sequencing dataset aligned with the proteomic data. The top two most abundant small RNAs, the let-7 and miR-23 families (
Fig. 4
A), were predicted to target several prominent signaling pathways that overlapped with the proteomic dataset, including MAPK signaling and the WNT signaling pathways (
Fig. 4
C). A recent study has indicated that partially inhibiting p38-MAPK activation in a diabetic neuropathy rat model led to anti-hyperalgesic effects, suggesting a significant role for MAPKs in nociception modulation (
Vieira, 2022
). KDEVs from both our groups contained a diverse range of MAP kinases (MAPKs), with Mapk1 differentially expressed in our HFD model. Both let-7 and miR-23 have been shown to modulate MAPK activity (
Aghaee-Bakhtiari, 2015
,
Perdas et al., 2016
). Additionally, two differentially expressed microRNAs suggested altered keratinocyte-to-neuron communication (
Fig. 4
F-G). An altered expression of miR-24-3p has been reported in cancer (
Mukherjee et al., 2022
,
Cui, 2020
,
Wang, 2020
,
Lin, 2018
) and diabetes (
Xiang, 2015
). By cross-referencing three separate databases for predicted targets and running a gene ontology enrichment analysis, one predicted pathway for miR-24-3p modulation is plexin-semaphorin activity, and our proteomic dataset suggested plexin-b2 as a cargo protein (
Fig. 3
C). However, the precise mechanism by which these microRNAs regulate axon guidance and potentially contribute to PDN remains unclear. Further studies are necessary to understand how these microRNAs modulate target proteins and pathways, either directly or indirectly.
The DRG transcriptome is substantially altered in PDN (
George, 2022
), resulting in hyperexcitability of the nociceptive neurons that drive neuropathic pain (
Jayaraj, 2018
,
George, 2022
) and presenting a possible druggable system for future therapeutics. In our studies, using an EV-reporter mouse line in which KDEVs are labelled with CD63-emGFP (
Fig. 6
A), we detected a GFP signal in the DRG neuron cell bodies and neurites (
Fig. 6
F). This indicates that GFP-containing EVs originating in the epidermis are retrogradely trafficked into the DRG neuron cell body, where presumably they can initiate transcriptional changes due to their cargo. It should be noted that the GFP species detected, at least from our immunoblotting, was the non-fused soluble form of GFP rather than the CD63-GFP fusion form (
Fig. 6
C), but that soluble GFP was also detected in the epidermis (
Fig. 5
C) and KDEV fractions (
Fig. 5
E) in high abundance. It may be that the GFP-tagged KDEVs are trafficked to DRG neurons, where they release their transcription-altering cargo, and the fusion protein is subsequently degraded into the soluble GFP that we detected in immunoblotting.
We recognize the limitations of this study. In this manuscript, we aimed to provide a sophisticated and rigorous isolation and molecular characterization of KDEV protein and micro-RNA cargo in both RD and HFD mice. Our molecular characterization suggests a communication pathway between neurons and keratinocytes mediated by KDEVs, which was significantly altered in the HFD group. However, the specific mechanism of action remains to be explored. Further well-designed, rigorous, and controlled preclinical studies are essential to deepen our understanding of the functional role of KDEVs in PDN.
Nevertheless, this study enhances our understanding of the communication between keratinocytes and DRG neurons via KDEVs. Our study suggests a novel strategy for treating PDN by focusing on the skin rather than the entire body. One of the challenges with current PDN treatments is their systemic administration and off-target effects (
Finnerup, 2015
,
Attal and Bouhassira, 2021
,
Shinu, 2022
). Here, we present evidence for a delivery pathway from epidermal keratinocytes to the peripheral nervous system, which could be utilized to develop and deliver improved topical treatments for PDN and other nervous system diseases. Indeed, EVs hold great promise as novel disease biomarkers, therapeutic agents, and drug delivery systems. This potential extends beyond PDN, laying the groundwork for exploring new avenues in pain and peripheral neuropathy research and treatment.
Methods
Animals
.
Animals were housed on a 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water. We used the following mouse lines: WT C57B6; K14-Cre, homozygous; CD63-emGFP fl, homozygous; K14-Cre::CD63-emGFP fl heterogenous.
High-Fat-Diet (HFD)
.
Mice were fed 42% fat (Envigo TD88137) for 10 weeks as a rodent model of type 2 diabetes. Control mice were fed a regular diet (RD) of 11% fat. After 10 weeks of RD or HFD, a glucose tolerance test was performed as described (
Jayaraj, 2018
). A cutoff of (≥140 mg/dl) at 2 SD above the mean for glucose 120 min after glucose challenge in WT littermate mice was used as a ‘diabetic’ classification (
Jayaraj, 2018
).
Behavioral testing
.
von Frey behavioral studies were performed as previously described (
Jayaraj, 2018
) with random experimental group assignments and double-blind investigator and endpoint analysis conditions.
Primary keratinocyte cultures
.
Glabrous paw skin is dissected from the mouse and incubated in dispase (2.3 mg/ml) overnight. The epidermis is separated from the dermis and incubated in TrypLE Express (10 min, 37C; Gibco 12604–013); the keratinocytes are dislodged using gentle agitation with forceps and then plated on 15cm
2
plates with 154CF epidermal medium (M154CF500) supplemented with 170ul of 0.2M CaCl
2
and 5ml of HKGS (S-001–5), which was depleted of EVs following 18 h of ultracentrifugation at 100,000
g
. Medium change occurs 24 h after plating and then every 48 h. Complete cell culture medium and all other reagents have been confirmed to be EV-free prior to use with the keratinocyte cultures.
Wound healing scratch assay
.
Primary keratinocyte cultures were grown on 6-well plates (Fisherbrand
FB012927
) with a 300,000-seeding density and grown to 90% confluency. The tip of an Eppendorf 200ul pipette tip (Fisher 02707409) was used to form a vertical scratch down the center. Cultures were tracked every 24 h on a Leica 2000 LED microscope and analyzed using ImageJ software to measure the rate of gap closure. One-tail paired t-tests were used on the raw dataset to obtain a p-value between groups for each time point (n=58 RD, n=61 HFD for each time point across three separate biological replicates for each group; significance p ≤ 0.05).
EV isolation with size exclusion columns
.
Cell-conditioned medium from keratinocyte cultures is collected between culture confluency of 60–90%, is centrifuged at 3000
g
for 30 min to remove cell debris and is then concentrated to 500 μL using centrifuge size filters (Pierce Protein Concentrators PES 10
K
, 88528). The sample is then run through an IZON SEC qEV 35nm column (IZON, ICO-35) as previously reported (
Böing, 2014
). Fractions 2–3 are used for downstream applications.
Western Blot
.
Fractions 1–10 of 400ul each are concentrated to 20ul using Millipore centrifuge filters (Microcon 30kDa, MRCF0R030) and combined with one volume equivalent of BioRad 2x Lammalenni loading buffer (BioRad 1610737) with 5% BME. Each fraction is run through a 3–15% gradient gel (BioRad 45610840) alongside full keratinocyte cell lysate (+) and a 10
K
pellet (large EVs), running a BCA assay (Thermo Scientific) to load ∼3 μg of protein per well. Protein was then transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore) and blocked (BioRad Everyblot 12010020) for 15 min. Primary antibodies are applied overnight at 4 °C with secondary antibody at room temperature for 2 h with 3 TBST washes between each step. Proteins are visualized with a chemiluminescence detection system (Thermo Scientific 32209). All western blot gels were run at minimum in triplicate. Blots were visualized on a Li-Cor Odyssey Fc.
Western blot antibodies.
The following primary antibodies were used with a dilution of 1:1000: Alix (Abcam ab88388), Syntenin-1 (Abcam ab19903), Tsg101 (Invitrogen
PA531260
), CD63 (Invitrogen 2H5I1), CD81 (Abcam ab109201), GM130 (Abcam ab52649), Calnexin (Abcam ab22595), K14 (BioLegend 906004), GFP (Abcam ab13970), β-tubulin (ProteinTech 80713–1-RR100UL), GAPDH (Abcam ab181602). The following secondary antibodies were used with a dilution of 1:2000: Goat anti-rabbit HRP (Abcam ab97080), Goat anti-chicken HRP (Invitrogen A16054).
Silver stain.
Samples were run through a 4–20% 12-well Mini-PROTEAN® TGX™ Precast Protein Gel (19.5 µL loaded per well; BioRad 4561095) and run for 90 min at 120V. Silver stain was performed on gels using the Pierce™ Silver Stain Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific 24612) according to the manufacturer’s protocol with a development time of 90 s.
Dynamic light scattering
.
80ul of samples are pipetted into cuvettes (Malvern Catalog#759200) and run through the zetasizer spectrophotometer (Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZSP) with an EV refractive index of 1.35 in ANTEC through Northwestern University. Malvern analytical software is used to analyze the output using particle counts relative to the signal intensity.
Nanoparticle tracking.
KDEV samples from RD and HFD mice were diluted in PBS 1:50 and then analyzed using a Malvern NanoSight NS300 in ANTEC through Northwestern University. Malvern analytical software was used to analyze the output measuring particle counts and intensities to obtain a size distribution and concentration.
Tunable resistive pulse sensing.
TRPS was performed using the IZON Exoid according to the manufacturer’s user manual. A 200 nm nanopore was used with a stretch of 47 nm for the RD KDEV analysis. Charged carboxylated polystyrene nanoparticle 100s (CPN100s; 1:3000 dilution) were used. KDEVs were eluted through the SEC columns using PBS, so CPN reference nanoparticles were diluted in PBS.
TRPS data analysis.
Figures were generated using the IZON Data Suite software after calibrating sample measurements to their appropriate reference measurement.
Negative stain EM
.
Samples suspended in PBS are prepared using the standard uranyl acetate fixation for 5 mins seeded on EMS grids (TMS Catalog#71150) and imaged on a FEI Spirit 2 electron microscope. Images were processed using ImageJ software.
Cryo-EM
.
CryoEM images are obtained through the northwestern BioCryo core facility (NUANCE) with samples prepared as previously described (
Asadi, 2017
).
Primary DRG Cultures.
DRG sensory neurons from WT and K14-CD63-GFP mice were dissociated as described (
Menichella, 2016
) at 18 weeks of age.
Microfluidics.
Primary DRG cultures were deposited into one compartment chamber connected to the microchannel column in a microfluidic system (XONA Microfluidics SND450) with or without primary keratinocyte cultures in the other compartment chambers. Keratinocytes were cultured for 3 days before depositing DRG cultures unless otherwise specified. The medium was a combination of 50% DRG culture medium as previously described (
Menichella, 2016
) and 50% keratinocyte culture medium when both cell types were present in the chambers.
DiR Labelling
.
Concentrated CCM was labeled with DiR (Invitrogen
D12731
; 2ug/ul) at a ratio of 1:100 by volume and then passed through the IZON 35nm columns. Isolated DiR-labeled EVs from fractions 2–3 were then used for downstream applications with a negative DiR control, which was DiR-added to concentrated EV-depleted unconditioned medium passed through the column with the same fractions collected for experiments.
DiR-EVs
. DRG Cultures: DiR-labeled EVs were directly applied to DIV-1 primary DRG cultures and visualized after 16 h post-treatment by confocal microscopy. Microfluidics: DiR-EVs were applied to the empty compartment after DRG neuron neurites occupied all microchannels, preventing medium exchange between the two compartments. The microfluidic chambers were visualized after 16 h by confocal microscopy.
MS sample preparation
Trichloroacetic acid (TCA, Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# T0699) precipitation was used to clean and precipitate proteins from EV samples. Protein pellets were resuspended in 8 M urea (ThermoFisher Scientific, Cat # 29700) prepared in 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate solution (Fluka, Cat # 09830) and processed with ProteaseMAX (Promega, Cat # V2072) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The samples were reduced with 5 mM Tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP, Sigma-Aldrich, Cat # C4706; vortexed for 1 h at RT), alkylated in the dark with 10 mM iodoacetamide (IAA, Sigma-Aldrich, Cat # I1149; 20 min at RT), diluted with 100 mM ABC, and quenched with 25 mM TCEP. Samples were diluted with 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate solution, and digested with Trypsin (1:50, Promega, Cat # V5280) for overnight incubation at 37°C with intensive agitation. The next day, reaction was quenched by adding 1% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA, Fisher Scientific, O4902-100). The samples were desalted using Peptide Desalting Spin Columns (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat # 89882). All samples were vacuum centrifuged to dry.

MS data analysis and quantification
Protein identification/quantification and analysis were performed with Integrated Proteomics Pipeline − IP2 (Bruker, Madison, WI. https://www.integratedproteomics.com/) using ProLuCID (
Eng et al., 1994
,
Xu, 2015
), DTASelect2 (
Cociorva, 2007
,
Tabb et al., 2002
), Census and Quantitative Analysis. Spectrum raw files were extracted into MS1, MS2 files using RawConverter (https://fields.scripps.edu/downloads.php). The tandem mass spectra (raw files from the same sample were searched together) were searched against UniProt mouse (downloaded on 07–29-2023) protein databases (
UniProt, 2015
) and matched to sequences using the ProLuCID/SEQUEST algorithm (ProLuCID version 3.1) with 50 ppm peptide mass tolerance for precursor ions and 600 ppm for fragment ions. The search space included all fully and half-tryptic peptide candidates within the mass tolerance window with no-miscleavage constraint, assembled, and filtered with DTASelect2 through IP2. To estimate protein probabilities and false-discovery rates (FDR) accurately, we used a target/decoy database containing the reversed sequences of all the proteins appended to the target database (
UniProt, 2015
) (UniProt, 2015). Each protein identified was required to have a minimum of one peptide of minimal length of six amino acid residues. After the peptide/spectrum matches were filtered, we estimated that the protein FDRs were ≤ 1% for each sample analysis. Resulting protein lists include subset proteins to allow for consideration of all possible protein isoforms implicated by at least three given peptides identified from the complex protein mixtures. Then, we used Census and Quantitative Analysis in IP2 for protein quantification. Static modification: 57.02146C for carbamidomethylation. Quantification was performed by the built-in module in IP2.
Proteomic Analysis Statistics
.
The spectral counts for each protein accession ID for the three RD and HFD biological replicates are used to run a one-tailed, paired
t
-test for statistical significance. The average spectral counts for RD and HFD were used to obtain a HFD/RD fold change. The volcano plot was generated in R using ggplot2. The heat map was generated using the R package pheatmap. Gene ontology enrichment analyses were generated using the R packages clusterprofiler, msigdbr and ggplot2. GO enrichment analyses used proteins present in all biological replicates of each group only.
Small RNA sequencing
.
Samples underwent small RNA sequencing through Northwestern’s core facilities. Biological triplicate RD and HFD RNA samples were prepared using the total EV RNA and protein extraction kit from Invitrogen (Invitrogen Catalog#2743605). RNA samples were quantified by Qubit RNA HS assay and the quality was confirmed by Bioanalyzer RNA pico chip assay. Then, 1ng of RNA was used as input for library preparation with NextFlex small RNA-seq kit v4 according to manufacturer’s protocol. Each sample was barcoded with a unique index and multiplexed libraries were pooled for sequencing on Novaseq X Plus 10B flowcell using single end 50nt mode.
RNA sequencing Statistics
.
Data analysis was carried out in R using the standard workflow of DESeq2 paired with the libraries apeglm and ggplot2 for figure generation. Bonferroni post-hoc adjustment was used for the reported adjusted p-values ≤ 0.05 for significance. Gene ontology enrichment analyses were generated using the R packages clusterprofiler, msigdbr and ggplot2 with Bonferroni post-hoc adjusted P values < 0.05 for significance.
Protein target analysis
.
The direct or indirect protein targets for each microRNA was predicted using several target prediction programs, including miRDB (https://mirdb.org/) (
Chen and Wang, 2020
); TargetScan v7.0 (https://www.targetscan.org/vert_72/) and DIANA-microT v5.0 (https://bio.tools/DIANA-microT). Only the predicted proteins identified by all 3 programs were included in the subsequent enrichment analyses.
Immunohistochemistry
.
Glabrous hind paw dermis/epidermis was separated from the paw and whole DRGs (lumber 2–4) were isolated from 18 week old mice and fixed with 4% PFA for 1 h, 30% sucrose for 1 h, and then embedded in OCT. Samples were processed as previously described (
Menichella, 2014
) and analyzed by confocal microscopy.
