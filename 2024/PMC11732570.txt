1
Introduction
Sarcomeres, the smallest contractile units of striated muscle, play a critical role in regulating cardiac metabolism [
1
]. The most abundant and energy (ATP)-consuming protein within these sarcomeres is myosin. Indeed, myosin molecules adopt various biochemical and structural states promoting or inhibiting ATP hydrolysis [
1
]. Biochemically, myosin exists either in the super-relaxed (SRX) state, in which ATP consumption is minimal, or the disordered-relaxed (DRX) state, in which ATP demand increases tenfold. The myosin molecules in the SRX state have been proposed to often correlate to the structural OFF state, in which they are in close proximity to the thick filament backbone restricting their interaction with actin monomers. In the DRX state, myosin molecules often adopt an ON state, allowing a fast transition to the active state when actin filaments are switched on by Ca
2+
ions [
1
,
2
]. Based on these differences, it is now known that shifting from one state to another (DRX/SRX or OFF/ON) can modulate the reserve pool of myosin molecules capable of interacting with actin and influence the overall cardiac ATP demand [
3
].
Hibernation is a well-defined seasonal and physiological adaptation allowing animals to survive long periods of low environmental temperature, shortened daylight and food shortage. Hibernating mammals such as bears reduce their activity levels by more than 90% and enter a whole-body energy conserving state while maintaining a relatively normal body temperature [
4
]. As part of the metabolic rate (ATP demand) depression, the cardiac output is affected through decreased heart rate and stroke volume during hibernation [
5
]. As the exact underlying molecular mechanisms remain to be determined, here, we aimed to investigate whether the ATP-consuming myosin plays a role. We initially hypothesized that in hibernating bears the reserve pool of myosin heads in the ATP-conserving SRX state increases, allowing the cardiac ATP consumption to decrease. To test our hypothesis, here, we used cardiac tissue from hibernating and active grizzly bears (
Ursus arctos horribilis
) [
6
].
2
Materials and methods
2.1
Grizzly bear subpopulation
As in [
6
], subadult grizzly bears were used for this study. The animals were housed at the Washington State University Bear Research, Education and Conservation Facility. The animals were maintained according to the Bear Care and Colony Health Standard Operating Procedures, and all procedures were approved by the Washington State University (WSU) Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) based on the National Institutes of Health (NIH) guidelines.
2.2
Cardiac tissue collection
Samples were obtained immediately after euthanization from thirteen healthy animals euthanized for reasons unrelated to the present work. Six samples were collected during June, July or August for the active group and seven samples were collected in January or February for the hibernating group (
Supplementary Table 1
). Sample collecting procedures adhered to guidelines provided by the IACUC, WSU College of Veterinary Medicine. They were obtained from left and right ventricular free wall, left and right atria, snap frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C until use.
2.3
Cardiac strip preparation and permeabilization
The cryopreserved samples were dissected into thin bundles and immersed in a membrane-permeabilising solution (relaxing solution containing glycerol; 50:50 v/v) for 24 h at −20 °C, after which they were transferred to 4 °C. These bundles were kept in the membrane-permeabilising solution at 4 °C for an additional 24 h (to allow a proper skinning/membrane permeabilization process). As our storage buffers did not contain any phosphatase/kinase inhibitors, Mant-ATP chase experiments as well as X-ray diffraction recordings were all performed on the day following the thaw/skinning processes. Nevertheless, bundles were kept and stored in the same buffer at −20 °C for up to one week before being discarded [
7
].
2.4
Mant-ATP chase experiments
As previously published [
7
], the relaxing solution contained 4 mM Mg-ATP, 1 mM free Mg
2+
, 10
−6.00
mM free Ca
2+
, 10 mM imidazole, 7 mM EGTA, 14.5 mM creatine phosphate and KCl to adjust the ionic strength to 180 mM and pH to 7.0. Additionally, the rigor buffer for Mant-ATP chase experiments contained 120 mM K acetate, 5 mM Mg acetate, 2.5 mM K
2
HPO
4
, 50 mM MOPS, 2 mM DTT with a pH of 6.8. On the day of the experiments, bundles were transferred to the relaxing solution and thin cardiac strips (approximately 50 μm wide) were isolated. Their ends were individually clamped to half-split copper meshes designed for electron microscopy (SPI G100 2010C-XA, width, 3 mm), which had been glued to glass slides (Academy, 26 × 76 mm, thickness 1.00–1.20 mm). Cover slips were attached to the top using double-sided tape to create flow chambers (Menzel-Gläser, 22 × 22 mm, thickness 0.13–0.16 mm) [
7
]. Subsequently, at 25 °C, for each cardiac strip, the sarcomere length was measured using the brightfield mode of a Zeiss Axio Scope A1 microscope, a Zeiss AxioCam ICm 1 camera and a LD Plan-Neofluar 40×/0.60 objective. Preparations with a sarcomere length of 2.00 ± 0.05 μm were further subjected to the following. Similar to previous studies [
7
], each thin strip was first incubated for 5 min with a rigor buffer. A solution containing the rigor buffer with 250 μM Mant-ATP was then flushed and kept in the chamber for 5 min. At the end of this step, another solution made of the rigor buffer with 4 mM ATP was added with simultaneous acquisition of the Mant-ATP chase. For fluorescence acquisition, a Zeiss Axio Scope A1 microscope was used with a Plan-Apochromat 20×/0.8 objective and a Zeiss AxioCam ICm 1 camera. Frames were acquired every 5 s with a 20 ms exposure time using a DAPI filter set, for 5 min. Tests were run prior to starting the current study where images were collected for 15 min instead of 5 min – these tests did not reveal any significant difference in the parameters calculated. Three regions of each individual strip were sampled for fluorescence decay using the ROI manager in ImageJ as previously published [
7
]. The mean background fluorescence intensity was subtracted from the average of the fiber fluorescence intensity (for each image taken). Each time point was then normalized by the fluorescence intensity of the final Mant-ATP image before washout (T = 0). These data were then fit to an unconstrained double exponential decay using Graphpad Prism 9.0:
Normalised Fluorescence = 1−P1(1−exp
(−t/T1)
)−P2(1−exp
(−t/T2)
)
Where P1 is the amplitude of the initial rapid decay approximating the ATP-consuming DRX state with T1 as the time constant for this decay. P2 is the slower second decay approximating the proportion of myosin heads in the ATP-conserving SRX state with its associated time constant T2. In the present article, P1, P2, T1 and T2 are presented without any correction for the rapid wash-out of non-specific binding of Mant-ATP [
7
].
2.5
X-ray diffraction recording and analysis
On the day of the experiments, left ventricle was prepared as described previously [
8
]. Briefly, frozen muscle was thawed in skinning solution (pCa8 solution: 91 mM K + -propionate, 3.5 mM MgCl, 0.16 mM CaCl2, 7 mM EGTA, 2.5 mM Na2ATP, 15 mM creatine phosphate, 20 mM Imidazole) plus 30 mM BDM, 1% Triton-X100 at room temperature for ∼ 1 h before dissection into smaller strips which then were skinned for another 2 h. The muscle stripes were then washed with pCa 8 solution three times, 10 min each before further dissected into preparations with a diameter of ∼300 μm prior to attachment of aluminum T-clips to both ends. The preparations were stored in pCa 8 solution with 3% dextran on ice for the day of the experiment. X-ray diffraction experiments were performed at the BioCAT beamline 18ID at the Advanced Photon Source, Argonne National Laboratory as described previously [
9
]. The experiment was performed between 25 °C and 28 °C at a sarcomere length of 2.0 μm as adjusted by laser diffraction. X-ray diffraction patterns were collected on a MarCCD 165 detector (Rayonix Inc., Evanston IL) with a 1 s exposure time.
X-ray analysis. For each bear, all exposures collected for each fiber were averaged together in ImageJ and the resulting image saved to be analyzed in Fit2D. The 2D projection of the equatorial and meridional patterns was created in Fit2D (v18) and saved as a chiplot to be analyzed later. For the meridional patterns Fit2D's mfit tool was used with the peaks fit to a gaussian and the background assumed to be a 3rd order polynomial. For the equatorial patterns the images were first split with chiplot analyze and the resulting split images fit in Fityx using the sub-routine provided previously [
10
].
As external load is thought to contribute to the shift from OFF/SRX to ON/DRX state [
11
], our measurements related to both Mant-ATP chase experiments and X-ray diffraction were made at short sarcomere lengths (2.0 μm), implying minimal external load on myosin filaments for all cardiac strips.
2.6
Identification of phosphorylation on beta-myosin heavy chain
For the detailed description of protein separation (SDS-PAGE gel), band excision, tryptic in-gel digest, peptides separation and sequencing, please see [
12
,
13
]. Raw mass spectrometry data were analyzed with MaxQuant (v1.6.15.0) and phosphorylation (STY) sites analysed. The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD054499 [
14
].
2.7
EvoEF protein stability simulations
EvoEF (version 1) was used to calculate the stability change upon mutation, in terms of ΔΔG. To this end, we first used “EvoEF --command = RepairStructure” to repair clashes and torsional angles of the wild-type structure. “EvoEF --command = BuildMutant” was then used to mutate the repaired wild type structures into the mutant by changing the side chain amino acid type followed by a local side chain repacking. “EvoEF --command = ComputeStability” was subsequently applied to both the repair wild-type and the mutant to calculate their respective stabilities (ΔGWT and ΔGmutant). The stability change upon mutation can then be derived by ΔΔG = ΔGmutant-ΔGWT. A ΔΔG below zero means that the mutation causes destabilization; otherwise, it induces stabilization [
15
,
16
]. The sequence of the MYH7 coiled-coil backbone was used for EvoEF stability calculations due to size limitations in the software when using the entire MYH7 protein sequence.
2.8
Western blotting
SDS-PAGE and Western blot experiments as previously described [
17
,
18
]. Tissue was ground to a fine powder using Dounce homogenizers precooled in liquid nitrogen. Tissue powder was acclimated to −20 °C for 30 min before being resuspended in a 1:1 mixture of an 8 M Urea buffer (in M; 8 urea, 2 thiourea, 0.05 Tris–HCl, 0.075 dithiothreitol, as well as 3% SDS and 0.03% bromophenol blue, pH 6.8) and 50% glycerol containing protease inhibitors (0.04 mM E−64, 0.16 mM leupeptin, and 0.2 mM PMSF). The solutions were mixed for 4 min, followed by 10 min of incubation at 60 °C. Samples were centrifuged at 12.000 rpm and the supernatant was flash frozen for storage at −80 °C.
For total phosphorylation proteins were electrophonically separated by gradient SDS-PAGE 4–20% and stained using Pro-Q Diamond Gel Stain for phosphorylation and SYPRO Ruby (Thermo Fisher) for total protein following standard procedures. Ovalbumin in the Spectra Multicolor Broad Range Protein Ladder (Thermo) served a s a positive control. After staining, gels were scanned using a commercial scanner Gbox GBOX (Syngen) and analyzed using Genetools analysis software. ProQ signal was divided by Sypro signal do determine normalized phosphorylation levels for myofilament proteins (titin, myosin, Mybpc, desmin, TnI, RLC).
Sample loading was equalized to MyHC levels, determined from Coomassie stained initial gels. For western blot, proteins were electrophonically separated by gradient SDS-PAGE 4–20% and transferred onto Immobilon-P PVDF 0.45 μm membranes (Millipore) using semi dry transfer (Bio-Rad). Membranes were blocked with Odyssey blocking buffer (Li-Cor Biosciences) for 1 h, and subsequently probed with OXPHOS antibodies at 4 °C overnight. We specifically used Abcam ab110413 which is a complex of antibodies related to proteins of the mitochondrial electron transport chain. Near Infra-Red dyes conjugated to secondary antibodies were used for detection with Odyssey CLx Imaging System (Li-Cor Biosciences). Proteins were analyzed using Image studio light (Li-Cor Biosciences) and normalized to total protein OD from Coomassie stain of the blot.
2.9
Statistical analyses
Student's unpaired t-tests were used to determine significant differences between groups. Analysis was performed with Graphpad Prism. p < 0.05 was considered significant.
3
Results
To determine whether biochemical DRX and SRX states and their respective ATP turnover times were affected by hibernation, we started by performing loaded Mant-ATP chase experiments in permeabilized thin cardiac strips from six active and seven hibernating grizzly bears. A total of 112 individual left ventricle free wall strips were tested at a set sarcomere length of 2.0 μm (minimum of eight per animal). Interestingly and as initially hypothesized, we found a lower proportion of DRX (based on P1) and greater amount of SRX (based on P2) in hibernating bears than in active grizzlies (
Figure 1
A–B). However, we did not observe any differences in their respective ATP turnover times, T1 and T2 (
Figure 1
C–D).

To get insights into the potential causes triggering the disproportionate SRX state and thick filament remodelling in hibernating grizzly bears, we assessed the phosphorylation levels of myosin and neighboring proteins. Using ProQ Diamond/Sypro Ruby (phosphor-)protein stains, we did not detect any difference in the phosphorylation levels of myosin regulatory light chain (RLC), cardiac myosin-binding protein C (cMyBP-C) or cardiac beta myosin heavy chain (beta-MyHC,

To investigate whether these aberrant phosphorylation levels have functional consequences on the myosin heavy chain tail region, we ran an in-silico programme named EvoDesign physical Energy Function (EvoEF). For these simulations, we used mutations chemically resembling Thr-P and Ser-P. Indeed, we substituted the three native residues Ser1300, Thr1309 and Ser1543 by Ser1300Asp, Thr1309Asp and Ser1543Asp. Taken separately, these mutations do not seem to have any consequence on ΔΔGStability (

Fig. 3
G) and may reflect an increase in myosin backbone packing. These three amino acids are located in the light meromyosin region known to be crucial for the DRX to SRX ratio regulation (
Fig. 3

19

20

19

20

Discussion
As previously shown in a limited number of thirteen-lined ground squirrels -
Ictidomys tridecemlineatus
[
21
] and as initially hypothesized, here, we unravelled a preferred cardiac myosin SRX state in hibernating grizzly bears. This molecular mechanism is likely to involve beta-myosin heavy chain phosphorylation and a more stable interaction between the tail region and the highly conserved Inter-Head Motif region [
2
,
22
], providing a necessary ATP conservation during hibernation. Our molecular findings correlate with the limited energy supply as well as with the depressed cardiac output and energetic demand observed in hibernating grizzly bears. Interestingly, this seasonal and physiological molecular adaptation at the cardiac sarcomere level is unique to the heart and cannot be translated to other types of striated muscles. Indeed, the myosin of skeletal muscle of hibernating bears does not undergo any significant remodelling [
23
], emphasizing organ-specific rather than systemic regulation of the SRX state and thick filament.
In conclusion, cardiac myosin ATP consumption can be seasonally modulated through an adaptation of the DRX:SRX ratio. Myosin fine-tuning can then be seen as a molecular strategy to lower the energy requirements of the sarcomere and whole heart during hibernation.
