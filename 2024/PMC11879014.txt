Introduction
SP140 is an IFN-γ inducible, 85.9kDa nuclear leukocyte-specific protein. It is a member of the speckled protein (SP) family, which also comprises SP100, SP110 and SP140-like (SP140L) proteins (
1
). Originally described as autoantigen in primary biliary cirrhosis, SP140 is a component of promyelocytic leukemia (PML) nuclear bodies (NBs) in human cell lines (
2
,
3
). As archetype of the SP family, SP140 has a multidomain architecture composed of a nuclear localization signal, the multimerization Caspase Recruitment Domain (
4
) and three chromatin-related domains, namely the DNA binding domain SAND (named after proteins containing it:
S
P100,
A
ire,
N
ucP41/P75 and
D
EAF) (
5
) and the histone- and SUMO-binding PHD-BRD cassette (
P
lant
H
omeo
D
omain and
B
romo
D
omain) (
6–8
).
Mutations and alterations of the
SP140
gene, which generally lead to reduced SP140 protein levels, are linked to various human immunological disorders, such as Crohn's disease (CD) (
9
,
10
), autoimmune diseases (e.g. Multiple Sclerosis) (
11
) and to B cells malignancies (e.g. Multiple Myeloma (
12
), Chronic Myelomonocytic Leukemia (
13
) and Chronic Lymphocytic Leukemia (CLL) (
14
)). SP140 is upregulated in a particular subtype of diffuse large B cell lymphoma (ABC-DLBCL) (
15
) and in gliomas (
16
).
Despite the pathophysiological relevance of SP140, the molecular mechanisms at the basis of its function are just beginning to be elucidated. SP140 is a chromatin-bound protein working as a transcriptional regulator (
17
). In particular, it contributes to homeostatic immune cell identity, organizing macrophage transcriptional programs by occupying and repressing lineage-inappropriate genes, such as
HOX
(
17
). As such, SP140 has the typical transcriptional repressor signature, as it preferentially localizes to repressed heterochromatic regions characterized by high levels of Histone H3 tri-methylation on Lys27 (H3K27me3), an epigenetic mark commonly associated to gene repression (
17
).
SP140 repressive function has been further confirmed in lymphoblastoid cell lines, where its silencing resulted in the up-regulation of genes related to NF-kB pathway, cytokine production, multiple sclerosis and other autoimmune diseases (
18
). More recently, another study has shown that in lungs SP140 is a repressor of the innate antiviral response (type I IFNs) during bacterial infections (
19
).
While the repressive role of SP140 has now been established, the molecular mechanisms by which it exerts this function are still underexplored. Specifically, it is unclear how SP140 binds to chromatin repressed loci, which are the interactors that mediate gene silencing, and why reduced SP140 protein levels result in detrimental effects on the immune response and cellular growth.
Here, to identify interactors and target genes of endogenous SP140, we have applied multi-omics (i.e. interactomics, ChIP-seq and proteomics) and biochemical approaches in Burkitt lymphoma cell lines. We demonstrated that the Polycomb Repressive Complex 2 (PRC2) and the nucleosome remodeling and deacetylase complex (NuRD) are SP140
bona fide
interactors. Further, we showed that
SP140
knockout leads to a reduction of H3K27me3 levels on specific genetic loci and alters the chromatin distribution of MBD3, a core member of the NuRD complex. Interestingly, among the genes controlled by SP140, we identified those related to the
Hippo/Yap1
and
Wnt
signaling pathways, which are well known to regulate proliferation, differentiation, cellular fate (
20
,
21
) and immunity (
22
,
23
), thus supporting the role of SP140 in autoimmune diseases and hematological malignancies.
Materials and methods
Biological resources
Daudi and Ramos cells were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection. Both cell lines were grown in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI 1640, Invitrogen), supplemented with 10% FBS (PAA), 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Euroclone) and non-essential aminoacids (Euroclone).
Generation of
SP140

SP140

SP140
gene by the CRISPR/Cas9 technology. Four different sgRNAs designed to maximize the on-target activity were evaluated for their ability to introduce indels in an easy-to-transfect cell line. Finally, the most active guide RNAs were used to target the
SP
140

GFP-positive cells were sorted by FACS analysis at 48h post-transfection. Limiting dilution (0.5 cells/well) and FACS sorting were used to sub-cloning. Then, generated clonal cell lines were screened to find out biallelic out-of-frame mutants by fragment analysis (IDAA: Indels Detection by Amplicon Analysis). Finally, the complete knock-out of the
SP140

Subcellular fractionation, nuclear enrichment
Daudi cells were cultured in suspension in RPMI-1640 media supplemented with 5% FBS to a final density of 1.5 × 10
6
cells/ml. To obtain the cytoplasmic fraction, cells were resuspended with Hypotonic Buffer (HB; 10 mM Tris HCl pH 7.6, 1.5 mM MgCl
2
and 10 mM KCl, protease and phosphatase inhibitors, Roche) for 10 min on ice. Then, Triton X-100 was added at a final concentration of 0.5% to release nuclei. By centrifugation at 11 000 rpm for 1 min the cytosolic fraction (supernatant) was separated by the nuclei (pellet). Nuclei were lysed by adding the Nuclear Extraction Buffer (NEB) (50 mM Tris HCl pH 7.6, 150 mM NaCl and 0.5% NP-40, protease and phosphatase inhibitors, Roche) and incubated at 4°C for 1 h under rotation. The supernatant, containing the released nuclear proteins, was recovered by centrifuge at 16 000 g for 30 min. To release and collect chromatin bound proteins by benzonase treatment, Daudi cells were subcellular fractionated according to the protocol reported above, but nuclei were resuspended in NEB supplemented with 2 mM MgCl
2
and Benzonase endonuclease (250 U/μL). After incubation at 4°C for 1 h, the nuclease reaction was stopped by addition of 1 mM EGTA and the supernatant was recovered (centrifugation at 16 000 g, 30 min). To release and collect chromatin bound proteins by sonication, cell nuclei were resuspended in NEB and sonicated six times for 30 seconds (Branson Sonifier 250 sonicator; settings: output control 1, duty cycle 30, 1 min stop at 4°C between every cycle). Supernatant was collected upon centrifugation at 16 000 g for 30 min at 4°C.
Immunoprecipitation
Daudi and Ramos cells were fractionated as previously described. Then, nuclei were lysed in mild conditions (50 mM Tris HCl pH 7.6, 150 mM NaCl, 0.3% NP40, 25 mM NEM, 2 mM MgCl
2
, benzonase) or in stringent condition (50 mM Tris HCl pH 7.6, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP40, 25 mM NEM, 2 mM MgCl
2
, benzonase) and used to immunoprecipitate endogenous SP140 or SUZ12. Briefly, nuclear extract (∼12.5 mg) was incubated with 14 μg of primary antibody anti SP140 or SUZ12. IgG were used as negative control of the immunoprecipitation. After addition of 40 μL of Sepharose Protein G beads and incubation for 2 h at 4°C, beads were washed with NEB (50 mM Tris-Cl pH7.6, 150 mM NaCl, 0.3% NP-40 for the mild conditions and 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.6, 150 mM NaCl, 0.3% NP40, 25 mM NEM, 2 mM MgCl
2
, benzonase for stringent conditions). Immunocomplexes were eluted in Laemmli buffer and resolved on pre-cast 4–12% NuPage gels (Invitrogen, CA), stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue after electrophoresis or used for Western Blot.
Crosslinking immunoprecipitation
Daudi cells were harvested and resuspended in HB for 10 min on ice. Then, Triton X-100 was added at a final concentration of 0.5% to release nuclei. By centrifugation at 11 000 rpm for 1 min the cytosolic fraction (supernatant) was separated by the nuclei (pellet). Washed nuclei were crosslinked by incubation with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. Crosslinking was quenched by adding Glycine (0.125 M final concentration). Crosslinked nuclei were resuspended in NEB (50 mM Tris HCl pH 7.6, 300 mM NaCl, 0.5% NP40, 20% Glycerol, 25 mM NEM), incubated for 30 min at 4°C under rotation and sonicated four times for 30 s, with 1 min stop at 4°C between every cycle. Sonicated nuclei were incubated with NEB buffer implemented with 2 mM MgCl
2
and benzonase was added to the sonicated nuclei, incubation for 40 min at 4°C was performed and the extracted nuclear proteins were collected in the supernatant by centrifugation at 16 000 g for 30 min. Supernatant (7 mg) was incubated for 2 h at 4°C with 9.8 μg of anti SP140 antibody and 50 μL of Sepharose Protein G beads. IgG (15 μg) were used as negative control. Then, beads were extensively washed (seven washes) with NEB and then submitted to digestion in solution.
Expression and purification of GST and GST-tagged SP140 PB construct
GST protein and the recombinant GST-tagged SP140 construct comprising the PHD finger and the bromodomain (GST-PB; NCBI accession code
NP_001265380

Cells were sonicated in lysis buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8, 10 mM imidazole, 2 μg/ml DNase I, 20 μg/ml RNase A, 0.4 mM DTT, 1 mM TCEP, 150 mM NaCl, 0.2% NP-40, cOmplete ™ EDTA-free Protease Inhibitor Cocktail, Roche). The soluble fraction was filtered by 0.22 um filter and loaded on 5 ml Ni-NTA agarose resin (Qiagen) equilibrated with five volumes of lysis buffer. The Ni-NTA column was washed with five volumes of Wash1 buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8, 10 mM imidazole, 4 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 150 mM NaCl) and five volumes of Wash2 buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8, 10 mM imidazole, 4 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 1 M NaCl). Proteins were eluted from the Ni-NTA column with Elution buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8, 300 mM imidazole, 4 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 150 mM NaCl) and dialyzed two times at 4°C against 20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8, 6 mM 2-β-mercaptoethanol and 150 mM NaCl. Proteins were concentrated by Vivaspin® 20 concentrator, MWCO 10 KDa (Sartorius).

Western Blot analysis
After gel electrophoresis, proteins were electro-transferred on a nitrocellulose membrane (HybondTM-ECLTM Amersham Biosciences) for 1 h at 390 mA in Tris-Glycine Transfer Buffer (25 mM Trizma base, 192 mM glycine, 20% methanol, 0.1% SDS). Nitrocellulose membrane was stained for 5 min with Red Ponceau Solution to control the transfer, washed with water and then saturated with 5% non-fat milk (Regilait) in TBS buffer (Tris-HCl 20 mM, NaCl 0.5 M) for 1 h at room temperature or 16 h at 4°C. After a 5 min wash with TBS-T (20 mM Tris-HCl, 0.5 M NaCl, 0.1% Tween 20), the membrane was incubated for 1 h at room temperature or 16 h at 4°C with the primary antibody dissolved in a 5% milk-TBS (different antibody dilution according to the antibody datasheet). After three 15 min washes with TBS-T, the membrane was incubated with the proper HRP-conjugated secondary antibody dissolved in a 5% milk-TBS solution (different antibody dilution according to the antibody datasheet). After exhaustive washes with TBS-T, bands were visualized by enhanced chemiluminescence reaction (ECL-Plus
TM
Western Blotting Reagents, Amersham Biosciences), followed by autoradiography or CCD detection (ChemiDoc™ MP System, Biorad).
Protein quantification
Protein quantification was performed using Bradford assay (Bio-Rad) following manufacturer's instructions. For each sample, the absorbance was measured by a spectrophotometer at a wavelength of 595 nm. Sample protein concentration was determined based on a bovine serum albumin (BSA) standard curve.
Immunofluorescence
Glass coverslips were incubated at 37°C for 30 min and then washed twice with PBS to eliminate Poly-L-ornithine. Around 1 × 10
6
Ramos cells were allowed to seed on the coated coverslips by incubation at 37°C for 2 h. After one gentle wash with PBS, cells were fixed by incubation with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature, gently washed twice with PBS and then permeabilized with 0.25% TritonX-100 in PBS supplemented with 1% (w/v) BSA. Cells were labeled with anti-SP140 antibody in PBS/BSA through overnight incubation at 4°C. After three 15-min long washes with PBS the fluorescent dye-labeled secondary antibody was added and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. After three 15-min long washes with PBS, Dapi staining and glasses mounting were performed. Images were obtained using a Leica TCS SP2 or TCS SP2 AOBS confocal microscope equipped with a 63X oil objective and processed using ImageJ.
Sample preparation and mass spectrometry analysis
For in-gel tryptic digestion, the lanes containing the immunoprecipitated proteins and the relative controls were cut from the Comassie-stained gels and divided in 10 gel slices. Concerning the crosslinking immunoprecipitation, on beads digestion was performed. Beads were washed twice with 50 mM Ammonium Bicarbonate and then digested overnight at 37°C with bovine trypsin. For global proteomics analysis by label free quantitation 50 μg of extracted proteins were digested. Disulphide bonds were reduced with 10 mM dithiothreitol, 30 min at 56°C, alkylated with 55 mM iodoacetamide, 20 min at room temperature, and digested overnight at 37°C with bovine trypsin (Sigma), as previously described (
24
). The resulting peptide mixture was purified from the excess of salt and concentrated using home-made C18 StageTips. Around 1 μg of digested sample was injected onto a quadrupole Orbitrap Q-exactive HF mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific). Peptide separation was achieved on a linear gradient from 95% solvent A (2% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid) to 55% solvent B (80% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid) over 45 min and from 55% to 100% solvent B in 3 min at a constant flow rate of 0.25 μl/min on UHPLC Easy-nLC 1000 (Thermo Scientific) where the LC system was connected to a 23-cm fused-silica emitter of 75 μm inner diameter (New Objective, Inc. Woburn, MA, USA), packed in-house with ReproSil-Pur C18-AQ 1.9 μm beads (Dr Maisch Gmbh, Ammerbuch, Germany) using a high-pressure bomb loader (Proxeon, Odense, Denmark).The mass spectrometer was operated in DDA mode as described previously (
25
), dynamic exclusion enabled (exclusion duration = 15 s), MS1 resolution = 70 000, MS1 automatic gain control target = 3 × 106, MS1 maximum fill time = 60 ms, MS2 resolution = 17 500, MS2 automatic gain control target = 1 × 105, MS2 maximum fill time = 60 ms and MS2 normalized collision energy = 25. For each cycle, one full MS1 scan range = 300–1650 m/z was followed by 12 MS2 scans using an isolation window of 2.0 m/z. The accession number for the raw and processed data reported in this paper is PXD036287 (PRIDE proteomeXchange repository).
iBAQ calculation
The stoichiometry of SP140 interactome were revealed by label-free quantitative proteomics. Raw data were analyzed by MaxQuant software with the further addition of the intensity Based Absolute Quantitation (iBAQ) algorithm, which allows a more accurate estimation of the protein abundance in the sample. We measured the relative abundance of the identified interactors by the iBAQ intensities after subtracting the background binding level of proteins measured in the different control samples. Finally, these relative abundance values were normalized on the SP140 iBAQ intensity.
Protein identification and quantitation
Raw data files were analyzed using the peptide search engine Andromeda, which is integrated into the MaxQuant software environment (version 1.5.2.8) (
26
), with the following parameters: uniprot_cp_hum_2014_01 as protein database, Oxidation (M), Acetyl (Protein N-term), as variable modifications, peptide false discovery rate (FDR) 0.01, maximum peptide posterior error probability (PEP) 1, protein FDR 0.05 (mild and stringent immunoprecipitation) and FDR 0.01 (crosslinking immunoprecipitation), minimum peptides 2, at least 1 unique, minimum length peptide 6 aminoacids. Statistical analysis was performed by using Perseus software (version 1.5.6.0) included in MaxQuant package. T-test and ANOVA statistical analysis were performed applying FDR < 0.05 or
P
< 0.05 as reported.
Chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP-seq) analysis
Daudi and Ramos cells were counted and 50 × 10
6
cells were crosslinked by adding formaldehyde into the medium to the final concentration of 1%, and rotating for 10 min at room temperature. Crosslinking reaction was quenched by adding Glycine (0.125 M final) to the solution. Crosslinked cells were then washed twice with PBS and lysed with SDS buffer (100 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8, 5 mM EDTA, 0.5% SDS). Cells were incubated on dry ice for 10 min and then pelleted at 1200 rpm for 6 min. Cells were resuspended in ice cold IP Buffer (IP Buffer: 1 volume of SDS Buffer: 0.5 volume of Triton dilution Buffer (100 mM Tris HCl pH 8.6, 100 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA, 5% Triton). Fixed cells were sonicated using a Branson Sonifier 250 (settings: output control 1, duty cycle 30) for six times on ice with 2–3 min rest in between. The quality and length of chromatin fragments (200–500 bp) were assessed by loading samples on agarose gel (2%) and using Agilent Bioanalyzer instrument. Then, sonicated chromatin was centrifuged at 20 000 g for 30 min at 4°C. Sonicated chromatin was quantified by Bradford and used for immunoprecipitation of SP140, H3K27me3 and MBD3 overnight at 4°C. IgG were used as negative control while input (1%, was saved as total control to normalize the signal). The next day, 40 μL of G-Sepharose beads were added and incubated for 4 hours. Then, the beads were washed 3 times with washing buffer 1 (1% Triton, 0.1% SDS, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA pH8, 20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8) and once with washing buffer 2 (1% Triton, 0.1% SDS, 500 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA pH 8, 20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8). Samples were de-crosslinked by adding the de-crosslinking solution (1% SDS, 0.1M NaHCO
3
) and incubated overnight at 65°C.

MBD3 ChIP-seq
Libraries were prepared following the manufacturer protocols for genome sequencing with the Ion Proton sequencer (Thermo Fisher Scientific/Life Technologies). Briefly, 10 ng of Chip DNA was end repaired and adapter ligated using the NEBNext® Fast DNA Library Prep Set for Ion Torrent™ (NEWENGLAND Biolabs, inc) and adapter barcode Ion Xpress Barcode Adaptors 1–16. After adapter ligation each sample was size selected using AMPure XP Bead (Beckman Coulter, inc). An amplification reaction was set up in a final volume of 50 μl. A SPRI cleanup with a 1.5X Bead:DNA ratio was performed post amplification and final libraries were eluted in 25 μl. Libraries were quantified on Qubit fluorometer with HS DNA (Thermo Fisher Scientific/Life Technologies) and checked for size on an Agilent Bioanalyzer with HS DNA kit (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA).
Each size selected library was diluted according to the final concentration of 11 pM and clonally amplified using the Ion Proton™ Hi-Q™Template Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific/Life Technologies) with IonOneTouch 2 instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific/Life Technologies). After emulsion PCR, DNA positive ISPs were recovered and enriched according to standard protocols with the IonOneTouch ES Instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific/Life Technologies). A sequencing primer was annealed to DNA positive ISPs and the sequencing polymerase bound, prior to loading of ISPs into Ion P1 sequencing chips. Sequencing of the samples was conducted according to the Ion Proton™ Hi-Q Sequencing Kit Protocol. One P1 sequencing chips with three libraries were loaded and run on an Ion Proton sequencer to generate reads with an average length of 150–200.

SP140 associates to PRC2 and NuRD transcription repressor complexes
To identify SP140 interactors, we performed a mass spectrometry interactome study in Daudi cells, where we had previously observed the highest levels of
SP140
mRNA compared to other leukocytic cell lines (
8
).
Since SP140 bears a PHD-BRD cassette, a well-known chromatin reader tandem domain (with a specificity for hypomethylated H3K4
i

Next, we evaluated the stoichiometry of SP140 interactors within the immunoprecipitated NuRD and PRC2 complexes comparing the relative abundance of detected interactors measured by the Intensity-based Absolute Quantitation (iBAQ) values (
Supplementary Table S1
) (

,

Supplementary Figure S1B
).
Based on its structural organization SP140 was expected to have highly dynamic chromatin interactions, we thus explored different IP-MS experimental conditions to define both the stable and the transient interactome. In stringent conditions with high detergent concentration, we identified 39 stable interactors (t-test FDR < 0.05), plus 33 on/off proteins (
Supplementary Table S1
) (Figure
1G
), 23 of which were also present in milder conditions (
Supplementary Table S1
-
S3
). Notably, GATAD2A and SUZ12 proteins, members of NuRD and PRC2 complexes respectively, were strongly associated to SP140.
Next, to identify SP140 transient interactors, we performed an interactome analysis after crosslinking with formaldehyde, which stabilizes weak interactions (Figure
1H
,
Supplementary Table S4
). In contrast to other protocols, such as chromatin immunoprecipitation coupled to mass spectrometry (ChIP-MS) where crosslinking is performed directly on intact cells (
40

Supplementary Figure S1C
,
Supplementary Table S4
,
Supplementary Table S5
). By comparison of the LFQ ratio of SP140 interactors in the three IP-MS experiments (i.e. mild, stringent and crosslinking conditions), we measured an increased ratio of
bona fide
transient interactors upon crosslinking. Thus, additional PRC2 components that were previously missing, such as EZH2, were retrieved in such conditions (Figure
1I
,
Supplementary Table S5
).
SP140 preferentially binds to chromatin regions associated to Polycomb repressed state
Prompted by the chromatin-related nature of SP140 interactors as well as by the ability of SP140 to bind chromatin (
17
), we searched for chromatin regions targeted by endogenous SP140. Chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by high-throughput DNA sequencing (ChIP-seq) in Daudi cells highlighted 8380 SP140 peaks (
Supplementary Figure S2A
), the majority of which were in intronic and distal intergenic regions while only 5% of the peaks (corresponding to 1135 genes) were called in regions close to the promoters (i.e. ± 1.5 Kb around the Transcription Starting Site, TSS) (
Supplementary Figure S2B
). We annotated 2457 SP140 target genes in Daudi cells, among which we validated SP140 binding to the promoters of
BCL2
and
MYC
genes by ChIP-qPCR (
Supplementary Figure S3A-B
).
Annotation of the SP140 peaks based on the chromatin states segmentation of the Burkitt Lymphoma cells genome using the publicly available data from the Blueprint consortium (
http://www.blueprint-epigenome.eu/
) indicated that SP140 was mainly bound to regions associated to the repressed-Polycomb state (‘LowRepr’ annotation,
Supplementary Figure S2C
). Accordingly, EnrichR highlighted a significant enrichment of SUZ12 target genes within SP140 target genes (
Supplementary Figure S2D
). These results, in line with the preferential binding of SP140 to PRC2-repressed chromatin in macrophages (
19
), suggest that SP140-PRC2 interaction might guide PRC2 to specific genetic loci.
To further corroborate our hypothesis, we explored SP140-chromatin interaction in Ramos cells, another Burkitt Lymphoma cell line (Figure
2A
) and we identified 31534 SP140 peaks and 4758 annotated SP140 target genes. Daudi and Ramos cells share a significative amount of SP140 target genes (31.5%,
P
-value < 2.2e-16,
Supplementary Figure S2E
); the observed differences may be ascribed to EBV infection (Daudi cells are EBV positive) and/or to the different genomic background of the two cell lines. We confirmed also in Ramos cells that SP140 binds mainly outside the gene promoters (Figure
2B
), to chromatin regions associated to repressed Polycomb state (Figure
2C
) and SUZ12 binding (Figure
2D
). Finally, biotype analysis of the SP140 target genes identified in Daudi and Ramos cells indicated that less than half of them code for proteins, while one fourth codes for lincRNAs (
Supplementary Figure S2F
). Collectively, ChIP-seq analysis performed in two different Burkitt Lymphoma cell lines indicate that SP140 preferentially binds to chromatin regions associated to PRC2-repressed state.

To identify the functional modifications induced by SP140, we knocked out

in Burkitt lymphoma cell lines. Unfortunately, we were able to produce a CRISPR/Cas9-mediated

), immunofluorescence analysis (

To validate in Ramos cells the interaction between SP140 and PRC2 observed in Daudi cells, we performed SP140 immunoprecipitation in both WT and KO cells. SP140 was immunoprecipitated in WT cells and not in KO cells, highlighting the specificity of the used antibody. In addition, SUZ12 and MTA1 co-precipitations with SP140 were confirmed in Ramos cells (

). The interaction of SP140 with SUZ12 was further corroborated by a reverse immunoprecipitation using an anti SUZ12 antibody (

SP140 regulates H3K27me3 levels on genes related to Hippo/YAP signaling and leukemia progression
To obtain a functional readout of SP140 in transcriptional regulation, we performed gene ontology enrichment analysis of the 2060 SP140 target genes coding for proteins identified in Ramos cells. We found a prevalence of regulators/receptors (e.g. cadherins, protocadherins, receptor tyrosine kinases, G proteins, α-catenins, axin2), signaling molecules (e.g. WNT5A, WNT2) and key transcription regulators (e.g. YAP1, TEAD1) belonging to the Hippo/YAP and Wnt signaling pathways or involved in their regulation (e.g. PI3K/AKT pathway) (Figure

). Manual inspection of SP140 target genes combined to Panther classification analysis uncovered protein-coding genes involved in inflammatory response, BCR signaling and pathways regulating NF-kB activation (e.g. RANKL, TNF-α and TGF-β pathways,
Supplementary Figure S5D
).

SP140 guides NuRD complex on specific genes belonging to the Hippo pathway
As our interactomic data indicated that SP140 interacts not only with PRC2 but also with NuRD transcription repressor complexes (Figure
1
), we hypothesized that SP140 might also affect the genomic localization of the NuRD complex. To verify this hypothesis, we performed ChIP-seq analysis of MBD3, a core member of the NuRD complex, in WT and
SP140
KO Ramos cells. Peak calling analysis showed a comparable number of MBD3 peaks and target genes in WT (11282 peaks corresponding to 4124 genes) and in KO cells (12571 peaks corresponding to 4623 genes) (Figure
5A
), indicating that SP140 does not affect NuRD overall binding to chromatin. In detail, MBD3 was bound to intergenic, intronic and promoter regions (Figure
5B
) with no significant differences in MBD3 feature and genomic distribution relative to TSS between SP140 WT and KO cells (Figure
5B
,
C
). However, upon SP140 depletion, MBD3 markedly changed its gene targets and bound to only 47% of the targets identified in WT cells (1973 genes out of 4214, Figure
5D
). These data suggest that SP140 plays a role in guiding NuRD to its targets. Among the 2241 MDB3 target genes that were no longer bound in
SP140
KO cells, 240 are also SP140 target genes (Figure
6E
). These genes appear to be simultaneously regulated by NuRD and SP140, and are enriched in genes belonging to the Hippo signaling pathway (Figure
6F
). The latter was also enriched, but with a lower ranking, within the protein coding genes shared by MBD3 and SP140 in WT cells (704 out of 4214,
Supplementary Figure S7
). Taken together, our results suggest that SP140 contributes to the recruitment of both PRC2 and NuRD complexes to specific chromatin regions, enriched mainly in genes coding for proteins belonging to the Hippo/YAP signaling pathway.

Global proteomic analysis of
SP140
KO Ramos cells sustains SP140 involvement in the regulation of Hippo and cytokine signaling pathways
We performed quantitative proteomic analysis of WT and
SP140
KO Ramos cells to evaluate SP140 role in transcriptional regulation at the protein level. Among the 6194 identified proteins in WT and KO cells, 156 were differentially regulated (in detail 85 up and 71 downregulated in KO cells) (Figure
6A
and
Supplementary Table S7
,
S8
). Nine of the deregulated proteins were coded by SP140 target genes (Figure
6B
and
Supplementary Table S7
,
S8
), seven of which were upregulated upon SP140 depletion, suggesting that SP140 affects their expression acting as a transcriptional repressor.
The proteomics and the ontology enrichment analysis of the ChIP-seq data agree in showing that SP140 modulates the expression level of proteins related to cytokine production and B cell differentiation (Figure
6E
) such as Janus kinase 3 (JAK3,
Supplementary Table S7
), essential for T cell and B cell development (
44
). Agreement is also observed for Hippo/YAP signaling. When the Hippo pathway is inactive (Hippo off), YAP1 enters the nucleus and activates genes promoting cell survival, proliferation and metastasis; when the Hippo pathway is active (Hippo on), YAP1 is phosphorylated and degraded in the cytosol, thus preventing promotion of cell growth and survival. Upon
SP140
KO the serine threonine kinase 3 (STK3 /MST2), is strongly reduced while IRF2BP2 (interferon regulatory factor 2 binding protein 2) is upregulated (
Supplementary Table S7
). STK3 ensures a Hippo on signaling as it phosphorylates LAST1/2, which in turn phosphorylates YAP1 for degradation, and increases transcription of proapoptotic genes (
45
). On the contrary, IRF2BP2 promotes YAP activity (Hippo signaling off) and tumorigenesis (
46
). Moreover, we showed that SP140 contributes to the recruitment of both PRC2 and NuRD complexes to
YAP1
and
TEAD1
genes (
Supplementary Figure S5
), whose transcriptional repression downregulates cell survival, proliferation and metastasis. Taken together, proteomics and ChIP-seq analysis agree in defining Hippo on and Hippo off conditions in WT and
SP140
KO cells, respectively.
Of note, COBLL1 was also found significantly upregulated in
SP140
KO cells by proteomics (Figure
6C
) and confirmed at mRNA level by RT-qPCR (Figure
6D
). This aligns with our ChIP-seq data, showing that the
COBLL1
gene is bound by both SP140 and SUZ12 and significantly hypomethylated upon SP140 depletion (Figure
4B
). These data support the model by which SP140 binding to PRC2 is responsible for directing PRC2 to SP140 target loci to deposit the H3K27me3 histone mark and repress transcription.
In summary, interactome, ChIP-seq and proteomics analysis of WT and
SP140
KO Ramos cells revealed a functional correlation between SP140-mediated transcriptional repression and Hippo/YAP, Wnt, PI3K/AKT signaling and genes involved in leukemia (COBLL1, HOXB genes and others).
In agreement with these findings, we observed an enhanced growth of
SP140
KO Ramos cells compared to WT cells (Figure
6F
), indicating that SP140 is a regulator of proliferation in these cells. As COBLL1 is a negative regulator of apoptosis in cultured tumor cells (
47
) and
SP140
KO leads to decreased H3K27me3 level in
COBLL1
gene and concurrent upregulation of COBL11 mRNA and protein, we chose
COBLL1
gene as paradigm to test the biological significance of dysregulated genes upon
SP140
KO. We therefore investigated the effects of
COBLL1
KD in both WT and
SP140
KO Ramos cells (
Supplementary Figure S8A
).
COBLL1
KD led to decreased cell proliferation in
SP140
WT cells, underscoring COBLL1 oncogenic potential in Burkitt lymphoma. Moreover,
COBLL1
KD rescued the increased proliferation due to
SP140
KO, as
SP140-/- COBLL1-/-
cells showed a growth rate similar to
SP140
WT cells (
Supplementary Figure S8B–D
). These findings substantiate the notion that the increased proliferation observed in
SP140
KO cells is at least partially driven by COBLL1 upregulation caused by absence of SP140 binding on
COBLL1
gene and by the consequent loss of PRC2 mediated transcriptional repression.
Discussion
SP140 is a lymphocytic-restricted chromatin reader, highly expressed in mature B cells, dendritic cells, macrophages and granulocytes (
2
,
8
); its dysregulation has been associated to different autoimmune disorders (
14
,
18
) and haematological malignancies (
12
,
13
). SP140 localizes to PML NBs (
1
), nuclear membrane-less organelles, where it exerts its gene silencing activity (
2
,
3
,
5
,
6
). A major unmet question in SP140 biology is the molecular mechanism by which it represses gene transcription.
In this work, taking advantage of a multi-omics approach combining mass spectrometry and ChIP-seq, we provide evidence that in Burkitt lymphoma B cells endogenous SP140 localizes to H3K27me3 rich chromatin and interacts with PRC2 and NuRD repression complexes. This interaction is functional, as SP140 depletion alters both H3K27me3 and NuRD distribution on genes. All together our data suggest that SP140 is a lymphocytic-restricted member of PRC2 and NuRD complexes, that contributes to their chromatin localization, thus modulating their transcription regulation on specific loci.
Of note, our results describe the physiological function of endogenous SP140 in human B cell lines (Burkitt lymphoma) without any cellular stimulation. In this context, SP140 is coimmunoprecipitated with PRC2 and NuRD complexes and share with them common target genes. In particular, genes belonging and regulating the Hippo/YAP pathway (YAP, TEAD1, cadherins, Wnt, PI3K/AKT and G protein signaling). Accordingly, genetic loss of SP140 results in a significant decrease of H3K27me3 deposition and NuRD displacement on genes related to Hippo/YAP and Wnt pathways and leukemia progression (i.e. HOXB genes, SHH, NOTCH, ERG2, ITGB4, SOCS3, COBLL1). Tumorigenesis, hematological malignancies and resistance to chemotherapies are often linked to YAP overexpression or to genetic abnormalities decreasing Hippo signaling and thus leading to abnormal YAP activation (
20
). It is tempting to speculate, and worth to investigate, that a differential expression of SP140 may modulate the Hippo/YAP or Wnt pathways also in CLL, where
SP140
is a susceptibility locus associated to reduced SP140 expression (
14
), and in multiple myeloma, where
SP140
truncating and missense mutations have been observed in patients (
12
).
Based on our omics-data, we suggest the following model for SP140 modulated transcriptional repression: the physical interaction between SP140 and PRC2 drives the latter on specific SP140 target loci to deposit the H3K27me3 mark and to repress transcription.
COBLL1
appears to be the prototypical gene whose expression is regulated by SP140: it is bound by both SP140 and SUZ12 and upon SP140 depletion it loses H3K27me3 mark and is upregulated both at mRNA and protein level. Notably, we observed that depletion of SP140 in Ramos cells induces cell overgrowth, which can be rescued by COBLL1 silencing, establishing a functional role of SP140 on this target gene. In addition,
SP140
+/+
COBLL1
-/-
Ramos cells showed decreased cell proliferation, underscoring COBLL1 oncogenic potential in Burkitt lymphoma. This is in line with previous data indicating COBLL1 as a negative regulator of apoptosis in cultured tumor cells (
47
).
In the CLL pathological context, where SNPs in
SP140
gene are associated to reduced SP140 expression (
14
), it would be interesting to evaluate whether the subgroup of patients with not mutated IGHV, high COBLL1 and unfavorable disease prognosis (
42
) show low SP140 mRNA/protein.
We also found SP140 bound to genes linked to the inflammatory response (i.e. BCL11B, PTX3, interleukins and their receptors) and to proteins belonging to RANKL, TNF-a and TGF-b pathways, which have an important role in immunity (
48
). Accordingly, SP140 depletion resulted in a significant reduction of the H3K27me3 levels of genes related to inflammation and immune response, such as NIK and NKIRAS2, important members of the NF-kB signaling. Also in this case, our data could provide a mechanistic explanation of previously published data showing SP140-mediated repression of genes involved in NF-kB pathway and pro-inflammatory cytokines production, and containing SNPs associated to autoimmune diseases (Multiple Sclerosis, CD, inflammatory bowel disease) (
17
).
In inflammatory macrophages generated
in vitro
by LPS stimulation, SP140 was also enriched at gene loci encoding pro-inflammatory cytokines and related to inflammatory and innate immune pathways (
49
).
In that context SP140 preferentially occupied strong transcription associated regions, enhancers and TSS regions, at variance to Burkitt lymphoma cell lines where the SP140 occupancy on TSS is small. We hypothesize that this difference might be stimulus and/or cell line dependent. Interestingly, in another work, where human macrophages were stimulated by INF-γ and LPS, SP140 occupied H3K27me3 rich chromatin regions (
2
). In that case SP140 maintained macrophage identity by repressing lineage-inappropriate genes, such as
HOX
,
OLIG
and
FOX
genes (
2
), with silencing of
HOX
genes being determinant for the development of macrophages. In Ramos cells we found that
HOXB3
,
HOXB5
and
HOXB6
are targets of both SP140 and SUZ12 and are strongly hypomethylated upon
SP140
depletion.
Possibly, regulation of PRC2-mediated H3K27me3 deposition and NuRD chromatin distribution are not the only mechanisms by which SP140 controls gene expression. Indeed, SP140 interactome in Burkitt lymphoma B cells comprises a multitude of proteins related to chromatin remodeling, DNA methylation and RNA processing. This is in agreement with SP140 interactome in HEK293T nuclear lysates overexpressing FLAG-SP140, where SP140 interacts with topoisomerases 1–2 and DNA unwinding and chromatin remodeling proteins (
6
). Interestingly, we also found a strong reduction of H3K27me3 levels on
TOP2A
gene upon
SP140
KO, which may indicate an altered topoisomerase activity upon SP140 depletion also in Burkitt lymphoma cells.
Finally, it is intriguing to note that less than half of SP140 target genes codes for proteins, both in Ramos and Daudi cells (
Supplementary Figure S2F
) and more than one fourth of SP140 target and hypomethylated genes upon
SP140

In conclusion, this study adds new insights into mechanisms underlying SP140-driven transcriptional regulation and pathogenicity. It unravels for the first-time the functional interactions between SP140 and both NuRD and PRC2 complexes. Through them the epigenetic reader SP140 controls H3K27me3 deposition and NuRD localization on genes linked to Hippo/YAP, NF-kB, cytokine and PI3K-AKT signaling pathways, all implicated in tumor progression and proliferation. These data are in agreement with SP140 loss-of-function mutations associated with several diseases, such as CD, multiple sclerosis and CLL (
