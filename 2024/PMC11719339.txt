Introduction
Acute Myeloid Leukemia (AML) stands as a formidable challenge in the realm of hematologic malignancies, characterized by its rapid progression and a marked heterogeneity that complicates its diagnosis, treatment, and prognostic evaluation [
[1]
,
[2]
,
[3]
]. As a clonal disorder, AML arises from the myeloid line of blood cells, manifesting a wide array of genetic aberrations that contribute to its complex pathophysiology [
4
,
5
]. Despite advances in our understanding and management of the disease, the prognosis for AML patients remains variable, with a 5-year survival rate that underscores an urgent need for more effective therapeutic strategies [
[6]
,
[7]
,
[8]
].
The heterogeneity of AML at the genetic, molecular, and cellular levels poses significant hurdles in developing universally effective treatments [
9
,
10
]. This diversity not only influences the initial response to therapy but also contributes to the risk of relapse, which is often marked by resistance to previously effective treatments [
[11]
,
[12]
,
[13]
]. The advent of personalized medicine, which tailors treatment to the individual characteristics of each patient's disease, holds promise for improving outcomes in AML [
14
,
15
]. However, realizing this potential requires a deeper understanding of the molecular drivers of AML pathogenesis and progression.
In recent years, the molecular characterization of AML has become an integral part of its diagnostic and prognostic framework, with specific genetic mutations informing risk stratification and treatment choices [
[16]
,
[17]
,
[18]
]. Yet, while this molecular profiling has led to significant advancements, the complex interplay of these genetic factors with the bone marrow microenvironment, particularly the role of cytokines in modulating immune responses and leukemic cell behavior, remains less understood [
19
,
20
]. Cytokines, as key mediators of inflammation and immune responses, are believed to play crucial roles in the leukemic niche, affecting proliferation, survival, and the evasion of immune surveillance by leukemic cells [
21
,
22
].
The advent of single-cell sequencing technologies has revolutionized precision oncology, offering a detailed view of the cellular and molecular intricacies of tumors, including AML [
23
,
24
]. This method allows for an in-depth analysis of the diverse cell populations within AML and their roles in disease progression and treatment resistance. Our study leverages this technology to delve into the AML landscape, focusing on cytokine-related gene signatures to uncover potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets. By creating a risk score model from these findings, we aim to enhance the prediction of treatment outcomes, contributing to more personalized and effective AML management strategies.
Methods
Data collection
In this investigation, a comprehensive dataset of gene expression profiles from bulk RNA-seq and detailed clinical records was compiled, along with the retrieval of microarray data and high-throughput information obtained from individuals diagnosed with AML from the The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA,
https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/
). The gene expression data and clinical characteristics of each cohort were meticulously organized and samples with survival data were specifically retained for analysis. Furthermore, for validation purposes, the researchers incorporated datasets from the publicly available
GSE12417
,
GSE37642
as well as
GSE235923
(
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/
) for single-cell scRNA-seq data scrutiny (2 primary AML and 2 recurrence AML). Proteomic data included the bone marrow protein abundance measurements of 44 acute AML patients, 3 healthy Lineage-depleted controls, and 3 healthy CD34-selected controls (labeled free quantification (LFQ) mass spectrometry data). In addition, the cytokine gene dataset (
n
= 263) was derived from the MSigDB database [
25
]. TCGA and the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) are open-access databases, freely available for retrieval and utilization within the bounds of regulatory guidelines, without the need for additional ethical approval. Throughout the data acquisition and analysis processes, we strictly adhered to all relevant regulations. Detailed information regarding sequencing platforms and other instruments can be found within the data sets and associated links provided.
Single cell sequence analysis
Single cell sequencing data (
GSE235923
) were analyzed. Analysis of single cell sequencing data based on seurat package, cell quality control conditions: nFeature_RNA > 100 &nFeature_RNA <5000&percent.mt <25. Based on the ligand–receptor information, we used the single-cell gene expression matrix to unravel the communication between immune cell subtypes which was contained in CellChat software (
http://www.cellchat.org/
) with default parameters, modeling the communication probability and identifying significant communications.
Functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes
The cutting-edge limma R software package was effectively utilized for the pinpointing of differentially expressed gene (DEGs) in cancer and paracancer samples sourced from TCGA-AML dataset (|log
2
FC| >1 & adj.Pvalue <0.05). The R package ggplot was used to draw the volcano map, and the pheatmap package was used to draw the protein abundance heatmap of topDEPs. The functional analysis including GO, KEGG and GSEA about the DEGs between high and low-risk cohorts was meticulously conducted through the utilization of the sophisticated R package known as ``clusterProfiler'' (version 4.0.5), adhering to a stringent false discovery rate (FDR) threshold of <0.05 to ascertain the presence of substantial enrichment [
26
]. Using Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins (STRING,
https://string-db.org/
), the PPI analysis was conducted. And the visualization of enrichment analysis was performed by GseaVis.
Construction and validation of a prognostic model
The researchers applied COX and Lasso regression analysis to screen for genes strongly associated with prognosis of AML using R package ``glmnet'' [
27
].Then we calculated the risk scores of each patient based on the following model formula: Risk score = Σi Coefficient (mRNAi) × Expression(mRNAi). The ``surv_cutpoint'' function from the R package ``survminer'' was utilized to establish the cut-off value, which computes statistics grounded in maximally selected rank statistics. Patients were classified into high-risk and low-risk categories employing this cutoff value as a demarcation point. To analyze the overall survival (OS) between these two groups, the Kaplan–Meier (K-M) method was applied. Additionally, the prognostic model's predictive validity and reliability were evaluated through time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, utilizing the R package timeROC.
Immune infiltration analysis
Using IOBR R package (
https://github.com/IOBR/IOBR
) built-in 8 TME algorithm, including CIBERSORT, TIMER, xCell, MCPcounter, ESITIMATE, EPIC, IPS, quanTIseq analyzed the bulk RNA-seq dataset [
28
,
29
]. The ESTIMATE algorithm was used to analysis the difference of stromal score, immune score and ESTIMATE score by the R package “estimate”. The MCP counter to calculate the abundance of immune infiltrating cells. The CIBORSORT, EPIC and TIMER etc. algorithm was also used to assess the level of immune cell infiltration in high and low risk groups. And immunosuppression, immune rejection, immune checkpoint related molecules were also compared between high and low risk group. The above results were visualized by violion plot using R package ggplot2 and ggpubr.
Cell culture and transfection
In this study, the cell lines MV-4-11 and AML-193, representing human acute monocytic leukemia, were utilized and were sourced from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. Both cell lines were cultured in Iscove's Modified Dulbecco's Medium (IMDM, KeyGen, China) supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS, KeyGEN, China) and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin mixture (Procell, China) to ensure cell growth and minimize contamination risks. Cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5 % CO₂ and passaged every 48 h. Regular observations were conducted to ensure cell health prior to passaging, and strict aseptic techniques were adhered to throughout the process to prevent contamination.
Transfection experiments were performed to knock down INHBA expression in MV-4–11 and AML-193 cell lines using siRNA (Sangon, China) designed by a biotech company. After digestion with trypsin (KeyGEN, China), the cells were thoroughly suspended in the culture medium and then evenly plated into 6-well plates, with approximately 3 × 10
5
cells added to each well. Complete culture medium was then added to bring the final volume to 2 ml per well, and the plates were gently agitated to ensure uniform distribution. After observing cell adhesion under the microscope, we mixed the siRNA and transfection reagent Lipofectamine™ 3000 (Thermo, USA) in a fixed ratio according to the manufacturer's instructions, incubated at room temperature for 20 min, and then added the mixture uniformly to the corresponding wells. Five hours after transfection, the medium was replaced, and the subsequent experiments were carried out 48 h thereafter.
RT-qPCR
RNA samples were subjected to genomic DNA removal using the PrimeScript RT Kit (TaKaRa, Japan), followed by reverse transcription into cDNA (20 μl reaction system). SYBR GreenER Supermix (TaKaRa, Japan) was used for qPCR, with all reagents pre-mixed. An 18 μl pre-mixed solution was added to each well of an 8-tube strip, followed by 2 μl of cDNA sample and a brief centrifugation. Using the 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR analysis was conducted with reaction parameters configured per the manufacturer's guidelines. Relative INHBA expression levels were evaluated using the 2
−ΔΔCt
method, with β-actin used for normalization.
Western blot
Cell lysates were prepared by centrifuging cells from 6-well plates into centrifuge tubes, retaining the cell pellet, and adding protein lysis buffer (RIPA:PMSF = 100:1, Beyotime, China). The mixture was incubated on ice with intermittent shaking/centrifugation every 10 min for three cycles, followed by centrifugation at 1.2 × 10
4
rpm, Incubated for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected, and the protein concentration was determined using the BCA method. The samples were then mixed with the loading buffer, kept at 95–100 °C for 5 min, and then allowed to cool. Proteins were resolved through electrophoresis on 10 % SDS-PAGE gels (Epizyme, China) and subsequently transferred to 0.45 μm PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked with rapid blocking solution (Beyotime, China) maintained for 10 min, washed thrice with TBST and then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Following this, the samples were washed three more times with TBST., membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies for 2 h, washed again with TBST, and developed using ECL reagent (Beyotime, China). Protein bands were visualized and analyzed using image analysis software.
CCK8 assay
Cell proliferation was assessed using the CCK8 assay. Forty-eight hours post-transfection, MV-4-11 and AML-193 cells were plated into 96-well plates at a density of 3000 cells per well and then incubated for 12 h. CCK8 reagent (KeyGEN, China) was added to each well according to the manufacturer's instructions, and plates were incubated in the dark. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured 1.5 h later using a spectrophotometer. This process was repeated at 24, 48, 72, and 96 h, and results were plotted.
Flow cytometry
We employed flow cytometry to measure cellular apoptosis by transferring cells to a centrifuge tube and centrifuging them at 2000 RPM/min for 5 min to obtain a cell pellet. The cells were washed twice with PBS, and 5 × 10
5
cells were collected and transferred to a flow cytometry tube. 500 uL of Binding Buffer was added to suspend the cells, followed by the addition and mixing of 5 uL of Annexin V-FITC and 5 uL of Propidium Iodide. The reaction was incubated for 15 min in a dark environment at 37 °C in a water bath, and cellular apoptosis was assessed using flow cytometry.
Statistical analysis
All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.1.3). Unless otherwise specified, all figures were generated with the “ggplot2” package. A
p
-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant (*
p
< 0.05; **
p
< 0.01; ***
p
< 0.001; ****
p
< 0.0001).

Establishment and evaluation of a risk score model
Researchers conducted a differential analysis using the TCGA combined with the GETX database (Tumor = 173, Normal = 70), identifying 6813 differentially expressed genes. Intersection with cytokine-related genes yielded 156 differentially expressed cytokine-related genes (Fig. S2). In the TCGA-AML and
GSE12417
datasets, univariate Cox regression prognosis analysis was performed on the 156 intersecting genes. Based on a
P

Discussion
Our study presents a comprehensive analysis of the cellular and molecular heterogeneity in AML using single-cell sequencing technology, with a particular emphasis on cytokine-related gene signatures and their prognostic significance. By integrating these insights, we developed a novel risk score model that holds promise for enhancing the prediction of treatment outcomes in AML patients. Here, we discuss our findings in the broader context of existing literature, reflecting on their implications, limitations, and potential avenues for future research.
The identification of cellular subgroups within AML samples in our study aligns with previous research that has highlighted the immense cellular diversity in AML. However, our findings extend this knowledge by providing a more granular view of the cellular landscape, including the delineation of cytokine-related gene expression patterns within these subgroups. This level of detail offers new insights into the functional roles of these cells in AML pathogenesis and their interactions with the bone marrow microenvironment. Compared to earlier studies, our approach reveals a more complex picture of the AML ecosystem, emphasizing the importance of understanding this heterogeneity for developing more effective treatments.
The cytokine-related gene signatures identified in our study represent a significant contribution to the understanding of the immunological underpinnings of AML. While cytokines have been recognized as critical players in cancer biology, their specific roles and expression patterns in AML have not been fully elucidated [
30
,
31
]. Our findings not only confirm the importance of cytokines in AML but also highlight specific signatures that could serve as biomarkers for disease prognosis or targets for therapeutic intervention. This aspect of our study is particularly novel, as it combines single-cell resolution with a focus on cytokine signaling, a combination that is relatively unexplored in AML research.
The development and validation of a risk score model based on cytokine-related gene expressions is another key achievement of our study. Existing prognostic models in AML often rely on clinical and genetic factors, but the integration of cytokine-related signatures provides a new dimension that could enhance the predictive accuracy of these models [
32
,
33
]. Our risk score model demonstrated significant prognostic value across multiple patient cohorts, suggesting its potential utility in clinical settings. This finding is especially relevant in the era of precision medicine, where such models can guide treatment decisions and risk stratification in a more personalized manner.
Despite these contributions, our study is not without limitations. The complexity of single-cell sequencing data necessitates careful interpretation, and the potential for technical variability or bias cannot be overlooked. Furthermore, the translation of findings from single-cell studies to clinical applications involves additional challenges, including the validation of biomarkers in larger, more diverse patient cohorts. Our study represents an important step in this direction, but further research is needed to confirm and expand upon our findings.
Looking ahead, future research should focus on validating the identified cytokine-related gene signatures in larger, independent cohorts of AML patients. Functional studies are also crucial to unravel the biological mechanisms underlying these signatures and their roles in AML progression and treatment resistance. Moreover, exploring the therapeutic potential of targeting cytokine pathways identified in our study could open new avenues for the development of novel treatments for AML. Ultimately, the integration of single-cell sequencing data with clinical and functional analyses holds great promise for advancing our understanding and management of AML, paving the way for more effective and personalized therapeutic strategies.
The robustness of our risk score model, particularly its predictive value across different AML cohorts, sets a foundation for its potential clinical application. However, translating such models into clinical practice requires extensive validation in prospective studies, encompassing diverse patient populations to ensure its broad applicability and effectiveness. This process is vital for overcoming one of the critical barriers to personalized medicine in AML—the variability in patient responses to treatment. By providing a more nuanced understanding of individual risk profiles, our model could significantly enhance the stratification of patients for tailored therapeutic approaches, potentially improving outcomes. The prognostic model for AML that we developed employed high-resolution single-cell RNA sequencing technology to conduct a detailed analysis of the complex cellular landscape associated with AML, and created a risk score model that focused on essential cytokine-related genes. This model was constructed based on genes associated with key cytokines, forming a risk scoring system. It demonstrated robust prognostic stratification capabilities across multiple patient cohorts and provided insights into immune-mediated mechanisms. Compared to conventional prognostic stratification methods, our model offers more precise and comprehensive information, facilitating more accurate risk assessment and advancing personalized medicine in AML. It also opens new avenues for future research and therapeutic optimization.
In addition to clinical validation, an important direction for future research is the exploration of the molecular pathways underpinning the cytokine-related gene signatures identified in our study. This entails not only mapping these pathways but also understanding their interactions and impact on the AML microenvironment. Such insights could reveal novel therapeutic targets and strategies, particularly in the realm of immunotherapy, where manipulating cytokine signaling could enhance anti-leukemic immune responses or overcome immune evasion mechanisms employed by leukemic cells.
Another promising avenue for future investigation is the integration of our findings with other omics data, such as proteomics and metabolomics. This multi-omics approach could provide a more comprehensive view of the AML landscape, revealing additional layers of complexity and novel biomarkers or therapeutic targets. Moreover, the application of advanced computational and machine learning techniques to integrate and analyze these diverse data types could further refine prognostic models and uncover new insights into AML pathogenesis.
The role of the bone marrow microenvironment in AML progression and treatment resistance is another critical area that warrants further exploration. Our study highlights the importance of cytokine signaling within this microenvironment, but the dynamic interactions between leukemic cells, stromal cells, and immune cells remain to be fully elucidated. Understanding these interactions could inform the development of therapies that target the microenvironment to disrupt the supportive niche for leukemic cells.
Finally, the pursuit of personalized medicine in AML necessitates not only advancements in research but also in healthcare infrastructure and policy. The implementation of precision medicine approaches, such as the use of our risk score model in clinical settings, requires the integration of advanced diagnostic tools, data management systems, and multidisciplinary care teams. Moreover, ethical, regulatory, and economic considerations must be addressed to ensure equitable access to personalized therapies and to navigate the challenges associated with genetic and data privacy.
In conclusion, our study contributes valuable insights into the cellular and molecular heterogeneity of AML and introduces a novel risk score model based on cytokine-related gene expressions. While our findings represent a significant step forward, they also highlight the complexity of AML and the need for continued research to translate these insights into improved patient care. By building on the foundations laid by this and other studies, the research community can advance toward the goal of more effective, personalized treatments for AML, ultimately improving outcomes for patients afflicted with this challenging disease.
