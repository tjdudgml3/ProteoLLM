BACKGROUND
Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disease characterized by cognitive impairments. Extracellular amyloid beta (Aβ) plaques and intraneuronal neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) composed of hyperphosphorylated tau (ptau) are thought to initiate a cascade of events that will ultimately result in synaptic loss and neuronal dysfunction, leading to cognitive decline. While Aβ deposits and ptau are required for a diagnosis of AD, one‐third of community‐dwelling elderly adults with significant amounts of AD neuropathology at autopsy displayed no cognitive deficits.
1
Studying individuals that are less susceptible to AD pathology, a phenomenon that is referred to as resilience,
2
may provide new insights into therapeutics that could prevent cognitive decline despite the presence of brain pathology.
While the exact cellular and molecular substrates of resilience are not fully understood, previous studies identified processes associated with resilience or vulnerability. In particular, changes in synaptic plasticity‐associated proteins,
3
,
4
dendritic spine morphology,
5
and glia cells
6
,
7
,
8
have been found in resilient cases compared to AD patients. Moreover, resilience has been linked to alterations in inhibitory neurons such as somatostatin (SST)
9
,
10
or parvalbumin (PV)
11
expressing neurons. As it has been shown that inhibitory neurons, including PV interneurons, are among the first cells to show deficits in AD,
12
,
13
,
14
maintenance of inhibitory input on the circuitry could be important to prevent cognitive decline. The activity of PV neurons largely depends on the integrity of their perineuronal net (PNN).
15
PNNs are condensed aggregates of extracellular matrix (ECM) surrounding the cell body and dendrites of neurons, including PV neurons. PNNs contribute to the end of critical periods, developmental time windows characterized by elevated levels of plasticity. The main components of PNNs are chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans (CSPGs).
16
CSPGs consist of a core protein and chondroitin sulfate‐glycosaminoglycan (CS‐GAG) side chains, which exist in various isoforms with different sulfation patterns.
17
PNNs form a mesh‐like structure around synaptic terminals, contributing to the stability of synaptic connections. Moreover, they provide neuroprotection against oxidative stress.
18
At the same time, PNNs are dynamic structures, and a reduction in PNNs has been linked to increased plasticity.
19
,
20
,
21
Accordingly, manipulation of PNNs results in enhanced plasticity in both healthy animals
22
,
23
,
24
,
25
and AD animal models.
26
,
27
,
28
The effect of the pathophysiology of AD on PNNs remains unclear. Several studies have reported a reduction in PNNs in the frontal cortex of AD patients.
29
,
30
However, others have reported no decrease in PNNs in subcortical regions
31
or in the temporal cortex.
32
Interestingly, a decrease in sulfated CS‐GAG and an increase in non‐sulfated CS‐GAG in the frontal cortex of AD patients were found, which correlate with disease pathology and severity of cognitive impairment.
33
Thus, another possibility is that AD is characterized by changes in PNN composition instead of a structural loss of PNNs. Furthermore, subjecting AD mice to an enriched environment improves cognition and restores the number of PV cells with a PNN, which is reduced in standard housed mice.
34
This suggests that PNNs may be neuroprotective and that the amount of PNNs may be linked to resilience to AD. Overall, there appears to be a dual role of PNNs in neuroprotection and restriction of plasticity, which might be disentangled by studying individuals resilient to AD. Here, we hypothesize that PNNs are differentially altered in AD and resilient donors, where PNN changes may be favorable to enhanced plasticity, thereby helping to maintain cognition.
RESEARCH IN CONTEXT
Systematic review
: The authors reviewed the most recent literature on PubMed to evaluate the current state of PNNs in AD. No data were available on PNNs in brains of human individuals resilient to AD.
Interpretation
: We demonstrate that the PNN component aggrecan around PV neurons is decreased in both resilient and AD subjects, whereas PNN‐chondroitin sulfates are reduced in resilient subjects. However, only in AD are presynaptic terminals on PV neurons decreased. Expression levels of genes involved in PNN degradation are increased in AD but not in resilient subjects.
Future directions
: The presence of distinct PNN changes in individuals resilient to AD when compared to AD patients is an avenue worth investigating. Follow‐up studies employing immunohistochemistry, spatial transcriptomics, and liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry will advance our knowledge on possible changes in other PNN components and/or in the sulfation pattern of chondroitin sulfates.
To test this hypothesis, we evaluated PNN number, staining intensity, and morphology in the frontal cortex of cognitively intact controls, AD patients, and resilient donors. Furthermore, to assess how dynamic changes in PNNs could influence cognition, the number of synapses were quantified around PNN‐enwrapped PV neurons. Moreover, we evaluated whether PNN‐bearing neurons were spared from ptau pathology. Finally, we compared the expression levels of genes related to PNN synthesis or degradation in controls, AD, and resilient donors.

METHODS
2.1
Human brain tissue
Snap‐frozen human brain tissue from the superior frontal gyrus (SFG) and formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) tissue from the SFG, medial frontal gyrus (MFG), or inferior frontal gyrus (IFG) were obtained from the Netherlands Brain Bank (NBB). Informed consent for brain autopsies and use of the brain material and clinical data for research purposes was obtained by the NBB according to international ethical guidelines. Autopsy procedures were approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Vrije Universiteit Medical Center, Amsterdam, the Netherlands. Neuropathological assessments were performed using standardized protocols. Donors were selected as described previously.
35
In brief, donors were selected based on the amount of AD pathology and cognition using the clinical dementia rating (CDR) scale
36
or global deterioration scale
37
(GDS): demented AD patients with intermediate or high amounts of AD pathology (CDR 3, Braak 4 to 6, Thal ≥ 3), resilient donors with intact cognition and intermediate to high amounts of AD pathology (CDR ≤ 0.5, Braak 3 to 5, Thal ≥ 3), and cognitively intact age‐matched controls with low amounts of AD pathology (CDR ≤ 0.5, Braak 1 or 2, Thal ≤ 2) were selected. Cases with any signs of psychiatric or neurological disease other than associated with AD were excluded. Donors with comorbid pathology (eg, Lewy bodies, TDP‐43 inclusions, hippocampal sclerosis) were excluded. Donors were matched as closely as possible for sex, age,
post mortem
delay (PMD), pH, and the presence of the apoliprotein E (APOE) ε4 allele (e.g. APOE εX/ε4 vs. APOE εX/εX, X = 2,3) (Table
1
).

2.2
Immunohistochemistry
The list of primary antibodies used in this study can be found in Table
2
. For all immunohistochemical procedures, two sections per donor were used. Both snap‐frozen tissue and FFPE tissue were used (Table
1
), which were cut using a cryostat into 20‐µm cryosections or using a microtome into 8‐µm sections, respectively. In brief, FFPE sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated, after which the sections were washed, and antigen retrieval was performed using citrate buffer (pH 6) in a microwave at 700 W for 10 min. Sections used for DAB development were treated with 0.3% H
2
O
2
diluted in methanol to block endogenous peroxidases. Sections were pre‐incubated with 10% fetal calf serum (FCS) diluted in antibody diluent (1% bovine serum albumin, 0.5% triton in PBS) to block aspecific binding of the secondary antibodies staining. Cryosections were defrosted and post‐fixated with 4% paraformaldehyde, after which the sections were washed in PBS and pre‐incubated with 10% FCS in antibody diluent. For all immunohistochemical experiments containing aggrecan antibodies, sections were incubated with chondroitinase ABC (ChABC) (0.1 U/mL, Seikagaku, Japan) in ChABC buffer (0.1 M Tris–HCl, 0.03 M sodium acetate, pH 8.0) at 37°C for 2 h. Treatment with ChABC was used for the enzymatic digestion of chondroitin sulfate side chains to increase the signal of aggrecan staining. Both FFPE and cryosections were incubated with primary antibodies in antibody diluent at 4°C O/N. The next day, sections were incubated in fluorescent secondary antibodies or streptavidin in antibody diluent (1:400; Jackson Immunoresearch) for 1 h at room temperature. Sections with biotinylated secondary antibodies were incubated with avidin–biotin–peroxidase complex (ABC; 1:800; Vector Labs), after which the sections were developed with DAB. The reaction was stopped in distilled water, and sections were dehydrated, cleared, and coverslipped with Entellan (Merck). Sections with immunofluorescent secondary antibodies were incubated with DAPI and subsequently treated with 0.1% Sudan black in 70% EtOH to quench autofluorescence. Finally, sections were coverslipped with FluorSave (Millipore).

2.3
Microscopy
Histological preparations were examined with an Axio Scan Z1 (ZEISS, Oberkochen, Germany) at 20× or a Leica TCS SP5 confocal microscope at 60× magnification. Confocal images were taken at a resolution of 1024 × 1024 dpi and 50 Hz speed. A small z‐stack of 5 µm was taken using three steps. Laser intensity, gain, and offset were kept constant in each analysis. Quantitative evaluations were performed using FIJI or QuPath and were conducted by a blinded experimenter.
2.4
Number and fluorescence intensity of PNNs
Two regions of interest (ROIs) per section were selected. As the majority of PNNs are found in cortical layers III and V, in line with previous observations,
38
the ROIs were outlined covering these layers. Wisteria floribunda agglutinin (WFA) signal was not uniform throughout the FFPE sections, as in some sulci no signal could be observed. To overcome this, ROIs only covered areas that had sufficient signal (at least three times the background). This resulted in the exclusion of four donors (one control, two resilient, and one AD) for the WFA staining on FFPE sections. For the aggrecan staining in FFPE tissue, one control donor consistently showed no signal in all experiments and was therefore excluded. Cortical layers were distinguished based on DAPI or NeuN. NeuN‐positive (NeuN+) and PV‐positive (PV+) cells were semi‐automatically annotated and counted using the cell detection function in QuPath based on DAPI overlapping with NeuN or PV. PNNs, visualized by either aggrecan or WFA, were manually outlined. The amount of PNNs, NeuN, or PV+ cells/mm
2
was calculated in the surface area of the ROIs. The intensity of the PNNs was determined by measuring the intensity of the individual PNNs, from which the background intensity, taken from areas in the gray matter between PNNs from at least two different locations, was subtracted in each individual section. Differences in the distribution of intensity of PNNs were determined by random sampling (using the R package dplyr) the same number of PNNs per donor, which was determined by the absolute number of PNNs of all donors. PNNs were assigned to quartiles based on their intensity, ensuring that over 80% of all donors had PNNs represented in each quartile.

2.6
Density of presynaptic terminals in PNNs
The density of presynaptic terminals in PNNs was determined by manually counting the number of SV2+ boutons with an area of at least 0.15 µm
2

2.7
PNNs around KGA+ excitatory neurons
Kidney‐type glutaminase (KGA) was used as a marker for excitatory cells. KGA‐positive (KGA+) cells in cortical layers III to V were manually annotated based on intensity and overlap with DAPI. PNNs visualized by WFA were manually outlined around KGA+ neurons.
2.8
AT8 levels in KGA+ excitatory neurons
AT8 intensity was measured in each KGA+ excitatory neuron in AD and resilient donors, after which KGA+ excitatory neurons with or without a WFA‐positive (WFA+) net were compared using one ROI per section. Only resilient donors with sufficient AT8 signals were used for this analysis (
n

2.9
Total, neuropil, and PNN levels of aggrecan and WFA
The total levels of aggrecan and WFA were determined in sections developed with DAB based on the optical density (OD). Two ROIs per section, covering all cortical layers, were used. Within each ROI, areas with at least three times the background intensity were considered the PNN‐positive surface area as the majority of the PNNs were included. Similar to the immunofluorescent WFA immunohistochemistry, signal varied between and within the same tissue sections. To overcome this, ROIs only covered areas that had sufficient signal (at least three times the background). This resulted in the exclusion of three donors (one control and two resilient). The integrated optical density (IOD) was calculated by multiplying the positive surface area by the OD, which was divided by the total area of the ROI to obtain the corrected IOD (cIOD). To quantify the amount of aggrecan and WFA found in the general ECM outside the PNNs, areas with at least three times the background intensity were excluded and the OD was measured in the remaining area.

Data analysis and statistics
Statistical analysis was performed with Prism GraphPad (version 10.2.3) or rstudio (R‐4.3.0). Data were tested for normality by the Shapiro‐Wilk test, followed by either ANOVA with the Tukey multiple‐comparisons test or Kruskal‐Wallis with the Dunn multiple‐comparisons test. Within‐subject comparisons or comparisons between two groups were performed with the two‐tailed paired

Transcriptional changes related to matrisome, PNNs, and microglia
To gain insight as to whether alterations in PNNs can be induced by changes on a transcriptional level, we explored a dataset of bulk RNA‐seq previously generated by our group in the SFG.
35
Here, we investigated the expression of genes related to the matrisome
41

Importantly, examination of individual genes used for the GSEA input revealed very few genes that were significantly different between the groups (Table

4
DISCUSSION
In the this study, we demonstrated that (1) aggrecan perisomatic staining and peridendritic coverage decrease in the frontal cortex of both AD and resilient subjects, particularly around PV neurons; (2) WFA‐labeled CS‐GAG around neuronal somata are substantially reduced in resilient subjects; (3) WFA peridendritic staining is less extended in AD subjects; (4) PNN changes in AD are accompanied by synaptic loss around PV neurons and increased expression of matrix‐degrading genes; and (5) excitatory neurons bearing a PNN show low levels of ptau. Overall, we showed that PNNs in AD and resilient subjects were differently affected by AD pathology. These changes in PNN morphology and composition may contribute to resilience to AD.
Decreased aggrecan expression was previously reported in the cortex of AD subjects.
29
Accordingly, here we detected a decrease in perisomatic and peridendritic aggrecan levels in AD, with a similar decrease in resilient subjects. However, the facts that synapses are less preserved only in the AD cases and genes related to PNN degradation are more highly expressed in AD point to the existence of distinct processes in the AD and resilient groups in relation to PNN maintenance. The dynamic remodeling of PNNs is orchestrated by a complex interplay between PNN‐enwrapped neurons themselves and surrounding glial cells. It has been shown that PNN components are expressed by PNN bearing neurons, astrocytes,
56
,
57
,
58
oligodendrocytes, and oligodendrocyte precursor proteins,
38
while matrix‐degrading enzymes are expressed by neurons, astrocytes, and microglia.
59
,
60
Microglial cells have been implicated as crucial modulators of PNNs in AD. For instance, microglial states associated with PNN degradation are more abundant in AD patients,
50
inclusions of PNN material have been found in mouse and human microglia in AD,
29
,
61
and inhibiting microglia prevents PNN loss in an AD animal model.
29
Interestingly, the more pro‐inflammatory states of microglia or astrocytes often associated with AD
42
,
62
are found in lower amounts in resilient donors.
6
,
7
,
63
Thus, microglia‐mediated phagocytosis of PNNs or synthesis/release of proteolytic enzymes may be reduced in resilient individuals. Indeed, some of the previously identified microglial signatures related to AD were more highly expressed in the AD compared to the resilient group. However, genes associated with these microglial signatures or related to PNNs are expressed by multiple cell types, so changes in their expression in the bulk RNA‐seq dataset used in our study may also be driven by cells other than microglia. This further explains why the differences that were picked up are subtle. Nevertheless, our data indicate that the reduction in PNNs in AD donors could be related to increased levels of matrix‐degrading enzymes, while this might not be the case in resilient donors.
Examination of the CS‐GAG changes by WFA labeling revealed that PV peridendritic coating was preserved in resilient individuals, whereas it decreased in AD. Because PNNs are required for astrocytic uptake of synaptically released glutamate and K
+
,
64
disruption of PNN dendritic coverage in AD may impair the clearance of these molecules, potentially altering PV neuron firing and contributing to excitation/inhibition imbalance. WFA changes may indicate a decrease in the total amount of CSPGs and a change in CS amount or in CS sulfation. CSs are carbohydrates that can be sulfated in various position. The sulfation positions result in different forms of CS (CS‐A, CS‐C, CS‐D, CS‐E), which are crucial for CS interaction with specific molecules, including the plasticity regulators orthodenticle homeobox 2 and semaphorin 3A.
65
,
66
,
67
Importantly, it has been hypothesized that WFA binds to non‐sulfated CSs,
68
,
69
which indicates that sulfation patterns can influence WFA labeling. Changes in CS amount or in CS sulfation may be caused by changes in the expression of CS‐GAG catabolic enzymes (glycosidases, hydrolases, and sulfatases) and/or expression/activity of specific sulfotransferases,
17
without the involvement of matrix‐degradation genes. Previous studies showed changes in CS sulfation patterns in human AD brains, including a decrease in non‐sulfated CS, which correlates with the amount of ptau pathology.
33
Moreover, in animal models with tauopathy, alterations in CS sulfation patterns and WFA+ PNNs occur only in the presence of gliosis.
70
This implies that changes in PNN sulfation may be driven by glial cells as a reaction to ptau pathology, and sulfation patterns may be different between AD and resilient donors.
In resilient subjects we observed a decreased number and intensity of WFA+ nets. As a reduction in PNN‐CS has been associated with plasticity processes,
19
,
25
WFA decrease in resilient subjects might reflect CS changes, which may create a favorable environment for compensatory plasticity, contributing to intact cognition. Indeed, changes in CS sulfation pattern with age play a strong role in the control of plasticity, with CS‐C more abundant during critical periods and associated with increased plasticity
71
,
72
,
73
and CS‐A predominantly expressed in adulthood and involved in plasticity inhibition.
74
Interestingly, digestion of CS restores memory in both tauopathy
26
and Aβ‐driven AD mice.
27
Increased plasticity is also known to lead to maladaptive responses, as seen, for instance, with the development of a phantom limb following limb amputation
75
or excessive neuronal network activation in epilepsy or early AD.
14
However, we hypothesize that in resilient donors a lower amount of PNN sugar chains might exert a permissive role for axon growth by setting the responsiveness of neural circuits to environmental stimulation, which in resilient subjects is usually enriched and which would play an instructive role in shaping newly formed connections,
76
ultimately helping maintain cognition.
Moreover, we found that the number of synaptic terminals around PV neurons was decreased in the AD patients, but not in resilient cases, further substantiating the idea that alterations in PNNs in resilient donors might contribute to plasticity. While these data are specific to synapses in PNNs, they are consistent with previous studies showing in general higher levels of synaptic proteins
63
and alterations in spine density
77
in resilient cases compared to AD. The increase in plasticity associated with the reduction of PNNs might be a compensational effect to maintain inhibitory input. Although we did not find any difference in the amount of PV or NeuN cells, others have reported changes in the amount of inhibitory synapses or inhibitory input in AD patients.
78
,
79
In animal models, PV neurons change morphology to compensate for SST loss by increasing its dendritic length and complexity.
80
Thus, it is conceivable that decreased levels of PNN may allow structural plasticity of PV neurons.
Notably, in control donors, WFA+ PNNs were three times more numerous than aggrecan+ PNNs. Moreover, 40% of PV cells were enwrapped by aggrecan, while 70% of PV cells were surrounded by WFA, in line with previous literature,
38
suggesting that PNNs do not always bear both aggrecan and WFA positivity. These data corroborate previous evidence in the mouse cortex in which a number of PNNs are WFA positive but do not contain aggrecan.
81
In addition, we found that WFA levels in PNNs around excitatory neurons were lower compared to inhibitory cells. Together, this suggests that different populations of PNNs exist in the human brain, characterized by distinct molecular compositions, which may contribute to diverse biochemical properties.
To our knowledge, we are the first to quantitively demonstrate that there is more ptau in excitatory neurons devoid of a PNN than in those surrounded by a PNN, corroborating the notion proposed in previous studies that PNNs may protect from tau pathology.
31
,
51
Furthermore, we found a negative correlation between the amount of Aβ plaques (measured by 4g8) and intensity of WFA+ PNNs around PV cells. This shows that, in contrast to previous claims,
31
alterations in PNNs correlate with plaque levels. The lack of ptau in PNN‐enwrapped cells further highlights the neuroprotective properties of PNNs, as they act as a protective shield against oxidative stress
18
,
82
and Aβ1‐42.
83
Moreover, they are proposed to buffer cations.
84
PNNs are mostly found around fast spiking interneurons, which have high activity and, thus, require efficient ion buffering. The observation that excitatory neurons enwrapped by PNNs have the highest KGA levels could indicate that also excitatory neurons require ion buffering as they might have higher levels of activity. These neurons may be protected from tau pathology in accordance with the “use it or lose it” hypothesis, which suggests that neuronal excitation might stimulate the action of protective mechanisms.
85
Of note, neurons containing KGA are decreased in resilient subjects. Because this is not mirrored by a decrease in NeuN+ neurons, it suggests a decrease in activity in some excitatory neurons in response to AD pathology or a compensatory mechanism. Taken together, our data point to a role of PNNs in providing cellular resilience and neuroprotection, allowing efficient neuronal activity and preventing ptau accumulation.
In the FFPE tissue of a small number of patients, we observed a lack of WFA in cortical sulci, which we attribute to fixation artifacts. Previous studies also reported difficulties in detecting WFA in FFPE tissue.
32
This suggests that the GalNAc residues of PNNs could be sensitive to PMD, tissue processing, and/or fixation procedures. Importantly, snap‐frozen tissues or tissues with a short fixation protocol do not show these artifacts. Such observations were not made using other PNN markers like aggrecan. However, in the current study, there was a mild negative correlation between PMD and the number of both aggrecan and WFA PNNs in cryosections. Furthermore, previous studies yielded contradictory results, with some reporting a decrease in aggrecan+ or WFA+ PNNs in AD patients
29
,
30
and others showing no change.
31
,
32
In this study, we have taken several steps to account for possible confounding effects. We have only included donors with a short PMD and used both snap‐frozen and FFPE tissues with sufficient WFA signal.
In conclusion, we provide the first evidence that alterations in PNNs occur in individuals resilient to AD. These alterations may be linked to plasticity and might contribute to maintaining cognitive function despite the presence of AD neuropathology.
