Introduction
Entry into mitosis and meiosis (M-phase) in the eukaryotic cell cycle is a sudden and all-or-nothing process that is controlled by mitotic Cdk, mainly the Cdk1-cyclin B (Cdk1/CycB) complex.
1
The current model on mitotic regulation has evolved around a positive feedback loop between mitotic Cdk and its activator, Cdc25 family phosphatases.
2
,
3
,
4
Mitotic Cdk is present in a latent form in interphase cells due to inhibitory phosphorylations on Cdk1, which are catalyzed by Wee1 family protein kinases. Thus, robust activation of Cdk1 at M-phase entry relies on removal of these inhibitory phosphorylations by Cdc25. However, Cdc25 is also present in a latent form in interphase cells; thus, its own activation is required for activating mitotic Cdk. According to the positive feedback loop model, Cdc25 activation consists of initiation and autocatalytic steps. In the initiation step, phosphorylation by upstream Cdc25 activating kinases generates a threshold level of active Cdc25, which generates a threshold level of active Cdk1. In the autocatalytic step, initially activated mitotic Cdk activates more Cdc25 and vice versa, amplifying each other’s activities. In addition to feedback activation of Cdc25, activated mitotic Cdk also inactivates Wee1 family protein kinases and activates the mitotic kinase Greatwall (Gwl), which activates the specific inhibitor of the Cdk1-opposing phosphatase PP2A-B55δ, Ensa/ARPP-19. These additional regulatory feedback loops act in concert with the Cdk1-Cdc25 positive feedback loop to produce switch-like kinetics in mitotic phosphorylation and M-phase induction.
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
Although the current model on mitotic regulation provides valuable insights on how graded upstream stimuli produce an all-or-nothing response in M-phase induction, it does not satisfactorily explain why the process of M-phase induction itself occurs in a sudden and complete manner. In particular, tightly associated with the M-phase induction are abrupt and substantial increases in the apparent molecular weight of numerous proteins of varied biological functions,
10
including multiple key mitotic regulators.
11
,
12
,
13
This hallmark phenomenon, collectively termed the M-phase supershift, is often attributed to multisite phosphorylations by the Cdk1/CycB/Cks complex.
14
,
15
,
16
However, there is no direct evidence that purified mitotic Cdk complex is capable of inducing sudden and quantitative supershifts of its substrate proteins. Instead, a nearly full-extent of the M-phase supershift of endogenous Cdc25C, Gwl, and APC3 and exogenous fission yeast Wee1 can be achieved by the addition of either a phosphatase inhibitor
17
,
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
or high concentrations of activated Gwl
22
to interphase-arrested Xenopus egg extracts in the absence of either mitotic cyclins or both mitotic cyclins and Cdk1/Cdk2 proteins. Further, such Cdk-independent induction of the M-phase supershift was also associated with induction of various cellular events that characterize mitotic entry.
20
These paradoxical observations suggest that the mechanism of protein phosphorylation that drives M-phase entry is distinct from the mechanism of protein phosphorylation that commits cells to undergoing M-phase entry.
We recently reconstituted the M-phase supershift of Xenopus Cdc25C, Myt1, Wee1A, APC3, and Gwl in
M
-phase stabilized Xenopus
e
gg
e
xtracts (MEE) and carefully analyzed the protein phosphorylation that produces their M-phase supershifts.
23
Our results showed that MEE-induced supershifts of the five mitotic regulators were each due to significant phosphorylation of most of the S/T/Y residues in a long intrinsically disordered regulatory region that is enriched in both S/T residues and S/TP motifs. Clearly, the site-specific phosphorylations of these supershift-producing domains by the major mitotic kinases in MEE, Cdk1, MAPK, Plx1 and/or RSK2, were neither sufficient to produce the supershifts by themselves nor required for MEE to produce the supershifts. Further, inhibition of the four major mitotic kinase activities in okadaic acid (OA) injected Xenopus oocytes inhibited neither the M-phase supershift nor M-phase entry. These findings indicate that the M-phase supershift is produced by a previously unrecognized category of mitotic phosphorylation, which we term the site-comprehensive phosphorylation.
Since the current understanding on mitotic regulation is based only on the knowledge of the site-specific phosphorylation by major mitotic kinases, our recent discovery of the site-comprehensive phosphorylation of five key mitotic regulators necessitates incorporation of the two distinct types of mitotic phosphorylation into our understanding of mitotic regulation. To address this new challenge, we designed this study to define their respective roles in the gel mobility shift and activity regulation of Xenopus Cdc25C both
in vitro
and in M-phase induction. Our results demonstrate that the two distinct types of mitotic phosphorylation of Cdc25C are mechanistically distinct, functionally different, and physiologically involved in different stages of Cdc25C activation during M-phase induction.

Phosphorylation of multiple conserved S/TP motifs in the regulatory domain of Cdc25C is a prerequisite for the supershift-producing phosphorylation

Abruptness of the M-phase supershift of Cdc25C stems from intrinsic properties of the site-comprehensive phosphorylation process
The M-phase supershift of Cdc25C is sudden and quantitative with a lag period of variable lengths regardless of physiological and experimental conditions. This abrupt kinetics of the M-phase supershift of Cdc25C was initially attributed to a positive feedback loop between Cdk1 and Cdc25C
29

38

6

Discussion
In this study, we determined respective roles of the conventional site-specific phosphorylation and recently discovered site-comprehensive phosphorylation in the gel mobility shift and phosphatase activation of Xenopus Cdc25C both
in vitro
and during M-phase induction. Our results demonstrate that the site-specific phosphorylation of the regulatory domain of Cdc25C by Cdk1 and other major mitotic kinases generates slight gel mobility shifts and modest activation of G2-state Cdc25C and occurs prior to M-phase onset. In contrast, the site-comprehensive phosphorylation of the regulatory domain of Cdc25C, which does not require Cdk1 or other major mitotic kinase activities, produces both the M-phase supershift and robust activation of G2-state Cdc25C and occurs at M-phase onset. These findings generate a two-stage framework for Cdc25C regulation during M-phase induction.
Refutation of the current positive feedback loop model between Cdk1 and Cdc25
The current positive feedback loop model between Cdk1 and Cdc25 evolved through four major conceptual steps. The first step derived from the observation that a threshold level of recombinant cyclin B induced a sudden and steep increase in Cdk1 activity in Xenopus egg extracts after a significant lag period.
53
This observation generated a framework in which a slow initial rise in Cdk1 activation produces further changes in Cdk1 modifying enzymes through feedback loop, leading to accelerated activation. The second step stemmed from the discovery that Xenopus Cdc25 underwent sudden and substantial gel mobility shifts at M-phase onset in both progesterone-stimulated Xenopus oocytes and cycling Xenopus egg extracts due to extensive phosphorylation of its N-terminal regulatory domain.
18
This discovery unraveled key roles of multisite phosphorylation in regulating a key Cdk1 modifying enzyme during M-phase induction. The third step originated from the results that both the M-phase supershift and phosphatase activation of Xenopus Cdc25 were largely due to phosphorylation of multiple conserved S/TP motifs by mitotic Cdk1.
29
These results led to the proposal that mitotic Cdk and Cdc25 form a positive feedback loop, accounting for abrupt kinetics in both the M-phase supershift of Cdc25 and activation of mitotic Cdk at M-phase onset. The fourth step resulted from the finding that the mitotic kinase Plk1 phosphorylated and activated Cdc25.
35
This discovery provided a plausible explanation as to how the positive feedback loop can be initiated. Note that Xenopus Cdc25 in these previous studies is referred to as Cdc25C in this study because it is much more homologous to human Cdc25C than to human Cdc25A and Cdc25B.
Although the current positive feedback loop model was formed logically, its foundation has three weak points that deserve further investigation. First, although phosphodefective mutations of multiple conserved S/TP motifs in Xenopus Cdc25 consistently inhibited its M-phase supershift and phosphatase activation in previous studies,
19
,
29
,
30
there has been no convincing evidence that phosphorylation of these motifs directly produces these outcomes. In this study, we showed that phosphorylation of multiple conserved S/TP motifs in Cdc25C is a prerequisite for, rather than a direct cause of, its M-phase supershift (
Figures 2
and
3
). Second, although purified mitotic Cdk and Plk1 induced supershifts and activation of Xenopus Cdc25 in previous studies under described experimental conditions,
29
,
35
these results fell short of demonstrating that physiologically relevant levels of these mitotic kinases were able to mimic crude M-phase lysates in phosphorylating and activating a physiologically relevant state of Xenopus Cdc25. In this study, we produced the major mitotic kinases by TNT, which avoided potential contamination of kinase samples by other factors from M-phase lysates. We matched the concentration or activity of each of TNT kinases to that of the corresponding kinase in MEE, which avoid kinase overkill in phosphorylation reactions. We also treated immunopurified Cdc25C with IOE to produce G2-state Cdc25C for activity assays. With these technical improvements, our results showed clearly that M-phase levels of active Cdk1 and/or Plx1 did not come close to MEE in phosphorylating and activating G2-state Cdc25C (
Figures 6
A–6D). Third, although a positive feedback loop between Cdk1 and Cdc25 has been assumed to account for the abrupt kinetics in the M-phase supershift of Xenopus Cdc25 and activation of mitotic Cdk at M-phase onset, there has been no direct evidence supporting this assumption. In this study, our results showed that inhibition/depletion of all four of the major mitotic kinases in MEE (
Figures 4
E and 4F) or inactivating mutation/deletion of the catalytic domain of Cdc25C (
Figures 5
C and 5D) did not abolish the abruptness in MEE-induced supershifts of Cdc25C. We also found that the abrupt kinetics in the M-phase supershift of the regulatory domain of Cdc25C stems from intrinsic properties of the site-comprehensive phosphorylation process (
Figure 5
). Together, these new results refute the current positive feedback loop model.
Regulatory roles of the site-specific phosphorylation of the regulatory domain of Cdc25C
Although none of the four major mitotic kinases in Xenopus oocytes was able to induce the supershift and robust activation of G2-state Cdc25C
in vitro
(

), ectopic expression of a threshold level of a constitutively active form of Cdk1, Plx1, MEK, MAPK or RSK1 in Xenopus oocytes was each able to induce the M-phase supershift of Xenopus Cdc25 and M-phase entry after a significant lag period of several hours in previous studies.

,

,

,

,

,

Conversely, inhibition of the activity of MEK, MAPK, - Cdk1 or Plx1 each delayed or inhibited the M-phase supershift of Xenopus Cdc25 and M-phase entry in progesterone-stimulated Xenopus oocytes.
45
,

,

,

,

Moreover, low levels of MAPK, RSK2, Cdk1 and Plx1 activations were early responses of Xenopus oocytes to progesterone stimulation,
33
,

,

,

which positively regulated the Cdc25 supershift and Cdk1 activation at M-phase onset.
60
,
61
In the context of the new results obtained in this study, these previous findings indicate that the site-specific phosphorylation catalyzed by diverse mitotic kinases plays functionally redundant and quantitatively collective roles in activating the site-comprehensive phosphorylation mechanism for Cdc25C in Xenopus oocytes.
Then what are the critical targets of the site-specific phosphorylation catalyzed by these diverse mitotic kinases? Although previous studies implicated Cdk1, RSK2 and Plx1 in inactivating Myt1 in Xenopus oocytes or egg extracts,
62
,
63
,
64
M-phase levels of TNT-produced Cdk1, Plx1 and MAPK/RSK2 did not inactivate G2-state Myt1 in our Myt1 activity assays (T.T. et al., unpublished data). On the contrary, our Cdc25 activity assays showed that sub-M-phase levels of TNT-produced Cdk1, Plx1 and MAPK/RSK2 were each able to modestly activate G2-state Cdc25C (
Figure 6
B). Further, early activation of MAPK correlated with both the slight shift of Cdc25C and modest activation of its phosphatase activity in progesterone-induced oocyte maturation (
Figures 7
A and 7B). Complete inhibition of the activation of the MAPK pathway inhibited not only the slight shift of Cdc25C prior to M-phase onset but also the M-phase supershift of Cdc25 C at M-phase onset (
Figure 7
D). In both Cdc2-AF induced oocyte maturation, which did not require MAPK activation (
Figure 7
E), and cyclin-induced M-phase entry in interphase-arrested Xenopus egg extracts (
Figure 7
F), which also did not require MAPK activation,
22
the slight gel mobility shift of Cdc25C and modest activation of its phosphatase activity still occurred prior to the supershift and robust activation of Cdc25 C at M-phase onset. Together, these findings indicate that Cdc25C is a critical target through which the site-specific phosphorylation by diverse mitotic kinases promotes the activation of the site-comprehensive phosphorylation mechanism for Cdc25C during M-phase induction.
Transition from the site-specific to site-comprehensive phosphorylation of the regulatory domain of Cdc25C
With the biphasic regulation of Cdc25C during Xenopus oocyte maturation, the next key question would be how the site-specific phosphorylation of Cdc25C positively regulates the activation of the site-comprehensive phosphorylation mechanism for Cdc25C. In dealing with this issue, several rules can be deduced from results in previous studies. First, although Cdk1 is not more active than other mitotic kinases in activating G2-state Cdc25C (
Figures 6
A and 6B), Cdk1 is the only mitotic kinase that is invariantly required for the M-phase supershift of Cdc25C and M-phase entry in diverse experimental systems.
22
,
30
,
45
,
46
,
65
,
66
Thus, activation of a threshold level of Cdk1 by modestly activated Cdc25C must be a key regulatory event in the activation of the site-comprehensive phosphorylation mechanism for Cdc25C. Second, addition of a high level of active Cdk1/CycB complex to interphase-arrested Xenopus egg extracts did not activate endogenous Cdc25C to prevent its own inactivation
67
or induce M-phase entry.
19
Activation of endogenous Cdk1 in Xenopus oocyte extracts by addition of recombinant Cdc25A
68
or MEE-phosphorylated myc-Cdc25C
23
did not induce the M-phase supershift of endogenous Cdc25C. Thus, activation of a threshold level of Cdk1 cannot be the only regulatory event in the activation of the site-comprehensive phosphorylation mechanism for Cdc25C. Third, addition of 1 μM OA to interphase-arrested Xenopus egg extracts protected exogenously added active Cdk1/CycB complex from inactivation
67
and allowed it to induce M-phase entry.
19
Addition of 0.5 μM OA to Xenopus oocyte extracts cooperated with recombinant Cdc25A to induce the M-phase supershift of Cdc25C.
68
In addition to these Cdk1-dependent effects of OA, addition of 3 μM OA or microcystin (MC) to interphase-arrested Xenopus egg extracts induced the M-phase supershift of Cdc25C in the absence of mitotic cyclins
18
or both mitotic cyclins and Cdk1/Cdk2 proteins.
20
Microinjection of 5 nL of 100 μM OA to Xenopus oocytes induced the M-phase supershift of Cdc25C and M-phase entry in the presence of both Cdk1 and MEK1 inhibitors.
23
Thus, negative regulation of multiple M-phase inhibitory phosphatase activities must be also involved in the activation of the site-comprehensive phosphorylation mechanism for Cdc25C during M-phase induction.
Key questions on the site-comprehensive phosphorylation mechanism for Cdc25C
Findings in this study raised three key questions on the site-comprehensive phosphorylation mechanism for Cdc25C. The first question is which kinases among numerous M-phase specific and constitutively active protein kinases are responsible for the phosphorylation. Thus far, our efforts have been focused on the major M-phase specific kinases in Xenopus eggs for Cdc25C, i.e., Cdk1, Plx1, MAPK and RSK2,
30
,
33
because the site-comprehensive phosphorylation of the regulatory domain of Cdc25C is an M-phase specific phenomenon. In addressing this issue, our recent studies showed that combined but not individual depletion of Cdk1, Plx1, MAPK and RSK2 in MEE moderately retarded the MEE-induced supershift of Cdc25C
9-375
.
23
Results in this study further showed that combined depletion or inhibition of Cdk1, Plx1, MAPK and RSK2 in MEE moderately delayed the MEE-induced supershift of a non-divided Cdc25C (
Figures 4
E and 4F). Logically, the delaying effects pointed to involvement of the four major mitotic kinases in the site-comprehensive phosphorylation of the regulatory domain of Cdc25C, whereas the still-happening outcomes indicated involvement of other protein kinases in the phosphorylation process as well. Thus, our current hypothesis is that Cdk1, Plx1, MAPK and RSK2 are part of a large pool of diverse protein kinases in MEE that redundantly and collectively catalyze the site-comprehensive phosphorylation of the regulatory domain of Cdc25C. The second key question is what factors are recruited to the regulatory domain of Cdc25C that execute the site-comprehensive phosphorylation without substrate dissociation. Thus far, none of the characterized protein kinases is capable of the site-comprehensive phosphorylation of a long protein region even if it is capable of substrate binding and processive phosphorylation.
40
Therefore, the unusual ability of a large pool of diverse protein kinases in M-phase to perform the site-comprehensive phosphorylation in Cdc25C must rely on participation of certain M-phase specific factors that enable those otherwise site-specific protein kinases to perform the site-comprehensive phosphorylation without substrate dissociation. Following this reasoning, factors that are recruited to the regulatory domain of Cdc25C must include both participating kinases and assisting factors, which we collectively term phosphorylation machinery. The third key question is why occurrence of the site-comprehensive phosphorylation of the regulatory domain of Cdc25C is contingent on the phosphorylation of multiple conserved S/TP motifs. Since our results showed that the rate-limiting step in the site-comprehensive phosphorylation of the regulatory domain of Cdc25C is to recruit all participating factors to the substrate, a reasonable explanation would be that the phosphorylation of multiple conserved S/TP motifs is a prerequisite for recruiting participating kinases or/and assisting factors to the regulatory domain of Cdc25C. Apparently, the site-comprehensive phosphorylation of the regulatory domain of Cdc25C is mechanistically distinct and much more complex than the currently understood site-specific protein phosphorylation, and thinking outside the box is required for final elucidation of its exact mechanism.
Limitations of the study
Due to various technical limitations, the mass spectrometry performed in this study fell short of precisely defining the phosphorylation stoichiometry for individual and total S/T residues in the long intrinsically disordered regulatory domain of Cdc25C. Since not all phosphorylations in the long intrinsically disordered regulatory domain of Cdc25C generate small gel mobility shifts, the magnitude of the gel mobility shift of Cdc25C may not reflect the exact extent of its phosphorylation. Since Cdc25 activity assay performed in this study measures a decrease in the inhibitory phosphorylation of Cdk1 by immunoblotting with phosphospecific antibodies, the substrate cannot be used in great excess as done in a standard enzymatic activity assay. Due to this limitation, results from the Cdc25C activity assay only provide qualitative information on respective roles of the two distinct types of mitotic phosphorylations in Cdc25C activation.

Experimental model and study paricipant details
Xenopus laevis
Female adult Xenopus leavis were used in this study to obtain unfertilized eggs and immature oocytes. To obtain unfertilized eggs, adult female frogs were injected with 800 IU of human chorionic gonadotropin to induce ovulation. The ovulated unfertilized eggs were first dejelled with 2% cysteine and then homogenized in an EB-based extraction buffer to prepare MEE. To obtain immature oocytes, ovarian tissues were surgically removed from adult female frogs under anesthesia. From acquired ovarian tissues, fully grown oocytes were obtained either through manual defolliculation under a dissecting microscope or through treatment with 0.2% collagenase at 22°C for 1–3 h. Oocytes acquired from manual defolliculation were used in microinjection of
in vitro
transcribed mRNA and time course examination of protein phosphorylation in progesterone-induced oocyte maturation. Oocytes acquired from collagenase treatment were homogenized in an EB-based extraction buffer to prepare IOE.
Cell culture
HEK293T cells were obtained from Procell (CL-0005), and its original source was ATCC. HEK293T cells were cultured in 60-mm cell culture dishes in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 2 mM L-glutamine and 1% penicillin/streptomycin in a 37°C incubator with 5% CO
2
. Cell growth was observed under an optical microscope, and confluent cultures were 1:3 subcultured by trypsinization per standard protocols.
