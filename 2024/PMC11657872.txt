Background
Cancer immunosurveillance is dependent on tumor-specific T cells that actively recognize and eliminate emerging tumors despite miniscule quantities of immunogenic antigens [
1
]. At this early stage, there is still a lack of well-defined mechanisms that generate co-stimulation necessary for priming T cell responses [
2
]. We previously showed that CD91 expression on dendritic cells (DCs) is essential for immunosurveillance of nascent tumors [
3
]. This has been shown in mouse models of cancer and in cancer patients [
3
,
4
]. Extracellular heat shock proteins (HSPs) released by tumors include the antigen chaperones, gp96, hsp70, and calreticulin, which serve as ligands for CD91 [
5
–
8
]. Following CD91-dependent endocytosis, tumor antigens chaperoned by these extracellular HSPs are efficiently cross-presented by DCs and are recognized by antigen-specific T cells [
9
–
11
]. In addition to its role in antigen cross-presentation, binding of HSPs to CD91 initiates intracellular signaling which results in release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and upregulation of co-stimulatory molecule expression [
12
,
13
]. When accessible, other ligands can engage CD91 to initiate similar overlapping signaling cascades [
14
]. These signaling events involve the phosphorylation of one or two tyrosine residues (Y4474 and Y4508) on the intracellular β chain of CD91 [
13
,
15
,
16
]. While the three HSPs perform some redundant functions in activating DCs, there are both overlapping and distinct mechanisms for how this is achieved. For example, gp96 and calreticulin (CRT) require only CD91 Y4508 to elicit NF-κB activation, while hsp70 requires phosphorylation of both tyrosines for this effect [
13
]. Distinct inflammatory cytokine profiles are associated with CD91
+
macrophages and DCs upon stimulation with different HSPs [
12
,
13
]. The differences in cytokines were shown to impact NK cell activation and the type of T helper (Th) cell priming [
13
,
17
,
18
].
The role of immune modulators like cytokines, including their effects on DC maturation and T cell co-stimulation capacity has mostly been examined for large and established tumors [
19
]. In such tumors, the microenvironment is dysregulated to varying degrees with respect to metabolism, pH, oxygen saturation, vascularization, stromal composition and immune infiltrates, compared to homeostatic tissues [
19
,
20
]. In this regard, double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) and ATP located in the tumor microenvironment can induce DC maturation through the receptors cGAS-STING and P2X7, respectively [
21
,
22
]. Tumor microenvironment-associated modulators have also been examined for their effects on the activation, proliferation, migration, and survival of other immune cells [
20
]. However, these pathways are largely a result of long-term evolution of tumors, which develop mechanisms to resist immunological pressure. Such effects do not apply to nascent tumors, i.e. prior to establishing immune responses and tumor microenvironment-associated adverse events. We have thus proposed that tumor-derived HSPs acting through CD91 is the tumor-recognition and immune-initiating event that directly leads to DC maturation, cytokine release and subsequent T cell priming and NK cell activation [
23
].
In this study we investigate the CD91 signaling pathways that culminate with reprogramming of DCs, leading to the release of cytokines and acute responsiveness to further immune modulators. While activation of downstream transcription factors and cytokine production has been observed upon CD91 stimulation, the proximal and distal signaling has not been elucidated. As no intrinsic kinase activity is possessed by CD91, we first identified and examined the kinases AXL and Fgr that associate with, and phosphorylate CD91, in response to HSP stimulation. Pharmacological inhibition of these protein kinases in DCs abolishes the HSP-mediated signaling pathway. We show selective usage of these kinases by different HSPs and therefore establish a mechanism of increasing the breadth of immunological output triggered by these HSPs. Using bioinformatic approaches, we show that CD91-directed signaling reprograms DCs to be responsive to other secondary immune modulators.
These studies offer a comprehensive examination of the initial immune events triggered by HSPs binding to CD91 on DCs. These steps explain how nascent tumors are detected by tissue resident APCs and how immune responses against the tumors develop as part of the cancer immunosurveillance mechanisms.
Methods
All methods below were carried out in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations as outlined by the University of Pittsburgh.
Mice
Female C57BL/6 mice, aged 6 weeks, were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME). The mice were housed in our DLAR facilities and used under experimental protocols approved by the University of Pittsburgh Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). Fgr KO mice were a kind gift from Drs. Joel Greenberger and Amitava Mukherjee. BMDCs were derived from these mice.
Isolation and culture of BMDCs
All cells were cultured in complete RPMI containing 1% pen-strep, 1% sodium pyruvate, 1% L-glutamine, 1% nonessential amino acids, 0.1% 2-mercaptoethanol, and 10% heat inactivated FBS (GIBCO). Bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) were isolated from 6-week-old C57BL/6 mice and cultured as described previously [
24
]. Bone marrow was flushed from the femur and red blood cells were lysed. Cells were then plated at a density of 5 × 10
5
/mL in 10 cm non-TC treated plates along with 20 ng/mL recombinant GM-CSF (R&D). RPMI and GM-CSF were supplemented on day 3 of culture. On day 6, non-adherent cells (BMDCs) were harvested in AIM V media (GIBCO) and plated at indicated densities for each assay. BMDCs recapitulate primary DCs as previously shown [
18
]. Splenic DCs were isolated from the spleens of naïve mice using the MACS pan DC isolation kit (Miltenyi Biotech).
Purification of HSPs
Calreticulin (CRT), gp96, and hsp70 were isolated from livers of C57BL/6 mice as previously described [
7
,
25
]. The purity and size of HSP preparations was verified via SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting. For all in vitro experiments, a final concentration of 100 µg/mL of gp96 and equimolar amounts of CRT or hsp70 were used.
Inhibitor treatment
Two molecules were used to specifically inhibit Fgr and AXL. TL02-59 (Fgr inhibitor, MedChemExpress) was used at a final concentration of 75 nM. This concentration has been determined to have major effects on Fgr and negligible effects on related family members [
26
]. This was added 16 h prior to HSP stimulation. Bemcentinib (AXL inhibitor, MedChemExpress) was used at a final concentration of 1 µM and was added 2 h prior to HSP stimulation [
27
].
Immunoprecipitation
BMDCs (4 × 10
6
) were plated overnight in 6-well plates. Cells were pulsed with gp96 for 15 min. Cells were washed with PBS and then incubated with the reversible crosslinker Di-tert-butyl peroxide (DTBP, ThermoFisher) at a concentration of 5 mM for 30 min at room temperature. The crosslinking reaction was quenched by addition of glycine to a 125 mM final concentration and incubated for 5 min. Cells were washed with cold PBS and then lysed with NP40 lysis buffer (ThermoFisher) with Halt protease and phosphatase inhibitor (ThermoFisher). Lysates were precleared with 1 µL of Dynabeads™ Protein A for one hour at 4⁰C. Dynabeads™ (ThermoFisher) were conjugated to EPR3724 (CD91 β-chain antibody, Abcam) according to manufacturer instructions. Ab-conjugated Dynabeads™ were added to BMDC lysates and incubated with rotation overnight at 4⁰C. Beads were then washed three times with PBS and transferred to fresh Eppendorf tubes. Beads were resuspended in 100 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) in HEPES buffer for 30 min at 37⁰C to reverse DTBP crosslinking. Following reversal of crosslinking, immunoprecipitates were sent off for mass spectrometry or resuspended in Laemmli buffer for subsequent detection via Western blotting.
Immunoblotting
To detect the phosphorylation status of signaling proteins, BMDCs (2 × 10
6
) cells were seeded in 6-well plates overnight. Wells were then pulsed with HSP for appropriate time courses and lysed with cold NP40 lysis buffer (Halt protease and phosphatase inhibitors added). Laemmli buffer was added to equivalent amounts of cell lysate (30 µg) and samples were boiled for 5 min prior to separation on SDS-PAGE gels. Gels were then transferred to PVDF membranes and probed with the following antibodies: rabbit α-CD91 (EPR3724, abcam), mouse α-Fgr (M03, Abnova), mouse α-SRC pY416 (2101), goat α-AXL (AF854, R&D), rabbit α-AXL pY779 (AF2228, R&D), rabbit α-SHC pY239/240 (2434), rabbit α-PI3K p85 pY458 (4228), rabbit α-AKT pS473 (9271), rabbit α-ERK1/2 pT202/pY204 (4376), rabbit α-NF-κB p65 pS536 (3033), rabbit α-STAT1 pS727 (9177), rabbit α-β-ACTIN (4970). EPR3724 has been shown to be specific to CD91 in CD91 KO BMDCs [
28
]. Membranes were then incubated with HRP-linked secondary antibodies. Antibodies were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology unless otherwise stated. Immunoblots of each timepoint and condition were run all on a singular membrane when possible. Membranes were developed using a Proteinsimple™ FlourChem E imager and quantified using ImageJ.
Confocal microscopy
BMDCs (1 × 10
6
) were plated on 35 mm glass bottom microwell dishes (MatTek) overnight. Cells were pulsed with gp96 for 15 min. After stimulation, culture media was aspirated and cells were fixed in 2% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton-X, and blocked with 2% BSA for 45 min. After blocking, samples were then incubated overnight with primary antibodies for CD91, pAXL, and pFgr (same as in Immunoblotting section). Cells were washed and incubated with secondary antibody and Alexa Fluor 488 Phalloidin for 1 h. The secondary antibodies used were donkey anti-rabbit Cy5, donkey anti-mouse Cy3, and donkey anti-goat Cy3. Another wash step was performed prior to staining with DAPI. Slides were viewed using a Nikon A1 Confocal microscope and analyzed using NIS-Elements. Colocalization was quantified as proportion of foci that are overlapping. Secondary antibodies and DAPI were provided by the Center for Biological Imaging at the University of Pittsburgh.
Sample preparation for LC–MS/MS
Captured proteins were eluted at 95° C from beads in a buffer containing 100 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.6, 100 mM DTT, 1% SDS, Complete C protease (Roche), and PhosSTOP phosphatase inhibitors (Roche). The resulting lysates were sonicated and centrifuged to clear lysate from any insoluble debris. In total, 300 μg of protein from the above lysate was reduced using 20 mM DTT (Sigma Aldrich) for 10 min at 95 °C. Protein alkylation was performed with 50 mM iodoacetamide (Sigma Aldrich) for 30 min. Samples were trypsin digested using the filter-aided sample preparation (FASP) to generate peptide fragments [
29
]. Tryptic peptides were eluted from the filter using 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate. A desalting step was performed using C18 columns (Supelco). Peptides were eluted in 90% acetonitrile and dried using a vacuum centrifuge.
Label free LC–MS/MS analysis
Peptides were resolved with liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry using a system composed of a Waters nanoACQUITY UPLC in-line with a Q-Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher). Samples were run in a randomized order. Solvent A (0.1% formic acid in water, Burdick & Jackson) and solvent B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile, Burdick & Jackson) were used as the mobile phase. Peptides were then eluted from a capillary column (100 μm inner diameter × 100 mm long; ACQUITY UPLC M-Class Peptide BEH C18 Column, 1.7-μm particle size, 300 Å (Waters) and resolved using a 48-min method at a flow rate of 0.9 μl/min (4–33% B for 38 min, 33–80% B for 2 min, constant at 80% B for 6 min, and then 80–0% B for 2 min to equilibrate the column). Data were collected in positive ionization mode.
PEAKS Studio 10.0 build 20,190,129 was used to sequence and identify peptides. The UNIProt_SwissProt: Mus musculus (house mouse) sequence database was used, which contained 17,013 entries. One missed cleavage by trypsin was permitted in the database search. Carbamidomethylation was treated as a fixed modification. Acetylation (Protein N-term) and oxidation (M) were set as variable modifications. The mass tolerance for precursor ions was set to 20 ppm, and mass tolerance for fragment ions set to 0.8 Da. Label-free quantitation was performed using the quantitative module in the PEAKSX software.
Reverse phase protein array
BMDCs (2 × 10
6
) were plated in 6-well plates overnight. Pervanadate was added at a 1:1 volume for 30 min at room temperature. BMDCs were washed with PBS and then stimulated with gp96 for 15 min. Cells were then washed with PBS, pelleted, and immediately placed on dry ice for shipment to MD Anderson Functional Proteomics Reverse Phase Protein Array (RPPA) Core for processes. All heatmaps and quantification were provided by MD Anderson RPPA core.
Measurement of cytokine production
BMDCs (2 × 10
6

After stimulation, supernatant was harvested and spun down to remove particulates. Cytokine production was measured in the supernatant using Proteome Profiler Mouse Cytokine Array Kit, Panel A (Biotechne). Cytokine panels were imaged using a Proteinsimple™ FlourChem E imager and quantified using ImageJ.

Generation of the aggregated gene-barcode matrix
The CellRanger (v7.2.0, 10X Genomics) pipelines were used to process the samples. FASTQ files were created for each sample and these files were used to generate a counts matrix by mapping FASTQ files against mm10 mouse reference genome. The output of this pipeline is a counts matrix comprised of gene expression by barcode of each cell in the samples. SoupX was used on R to remove ambient RNA contamination across each sample. All count matrices were aggregated into a single gene-barcode matrix.
Quality control (QC) and filtering the data set
QC, dimension-reduction, and clustering were performed using Scanpy package (version 1.9.3). Samples were concatenated and the expression matrix was filtered through the Scanpy package based on the following exclusion criteria: 1) cells with fewer than 200 genes; 2) genes expressed in less than three cells; 3) cells with less than 20% mitochondrial gene content; and 4) cells with more than 5% ribosomal gene content. For the screened matrix, normalization was performed, natural logarithm was calculated, and doublets were identified and removed using Scrublet.
Calculating differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and gene set enrichment analysis
Highly-variable genes were selected for downstream analysis using

scanpy.tl.pca

We found and excluded small numbers of undefined or contaminant subsets of T cells, mast cells, fibroblastic reticular cells (FRCs), B cells and NK cells from downstream analysis. A second round of dimension reduction and unsupervised clustering were performed to characterize and compare APCs. In order to classify cell types and cell states, we applied differential gene analysis by Wilcoxon rank-sum using threshold of 0.58 and
p

Immune response enrichment analysis
Immune Response Enrichment analysis on the Immune dictionary app (

The platform assesses statistical significance using a two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test between gene set scares on WT cells and gene set scores on CD91-deficient cells, and an FDR correction is applied to the cytokine calculations. The enrichment scores are calculated by the hypergeometric test on significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) as described [

Results
AXL and Fgr directly associate with, and phosphorylate, CD91 in response to gp96 stimulation
We first performed a time course study to establish the kinetics of CD91 phosphorylation. BMDCs pulsed with gp96, the prototypical immunogenic HSP, were harvested at various time points. Immunoblots for phosphorylation of CD91 showed a peak signal at 15 min post-stimulation over non-stimulated cells (Supplementary Fig. 1A). We used this time point to investigate the proximal signaling pathway that is activated upon gp96 binding to CD91. We used three approaches to identify adaptor proteins that are associated with phosphorylated CD91. First, BMDCs were stimulated with gp96 for fifteen minutes, proteins were crosslinked and CD91 was immunoprecipitated. Crosslinkers in CD91 immunoprecipitates were reversed by reduction and CD91-associated adaptor proteins were analyzed by quantitative mass spectrometry. Proteins enriched in CD91 immunoprecipitates from gp96 stimulated cells over unstimulated cells and identified by mass spectrometry are shown in Fig.
1
A. Only two kinases, AXL and Fgr, were identified that increase in association with CD91 after gp96 treatment. Gp96 was also found to be enriched in stimulated samples, serving as an internal control for successful immunoprecipitation. Second, we confirmed the association of AXL and Fgr with CD91 by immunoblotting of CD91 immunoprecipitates. We observed an increased immunoprecipitation of AXL and Fgr with CD91 in gp96 stimulated BMDCs over unstimulated BMDCs (Fig.
1
B, C). We noted that there was baseline association of CD91 with these kinases in unstimulated BMDCs. In complementary studies, immunoprecipitation of AXL was shown to contain CD91 (Supplemental Fig. 1B). Second, both AXL and Fgr themselves were increasingly phosphorylated in response to gp96 stimulation of cells relative to both unstimulated cells and corresponding total protein (Fig.
1
D-G). Third, we further confirmed the association of AXL and Fgr with CD91 by confocal microscopy, using antibodies specific to CD91, and to phosphorylated Fgr and AXL (Fig.
1
H, K). An increase in phosphorylation of both kinases was observed (Fig.
1
I, L). We quantified confocal images for colocalization of each phosphorylated kinase with CD91. Following gp96 treatment, we observed an increased proportion of both pAXL and pFgr staining that colocalized with CD91. Interestingly, these increased proportions were comparable to the overall increase in total pAXL and pFgr staining. Additionally, there was no difference in the reverse measurement, i.e. CD91 colocalization with pAXL and pFgr was the same regardless of treatment with gp96. These data suggest that while gp96 does induce phosphorylation of both AXL and Fgr, this phosphorylation is not exclusive or preferential to kinases in contact with CD91. Importantly, we show that the association signal overall of AXL or Fgr with CD91, is specific to CD91, as there is no observable colocalization of pAXL or pFgr with a random surface protein such as MHC (Supplemental Fig. 2). Taken together, these data show that AXL and Fgr are phosphorylated and physically associated with CD91 upon gp96 stimulation BMDCs.

Gp96-mediated cellular alterations via CD91 are controlled by AXL and Fgr
To determine the importance of AXL and Fgr, and their phosphorylation, in signaling downstream of CD91, we used small molecule inhibitors for AXL or Fgr. The inhibitors for each kinase, bemcentinib and TL-0259 respectively, have been shown to prevent AXL and Fgr phosphorylation [
26
,
27
]. Samples were obtained from BMDCs stimulated with gp96 in the presence or absence of inhibitors or left unstimulated and analyzed by Reverse Phase Protein Array (RPPA) to find cell-wide changes in protein phosphorylation (Fig.
2
A). The data distribution of these proteins for two sample sets is shown for AXL inhibition and Fgr inhibition, indicating the total number of phosphorylation changes between each sample (Fig.
2
B). Of the 81 phosphorylation sites analyzed via RPPA (498 antibodies total), 32 were altered by gp96 treatment within both replicates (cutoff of ± 0.25 log2 fold change) (Fig.
2
C). In comparison to gp96, addition of each inhibitor led to changes in detection of several phosphorylated proteins (Fig.
2
D, E). There were 29 differences between gp96 and gp96 + AXL inhibitor (Fig.
2
D), and there were 28 differences between gp96 and gp96 + Fgr inhibitor (Fig.
2
E). The Src family kinase pY416 signal is decreased upon Fgr inhibition, indicating Fgr inhibition and serving as an internal control for the RPPA. The effects of gp96 treatment were reversed in 15 and 18 phosphoproteins by AXL or Fgr inhibition, respectively (Fig.
2
B). Several proteins previously shown to be associated with AXL or Fgr phosphorylation, including AKT, SHC, and SHP2, were phosphorylated upon gp96 stimulation (Fig.
2
C) [
31
,
32

2

There were ten proteins that were inhibited by both inhibitors suggesting that Fgr and Axl were acting in tandem to control phosphorylation of these specific proteins, including SHP2 pY542, FRS2α pY196, c-Abl pY412, EphA2 (pY588), p90 RSK (pT573), AMPKa (pT172), DDR1 (pY513), LRP6 (pS1490), VEGFR2 (p1175), and SHC (pY317). Not all gp96-mediated protein changes were controlled by AXL and Fgr, because twelve phosphoprotein changes were not affected by either inhibitor (Fig.
2
B). We prioritized specific molecules to probe further.

AXL- and Fgr-dependent phosphorylation events
SHC, PI3K, AKT, and ERK1/2 have previously been shown to be important for signaling via AXL and Fgr and/or were also identified in our RPPA dataset [
31
]. We assessed their phosphorylation in BMDCs in response to gp96 and in the presence of AXL or Fgr inhibitors (Fig.
3
A). We observed gp96-dependent phosphorylation of the signaling transducers SHC, PI3K, AKT, and ERK1/2, as well as the transcription factors NF-κB and signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1) (Fig.
3
B). There was a peak in phosphorylation at ~ 15 min after gp96 treatment, consistent with the time observed for CD91 phosphorylation (Supplemental Fig. 1A). The phosphorylation was transient for a majority of these signaling molecules. However, pSHC returned to baseline closer to two hours, compared with more rapid reversal for the others. Transcription factors also had distinct phosphorylation time courses, with NF-κB peaking at 60 min and STAT1 peaking at 120 min. Pretreatment of cells with either Fgr or AXL inhibitors prior to gp96 stimulation abolished the phosphorylation of all of these signaling proteins, compared to the vehicle-treated cells (Fig.
3
C, D). The phosphorylation in gp96-treated cells was also significantly higher than when compared to inhibited samples at the same time point. This decrease in phosphorylation of NF-kB and STAT1 was also observed in splenic DCs (Supplemental Fig. 3A). These inhibitors appeared to have no significant impact on BMDC viability, since LPS-induced phosphorylation of ERK1/2, which occurs via TLR4 [
33
], was unaffected by the inhibitors (Supplemental Fig. 3A, B). Quantification of phosphorylation of each protein induced by gp96, in the presence or absence of each inhibitor, is shown in Fig.
3
E-J. These data reveal signaling molecules associated with CD91, downstream of AXL and Fgr, and suggest that AXL and Fgr activities occur consecutively.

CD91-signaling axis controls cytokine release from dendritic cells
Gp96 has been shown to induce cytokines and chemokines from DCs and macrophages through the CD91-signaling axis [
12
,
13
]. We used a similar approach to understand the importance of AXL and Fgr in this process. Supernatants from BMDCs untreated or treated with gp96 in the presence or absence of AXL and Fgr inhibitors were probed for an array of cytokines (Supplemental Figs. 3C and 4A). Stimulation with gp96 led to production of twelve cytokines and chemokines including ICAM-1, CCL5, TIMP-1, TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1ra, CCL17, CCL2, IL-16, CXCL1, CXCL2, CXCL9, and CXCL10 (Fig.
4
B). All cytokines produced, except IL-1ra, CXCL2, and CXCL9, were significantly inhibited to some degree either inhibitor (Fig.
4
B, Supplementary Fig. 3C). TNF-a secretion was significantly reduced by inhibition of either AXL or Fgr. Production of CCL17, ICAM-1, and CXCL1 was most dramatically reduced when cells were treated with both AXL and Fgr inhibitors, but not with either alone, indicating there may also be parallel signaling pathways used by AXL and Fgr (Fig.
4
B, C). The CD91-dependent nature of cytokine release by DCs in response to gp96 has previously been shown using this assay in vitro [
12
,
13
]. To evaluate the role of CD91 in vivo, we introduced tumor cells into the dermis of CD91
fl/fl
CD11c
Cre
or WT littermate CD91
fl/fl
mice. We harvested tumor draining lymph nodes and performed single-cell RNA sequencing on DCs (CD11c
+
) (Fig.
4
D). CD91-dependent TNF-α and NF-κB and proinflammatory pathways were observed in DCs (Fig.
4
E). This supports the in vitro data and is consistent with an anti-tumor immune phenotype. We conclude that the CD91 signaling axis is required for cytokine and chemokine release from dendritic cells, both in vitro and in vivo and show the requirement for functional kinase activity of AXL and Fgr in this process.

Differential activation of CD91 signaling pathways by immunogenic HSPs
In addition to gp96, CD91 engages hsp70 and calreticulin [
9
,
10
]. Previously we showed differences in usage of CD91 β-chain tyrosine residues for signal transduction and cytokine patterns between gp96/calreticulin and hsp70 [
13
]. We examined the phosphorylation profile of BMDCs stimulated with hsp70 and calreticulin via immunoblotting (Fig.
5
A). Calreticulin elicited a similar pattern and kinetics of phosphorylation of AXL, Fgr, PI3K, AKT, and ERK1/2 (Supplemental Fig. 4). Hsp70 had a distinct profile, inducing phosphorylation of Fgr, PI3K, and NF-κB with a peak at 15 min (Fig.
5
B-F, and no detectable phosphorylation of AXL, AKT, SHC, ERK1/2, or STAT1 (Supplemental Fig. 5A). These results suggested that hsp70 engagement of CD91 resulted in the exclusive use of Fgr for signal transduction, so we treated BMDCs with Fgr inhibitor to assess the impact on signaling. Inhibition of Fgr prevented hsp70-induced signaling events (Fig.
5
C-F). Effects of the Fgr inhibitor on production of cytokines in response to hsp70 were also assessed (Fig.
5
A, Supplemental Fig. 5B). In response to hsp70, BMDCs produced IL-16, CCL2, TNF-α, CCL5, CXCL2, TIMP-1, ICAM-1, and IL-1ra (Fig.
5
G). Upon Fgr inhibition, the secretion of most of these factors was significantly inhibited, with the exceptions of TIMP-1 and ICAM-1 (Fig.
5
G,H). BMDCs from Fgr KO mice and stimulated with hsp70 phenocopied the effects of Fgr inhibition on WT BMDCs (Supplemental Fig. 5C, D). Our results show that hsp70 induces distinct activation of the CD91 signaling axis in comparison to gp96 and CRT, one that is dependent on Fgr but not AXL.

CD91 signaling axis primes APCs for responsiveness to immune mediators
Dendritic cell maturation by tumor-derived molecules such as HSPs induces phenotypic changes in the DC. These changes remodel cell surface expression of receptors making them (i) holistically responsive to immune mediators from other sources and/or (ii) capable of providing signals to other cells. We tested the first point in vivo by introducing tumor cells into WT or CD91
fl/fl
CD11c
Cre
mice, isolating draining lymph node DCs two days later and performing scRNAseq on them. Using previously described bioinformatic algorithms [
30
], we examined the CD91-dependent reprogramming of two DC subsets by way of their potential responsiveness to cytokines, as key immune modulators from other sources, as described in methods (Fig.
6
A). The presence of tumors in tissues (skin) rapidly induced CD91-dependent changes in cDC1 and cDC2 in draining lymph nodes, making them more or less receptive to various cytokines (Fig.
6
B, C). cDC2 produced a tumor-associated phenotype suggesting they were unresponsive to almost all cytokines when CD91 was absent, while cDC1 appeared phenotypically less responsive to pro-inflammatory and anti-tumor cytokines, such as TNF-a, IL-1b, IL-12, IFN among others, in the absence of CD91.

Discussion
Cancer immunosurveillance requires expression of CD91 on dendritic cells [
3
]. Here we examine one of two known functions of CD91-signaling and reprograming of DC, that allows the DCs to prime T cells and activate NK cells [
13
,
17
,
18
]. We show the involvement of AXL and Fgr as adaptor kinase proteins necessary for CD91-mediated signal transmission, downstream cytokine release, and reprogramming of CD91
+
DCs.
Our previous findings showed, by selective mutation of each tyrosine on CD91 β-chain, that hsp70 relied on phosphorylation of both Y4474 and Y4508 for NF-κB activation, while gp96 and CRT relied on phosphorylation of Y4508 for the same effect [
13
]. These effects may be linked to the usage of AXL and/or Fgr for signal transduction observed here. It remains to be tested if AXL and Fgr each associates specifically with either of these tyrosine residues on the β-chain of CD91. We have not formally tested the reciprocal effects of AXL or Fgr inhibition on each other, however, in the context of regulation of cytokine expression our results suggest that signal transduction with AXL and Fgr could occur in a consecutive (serial) or concurrent manner. This remains to be tested to evaluate the controlled release of each cytokine, in response to specific HSP. The inhibitors used, bemcentinib and TL-0259, have been shown to be specific, with 10–100-fold selectivity for AXL and Fgr over other kinases in the TAM and Src families, respectively [
26
,
27
,
34
]. We have not ruled out the possibility that other Axl and/or Fgr kinase family members may be utilized under inhibitory conditions as compensatory pathways [
35
,
36
]. At baseline, in BMDCs not stimulated with HSPs, there appears to be some residual association of AXL and Fgr with CD91 and phosphorylation, and this may be related to other mechanisms of AXL and Fgr activation and/or other undetected CD91 ligands, or for functions other than cytokine release by DCs.
DCs are reprogrammed by the tumor-derived HSP-CD91 pathway in three major ways. First, DCs that engage HSPs through CD91 release cytokines and chemokines. These are important for priming T cell responses and for activation of NK cells. The increased breadth of the cytokine responses by multiple HSPs diminishes possible tumor escape mechanisms and allows for varied Th responses [
13
,
37
,
38
]. We suggest that this will be the picture with the release of multiple HSPs from cancer cells. Second, HSP-stimulated DCs upregulate co-stimulatory molecules including CD80, CD86, CD40 and MHCII [
12
]. The increased expression of co-stimulatory molecules contributes to “signal 2” for T cell priming. Thirdly, DCs modify their expression profiles that make them responsive to other immune molecules such as cytokines which play a key role in anti-tumor immunity. DC subsets are selectively more or less responsive to sets of cytokines when CD91 is removed. DCs deficient in CD91 appear to have reduced anti-tumor properties. While signaling networks that lead to DC maturation in response to pathogen associated molecular pattern during infection have been well documented, the signaling events controlling DC maturation in response to tumors have, until now, remained elusive. Our study elucidates a mechanism through which this occurs but remains to be tested in tumor models in vivo. Responsiveness of DCs to each cytokine that arose in our panel remains to be formally tested for DC output. Recently we have implicated CD91 in priming T cell responses to emerging tumors and showed that abrogation of CD91 expression or function promoted tumor formation [
3
,
39
]. Given our identification of signaling adaptors and molecules that CD91 utilizes for providing signals to T cells, our study here directly links CD91 function with cancer immunosurveillance.
While our studies have focused on DCs, other myeloid cells in the tissues where tumors emerge also express CD91. The effects of HSPs on other APCs remain to be investigated in a similar manner, for utilization of AXL and Fgr, for cytokine release and reprogramming profiles. Although no strain and/or sex-specific effects related to HSP immunobiology have been observed [
3
,
39
], it remains to be tested whether the CD91-AXL/Fgr pathway is applicable to other systems. AXL and Fgr are both myeloid-expressed kinases that have been implicated in immune cell regulation, controlling T cell priming by DCs and for proinflammatory macrophage activation [
40
–
42
].
Conclusions
In summary, our findings represent a significant advancement in the understanding of CD91-mediated signaling pathways in response to tumor-derived HSP and we uncover the dependency of this pathway on the kinases AXL and Fgr. This picture emerges as a mechanism for recognition of, and response to, emerging tumors. This pathway reveals a therapeutic avenue in which to pharmacologically enhance CD91-mediated signaling and in turn, immunosurveillance.
