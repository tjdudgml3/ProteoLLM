Introduction
Cancer is a worldwide disease, with 18 million new cases and 9.5 million cancer-related deaths reported each year. Cancer is a wide collection of diseases that includes over 100 distinct and distinct diseases
[10]
. Cancer comes in many forms, but they all include unregulated and aberrant cell development. Normal cells grow and divide in a controlled manner to deliver healthier cells and keep the body healthy (Fig.
1
). When they become old or injured, they may be replaced with fresh cells. Cancerous cells, unlike normal cells, continue to develop and produce more abnormal cells. They may also grow uncontrollably, forming a mass of tissue (tumor) or invading other organs (metastases) [
16
]. Early diagnosis is a pivotal stage in the treatment of cancer. Treatment options for late-stage cancers are frequently ineffective (A. C. [
38
]). The disparity in survival rates between early and late-stage cancer diagnoses underscores the critical importance of early detection. Surgical intervention or less aggressive medication regimens are considerably more effective when employed during the initial stages of cancer, yielding a 5-year survival rate of 91%, in stark contrast to the 26% survival rate associated with late-stage detection [
88
]. Therefore, the early detection of tumors is crucial for the successful treatment of cancer. Conventional cancer diagnosis typically involves a combination of medical history assessment, physical examination, and various diagnostic tests [
87
]. The most commonly used diagnostic modalities for cancer encompass a spectrum of imaging methodologies like computerized tomography (CT) scan, bone scan, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), positron emission tomography (PET) scan, ultrasound, and X-ray [
29
]. Using strong magnetic fields and radiofrequency pulses, MRI enables high-resolution cross-sectional imaging, which is especially useful for identifying soft tissue structures. It has broad applications in the assessment of neoplastic lesions in the musculoskeletal, breast, and cerebral domains [
19
]. Meanwhile, CT uses computer processing and X-ray technology to produce high-resolution cross-sectional images [
44
]. It is a versatile tool for identifying and characterizing tumors in a variety of anatomic locations, including the pelvic, abdominal, and pulmonary domains [
77
]. Traditional X-ray imaging, which uses ionizing radiation to clarify structure, continues to play a fundamental function, mostly in identifying cancers connected to the bones and certain anatomic abnormalities. Based on the propagation of sound waves, ultrasound is a valuable real-time imaging tool that may be used for a variety of purposes, including the evaluation of abdominal, pelvic, and mammary tissue and the guidance of procedural operations such as biopsies ([
32
], [
84
]). Endoscopy is a vital diagnostic and staging procedure for malignancies of the hollow organs, especially those of the digestive and respiratory systems. It involves inserting a flexible tube with a camera inside internal cavities [
52
]. Despite these widely used imaging techniques, they can only detect cancer when there is a noticeable anomaly in the tissue [
1
,
55
]. By the time such abnormalities become apparent, thousands of cancer cells may have already disseminated, potentially leading to metastasis. Additionally, the current imaging methods cannot differentiate between benign and malignant lesions [
42
]. The consequential delay in cancer detection and the ambiguity in lesion classification underscore the critical need for advancements in diagnostic approaches that can facilitate earlier and more accurate identification of malignancies. Therefore, advancements in early cancer identification play a pivotal role in expanding eligibility for curative treatments and reducing cancer-related mortality. In recent years, various biomarkers have been used for early-stage cancer detection. Biomarkers are biomolecules found in blood, other body fluids, or tissues that indicate an abnormal process, condition, or disease [
41
,
61
]. They can be effectively used to detect cancer in its early stages. Examples of such biomarkers include miRNA and circulating DNA in blood, which are currently being used for cancer detection [
61
]. In addition to blood-based components, saliva, urine, and breath also contain biomarkers that can be used for early-stage cancer detection [
53
,
67
,
114
]. For example, the development of an electronic tongue to detect early-stage cancer using urine is advantageous due to its convenient collection, particularly for screening purposes, compared to blood samples [
114
]. Another alternative to blood in this context is saliva, which can be utilized as a biomarker for head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) [
53
]. Numerous clinical trials are actively investigating the efficacy of these biomarkers across various cancer types. In this review, we focus on various biomarkers for early-stage cancer detection in recent years, including blood-based, non-blood-based, optical imaging, and ultrasound imaging non-invasive techniques.

Circulating carcinoma proteins
Circulating carcinoma proteins, which are secreted by tumors and found in the bloodstream, serve as essential cancer biomarkers [
97
]. These proteins offer valuable insights into the health and physical condition of individuals, with changes in their levels serving as indicators of disease progression [
85
]. The blood proteome, representing a subset of tissue proteome, is a crucial source of information about a patient's well-being. Alterations in plasma proteins can be utilized to monitor disease progression, and they may reflect or promote various capabilities acquired by cancer cells, including immune response manipulation, growth stimulation, angiogenesis induction, and invasion into surrounding tissues [
46
]. Research in cancer biomarkers aims to identify specific proteins in the blood that signal the presence of cancer, its stage, and its response to treatment. Methods such as proteomic analyses are commonly employed to comprehensively study the protein composition of biological samples like blood plasma. As of now, a multitude of protein biomarkers have been discovered in common cancers. Examples include prostate-specific antigen (PSA) in prostate cancer [
23
], CA125 in ovarian cancer [
25
], carcinoma embryonic antigen (CEA) in colorectal cancer [
71
], alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) in liver cancer [
30
], and CA19-9 in pancreatic cancer (G. [
63
]).
Circulating tumor cells
Circulating tumor cells (CTCs) have emerged as a compelling reservoir of biomarkers with profound implications for cancer diagnosis, prognosis, and recurrence prediction [
75
]. In the realm of diagnostics, the identification of CTCs in the bloodstream holds the potential for signaling the presence of a primary tumor. CTCs offer a distinctive advantage as they represent intact tumor cells released from primary or metastatic lesions [
3
]. This unique characteristic endows CTCs with the ability to furnish comprehensive information across various molecular levels, encompassing DNA, RNA, protein, and metabolites. Unlike fragmented genetic material or encapsulated vesicles, the intact nature of CTCs furnishes a direct and holistic portrayal of the tumor's molecular landscape. This feature amplifies the potential of CTC analysis to provide intricate insights into the genetic, transcriptional, translational, and metabolic facets of the tumor, thereby contributing to a more comprehensive understanding of its biology. Notably, monitoring changes in the quantity and attributes of CTCs over time proves to be a promising indicator for predicting cancer recurrence [
76
]. From a clinical standpoint, CTC analysis plays a pivotal role in guiding treatment decisions, enabling the customization of therapies based on individual patient profiles, and contributing to the evolving landscape of personalized medicine. The non-invasive nature of CTC monitoring accomplished through routine blood draws, contrasts with the invasiveness of tissue biopsies, providing a more accessible and repeatable approach for monitoring cancer progression [
58
]. Consequently, CTCs emerge as a promising and robust source for acquiring multifaceted information essential for advancing our understanding of tumor characteristics and guiding personalized therapeutic approaches.
Circulating cell-free tumor DNA
Cell-free DNA (cfDNA) originates from both normal cells and tumors, released through programmed cell death (apoptosis). It consists of small nucleic acid fragments not associated with cells or their fragments and is present in the plasma of all individuals [
80
]. cfDNA can be detected in the blood at an extremely early stage of cancer, even before conventional clinical tests can identify the cancer, increasing the window of opportunity for early intervention. cfDNA-based tests provide information about the tissue of origin (TOO), enabling the simultaneous diagnosis of multiple tumors, as opposed to the conventional approach of testing one malignancy at a time. In addition, cfDNA testing requires only one blood draw, which is less invasive, safer, and less complicated than conventional screening techniques, increasing patient compliance. In addition, cfDNA is able to represent the entire molecular and genetic landscape, regardless of heterogeneity within the tumor, which supports precision medicine and recurrence monitoring after early detection. It is used to detect lung cancer, colorectal cancer, and renal cancer. For clinical applications, cfDNA-based detection technology is required to improve sensitivity for minuscule cfDNA concentrations, particularly for early-stage cancer.
Circulating miRNAs
In recent years, circulating microRNAs (miRNAs) have emerged as promising biomarkers for cancer detection, playing a pivotal role in the development of non-invasive diagnostic strategies. These small non-coding RNAs, found in various body fluids such as blood, urine, and saliva, exhibit altered expression profiles in cancer patients compared to healthy individuals. The stability of circulating miRNAs in the bloodstream, coupled with their association with cancer-related processes, makes them attractive candidates for early detection and prognostic assessment [
18
]. Tumor-associated miRNAs exhibit heightened expression, while suppressors display reduced levels, contributing to a unique signature associated with cancer development. These tissue-specific miRNAs are emerging as valuable candidates for improving cancer diagnostics. Notably, specific circulating miRNAs in plasma/serum have demonstrated discriminatory potential for various cancers, including breast, colorectal, gastric, lung, pancreatic, and hepatocellular cancers. Examples of such discriminative miRNAs have been reported in studies such as those conducted by Antolin et al. (2015), Zanutto et al. (2014), Zhang et al. (2015b), Zhao et al. (2017), Kawaguchi et al. (2013), and Mirzaei H. R. et al. (2016). The identification and validation of these circulating miRNAs offer a promising avenue for the development of non-invasive tools facilitating earlier and more effective cancer diagnosis.
Extracellular vesicles
Effective bidirectional communication between cells and their microenvironment is essential for normal and pathological physiological processes [
106
]. Traditionally, this communication has been understood to occur through direct cell–cell contact or the secretion of soluble factors such as cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors [
105
]. However, in the last decade, a novel paradigm has emerged, highlighting extracellular vesicle (EV) trafficking as a significant mechanism for cell–cell communication. EVs, secreted by various eukaryotic cell types in vitro, have been identified in diverse bodily fluids, including blood, urine, bile, breast milk, synovial fluid, lacrimal fluid, seminal fluid, ascites, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, and feces [
24
].
The release of EVs can be triggered by various stimuli, including cell activation (e.g., platelet activation), changes in pH, hypoxia, irradiation, injury, exposure to complement proteins, and cellular stress [
107
]. EVs transport a range of molecules, including oncoproteins, oncopeptides, RNA species (microRNAs, mRNAs, long non-coding RNAs), lipids, and DNA fragments from donor to recipient cells, inducing significant phenotypic changes in the tumor microenvironment (TME) [
107
]. Recent evidence underscores the pivotal roles of EVs in cancer development, including their involvement in premetastatic niche formation and metastasis. Notably, cancer cells are now acknowledged to release a higher quantity of EVs compared to non-malignant counterparts, and these particles can be isolated from bodily fluids. Consequently, EVs show promising potential as blood-based or urine-based biomarkers for cancer diagnosis, prognostication, and surveillance. As an illustration, a recent investigation pinpointed numerous proteins linked to circulating extracellular particles, including extracellular vesicles (EVs), that exhibited distinctions between individuals with cancer and those without the condition. Notably, this study revealed that certain extracellular particle-associated proteins were specific to particular tumor types (mesothelioma, breast, colorectal, lung, and pancreatic cancer), irrespective of the disease stage. Despite these findings, only a limited number of small extracellular vesicle (sEV) biomarkers have undergone subsequent validation studies, and none has yet gained approval for clinical use by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) [
37
].
Liquid biopsy, or blood-based analysis, has become a revolutionary tool for the early detection of cancer by analyzing biomarkers in the blood, such as circulating tumor cells (CTCs), circulating carcinoma proteins, circulating cell-free tumor DNA (ctDNA), miRNAs, extracellular vesicles (EVs), and other components. Liquid biopsy shows great potential as a tool for the early detection of various cancers, including lung, colorectal, breast, and prostate cancer. Numerous clinical studies are currently investigating the effectiveness of liquid biopsy in early cancer detection. The potential of liquid biopsy biomarkers is further enhanced by next-generation sequencing (NGS) [
35
,
70
], which enables high-throughput RNA and DNA sequencing and allows for the detection of a wide range of genetic abnormalities and alterations with excellent sensitivity and specificity. Digital polymerase chain reaction (dPCR) is another technology that enables precise quantification of nucleic acids, detecting even low-abundance mutations [
70
] As a result, liquid biopsy becomes more effective even when biomarker concentrations are low. Therefore, combining these two methods could significantly improve the ability of liquid biopsy to detect cancer early in the future.

Urine analysis for cancer detection
The exploration of urine as a liquid biopsy for non-invasive cancer diagnosis is becoming increasingly appealing across various cancer types, owing to its unique advantages over blood. The non-invasive nature of urine makes it convenient for collection, even in a home setting, without requiring the assistance of a healthcare practitioner. Additionally, urine can be collected without constraints in terms of volume and repeatability, facilitating serial sampling and enabling comprehensive multi-omic analyses [
11
]. The analysis of urine samples from cancer patients has demonstrated promising capabilities in detecting both bladder cancer and breast cancer. Bladder cancer, a prevalent malignancy affecting the urinary tract, is a focal point in this context. Commercially available urine markers, such as the bladder tumor antigen (BTA) tests, including the qualitative BTA Stat and the quantitative BTA TRAK, detect human complement factor H-related protein in the urine of individuals with bladder cancer. ImmunoCyt, a diagnostic approach by Scimedx Corp, combines cytology with an immunofluorescence assay, identifying cellular biomarkers for bladder cancer. Urine tests are also utilized for cervical cancer [
82
], prostate cancer [
104
], and pancreatic detection [

]. The diverse urine-based diagnostic tools contribute significantly to the non-invasive detection landscape, providing varied options for effective screening and surveillance, particularly in the realm of bladder cancer [

Breath analysis for cancer detection
In recent times, various studies have brought attention to the potential of chemical breath analysis for non-invasive cancer detection. Specific volatile organic compounds (VOCs), believed to be either elevated in concentration or displaying distinctive patterns, have been proposed as potential biomarkers [
8
]. The non-invasive nature of breath samples is a major factor in the use of breath analysis in cancer detection. There are several VOC profiles associated with lung cancer [

The comparison of various studies on breath volatile organic compounds (VOCs) is challenging due to the diverse range of breath sampling equipment and detection principles employed for instrumental analysis. These techniques include solid phase microextraction (SPME) followed by gas chromatography-mass selective detection (GC–MS) [
67

Most of the VOCs found in exhaled breath serve as biomarkers for detecting various types of cancer, including breast, colorectal, gastric, lung, prostate, and squamous cell cancers [

Saliva analysis for cancer detection
Saliva is proving to be a key element in cancer detection as it is non-invasive, making it a readily available and user-friendly choice for individuals undergoing cancer screening [
53
]. The inherent non-invasiveness of saliva collection makes it a compelling option for inclusion in early-stage cancer detection. The diverse array of biomolecules in saliva, including proteins, cytokines, DNA, RNA, and exosomes, forms a comprehensive framework for assessing precancerous conditions [
47
,
53
,
89
]. The extensive range of salivary biomarkers provides a nuanced insight into molecular alterations associated with cancer, facilitating early detection and enabling monitoring of disease progression and treatment efficacy. Saliva emerges as a valuable diagnostic tool for the identification of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), ranking as the sixth most common cancer globally [
90
]. It has been demonstrated that specific mutations associated with tumors are present in 71% (5/7) of saliva specimens collected from patients with head and neck cancer [
109
]. Saliva from HNSCC patients has revealed the presence of tumor cells and tumor-derived circulatory DNA (ctDNA) [
15
],X. [
103
] and the non-invasive oral rub and rinse (ORR) technique offers an alternative avenue for identifying gene mutations and genetic alterations, enabling early detection of HNSCC through saliva analysis [
20
]. Furthermore, elevated expression of three salivary proteins—complement factor H (CFH), alpha-1-antitrypsin (SERPINA1), and fibrinogen alpha chain (FGA)—is associated with advanced grades of HNSCC tumors [
53
]. Therefore, saliva emerges as a crucial biofluid for cancer detection and monitoring due to its minimal risk of infection transmission, heightened patient compliance, and the cost-effectiveness of salivary testing. The ease of collection further underscores its practicality, making it a viable choice for routine screenings, particularly in resource-constrained settings. Saliva-based diagnostics demonstrate exceptional performance with reported sensitivity and specificity levels exceeding 95%, affirming the test's capacity to accurately identify true positive or negative cases [
53
]. Although it has high sensitivity and specificity, many biomarkers in saliva are awaiting clinical trials [
61
].
Non-blood tests for early-stage cancer detection include the use of urine, breath, and saliva. Urine offers the advantage of easy accessibility compared to blood samples and uses biomarkers such as mRNAs and proteins for cancer detection, especially for bladder, prostate, cervical, and kidney cancers [
22
]. Breath analysis relies on volatile organic compounds (VOCs) for early cancer detection [
96
], making it a convenient method for regular screening of breast, colorectal, gastric, lung, prostate, and squamous cell cancers. Saliva, rich in biomarkers and easily collectable, is particularly useful for detecting head and neck cancers [
90
]. While these methods offer many advantages, they still face challenges such as the need for proper instrumentation, as well as improvements in sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy to enhance their reliability and standardization as non-invasive cancer detection methods.
Non-invasive optical imaging methods
Optical imaging emerges as an economically viable, expeditious, and highly sensitive modality for the non-invasive detection of human cancers within regions amenable to optical imaging apparatus. This methodology relies on the utilization of light for the acquisition of images from biological tissues, capitalizing on the interaction between light and tissues for diagnostic purposes. The merits of optical imaging encompass its non-invasive nature, cost-effectiveness, rapid imaging kinetics, sensitivity to molecular perturbations associated with cancer, adaptability to diverse anatomic sites, and furnishing of functional insights into tissue dynamics. Two essential non-invasive optical imaging techniques that are vital for the diagnosis of cancer are fluorescence imaging (FI) and bioluminescence imaging (BLI).
Fluorescence imaging
Fluorescence imaging (FI) is characterized as a highly sensitive technique that enables multiplex imaging, in which different molecules or structures are made visible with different fluorophores. Unlike alternative imaging methods, fluorescence offers the advantage of real-time imaging, eliminating the need for extensive image reconstruction and post-processing. However, a notable challenge to its clinical applicability is the limited penetration depth of light into tissue, ranging from micrometers to centimeters, depending on the wavelength [
45
]. Fluorescence imaging for cancer cell targeting employs diverse imaging technologies to enhance the early detection of neoplasia by focusing on molecular signatures unique to cancer.
Near-infrared (NIR) fluorescence imaging in cancer diagnosis is an innovative technique that uses the properties of near-infrared light to detect and visualize tumors [
26
]. In this method, the patient is administered non-specific fluorescent agents or targeted fluorescent tracers. These agents emit fluorescence when exposed to near-infrared light, which is then recorded by a special camera [
74
]. The NIR-I region (650–900 nm) of the electromagnetic spectrum is characterized by its inherent safety and simplicity, devoid of hazardous ionizing radiation. Notably, NIR light exhibits a capability for relatively deeper tissue penetration into living organisms compared to visible fluorescence [
50
,
113
]. In the field of in vivo fluorescence-mediated cancer detection, researchers have a repertoire of non-specific and cancer-targeting agents at their disposal. Non-specific agents accumulate in tumors depending on variables such as vascularity and impaired capillary permeability and subsequently emit a characteristic fluorescence from the tissue of interest, particularly the cancerous region. In the realm of NIR dyes, their availability is limited primarily due to the constraints associated with conventional dyes. These limitations encompass poor hydrophilicity, inadequate photostability, low quantum yield, insufficient stability, and diminished detection sensitivity within biological systems. Recent advancements have addressed these challenges by introducing a new generation of NIR dyes, including cyanine dyes, squaraine, phthalocyanines, porphyrin derivatives, and BODIPY (boron dipyrromethane)(S. [
64
]). These emerging dyes exhibit significantly enhanced chemical and photostability, elevated fluorescence intensity, and prolonged fluorescent lifetimes (S. [
64
]). An example of the NIR dye is indocyanine green (ICG), a substance approved by the US FDA for used in lung and breast cancer detection [
51
]. The critical need for cancer-targeting agents for precise and sensitive cancer detection is hampered by the prevalent lack of targeting or extremely low targeting efficacy in most near-infrared (NIR) probes. To overcome this challenge, primary strategies involve the chemical or biological conjugation of cancer-specific ligands, such as chemical molecules, peptides, proteins, antibodies, and aptamers, capable of specifically recognizing biomarkers associated with cancer cells [
108
]. Another approach involves integrating imaging agents into nanoparticles or polymers, with or without target ligands, serving as carriers for delivering agents to tumors via receptor-mediated targeting or the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect in the tumor microvasculature [
4
]. A recent alternative approach introduces a target-activatable strategy, where imaging agents do not target specific cell-surface receptors but are designed to undergo a chemical reaction (e.g., enzymatic cleavage or oxidation) upon interaction with their intended target. This transition transforms the probe from a non- or weakly fluorescent state to a highly emitting form (R. R. [
112
]).

Nanoparticle conjugated NIR dyes for cancer detection and imaging
The use of nanoparticles for NIR optical imaging holds great potential for overcoming the limitations observed with conventional NIR-emitting contrast agents [
4
]. In particular, NIR fluorescent probes (NIRF) containing nanoparticles have successfully addressed the problems of conventional organic dyes. These probes can target tumors either through the effect of increased permeability and retention associated with the tumor microvasculature or through specific binding between tumor cell receptors and biomolecule-modified nanoparticles. The application of nanoparticle-based imaging probes has attracted considerable attention for both passive accumulation and active targeting of cancer receptors and offers promising opportunities for medical imaging for the early-stage detection of tumor. Nanoparticle-based NIRF probes such as NIR dye-containing nanoparticles, NIR inorganic fluorescent semiconductor nanocrystals, up conversion nanoparticles (UCNs), Au nanocluster, and Ag nanoclusters are recent additions to the list of new NIRF probes [
62
].
Shlomo Margel and colleagues in 2012 enhanced the fluorescence intensity and photostability of cyanine NIR dye by utilizing iron oxide nanoparticles coated with human serum albumin. These nanoparticles were explored for optical detection of colon cancer, showcasing reduced dye photobleaching when encapsulated. In 2013, Ute Resch-Genger and colleagues employed the asymmetric cyanine dye Itrybe, which was conjugated to amino-modified polystyrene NPs of various sizes (15, 25, 100 nm) through a one-step staining procedure. The NPs were surface-functionalized with polyethylene glycol (PEG) and the tumor-targeting antibody Herceptin. The resulting Itrybe-loaded NPs displayed robust fluorescence signals in both in vitro and in vivo settings. Notably, the Herceptin-conjugated NPs demonstrated binding specificity to the tumor-specific biomarker HER2. Nanocarriers with NIR dyes are also used for cancer detection. For example, silica nanoparticles, loaded with Cy5 conjugated with IgG, enable targeted imaging of breast cancer cells through selective IgG recognition [
34
]. Silica nanoparticles incorporating indocyanine green (ICG) are utilized for imaging various tumors via the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect [
54
]. These examples highlight the potential of these near-infrared probes for the sensitive detection and characterization of tumor development and progression. Nonetheless, there is a growing need for high-precision imaging, which raises the standard for fluorescence imaging in terms of penetration capacity and resolution.
In contrast to NIR-I fluorescence imaging, NIR-II fluorescence imaging has extended excitation and emission wavelengths, which leads to improved image depth and spatiotemporal resolution (T. [
99
]). The possibilities of NIR-II fluorescence imaging are promising for advancing the precise identification and localization of cancers and make a valuable contribution in this regard. A variety of cancer biomarkers (EGFR, HER2, PSMA, etc.) within the NIR-II spectrum present promising prospects for cancer detection and imaging. NIR-II molecular dyes are particularly well-suited for precise cancer imaging owing to their facile bioconjugation with peptides and proteins of interest [
57
,
91
]. The indispensable role of small molecule-derived probes is emphasized as crucial for advancing the clinical applications of NIR-II bioimaging [
69
]. Cutting-edge NIR-II bioconjugates offer a groundbreaking perspective for medical practitioners, providing an unprecedented insight into tissues for enhanced tumor detection at greater depths and improved contrast. This advancement enables early detection in cancer screenings and facilitates precise delineation of boundaries between healthy and cancerous tissues during solid tumor resection. The enhanced capabilities of NIR-II bioconjugates promise to significantly improve the accuracy and efficacy of medical procedures related to cancer diagnosis and treatment [
68
].
Bioluminescence imaging
Bioluminescence imaging (BLI) stands out as a widely utilized technology in cancer research due to its capability to enable the in vivo assessment and quantification of molecular and cellular processes, thereby enhancing the precision of cancer detection and staging. BLI achieves this by converting chemical energy into light or photons through the interaction of enzymes, such as luciferases or photoproteins, with luciferin substrates [
60
]. Recent advancements in BLI have markedly improved the visibility of individual tumor cells, surpassing traditional methods by a factor of 100–1000 [
40
]. BLI is a technique that enables the quantitative monitoring of individual biomolecular reactions and cellular behaviors within living organisms. BLI employs luciferase enzymes to catalyze the oxidation of luciferin, resulting in the generation of an excited-state molecule that emits bioluminescence. One notable characteristic of BLI is its independence from external excitation light, rendering it particularly sensitive in opaque and heterogeneous tissues. BLI serves as a non-destructive imaging technology applicable for quantifying tumor cells in tissue culture samples or on individuals. Its extensive dynamic range permits multiple-order adjustments in signal strength during tests. BLI proves valuable in elucidating various aspects of cancer biology, including metabolism, apoptosis, tumor hypoxia, angiogenesis, and responses to therapy. It is non-invasive and cost-effective nature facilitates real-time investigation of disease processes in living organisms. BLI is commonly employed in small animal models to enhance the safety and efficacy of stem cell treatments, enabling the measurement of parameters like survival, migration, differentiation, and exploration of areas such as miRNA, extracellular vesicles, and cellular aging. However, the identification of metastatic nodules using BLI is constrained by factors such as their size and depth.
Raman spectroscopy in cancer detection
Optical vibrational spectroscopy techniques, in particular Raman spectroscopy (RS), provide a higher degree of molecular specificity by focusing specifically on energy transitions between vibrational levels of molecules [
66
]. The interaction of photons with molecules can lead to various outcomes, including transmission, absorption, or scattering, with Raman scattering originating from perturbations that induce vibrational or rotational transitions [
49
].
A Raman spectrum offers quantitative information on the sample's chemical composition and serves as a molecular fingerprint of the material [
49
]. The characteristic Raman features of biological molecules, such as nucleic acids, proteins, and lipids, provide valuable information about structure and environment. These features allow Raman spectra to detect recognizable molecular and cellular changes associated with cancer, effectively distinguishing between normal and cancerous tissue [
83
]. These spectra are also promising in identifying transitional changes in precancerous tissues and benign abnormalities, such as inflammation, by characteristic Raman features. Raman spectroscopy can detect increased levels of cellular nucleic acids, a significant change in cancer and precancerous lesions based on extensive DNA studies [
9
,
65
]. In the context of cancer detection, Raman spectroscopy has been applied in vitro to various cancers of epithelial and mesenchymal origin, including breast, brain, colon, bladder, and gynecologic tissue [
17
,
21
,
33
,
93
]. The resulting Raman spectra effectively capture the gradual transitions from healthy tissue to tumor and provide valuable insights. Its label-free nature is a remarkable advantage that eliminates the need for labeling of tissue and reagents, or other preparations, thereby streamlining translation to clinical applications.
Raman spectroscopy-based biopsy guidance holds promise for reducing false positive biopsies and improving the precision of cancer diagnosis. The consistently reported overall sensitivities and specificities, falling within the range of 73–100% and 66–100%, respectively (W. [
101
,
102
]), underscore the robust potential of Raman spectroscopy as a valuable tool in the field of cancer diagnostics. The reiterated emphasis on these statistics reinforces the reliability and effectiveness of Raman spectroscopy in advancing cancer diagnostic capabilities.
These optical imaging methods include fluorescence, which uses fluorophores and modified fluorophores in the near-infrared (NIR) region for imaging, while bioluminescence converts chemical energy into light or photons through the interaction of enzymes, such as luciferases or photoproteins, with luciferin substrates, allowing for the quantification of tumor cells in tissue culture samples or in individuals. Raman spectroscopy can detect increased levels of cellular nucleic acids, which is a significant marker of cancer and precancerous lesions, based on extensive DNA studies [
9
,
65
]. Raman spectra also show promise in identifying transitional changes in precancerous tissues and benign abnormalities, such as inflammation, by characteristic Raman features. These methods offer several advantages, including high sensitivity, real-time imaging capabilities, and the ability to provide detailed molecular and cellular information. However, challenges such as light scattering in tissues and the need for specialized equipment must still be addressed for broader clinical adoption.
Ultrasound imaging for cancer detection
Ultrasound imaging (US) presents a compelling option for cancer detection due to its accessibility, cost-effectiveness, real-time capability, and minimal training requirements compared to other imaging modalities like MRI, CT, and PET scans [
43
]. It utilizes high-frequency sound waves to create internal organ images by translating rebounded waves into visual representations (G. [

Clinical considerations and future perspectives
The effective early detection of cancer should ideally be accessible, non-invasive, affordable, and highly sensitive [
27
]. However, many cancers are not routinely screened for due to their low prevalence rates in the general population, making individual screening inefficient. An alternative approach involves simultaneous screening for multiple cancers in a single test, which could be transformative for individuals with less common cancers that are currently not screened for. Early signs of cancer are often non-specific and may be overlooked by patients and healthcare providers, leading to delays in diagnosis and poorer outcomes. A rapid and low-cost test capable of detecting multiple cancer types could help identify patients at elevated risk of cancer, facilitating prioritized diagnostic investigations.
Blood-based tests hold promise for the early detection of multiple cancers, but existing biomarkers in these tests often suffer from low detection sensitivity. For example, in patients with advanced cancer, circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) levels can rise to 10% or more, facilitating the detection of actionable mutations and enabling disease monitoring. However, in patients with early-stage disease, the median concentration of ctDNA is notably lower, usually at least 1000 times lower [
27
]. Thus, there is a critical need to develop healthcare instruments with high sensitivity capable of detecting mutations at lower concentrations in the blood for early-stage cancer detection. AI can enhance blood-based analysis as a more powerful tool for early-stage cancer detection by rapidly analyzing hundreds of proteomic signatures linked to cancer-related circulating proteins and nucleic acid molecules, such as exosomes and microRNAs [
73
]. By training machine learning models on large datasets of protein and nucleic acid profiles, AI can identify patterns or biomarkers that signal cancer presence earlier than current methods. AI algorithms can also improve the sensitivity and specificity of liquid biopsy results, helping to differentiate between benign conditions and malignant tumors [
2
]. This reduces the risk of false positives and unnecessary interventions, making liquid biopsies a more reliable cancer screening tool. Additionally, AI can manage the complex, high-dimensional data generated by next-generation sequencing (NGS) and proteomics, uncovering correlations and actionable insights faster than traditional analytical methods. AI could also serve as a multimodal tool by integrating data from both liquid biopsies and optical imaging methods. AI utilized for early-stage lung cancer detection and treatment opens the door to future applications of AI technologies for detecting and treating other types of cancer as well (S. [
39
]).
Optical imaging techniques should be included in yearly health screenings and stored in national healthcare databases and personal records. Using personal and family cancer history with AI algorithms, optical imaging data in mobile hospitals can predict individual cancer risk early. This integration of cancer history, AI, and optical imaging in mobile hospitals aids in early cancer detection (Fig.
5
). In addition to periodic comprehensive health assessments, individuals should undergo regular clinical screenings based on their health profile, lifestyle, age, and family history [
27
]. This proactive approach to clinical screening tailored to individual risk factors and demographics can facilitate early detection and intervention, ultimately improving health outcomes. Looking toward the future, there may be a shift toward individuals taking greater responsibility for their health as part of their daily routines at work or home. This could involve incorporating healthy habits into daily life to promote overall well-being.

Conclusion
In conclusion, the quest for non-invasive methods in cancer detection has witnessed significant advancements, offering patients safer and more comfortable diagnostic alternatives. Imaging techniques, blood tests assessing tumor markers, liquid biopsies detecting circulating tumor DNA, endoscopic procedures, infrared imaging, breath analysis, and artificial intelligence-based analyses all contribute to the expanding array of non-invasive tools. While each method possesses its strengths and limitations, their collective impact enhances diagnostic accuracy and allows for more frequent monitoring, thereby contributing to early cancer detection and improved patient outcomes. Ongoing research and technological innovations continue to shape the landscape of non-invasive cancer detection, promising even more refined and accessible approaches in the future.
