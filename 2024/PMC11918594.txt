Introduction
Chronic gastritis (CG) is a condition marked by prolonged inflammation of the gastric mucosa, leading to varying degrees of damage to the glandular and superficial epithelial cells. The primary causes of CG are external factors, with
Helicobacter pylori
being the most significant, along with certain endogenous factors
[
1
]
. CG generates reactive oxygen species, which can induce DNA damage in the stomach lining cells. This DNA damage can trigger a cascade of events, potentially leading to cancer
[
2
]
. Many individuals with chronic atrophic gastritis (CAG) remain asymptomatic, and the condition is significant due to its potential progression to gastric cancer (GC)
[
3
]
. It is recommended that patients with advanced stages of CAG undergo high-quality endoscopy every three years, as they are at a higher risk for developing GC
[
4
]
. GC is a common malignancy of the digestive system with a global presence. The five-year survival rate for GC ranges from 30% to 65%. It is crucial to identify molecules linked to the development of GC and validate biomarkers for its diagnosis
[
5
]
. Metabolomic technology, with its potential to detect key metabolites that may be biomarkers for lung and gastric cancers, offers a robust disease diagnosis and management method
[
5
]
. Studies of cell lines, serum, plasma, urine, or gastric juice have shown that metabolites related to carbohydrates, amino acids, lipids, and nucleic acids are altered in GC. This technology helps uncover the ultimate outcomes of genome-environment interactions at an individual level and plays a vital role in managing cancer patient health
[
6
]
. Researchers can improve disease diagnosis, develop novel therapies, and better understand metabolic processes by utilizing targeted and untargeted metabolomics.
Methodology
A comprehensive literature search was conducted to identify studies related to chronic gastritis (CG),
H. pylori
, gastric cancer (GC), and the application of metabolomics in these conditions. The databases used included PubMed, Google Scholar, Web of Science, and Scopus. The search strategy employed keywords such as “chronic gastritis,” “gastric cancer,” “Helicobacter pylori,” “metabolomics,” “biomarkers,” and “oxidative stress.” Filters were applied to include only peer-reviewed, English-language publications. Articles were included if they focused on the progression of CG or GC, the role of
H. pylori
, or the use of metabolomics for biomarker identification, while studies lacking full texts, non-English articles, and those not directly relevant to the topic were excluded.
The study selection process followed a systematic approach inspired by the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines, adapted for narrative reviews. After identifying potential articles, duplicates were removed using reference management software. Titles and abstracts were independently screened by two reviewers, followed by a full-text assessment to confirm eligibility based on inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. The selected studies were then analyzed for relevant data, focusing on the methodology, findings, and implications of metabolomic profiling in CG and GC.
To enhance transparency, a PRISMA-style flow diagram has been included to visually depict the article selection process, outlining the number of records identified, screened, excluded, and finally included in the review (Fig.
1
).

Pathophysiology
Chronic atrophic gastritis:
CAG is a chronic inflammatory condition characterized by the reduction or loss of gastric mucosal glands, often accompanied by intestinal or pyloric gland metaplasia. One of the primary causes of CAG is infection by
H. pylori
, a spiral-shaped, Gram-negative bacterium.
H. pylori
infection triggers a severe inflammatory response in the gastric mucosa, leading to tissue damage and an increased risk of GC. Although many people with
H. pylori
remain asymptomatic, they are still at risk for developing CG.
H. pylori
is widespread, colonizing the gastric epithelium of approximately 50% of the adult population globally, with prevalence rates ranging from 30% in developed countries to 90% in underdeveloped regions, particularly in Eastern Asia
[
7
]
.
Autoimmune atrophic gastritis
Autoimmune atrophic gastritis (AAG) is a form of CG where the immune system attacks and damages the oxyntic mucosa of the stomach, primarily in the body and fundus, sparing the antral mucosa. It is also associated with producing anti-parietal cells and anti-intrinsic factor antibodies. The pathogenesis of AAG is not fully understood. Still, evidence suggests that the CD4+ Th1 response plays a crucial role in cellular immunity, with autoreactive Th1 cells potentially damaging the H
+
/K
+
ATPase in gastric parietal cells. This immune response leads to the destruction or loss of parietal cells, which are responsible for producing intrinsic factors and gastric acid
[
7
]
.
In contrast to non-autoimmune gastritis (NAIG), which is commonly associated with
H. pylori
and typically begins in the antrum before spreading to the corpus to cause pangastritis, autoimmune gastritis (AIG) usually affects the stomach corpus and fundus while sparing the antrum. AIG is a rarer condition where atrophic gastritis arises from autoimmune mechanisms rather than infection, leading to a different pattern of gastric involvement
[
8
]
.
AAG is more prevalent in individuals over 60 years old, with a higher occurrence in women, particularly those with a personal or family history of autoimmune diseases such as type 1 diabetes and autoimmune thyroiditis. Symptoms of AAG can be non-specific, including postprandial fullness, early satiety, bloating, abdominal discomfort, and epigastric pain. Pernicious anemia, due to vitamin B12 deficiency, is a classic symptom of AAG, leading to fatigue, weakness, and shortness of breath. Other hematological abnormalities, such as micro/normocytic anemia, can occur due to iron deficiency. Neurological symptoms, including peripheral neuropathy, myelopathy, and autonomic dysfunction, may result from vitamin B12 deficiency, manifesting as tingling, numbness, or paralysis in the limbs. Psychological symptoms, such as psychosis, depression, and cognitive impairment, have also been reported
[
9
]
. Additionally, AAG patients may be at risk for osteoporosis due to impaired absorption of vitamins like 25-OH vitamin D and micronutrients such as calcium, resulting from gastric hypo- or achlorhydria. Early in the disease, patients may present with non-specific gastrointestinal symptoms like nausea, bloating, and upper abdominal discomfort
[
9
]
.
Intestinal metaplasia and CAG are therefore recognized as precancerous conditions, as they independently elevate the risk of developing dysplasia and GC
[
7
]
. One of the most critical risk factors for developing non-cardia stomach cancer is
H. pylori
infection. This bacterium is implicated in approximately 75% of stomach cancer cases and is associated with a three-fold increase in the lifetime risk of developing non-cardia GC
[
8
]
.
H. pylori
was categorized as a class I carcinogen in 1994 by the World Health Organization (WHO) and the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), identifying it as a significant risk factor for non-cardia stomach cancers, including adenocarcinoma and lymphoma
[
7
]
.
H. pylori
infection plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis of GC through the “Correa’s cascade,” a model of gastric carcinogenesis. According to this model, non-CAG, typically caused by chronic
H. pylori
infection, progresses from normal gastric mucosa to a series of precancerous conditions, including intestinal metaplasia, dysplasia, and eventually adenocarcinoma. This sequence of events, known as the inflammation-atrophy-metaplasia-dysplasia-carcinoma cascade, is a critical pathway leading to GC
[
8
]
. Eradication of
H. pylori
in patients with CAG and non-CAG has effectively cured non-CAG and reduced the risk of developing GC. The prevention or interruption of this cascade through
H. pylori
eradication is considered a critical strategy in GC prevention
[
8
]
.
Tobacco use is the second most significant environmental factor in the development of GC, accounting for approximately 11% of cases. Smoking significantly increases the likelihood of intestinal metaplasia progressing to dysplasia and raises the overall risk of developing intestinal metaplasia
[
7
]
. In addition, bile acid reflux into the gastric lumen, especially in
H. pylori
-positive individuals, poses a risk for developing intestinal metaplasia and GC. Bile acids contribute to gastric carcinogenesis by several mechanisms. They indirectly damage cellular DNA by inducing oxidative stress and producing reactive oxygen species, which promote the progression of intestinal metaplasia and the accumulation of mutations, ultimately increasing the risk of cancer. Moreover, bile acids enhance the expression of CDX2, an intestinal-specific transcription factor that governs and sustains intestinal differentiation within the gastric mucosa, further promoting the development of intestinal metaplasia
[
7
]
. Dietary factors also play a crucial role in GC risk. The consumption of processed or preserved meats, particularly salted or smoked, is positively correlated with an increased risk of non-cardia GC in a dose-dependent manner. The carcinogenic potential of these foods is attributed to the presence of nitrates, nitrites, and other chemical compounds formed during processing, which can lead to the formation of carcinogenic N-nitroso compounds in the stomach
[
7
]
.
Gastric cancer
GC is a heterogeneous disease that can be categorized based on its anatomical location into two main types: proximal (cardiac GC) and distal (non-cardia GC). These categories reflect different etiologies, risk factors, and clinical features. At the histological level, GC is further classified into two primary subtypes according to the Laurén classification: intestinal and diffuse. The intestinal type is the most common globally, accounting for approximately 80% of cases
[
8
]
. It typically occurs in the distal stomach (non-cardia) and is strongly associated with chronic
H. pylori
infection. In contrast, the diffuse type of GC has a different histological appearance and pathogenesis. It is less common and characterized by poorly cohesive cells infiltrating the stomach wall, leading to a more aggressive disease with a worse prognosis. The diffuse type is not as strongly linked to
H. pylori
infection and does not follow the same precancerous cascade as the intestinal type
[
8
]
.
Adequate preoperative staging is crucial for the effective management of stomach cancer. While tumor resection remains the primary curative treatment, challenges in clinical staging can complicate the selection of the appropriate therapeutic approach
[
10
]
. For early-stage GC, treatment options include endoscopic procedures such as endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD), endoscopic mucosal resection, and laparoscopic surgery. These minimally invasive techniques are suitable for tumors that have not profoundly invaded the gastric wall or spread to lymph nodes. However, for intermediate stages, where the cancer may have invaded deeper layers or spread to regional lymph nodes, neo-adjuvant chemotherapy is often employed to improve the tumor’s status before surgical resection
[
10
]
. The two main pillars of therapeutic care in GC are assessing the depth of tumor invasion and evaluating the presence of malignant lymph nodes. Accurate clinical staging is essential for guiding treatment decisions and involves the use of multimodal imaging techniques
[
10
]
. Computed tomography (CT) and endoscopic ultrasonography are key imaging modalities in staging GC. CT scans, particularly multi-detector computed tomography (MDCT), play a vital role in evaluating both loco-regional involvement and distant metastases
[
10
]
.

Systemic and metabolic alterations of
H. pylori
infection
Systemic impacts of H. pylori infection
The presence of
H. pylori
is closely linked to the development of peptic ulcers and gastritis. If left untreated,
H. pylori
-induced gastritis can progress to a chronic state
[
11
]
. Massarrat
et al
, in a cohort study, demonstrated that CG generates reactive oxygen species that can damage DNA in gastric epithelial cells, potentially initiating a cascade of events leading to cancer, progressing through stages such as CG, CAG, intestinal metaplasia, dysplasia, and adenocarcinoma
[
12
,
13
]
. In the gastric environment, persistent antigenic stimuli may arise from non-
H. pylori
microbes, or
H. pylori
, might work synergistically to exacerbate the inflammatory response. Dysbiosis within this ecosystem allows for the invasion and proliferation of various pathogenic microorganisms, compromising the immune system and the integrity of the mucosal barrier
[
12
,
13
]
.
Diseases related to
H. pylori
are not confined to the gastrointestinal tract. There is substantial evidence linking
H. pylori
infection to hematological disorders such as idiopathic thrombocytopenic purpura, iron deficiency anemia, and vitamin B12 deficiency
[
14
]
. Additionally, a growing body of research supports connections between
H. pylori
and neurological disorders (e.g. Parkinson’s disease, Alzheimer’s disease, and age-related cognitive decline), cardiovascular diseases, liver diseases, and metabolic disorders (e.g. diabetes, obesity, and metabolic syndrome), as well as autoimmune and inflammatory conditions (e.g. AIG, immune thrombocytopenic purpura, autoimmune thyroid diseases, and inflammatory bowel diseases)
[
15
]
. It is hypothesized that the local inflammation in the stomach due to
H. pylori
can spread systemically through the release of proinflammatory cytokines
[
16
,
17
]
. This may promote low-grade chronic inflammation, a common feature of
H. pylori
-associated extra-gastroduodenal disorders, including cardiometabolic diseases like diabetes, atherosclerosis, and dyslipidemia
[
16
,
17
]
. However, this hypothesis has yet to be conclusively confirmed, and our understanding of how inflammation links
H. pylori
to metabolic disorders remains incomplete. The modulation of gut microbiota during
H. pylori
infection could play a role in triggering and exacerbating metabolic disorders, but human studies on this topic are still limited
[
18
]
.
Role of H. pylori in metabolic health
The connection between
H. pylori
infection and energy management, weight gain, and metabolic homeostasis is still debated
[
15
]
. Alterations in the gastrointestinal microenvironment due to
H. pylori
infection have been suggested to impair nutrient absorption, leading to micronutrient deficiencies. Malabsorption of iron and vitamins such as vitamin B12, vitamin A, vitamin C, vitamin E, and folic acid has been linked to
H. pylori
infection
[
19
]
. Consequently,
H. pylori
-positive patients have higher incidences of iron and B12 deficiency anemias than non-infected individuals. Other disorders related to micronutrient deficiencies have also been associated with
H. pylori
infection
[
19
]
.
Role of H. pylori in hormonal imbalances and weight management
H. pylori
infection has been associated with lower levels of the orexigenic gastric hormone ghrelin and higher levels of the anorexigenic adipokine leptin
[
17
]
. These hormonal changes suggest that
H. pylori
infection can cause growth retardation and malnutrition in children
[
17
]
. However, findings on weight management in adults with
H. pylori
infections remain controversial, as cohort studies, such as that by Suki
et al
, report mixed results, showing positive, negative, or no correlation between
H. pylori
infection and the increased body mass index (BMI)
[
20
,
21
]
.
H. pylori
eradication has been associated with weight gain, likely due to the improvement of postprandial dyspeptic symptoms and changes in ghrelin and leptin levels, as supported by Level I evidence from a randomized controlled trial by Lane
et al
[
22
,
23
]
. An intervention study in
H. pylori
-positive children with previous growth retardation and low ghrelin levels showed restoration of normal ghrelin levels and increased weight gain upon
H. pylori
eradication
[
24
]
. In contrast, other studies reported significant weight loss, decreased fat mass percentage, and increased fat-free mass after
H. pylori
eradication treatment or no effect on body weight in adults
[
25
]
.
An imbalance in hormones such as glucagon-like peptide 1 (GLP-1) and the generation of a proinflammatory environment might negatively affect metabolic homeostasis and weight in adulthood
[
17
]
. Low-grade inflammation is also associated with obesity, diabetes, insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and cardiovascular diseases
[
26
]
.
Lipid profile alterations
H. pylori
-infected patients often display an unfavorable pro-atherogenic lipid profile, characterized by high triglycerides, total cholesterol, and LDL-C and decreased HDL-C levels
[
27
]
. Zhao
et al
reported that in addition to the dyslipidemic profile, diminished bilirubin levels, which have powerful antioxidant properties and are inversely related to the risk of cardiovascular diseases and metabolic syndrome, have been observed in
H. pylori
-infected patients
[
28
]
. A meta-analysis of 27 studies concluded that most evidence points toward an unfavorable lipid profile in
H. pylori
-infected patients
[
29
]
. The causes of this altered lipid profile are not fully understood, but impaired intestinal absorption and altered bile acid dynamics may contribute
[
30
]
. Notably, gut microbiota modulates these processes and could act as mediators in establishing the dyslipidemic profile in
H. pylori
patients.
Glucose homeostasis and diabetes
The association between
H. pylori
infection and glucose homeostasis has also been explored
[
31
]
. Evidence suggests that
H. pylori
may be involved in both the onset of diabetes and impaired glycemic control in diabetic patients
[
31
]
. However, contradictory trends or a lack of association between
H. pylori
infection and type 2 diabetes have also been reported. One proposed mechanism for this relationship is the proinflammatory environment induced by
H. pylori
infection, which promotes gastric inflammation and cytokine secretion. Indeed,
H. pylori
infection has been linked to increased levels of C-reactive protein (CRP) and interleukin 6 (IL-6)
[
32
]
.
The connection between
H. pylori
and type 2 diabetes was first analyzed in 1989 by Simon
et al
, who found a higher prevalence of
H. pylori
infection in patients with type 2 diabetes than in asymptomatic volunteers
[
33
]
. Furthermore, HbA1c levels were higher in diabetic patients with
H. pylori
infection than those without. Yang
et al
concluded that diabetic patients with
H. pylori
infection had a higher risk of cardiovascular diseases and more severe peripheral arterial stiffness than those without the infection
[
34
]
. Conversely, diabetic patients with
H. pylori
infection exhibited worse symptoms related to the infection
[
35
]
.
More insulin resistance has been reported in
H. pylori
-infected patients across various populations
[
17
]
. This association was confirmed in all but one study included in a systematic review
[
36
]
. Although the link between
H. pylori
infection and type 2 diabetes has been controversial in some studies, the general trend suggests a higher susceptibility to
H. pylori
infection in diabetic patients. Likewise,
H. pylori
infection may promote the development of type 2 diabetes and worse glycemic control and insulin responsiveness, likely due in part to the enhanced inflammation
[
36
]
.
Metabolomic and microbiota changes in H. pylori-infected gastritis
A cohort study by Zang
et al
demonstrated that
H. pylori
infection alters serum metabolites in patients with gastritis
[
2
]
. The levels of specific metabolites were closely associated with the severity of gastritis symptoms and may play a role in promoting the progression of the disease toward GC
[
2
]
. For instance, compared to
H. pylori
-negative counterparts, patients with
H. pylori
-positive gastritis showed elevated caproic acid and arachidonic acid serum levels. Caproic acid, a medium-chain fatty acid, supports inflammation by promoting TH1 and TH17 cell differentiation by activating p38 MAPK signaling
[
37
,
38
]
. Arachidonic acid, a polyunsaturated fatty acid, can be converted into prostaglandin E2, a proinflammatory lipid mediator known to promote tumor progression
[
39
]
.
Conversely, some metabolites, such as indole-3-carboxylic acid and 3-indole-propionic acid, were reduced in individuals with
H. pylori
-positive gastritis. Indole metabolites prevent stress-induced injury in the gastrointestinal tract, enhance the barrier properties of epithelial cells, regulate the expression of proinflammatory pathways (e.g. IL-8), and enhance anti-inflammatory pathways (e.g. IL-10)
[
40
,
41
]
.
Endoscopic biopsy can reveal
H. pylori
infection and gastric mucosal histological features; however, this method is invasive, costly, and often intolerable
[
42
]
. Therefore, a non-invasive approach using blood serological assays is necessary. These assays are based on gastric mucosal gland secretory functions, including levels of pepsinogen I (PGI), pepsinogen II (PGII), and gastrin, as well as the PGI/PGII ratio
[
43
,
44
]
. The PGI/PGII ratio is used because gastric and non-gastric glands secrete PGII. In contrast, the PGI cut-off point alone may not distinguish between CAG and non-CAG
[
45
,
46
]
.
Multiregional clinical studies have confirmed the clinical importance of the combined serum gastric function index test, including Hp-IgG, PG, and G-17 levels, as surrogate biomarkers in determining different topographic locations and assessing the prognosis of gastric mucosal diseases
[
12
]
. Some studies have suggested elevated serum G-17, PGI, and PGII levels in
H. pylori
-infected subjects, especially PGII, and a reduced PGI/PGII ratio
[
12
]
.
Although PGI, PGII, and gastrin levels and the PGI/PGII ratio are biomarkers to assess gastric mucosal status and atrophic changes, they are not definitive diagnostic tools for
H. pylori
infection. Studies such as Yu
et al
and Shan
et al
indicate that while these markers may correlate with changes in the gastric mucosa commonly seen in Hp-induced gastritis, they lack specificity
[
12
,
47
]
. Di Mario
et al
further emphasize that these biomarkers should be interpreted alongside direct diagnostic methods, such as the urea breath test, stool antigen testing, or histological confirmation, to identify
H. pylori
infection accurately
[
48
]
. Additionally, Dondov et al highlight the need for multi-modal approaches that combine these biomarkers with
H. pylori
-specific tests to improve diagnostic accuracy
[
49
]
. Therefore, while the PGI/PGII ratio is informative in monitoring gastric health, it cannot independently confirm the presence of
H. pylori
without complementary testing modalities.
Furthermore, recent studies indicate that
H. pylori
infection may induce alterations in the gastric microbiota and distant microbial communities, including the gut and oral microbiota
[
50
]
. Metabolomic changes, including alterations in fatty acids, caused by
H. pylori
infection have been observed in humans
[
51
]
. Gut microbes influence the levels of circulating metabolites, and serum metabolomics provides a better understanding of the interactions between gut flora, distant organs, and metabolic pathways.
H. pylori
infection induces significant modifications in gut microbiota composition, which may consequently lead to changes in host metabolism, causing genetic and epigenetic alterations
[
2
]
.
Gastric cancer: metabolic alterations
Overview of gastric cancer
GC is a significant global health concern and remains a considerable clinical obstacle due to its high mortality rate. Its etiology is complex, and while the incidence is declining in certain regions, late diagnosis and limited treatment options continue to challenge the clinical realm
[
52
]
. In 2020, there were over 1 million new cases and 769,000 deaths, making GC the fifth most common cancer and the fourth leading cause of cancer-related deaths globally (Fig.
2
)
[
53
]
. The disease is more prevalent in men over 50 than in women, and there is a strong link between non-cardia GC and chronic
H. pylori
infection, which can lead to chronic inflammation and damage to the stomach lining, increasing the likelihood of malignant transformations
[
54
,
55
]
. Other influential factors include alcohol and tobacco use and dietary habits. The decline in GC incidence is attributed to changes in food storage and decreasing rates of
H. pylori
infection. However, an alarming trend of increasing incidence among youth is observed, possibly due to AIG and changes in the stomach’s microbiome
[
56
]
. Geographic patterns are also eye-opening, with Asian countries burdened and Europe and the Americas experiencing low incidences. There are two main histologic subtypes of GC, the intestinal and diffuse types, each with unique epidemiology and etiology
[
57
]

.
Current research has focused on the significance of metabolic reprogramming in tumorigenesis, including GC. Metabolomics has completely changed the field by introducing unique tumor cell metabolic profiles that have entirely revolutionized our diagnostic processes, how we follow up the metastasis and our ability to predict response to therapy
[
58
]
. The Warburg effect, defined by tumors relying on glucose as a significant energy source, is a widely recognized metabolic adaptation. Integrating omics technologies, including metabolomics, will fundamentally advance our understanding of cancer biology and impact the field regarding potential therapeutic advancements and individualized treatment
[
58
,
59
]
.
Metabolic pathways implicated in gastric cancer development
Various metabolic pathways are involved in the development of GC
[
60
]
. In the face of glucose deficiency, enhanced glycolysis and resultantly elevated lactate, decreased glucose, and elevated TCA cycle intermediates have been observed. Serine, glycine, and glutamine levels are increased in tissue and gastric fluid, while levels are reduced in blood, and these changes impact cell proliferation and immune modulation. The levels of lipid metabolism-related analytes indicate increased lipogenesis and fatty acid oxidation. Elevating these pathways is required for tumor membrane synthesis and signaling
[
58
]
. Early-stage GC shows increased glucose uptake and glycolysis, while advanced stages exhibit elevated glutaminolysis and lipid metabolism
[
58
]
. Nucleotide metabolism, aside, indicates changes in nucleotide degradation with elevations in uric acid, a pyrimidine degradation product, supportive of cell proliferation, and upregulation of UHMK1 in GC promotes cancer progression primarily by enhancing
de novo
purine synthesis
[
58
]
. Indicators of metabolic shifts driven by the Warburg effect, amino acid transporters, and
de novo
pathways are observed and underlie the oncogenic transformations and progression that occur in the development of GC
[
58
]
.
Untargeted metabolomics in gastric cancer
GC exhibits distinct metabolic features, with significant changes in glucose, amino acid, lipid, and nucleotide metabolism
[
58
]
. Utilizing untargeted metabolomics to examine serum metabolites in the context of GC has been pivotal for advancements in the field. Many metabolic pathways have been found in GC, of which glutamine metabolism, a central part of the metabolic pathways for producing cell energy and a reductive equivalent, has been identified as necessary in the context of GC. Other metabolic pathways, including N-glycan biosynthesis, have also been reported to be important in driving cancer cell growth, invasion, and metastasis, while other metabolic pathways, such as linoleic acid metabolism, play an essential role in mediating the mobility of cancer cells. Amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism, which generates proteins and lipids, is critical in tumors. Pantothenate and CoA biosynthesis are necessary for their role in modulating immune cells at the level of anti-tumor immunity. Viewing the metabolic changes of GC as a whole and again seeing how these processes might each be alone is crucial
[
61
]
. For example, high levels of citrate and succinate have been linked to chemotherapy resistance in GC; we have a vast array of new targets for future studies to try to stop or reverse the course of this severe disease
[
62
]
.
Identification of biomarkers and metabolic signatures in gastric cancer
Metabolic changes that occur with GC progress can illuminate the pertinent molecular pathways that may be involved in promoting tumor progression and metastasis. Critical changes observed within amino acid metabolism such as Asp, Arg, and Gly, among others, suggest a role in nucleoside synthesis and in supporting the rapid proliferation of tumor cells. In addition, differences in carnitine/acylcarnitine metabolism, represented by five metabolites (C2, C3DC, C4-OH, C5, and C18:1), demonstrate the metabolic shifts that can promote the process of fatty acid oxidation in GC
[
63
]
.
In addition to conventional diagnostic tests like CEA, CA19-9, and CA72-4, novel molecular methods, such as detecting CEA mRNA via RT-PCR and OSNA technology, aid in early detection and clinical follow-up of GC recurrence and metastasis
[
64
]
. Proteomic studies have been conducted to identify specific proteins and study the mechanisms by which GC develops. For example, it was observed through proteomic profiling that the protein levels of a few proteins, such as alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), and others, are higher in GC and are associated with the stage and prognosis of GC
[
65
]
. Also, metabolomic biomarkers such as urinary sarcosine and spermine have shown promise for non-invasive screening and early detection of GC
[
66
]
. Genomic studies have found genetic mutations and polymorphisms that could be used to identify biological markers that could improve personalized treatment strategies for GC. Genomic sequencing has identified genetic variants in TP53, HER2, CDH1, and other genes, and the genetic alterations associated with these genes provide insight into potential therapeutic targets and indications of prognosis
[
67
]
.
Metabolomic research has identified distinct metabolic profiles linked to GC progression, though there is an excellent opportunity for biomarker identification in this context. For example, changes in metabolites, such as lactate, glutamine, and citrate, are associated with GC tumor growth and aggressiveness. By combining multi-omics tools, the hope is to move beyond traditional markers and expand our understanding of potential biomarker utility to help in the diagnostic processes and personalized treatment of GC
[
68
]
.
Comparative analysis
In modern clinical practice, gastroscopy, biopsy, and pathological examination are the primary methods to diagnose CG
[
69
]
. Biochemical indicators derived from gastric juice and blood, such as gastrin and pepsinogen, are limited because of their invasive nature and high cost
[
70
]
. To address these challenges, there is a growing focus on developing non-invasive biomarkers for early detection of CG
[
69
]
. Metabolomic analysis, particularly the untargeted metabolomic technique, is a promising method that expedites the discovery of new tumor biomarkers and offers opportunities to clarify complex metabolic changes associated with tumors
[
5
,
71
]
.
Recent observational studies have highlighted notable similarities in the metabolic profiles of GC and CG, with lipid metabolites such as linoleamide, N-hydroxy arachidonoyl amine, and hexadecasphinganine being upregulated in both conditions, indicating their potential as biomarkers for GC and CG (as shown in Table
2
)
[
5
]
. While GC and CG share oxidative stress and inflammation markers, specific metabolic markers can differentiate benign gastritis from cancer. Li, Dehong
et al
demonstrated that five metabolites, including adipylcarnitine and arginine, were significantly elevated in GC patients compared to those with atrophic or superficial gastritis, offering high specificity and sensitivity for GC diagnosis
[
71
]
. Similarly, Shi, Lin-Yang
et al
found that plasma arginine levels were significantly higher in GC patients, potentially linked to high ASS1 expression
[
72
]
. Yu, Lin
et al
reported that serum levels of total cholesterol, HDL-C, and ApoA1 were lower in GC patients compared to healthy subjects and CG patients, suggesting a connection between these decreases and the progression from CG to GC
[
5
]
. Corona, Giuseppe
et al
identified Sphingomyelin SM(OH)22:1 and acetylcarnitine as significant risk markers for GC
[
73
]

Data processing, analysis, and interpretation
The methodological pipeline in untargeted metabolomics begins with processing spectral data to generate sample metabolic information, referred to as metabolic features. Data processing in untargeted metabolomics includes several steps: spectral pre-processing, feature detection, spectral alignment, and feature normalization
[
91
]
.
Pre-processing improves signal quality, while feature detection identifies and quantifies metabolites using peak-based methods. Spectral alignment corrects shifts in peak positions to ensure consistency across samples, and feature normalization removes systematic biases
[
92
]
. Spectral deconvolution resolves overlapping peaks. Alignment methods like correlation optimized warping (COW) and dynamic time warping (DTW) are employed, and peak-based alignment methods such as extracted ion chromatogram mass spectrometry (XCMS) are commonly used in mass spectrometry studies
[
93
,
94
]
.
During data analysis, metabolite features are quantitatively assessed, including spectral peak areas, metabolite concentrations, and spectral bin areas. Univariate methods like
t
-tests and ANOVA analyze features independently but may overlook interactions and confounding factors
[
95
,
96
]
. Multivariate approaches, such as unsupervised methods like principal component analysis (PCA) and supervised techniques like partial least squares (PLS) and support vector machines (SVMs), simultaneously analyze all features to detect patterns and construct predictive models
[
91
,
95
,
96
]
. Multiway methods, such as parallel factor analysis (PARAFAC) and multivariate curve resolution (MCR), handle multidimensional data from longitudinal studies.
Biomarker discovery utilizes supervised models to identify clinically relevant biomarkers, emphasizing rigorous performance assessment and model validation
[
91
]
. Interpretation aims to correlate identified patterns with physiological or pathological states, uncover potential biomarkers, and elucidate metabolic pathways. Integrating metabolomic data with other omics datasets, such as genomics or transcriptomics, enhances biological insights, providing a more profound understanding of disease mechanisms, drug responses, and other biological processes
[

Challenges and limitations
The field of metabolomics faces several challenges and limitations, starting with sample collection. Ensuring sample integrity and consistency is critical, as biological samples are susceptible to environmental changes, time delays, and handling procedures
[
97
]
. Any variation can introduce significant biases, making reproducibility a primary concern. During data analysis, metabolomic data’s vast and complex nature requires sophisticated bioinformatics tools and high computational power. Peak overlap, noise, and alignment errors in spectral data can impede accurate feature detection and quantification. Moreover, interpreting metabolic profiles is often complicated by integrating data from different omics layers and understanding the biological relevance of detected metabolites. The need for standardized protocols and comprehensive metabolite databases further hampers identifying and interpreting metabolic features, making it challenging to draw definitive biological conclusions
[
97
]
.
Suggestions
Several improvements can be made in study design and methodology to overcome these challenges. Standardizing sample collection protocols across labs and studies can minimize variability and enhance reproducibility. This includes defining precise handling, storage conditions, and time frames for processing samples. Advancements in bioinformatics tools that enhance data processing and analysis are also crucial. Developing more robust algorithms for peak detection, spectral alignment, and feature quantification can improve the accuracy and reliability of metabolomic data. Incorporating quality control measures and using internal standards can aid in normalizing data and correcting systematic biases. Enhancing metabolite databases with more comprehensive and accurate reference spectra will facilitate better identification and interpretation of metabolites. Last, fostering interdisciplinary collaboration among biologists, chemists, and data scientists can lead to more integrated and holistic approaches in metabolomics, ultimately improving the understanding and application of metabolic profiles in various biological contexts.

Conclusion
CG, particularly when associated with
H. pylori
infection, is not just a common gastrointestinal condition but a significant precursor to more severe diseases, including GC. The prolonged inflammation of the gastric mucosa in CG produces reactive oxygen species, inducing DNA damage that can initiate a cascade of pathological events culminating in malignancy. The dual nature of atrophic gastritis, both in its autoimmune and
H. pylori
-associated forms, underscores the complexity of this condition and its potential to progress to GC, especially in the presence of specific risk factors like smoking and dietary influences. Given the global burden of GC, with its high mortality rate and varied prognosis depending on the stage at diagnosis, early detection and precise intervention are crucial. Advancements in metabolomic technology provide a promising pathway for identifying biomarkers that could revolutionize the early diagnosis of GC. Metabolomics allows for the comprehensive profiling of metabolites involved in critical metabolic pathways, offering insights into the biochemical changes accompanying the progression from CG to GC. This approach enhances our understanding of the disease’s pathophysiology and opens new avenues for non-invasive diagnostic tools and personalized treatment strategies. Metabolomics’ role in GC is particularly significant as it highlights the distinct metabolic alterations that occur as the disease progresses. Identifying key metabolites linked to glucose, lipid, and amino acid metabolism can serve as potential biomarkers for early detection, aiding in differentiating benign gastritis from malignant transformations. Integrating metabolomic data with traditional diagnostic methods could improve the accuracy of GC staging and prognosis, ultimately leading to better clinical outcomes. However, translating these findings into clinical practice requires overcoming several challenges, including validating biomarkers in diverse populations and standardizing metabolomic methodologies. The future of GC management lies in the successful implementation of metabolomic profiling as a routine diagnostic tool, which could enable earlier intervention and more effective treatment, reducing the global burden of this deadly disease.
In conclusion, CG, particularly in its chronic and atrophic forms, represents a critical target for early intervention to prevent the development of GC. The application of metabolomic technology in this context offers a powerful tool for early diagnosis and personalized medicine. Future research should focus on validating these biomarkers and integrating metabolomic data into clinical workflows, ensuring that the advances in our understanding of CG and GC translate into tangible improvements in patient care. By embracing these innovations, we can move toward a future where GC is detected and treated earlier, ultimately improving patients’ survival rates and quality of life worldwide.
