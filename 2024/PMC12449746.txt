Introduction
Capillary electrophoresis (CE) has proven over the last decades to be a valuable technique for the separation of proteins, peptides, antibodies, and small molecules [
1
,
2
,
3
]. Coupling CE with mass spectrometry (MS) offers an efficient separation with sensitive detection; however, the coupling requires special attention. The widely applied, robust, and easy‐to‐handle coaxial sheath liquid (SL) interface (Agilent Technologies) establishes the electric contact by mixing the CE effluent with a pumped SL in the low µL/min range [
4
]. The high SL flow rates compared to the CE effluent lead to limited sensitivity [
5
]. To increase sensitivity, several approaches have been developed to reduce the SL flow rates being summarized as nano‐SL interfaces [
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
].
A common approach when using nano‐SL interfaces is to minimize dilution by using self‐supplied systems. Instead of diluting the CE effluent with a fixed volume of SL, the electrospray (ES) takes as much as it needs. One example of such a self‐supplied interface is the recently published nanoCEasy [
9
] interface, which has proven to be very sensitive with easy handling. Although the interface operates with low ES flow rates, it can be used for applications ranging from separation conditions with no or low electroosmotic flow (EOF) [
10
,
11
,
12
] over applying a small pressure for capillary isoelectric focusing (CIEF) systems [
13
] to high EOF separations [
14
,
15
] where a considerable amount of electrolyte (some hundreds nL/min) leaves the separation capillary. One of the main characteristics of the interface is the introduction of the SL by a second capillary, with the majority of the pumped SL being flushed backward out of the emitter [
6
,
16
]. However, this makes it difficult to estimate the amount of liquid sprayed into the MS and therefore hard to estimate sample dilution. Due to the concentration dependency of the ES process, analyte dilution is expected to lower signal intensity.
In general, nanoESI interfaces exhibit typically a flow rate in the range of 20–800 nL/min depending on the applied voltage, the used liquid, and the emitter tip diameter [
5
,
17
,
18
]. In the past, several approaches to calculate the flow rate of the nano ES were used. It is possible to calculate flow rates by weighting the devices at the start and the end of the measurement [
17
], by timing the meniscus movement in the emitter [
19
], or by determining the SL consumption from a reservoir [
5
]. Another more accurate way would be to calculate the SL flow by comparing it to a known flow from the CE. This known flow can be an electrically generated flow similar to the EOF, as shown by Chen et al. [
18
]. They did an estimation of the flow rate for the low‐flow CE/ESI MS Interface using two different analytes in the SL and CE capillary and comparing the intensity of the two signals.
Here, we present for the first time a method to determine accurate flow rates for nano‐SL interfaces using isotopically labeled standards. Therefore, a well‐defined pressure‐driven flow is supplied by the CE system, whereas SL is provided via a syringe pump in an open, self‐supplying system. Both liquids have the same composition but are spiked with different isotopically labeled standards to guarantee that ionization efficiency is identical for both analytes. The method allows for the calculation of flow rates for the nanoCEasy interface and other (nanoflow) SL interfaces depending on ES—voltage, emitter size, and geometry. Observed effects are transferred to and compared with CE–MS data for the separation of a model sample of three amino acids at different ES voltages.
2
Materials and methods
Propan‐2‐ol (IPA), methanol (LC–MS grade), and formic acid (FA; ≥98%) were obtained from Roth GmbH & Co. KG (Karlsruhe, Germany). Deuterated atrazine (d5‐atrazine, 100 µg/mL in acetonitrile) was purchased from Restek GmbH (Bad Homburg, Germany). Atrazine (analytical standard), hydrofluoric acid (40% (v/v)), sodium hydroxide, calcium carbonate, and hydrochloric acid (37%) were purchased from Merck KGaA (Darmstadt, Germany). The amino acids glutamic acid, phenylalanine, and serine were obtained from Fluka Biochemika (Buchs, Switzerland). For all solutions, ultrapure water (UPW, SG Ultra Clear UV from Siemens Water Technologies, USA) was used.
2.1
Emitter Characterization
Glass emitters with several orifice sizes (10, 15, 20, 30, and 40 µm) were purchased from Biomedical Instruments (Zoellnitz, Germany). To evaluate the emitter orifice, they were examined with the POLYVAR MET microscope (Reichert‐Jung, Wien, Austria) and a digital camera (ORCA‐Flash 4.0,
C11440
‐42U30, Hamamatsu Photonics K.K., Hamamatsu, Japan). The emitters were placed in an in‐house‐built sample holder formed from a massive aluminum block to keep it standing. To determine the emitter geometry, the spray angle, and the capillary positioning, the emitters were examined with a digital microscope (Dino‐Lite, Almere, The Netherlands).
2.2
Capillary Electrophoresis
Fused silica capillaries (separation capillary: 50 µm inner diameter [id], 365 µm outer diameter [od]; SL capillary: 100 µm id, 240 µm id) were obtained from Polymicro Technologies (Phoenix, AZ, USA). An CE 7100 A (Agilent Technologies, Waldbronn, Germany) was used for all experiments. Fused silica capillaries (60 cm, id 50 µm, od 365 µm) were used for the flow rate determination and the measurement of the amino acids. The capillaries were etched at one end using hydrofluoric acid to reduce the od [
9
,
20
]. New capillaries were initially conditioned as follows: 10 min methanol (950 mbar), 15 min 1 M sodium hydroxide solution (950 mbar), 10 min UPW (950 mbar), and 15 min background electrolyte (BGE) (950 mbar). The capillary was additionally rinsed with fresh BGE each morning for 15 min (950 mbar).
For flow rate experiments, atrazine was added to the SL and the solution in the CE capillary. To avoid mixing effects, the same solution was used in both capillaries, consisting of IPA: UPW 1:1 with 0.5% FA. SL was supplemented with 0.05 ppm of atrazine and the CE solution was spiked with 0.1 ppm of d5‐atrazine. No separation voltage was applied. A flow rate was imitated using 30 or 50 mbar internal CE pressure depending on the emitter orifice size. This equals a CE flow rate of 14 or 23 nL/min, respectively. From each emitter size, three emitters were measured three times. ES voltage was increased in 200 V steps. Close to the onset, it can happen that spray is not stable enough to execute all nine measurements.
Amino acid separation was done using 0.2 M FA as BGE. Before each measurement, the capillary was conditioned as follows: 60 s 1 M sodium hydroxide solution (950 mbar), 60 s 1 M hydrochloric acid (950 mbar), 80 s BGE (950 mbar), 60 s wait, 60 s 10 kV using BGE, 60 s 20 kV using BGE, and 60 s 30 kV using BGE. The sample containing all three amino acids was injected hydrodynamically using 50 mbar for 9 s. The separation was executed using 30 kV. The amino acid concentration was 0.1 ppm in BGE.
2.3
Mass Spectrometer
The CE was coupled to a maXis or compact (Bruker Daltonics GmbH & Co. KG, Bremen, Germany) using the nanoCEasy interface previously published by our group [
9
]. The emitter tip was placed 3 mm in front of the MS, and the spray voltage was ramped between 1000 and 2000 V depending on the used emitter. Inside the emitter, the capillary was placed as close as possible to the emitter tip, which differs for different emitter orifice sizes/emitter batches. In the case of the 30 µm emitter of Batch 3, the distance was between 2.2 and 2.4 mm. A dry gas flow of 3 L/min and a dry temperature of 150°C were used for all experiments.
2.4
Data Evaluation
All TOF data were evaluated using DataAnalysis 4.3 from Brucker. Data visualization was done using JMP Pro 16.1.0.

Results and discussion
3.1
Flow Rate Calculation and Applicability of the System

When using an open, self‐supplying system for CE–MS coupling, the exact amount of SL used to dilute the analytes migrating from the CE capillary is unknown. In the case of the nanoCEasy (Figure

) interface, a fixed amount of SL is supplied by the syringe pump, but the supplied SL is only partially sprayed into the MS while a majority of the liquid is flushed backward, leaving the open emitter at the backend (Figure

3.2
Evaluation of ES Voltage on Flow Rate and Intensity
Initial experiments on the influence of the ES voltage on the signal intensity and flow rates were performed using the 30 µm emitter (Figure
1C
). A change in the signal intensity of both the atrazine and d5‐atrazine is observed when the ES voltage is varied. D5‐atrazine that is supplied by the CE has the highest signal intensity for 1400 V and the lowest signal intensity for 2000 V, whereas for atrazine, the trend is reversed. Using the previously described equations (Equations
(1)
,
(2)
,
(3)
), lower flow rates were calculated at lower ES voltages and higher flow rates at higher ES voltages. This relation is due to the force pulling on the fluid increasing when the applied voltage increases [
21
]. When the 30 µm emitter is operated at 1400 V, a flow rate of 56 nL/min with a standard error of 3 nL/min (
n
= 9, 3 emitters × 3 repeats) is determined. When higher ES voltages are applied, the flow rate is increasing; for example, at 2000 V, the flow rate is 269 nL/min with a standard error of 25 nL/min. Looking individually at the three emitters at 1600 V, flow rates were determined to be 171 ± 60 nL/min, 157 ± 50 nL/min, and 84 ± 2 nL/min (
n
= 3, standard error), respectively, with an average flow rate of 137 ± 46 nL/min. There are some variations for different emitters and within a dataset of one emitter (Figure
S3
). Nevertheless, there is a clear correlation between total flow rate and ES voltage.
We previously estimated the highest possible CE flow for the nanoCEasy for different ES voltages using a 30 µm emitter. The flow rate of an aqueous solution pressurized through the CE capillary was increased, and the point was determined where the spray collapsed due to the high water content [
9
]. Here, values were postulated in the range of 20–130 nL/min for 1400 and 1700 V, respectively. These values are lower compared to flow rates determined here, which is to be expected as the spray can already collapse at these voltages when only small parts of the organic SL are present.

3.4
Emitter Orifice Size
The id of the emitter tip opening (orifice size) is a crucial parameter in CE–nanoESI–MS interfaces because it influences stability, robustness, lifetime (clogging), and potentially also the flow rate and, thus, the sensitivity of CE–MS. To estimate the influence, five orifice sizes (10, 15, 20, 30, and 40 µm) of Batch 3 were measured nine times (three emitters per orifice size with three repeats each). In general, for all emitter orifice sizes, the flow rate increases when increasing the ES voltage (Figure
3A
).

For ES voltages >1600 V, larger emitter orifices lead to higher flow rates. As an example, a 40 µm emitter has a flow rate of 375 nL/min when 2000 V is applied, whereas a 10 µm emitter only has a flow rate of 85 nL/min. Interestingly, if ES voltages of 1600 V or lower are applied, the total flow rate no longer correlates with the emitter orifice size. In the lower ES voltage area, it is therefore unimportant what emitter is used regarding sample dilution. All emitters can only be operated until a certain ES voltage. Below this onset voltage, no signal was measured by the MS. For the 30 µm emitter and SL used, it was observed that it cannot be operated below 1400 V, resulting in a flow rate of 56 nL/min. Compared to the 30 µm emitter, a 10 µm emitter has a 400 V lower onset value (1000 V), which leads to flow rates as low as 27 nL/min. A decrease in the total flow rate and therefore a decrease in sample dilution should lead to higher signal intensities. Höcker et al. already briefly described a correlation between ES voltage and signal intensity [
5
]. However, they only stated that the lowest ES voltage resulted in the highest signal intensity, and no further evaluation was done. As illustrated in Figure
3B
, signal intensities for d5‐atrazine are indeed increasing for each emitter size when the ES voltage is reduced. For the 30 µm emitter, the signal intensity at 1400 V is 2.3 times higher than at 2000 V. Even though a clear increase in signal intensity is observed, this increase is lower than expected based on the calculated d5‐atrazine concentration in the emitter, which is 4.7 times higher at 1400 V than at 2000 V. This value is based on the ratio of flow
(CE)
to flow
(total)
. This difference leads to the conclusion that the signal intensity is not only influenced by the total flow rate (dilution) but also by other factors. One possible explanation is that, especially at lower flow rates, losses might occur due to liquid drains via the surface of the emitter (surface wetting). Hydrophobic surfaces show better performance compared to other emitters when used for nanoESI measurements [
23
]. To prevent wetting of the surface, hydrophobic surfaces are used for the edges of microfluidic chips [
24
]. A second possible reason could be the unevenness of the emitter leading potentially to asymmetric spray and thus changing ES efficiency. Indeed, slight unevenness is visible under the microscope (Figure
S6
). The orifice mean diameter is rather accurate (deviations of 1–2 µm); however, slight faults are visible and might be more problematic at lower flow rates. Furthermore, the signal intensity does not increase with a smaller emitter orifice, as seen in Figure
3B
. On the basis of the experienced dilution, smaller emitter orifice sizes should have a higher signal intensity, but in the here described experiments, no clear correlation between orifice size and signal intensity was measured. In the literature, there are different results for the size–intensity relation as well. Early works of the Dovichi group [
7
] reported that a 2 µm emitter results in higher signal intensities than a 5 or 10 µm emitter. Several years later, the same group observed the reverse effect: An emitter with a 15 µm orifice showed an eight times higher signal intensity than an 8 µm emitter [
25
]. Because smaller emitters tend to clog more often, larger emitters should be preferred due to them being less problematic in handling, making the 30 µm emitter a good universal choice.
3.5
Visualizing the ES
Another possible influence on the signal intensities and therefore the total flow rate can be changes in the spray, like the spray angle or changes in the spray modus. In the ES ionization processes, the desired spray modus is the Taylor cone; however, several other spray modes are possible [
26
].
To observe the spray of the nanoCEasy interface, a laser was installed, and the flow rate determination was repeated for a 30 µm emitter of Batch 3. As Figure
4
illustrates, the same behavior for the flow rate was observed as previously described (Figure
1C
). Generally, an increase in the spray angle is observed when the voltage is increased. In rare cases, the spray showed jet behavior. For the here recorded data, only measurements showing the same optical behavior as illustrated by Figure
4
were taken into consideration. Here, the signal intensity of a 30 µm emitter increases by a factor of 2.8 when changing the ES voltage from 2000 to 1400 V, whereas the calculated d5‐atrazine concentration in the emitter is 3.9 times higher at 1400 V than at 2000 V. Compared to the earlier measurements (Factors 2.3 and 4.7, respectively), the difference between the increase in signal intensity and the calculated concentration becomes smaller. This leads to the conclusion that spray behavior may impact the signal intensity.

3.6
Applicability of the Standard System to the Separation of Amino Acids
To evaluate the results of the infusion experiment, a CE–MS analysis was performed. The major difference to the atrazine experiments is the use of an aqueous BGE for the separation, possibly changing the composition and mixture in the emitter tip. In the past, the remaining potential of the CE at the emitter tip was discussed [
5
]. Short, when CE voltage is applied, a portion of this potential remains at the emitter tip due to the resistance of the SL. This impacts the real voltage that is available for spray formation and differs from the set ES voltage at the MS. Thus, the set ES voltages are not identical to the real voltages at the tip and cannot be compared to the above‐presented results in terms of the given numbers; however, tendencies should be similar.
A separation system of three amino acids was measured at four ES voltages. The amino acids were separated in a bare fused silica capillary under acidic conditions, leading to low EOF and therefore low flow from the CE capillary. Figure
5A
shows one exemplary electropherogram for each ES voltage. For every amino acid, a decrease in intensity with increased voltages is measured. Hereby, no increase in peak width is detected. For all three amino acids, there is an increase in intensity by a factor of up to 3 when 2000 V is compared with 1400 V (Figure
5B
). This is in accordance with the previously observed factor of 2.3 for the intensity values for d5‐atrazine. Thus, the best settings for such low EOF systems using the 30 µm emitter are in the 1400–1600 V range leading to a stable, reliable, robust, and sensitive spray.
FIGURE 5
Amino acid separation using 60 cm fused silica capillary and 0.2 M FA BGE. SL: 50:50 UPW: IPA + 0.2%FA; 30 µm emitter. (A) amino acid separation using different electrospray voltages; (B) change in intensity normalized to the 2000 V intensity. ES, electrospray.
When applying a high EOF system, a large amount of aqueous solution leaves the CE capillary and reaches the emitter. In this case, it is important to have a higher total flow than CE flow to prevent the accumulation of analytes in the emitter tip and keep sufficient organic solvent in the SL for best ionization efficiency. Therefore, higher ES voltages are required. If the applied voltage is not high enough, the spray can collapse or peak widths can increase (Figure
S7
). For example, RNase had a peak width of 0.06 min at 2000 V, which increased to 0.41 min using 1700 V. Although broader peaks were obtained for the lower ES voltage, a higher signal intensity could be obtained by applying 1700 V compared to 2000 V. However, for best CE separation, high voltages (in this example, 2000 V) are mandatory for such high EOF systems.
4
Conclusion
In this work, we present a novel approach to determining flow rates using isotopically labeled standards for self‐supplied nanoESI interfaces. The concept is demonstrated using the nanoCEasy interface that can be used to couple CE to MS. The interface combines nanoESI sensitivity with valving functionality and enables the use of various ES voltages and emitter orifice sizes.
The total flow rate of the system was characterized by a pressurized, well‐determined flow rate in the CE capillary spiked with d5‐atrazine and non‐deuterated atrazine in the SL and the subsequent determination of the ratio in the ESI‐MS. The main influence on the flow rate of the nanoCEasy interface is the applied ES voltage. The flow rate increases when a higher ES voltage is applied for all emitter sizes. For voltages below 1600 V, the flow rate is independent of the emitter size. For each emitter size, lower voltages and therefore lower flow rates lead to higher CE signal intensities for both the infusion study using the isotopically labeled standards as well as for the CE‐MS analysis of amino acids. In both cases, the signal intensity can be improved by a factor of 3 when applying lower ES voltages.
The results show that the main influence on the signal intensity is the applied ES voltage, whereas other effects like the emitter tip geometry of different batches and the emitter orifice size only play a secondary role. Therefore, we propose for most applications the use of emitters with orifice sizes of around 30 µm to prevent clogging. Furthermore, we suggest the visualization of the spray to allow the identification of stable and reproducible spray conditions. For low EOF systems, we suggest using a low ES voltage to guarantee the best possible sensitivity, whereas when using higher ES voltages, the impact of sample dilution gets smaller, resulting in a higher robustness regarding intensity. Higher ES voltages are also required with high EOF separations to avoid the accumulation of aqueous solution inside the emitter, otherwise leading to inefficient separation.
