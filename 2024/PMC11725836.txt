The human gastrointestinal microbiome plays a critical role in metabolism, immune system function, and nutrient acquisition, with microbiome composition correlating with various conditions including Alzheimer’s, Crohn’s, diabetes, and obesity (
1
–
4
). Altering the gastrointestinal microbiome, such as through a fecal transplant, has been considered as potential treatments for recurring infections (
5
) and for inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD) (
6
). The gut microbiome is mainly composed of bacteria in the Bacillota (formerly Firmicutes) and Bacteroidota phyla (
7
). These “healthy” gut bacteria can outcompete pathogens for carbon sources and produce compounds, such as lactate and short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), as byproducts of their anaerobic metabolism (
8
,
9
).
Eubacterium
within the Bacillota phylum is the second-most abundant genus in the human gut and involved in SCFA production as well as in the transformation of cholesterol, methylated amines, and bile acids (
10
). The prevalence of
Eubacterium limosum
has been found to correlate with longevity, as it is 10-fold higher in people who live over the age of 100 (
11
), and have nonindustrial lifestyles (
12
). It is a strictly anaerobic bacterium that was isolated from human feces and produces SCFAs (
13
,
14
).
E. limosum
and the SCFAs it produces, specifically butyrate, have been shown experimentally to ameliorate colitis and attenuate colonic inflammation (
15
).
E. limosum
grows on D/L-lactate to produce the SCFAs acetate and butyrate (
16
) but the nature of the pathways involved and how they are regulated have not been determined. Interestingly, the
E. limosum
genome encodes a putative electron bifurcating lactate dehydrogenase–EtfAB complex (LCT,
SI Appendix, Fig. S1
), which couples lactate oxidation to the reduction of both NAD
+
and the iron-sulfur redox protein ferredoxin (Fd), rather than a conventional NAD
+
-dependent lactate dehydrogenase. LCT was identified in
Acetobacterium woodii
, a nongut microbe from the same Eubacteriaceae family, that converts lactate in the presence of CO
2
to acetate but butyrate is not produced (
17
). Both
E. limosum
and
A. woodii
contain the Wood–Ljungdahl pathway, which converts CO
2
to acetyl-CoA, and this is used by
A. woodii
during lactate conversion to acetate. Unlike
A. woodii,
however,
E. limosum
produces butyrate as well as acetate from lactate and how this is achieved is unknown.
Lactate is present in the gut of healthy adults at low mM concentrations due to microbial utilization and conversion into SCFAs (
18
) although patients with ulcerative colitis and other gut-related disorders can accumulate high concentrations of lactate (>90 mM) leading to dysbiosis (
18
,
19
). The production and utilization of lactate is an important part of nutrient acquisition in the human gut environment. Lactate producers in the gut, such as
Lactobacillus
and
Bifidobacterium,
ferment carbohydrates to both L- and D-lactate by the reduction of pyruvate produced through glycolysis (
20
), by the pentose phosphate pathway (
21
) or by the bifid shunt pathway (
22
). Many lactate producers, especially different
Lactobacillus
species, are often found in probiotics. Both L- and D-lactate are taken up and metabolized by lactate-utilizing microorganisms to produce SCFAs, such as acetate, propionate, and butyrate. Many lactate-utilizing bacteria have a lactate racemase to satisfy the stereospecificity of lactate-metabolizing enzymes (
23
). Propionate can be produced from lactate by the acrylate pathway (
24
), acetate can be produced through dissimilatory sulfate reduction (
25
) and butyrate is generated mainly through the butyryl-CoA: acetate-CoA transferase pathway (
26
). SCFAs are absorbed into the intestinal epithelium and play important roles in the anti-inflammatory immune response, promoting intestinal epithelium integrity, supporting liver and kidney function and modulating neurological function (
27
). The regulation of lactate metabolism in the gut and cross-feeding between gut microbiota is therefore an important aspect of human health.
Herein we investigated the metabolism of lactate by
E. limosum
and uncovered the unexpected involvement of the metal tungsten (W). W and molybdenum (Mo) are both group six metals that are utilized at the catalytic sites of enzymes while bound to an organic pyranopterin cofactor. While molybdoenzymes are found in virtually all life forms and consist of three well-studied families (
28
), W has been traditionally thought of as a Mo-antagonist and not to have a significant biological role. However, the tungsten oxidoreductase (WOR) family, which is phylogenetically distinct from the Mo-enzyme families, is surprisingly widespread in the microbial world. WORs are particularly abundant in human gut microbes, which are represented in 24 different WOR clades, two of which are found in
E. limosum
(
29
). We previously showed that
E. limosum
cells grown on glucose readily incorporate W but not Mo and when W was present in the growth medium, added propionaldehyde was detoxified by the organism to produce the SFCA propionate rather than propanol (
29
).
E. limosum
contains two WORs encoded in its genome, WOR1 (clade 87) and WOR2 (clade 81), as well as the tungstate-specific transporter, TupABC (
29
). The genome also encodes two putative molybdoenzymes, a DMSOR family member, formate dehydrogenase (FDH), and a member of the xanthine oxidase family of unknown function, and a molybdate transporter (ModABC). From sequence analysis, WOR1 is pentameric (Wor1ABCSL) while WOR2 is a dimer (Wor2SL) with predicted cofactor contents shown in
Fig. 1
. Like other characterized dimeric WORs (
30
,
31
), WOR2 is thought to oxidize an aldehyde of some type and to reduce the redox protein Fd. In contrast, based on the characterized pentameric WOR from the nongut microbe
Acetomicrobium mobile
(
29
), WOR1 is predicted to be an electron bifurcating enzyme that oxidizes an aldehyde and simultaneously reduces NAD
+
and Fd thereby coupling exergonic and endergonic reactions, respectively (
Fig. 1
). Cytoplasmic extracts of glucose-grown
E. limosum
have been shown to oxidize a range of both aliphatic and aromatic aldehydes (
29
), presumably catalyzed by one or both of its WORs.

In this study, the goal was to determine how
E. limosum
generates SCFAs from lactate by elucidating the pathways and major enzymes involved and how they are regulated. To our surprise we found that growth on lactate increases transcription of genes encoding electron bifurcating WOR1, but not WOR2, by two different W-based mechanisms at the level of both transcription and translation. We show that W plays an important role in lactate catabolism and SCFA production by
E. limosum.
Results
Tungsten Is Required for Optimal
E. limosum
Growth on Lactate but Not Glucose.
The standard growth medium for
E. limosum
contains 100 nM added W (as tungstate) and 100 nM added Mo (as molybdate). Cytoplasmic extracts of cells grown in the standard glucose medium contained 1.4 ± 0.2 µmol W/g protein whereas those of lactate-grown cells contained 6.0 ± 0.1 µmol W/g protein. In contrast, cellular Mo concentrations were only 0.1 ± 0.05 µmol Mo/g protein whether grown on glucose or lactate. Accordingly, as shown in
Fig. 2
A
, growth of
E. limosum
on glucose was unaffected by the absence of added W but growth on lactate was affected resulting in a longer lag phase (
Fig. 2
A
). Clearly, lactate metabolism has an increased need for W compared to glucose metabolism. In the absence of added W, the “-W” condition, the medium still contains ~6 nM W from the W that contaminates other media chemicals, e.g., 60 mM lactate (as used in growth;
Fig. 2
D
) provides ~2 nM W, hence W is available even though it was not added. Increasing added W to 1 µM or 10 µM did not significantly affect growth on lactate (or glucose) indicating that 100 nM is near saturation (
32
). These data suggest that W is metabolically connected to lactate metabolism in the gut.

Fractionation of the cytoplasmic extract of glucose-grown cells by anion exchange chromatography (QHP) and analysis by ICP-MS revealed a single major W peak (
Fig. 2
B

32

Fig. 2
B

Dataset S1
). Mo was barely detectable in the W-containing QHP fractions, and the Mo profile did not change significantly when comparing the two cell types (
Fig. 2
B
).
In terms of SCFA production, there were significant differences between growth on glucose and lactate. On glucose, acetate is the main SCFA product produced with fivefold less butyrate produced but on lactate equimolar amounts of acetate and butyrate were generated. For example, during growth on 30 mM glucose,
E. limosum
produced approximately 24 mM acetate and 4 mM butyrate after 48 h of growth (
Fig. 2
C

Fig. 2

Insights Into Lactate Metabolism from Transcriptional Analyses.
To provide insights into what role W has in the metabolism of lactate, we compared the transcriptomes of
E. limosum
grown on lactate and glucose in the standard medium harvested during mid-log phase (OD
600
~0.3;
Fig. 2
A
). Of the 4,167 genes in the genome, 128 are up-regulated and 43 are down-regulated ≥threefold during growth on lactate compared to glucose (
SI Appendix, Table S1
and
Dataset S2
; the complete genome dataset is available in
Dataset S3
). Supporting the observation reported above in which lactate-grown cells accumulated fourfold more W than glucose-grown cells, there was a fourfold increase in the expression of all six of the genes in the
wor1LSXABC
operon and, for unknown reasons,
wor1L
and
wor1S
have approximately threefold greater expression than
worXABC
(
SI Appendix, Fig. S2
). Expression of the genes encoding WOR2,
wor2SL
(
Fig. 1
), were unchanged during growth on lactate but there was a twofold increase in the expression of
tupA,
which encodes the substrate binding domain of the tungstate-specific transporter, TupABC. Other genes up-regulated on lactate encode enzymes involved in lactate transport and catabolism, in the Wood–Ljungdahl pathway and in the butyrate production pathway (
SI Appendix, Table S1
). For example, expression of genes encoding electron bifurcating LCT (
lctCBD
), lactate permease, and lactate racemase increased by eight-, 11-, and threefold, respectively. Lactate racemases are Ni-containing enzymes and consistent with their increased production, the Ni content of the QHP fractions derived from lactate-grown cells had a very different elution profile from that of glucose-grown cells (
SI Appendix, Fig. S3
). The LCT operon (
SI Appendix, Fig. S1
) is preceded by a transcription factor (TF) named LctR (
33
,
34
). We analyzed the corresponding intergenic regions from
E. limosum
and related genomes and identified a 19-nt palindromic DNA motif (
SI Appendix, Fig. S4
) and conclude that the LctR regulon is local and regulates only the lactate transporter and utilization enzymes (
Fig. 3
). The regulatory response to lactate of W-related genes involves other mechanisms, as discussed below.

This is in agreement with the production of just the two SCFAs and in similar amounts. This is despite the fact there was a ~threefold increase in the expression of genes involved in the propanediol microcompartment pathway during growth on lactate (

In addition, there was an increase in the expression of genes encoding a flavodoxin (Fld1; see below) and of the two gluconeogenic enzymes, as expected during growth on lactate (
SI Appendix, Table S1
).
Effect of W-Limitation on the Metabolism of Lactate.
Growth of
E. limosum
on lactate in a W-limited medium containing only 6 nM W (unavoidably introduced due to medium chemical impurities) compared to standard lactate medium with 100 nM added W resulted in the downregulation of 47 genes and upregulation of 27 genes (≥threefold, summarized in
SI Appendix, Table S2
and
Dataset S4
; the full genome dataset is given in
Dataset S5
). The most striking changes under W limitation are the five- to sixfold increase in expression of genes involved in the propanediol microcompartment pathway and a 10-fold increase in expression of the LDR gene (
Fig. 3
). However, the products of the propanediol pathway, propionate and propanol, were undetected and the concentrations of the major products, acetate and butyrate, did not change under W limitation (
Fig. 2
C
). It is possible that in the gut environment propanediol and lactaldehyde are both present, along with lactate, which are produced by other gut microbes during metabolism of other sugars (
35
). Thus, the coregulation of genes involved in the metabolism of lactate, lactaldehyde, and propanediol by
E. limosum
might reflect an adaptation to the availability of multiple carbon sources in a complex environment such as the human gut. Other genes up-regulated by W-limitation (
SI Appendix, Table S2
) include that encoding the Fld1 flavodoxin, which is already up-regulated on lactate. Production of Fld1 therefore appears to be dependent on W (see below).
Key transcriptional changes related to W utilization by RNA-sequencing were also analyzed by qRT-PCR using RNA extracted from
E. limosum
grown on glucose or lactate with or without W-limitation (
Fig. 4
A
). Expression of genes encoding WOR1 (
wor1A
and
wor1B
) increases approximately eightfold on lactate independent of whether W is limited while that of WOR2 (
wor2L
) is not significantly changed, confirming that only electron bifurcating WOR1 is involved in lactate metabolism. Expression of genes encoding the tungstate-specific transporter, TupABC (
tupA
), increases about threefold under lactate conditions, while those encoding FDH and the electron bifurcating LCT increase by approximately four- and 20-fold, respectively. This implicates these two-electron bifurcating enzymes, WOR1 and LCT, in lactate utilization, along with CO
2
-utilizing FDH (
Fig. 3
).

Although FDHs typically contain Mo, W-containing FDHs have been reported (
36
,
37
) and our data are also consistent with
E. limosum
FDH containing only W. The content of Mo in glucose- versus lactate-grown cells did not change (only 0.1 ± 0.05 µmol Mo/g of protein) and lactate-grown cells contained approximately 100-fold more W (6.0 ± 0.1 µmol W/g protein). Yet, the activity of FDH increases approximately sixfold in lactate-grown cells (
Fig. 2
E
), and after chromatographic fractionation of a cytoplasmic extract, the formate-oxidizing activity of FDH coincided with W-containing fractions (4.9 and 19.1 unit/mg in W peaks C and D, respectively, in
Fig. 2
B
) where there was >20-fold more W than Mo. Additionally, LC–MS/MS confirmed that FDH is present in fractions of both W peaks C and D (
Fig. 2

). Hence it seems reasonable to conclude that FDH contains W rather than Mo. In addition, W-limitation did not significantly affect the expression of genes encoding WOR1, TupABC, LCT, or FDH when growing on lactate (
Fig. 4
A
). These data are consistent with the TupABC system of
E. limosum
having a high affinity for tungstate such that approximately 6 nM W in the medium is sufficient to satisfy cellular needs on lactate, albeit resulting in slower growth (
Fig. 2
A
), which has implications in terms of the organism obtaining sufficient W in its natural gut environment.
E. limosum
WOR1 Oxidizes Aldehydes Related to Lactate Metabolism.
Previously characterized WORs are known to oxidize aldehydes to their corresponding acids and it was previously shown that cytoplasmic extracts of glucose-grown
E. limosum
oxidized aliphatic and aromatic aldehydes using the dye benzyl viologen (BV) as an artificial electron acceptor (
29
). Assays measuring the oxidation of aldehydes potentially involved in lactate assimilation were used to better understand the role of WOR in lactate catabolism in
E. limosum.
Specific activities with acetaldehyde and benzaldehyde in glucose-grown cells were 0.3 and 0.6 units/mg, respectively, but in lactate-grown cells, the activities increased ≥20-fold to ~11 units/mg with either aldehyde (
Fig. 4
B
). Clearly, given the upregulation of expression of the genes encoding WOR1, but not WOR2, coupled with the large increase in W uptake in lactate-grown cells, these high aldehyde-oxidation activities can be reasonably assigned to electron bifurcating WOR1. To assess the substrate specificity of WOR1, the partially purified enzyme obtained from QHP fractions after fractionation of a cytoplasmic extract of
E. limosum
grown on lactate (
SI Appendix, Fig. S6
) was screened for BV-coupled aldehyde oxidation activity with 50 different aldehydes and related compounds (
SI Appendix, Fig. S7 and
Table S3
). For those compounds that were active in the plate assay, specific activities were determined, and the results are shown in
Fig. 4
B
. WOR1 was most active with acetaldehyde, propionaldehyde, butyraldehyde, crotonaldehyde, and lactaldehyde, aldehydes that could all be generated during lactate conversion to acetate and butyrate by the decarboxylation of keto-acids or hydrolysis of CoA derivatives found within the pathway (
Fig. 3
). Accordingly, to quantitate individual intracellular aldehydes, cells were lysed in acetonitrile (50%, v/v) and aldehydes were derivatized and identified by HPLC. By this method, lactate-grown cells contained 606 ± 62 µmol of acetaldehyde/g of protein, approximately sixfold more than the acetaldehyde measured in glucose-grown cells (116.3 ± 10 µmol/g;
Fig. 2
F
). The two cell types contained similar concentrations of propionaldehyde (52 ± 8.6 µmol/g), but butyraldehyde, crotonaldehyde, and lactaldehyde were not detected. Hence, these data suggest that WOR1 has the primary function of oxidizing potentially cytotoxic aldehydes generated during lactate conversion to acetate and butyrate, which are produced at much lower concentrations in glucose-grown cells that generate primarily acetate (
Fig. 2
C
).
It has previously been shown that acetaldehyde is generated as a side-reaction of pyruvate oxidoreductase (POR) by the CoA-dependent nonoxidative decarboxylation of pyruvate, rather than its oxidative conversion to acetyl-CoA (
38
). In glucose-grown
E. limosum
cells, the specific activity of POR is already very high (3.7 units/mg), but this increases by almost 11-fold to a remarkable 41 units/mg in lactate-grown cells (
Fig. 2
E
). Hence it seems reasonable to conclude that the dramatic increase in intracellular acetaldehyde concentration in lactate-grown cells (
Fig. 2
F
) is due to the increased POR activity, and that the primary function of WOR1 is to minimize the acetaldehyde toxicity by converting it to acetate (
Fig. 3
). WOR1 had the highest activity with acetaldehyde in the standard assay (
Fig. 4
). Curiously, expression of POR, which is among the top five most highly expressed in
E. limosum
cells on glucose (
Dataset S3
), was up-regulated only 1.8-fold on lactate, which does not correlate with POR activity, and potential mechanisms are discussed below. In any event, we propose that acetaldehyde is the main physiological substrate for WOR1.
E. limosum
has a well-developed genetic system, and CRISPR-based deletion of several genes has been reported (
39
). We attempted to obtain
Δwor1L
and
Δwor1LSXABC
mutants using multiple approaches including a CRISPR-based method with two different guide RNAs specific to
wor1L
and homologous arm regions of 1.0 and 1.5 kb, and a linear homologous recombination method with antibiotic resistance (
40
,
41
). However, we were unable to obtain a WOR1 deletion strain when grown on either lactate or glucose. In contrast, we successfully made a
wor2L
mutant strain using the same procedures with the first guide RNA attempted and with homologous arms of 1.0 kb in length. The
Δwor2
mutant had no phenotype compared to the wild-type strain when grown on glucose or on lactate in the standard media (
SI Appendix, Fig. S7
), consistent with our hypothesis that WOR2 is not involved in the metabolism of lactate.
Tungstopyranopterin-Based Riboswitch Regulation of Lactate Metabolism.
Riboswitches are short RNA sequences that bind small molecules and control gene function at the level of transcription and/or translation.
E. limosum
has 41 riboswitches in its genome (
42
). Analysis by the RNA family database shows that five of them are controlled by metal-bound pyranopterin (ID RF01055). This organic cofactor coordinates the metal in tungsto- and molybdoenzymes, designated Tuco and Moco, respectively (
43
). As shown in
SI Appendix, Fig. S9
, the pyranopterin riboswitches are located immediately upstream of operons encoding WOR1, TupABC, two iron transporters (Fe ABC-type) and a gene of unknown function annotated as a formate/nitrite transporter [FocA; (
44
)]. Hence, Tuco rather than Moco is assumed to be involved in regulating the production of the active forms of WOR1, the Tup and Fe transporters, and FocA. Indeed, there is significant upregulation (>1.96-fold) in the expression of all the genes downstream of the Tuco riboswitches in lactate- compared to glucose-grown cells, except for that encoding FocA (
SI Appendix, Table S4
), which is already highly expressed (
Dataset S3
). In contrast, W limitation did not cause significant changes in expression of genes encoding WOR1, TupABC, and the two Fe transporters during growth on lactate (
SI Appendix, Table S4
). However, the cytoplasmic extract of cells grown with W limitation had specific activities with acetaldehyde, propionaldehyde, butyraldehyde, crotonaldehyde, lactaldehyde, furfural, and 5-hydroxymethylfurfural that were less than 10% of those measured using cells grown with 100 nM W added. Hence WOR1 appears to be regulated at the translational level by the Tuco riboswitch (see below). W limitation during growth on lactate also led to decreases of approximately 40% and 24% in the cytoplasmic activities of FDH and POR, respectively (
Fig. 2
E
), although there is no evidence for riboswitches of any type upstream of the genes that encode them. These data also support the conclusion that FDH is a tungstoenzyme.
The riboswitch data clearly support Tuco-dependent translational regulation of the uptake of tungsten and iron (
SI Appendix, Table S4
). Accordingly, the iron content of cells grown on lactate compared to glucose increased almost 12-fold, from 8.8 µmol/mg to 102 µmol/mg of protein, when grown on lactate compared to glucose. Moreover, QHP fractionation of extracts of glucose- and lactate-grown cells revealed very different elution profiles of proteins associated iron (
SI Appendix, Fig. S3
) with that from glucose cells generally tracing the protein profile while that from lactate cells contains three major Fe peaks, one of which had POR activity (
SI Appendix, Fig. S3
). The lactate cell elution profile had a peak Fe concentration of ~400 µM compared with a peak in the glucose-cell profile near 50 µM. Notably, POR (12 Fe atoms/mole) and FDH (18 Fe atoms/mole) are Fe-containing enzymes as, of course, is WOR1 (46 Fe atoms/mole;
Fig. 1
). Therefore, Tuco-based riboswitches not only regulate production of active WOR1 and the uptake of W, they also ensure sufficient iron is available. From a glance at
Fig. 3
, it is clear why Tuco-riboswitches also regulate iron uptake given all the Fe-dependent enzymes involved in lactate catabolism.
Alignment of intergenic regions of the genes preceded by five Tuco riboswitches identified conserved secondary structure elements (stems P1, P2, P4, and P5) and interloop regions in
E. limosum
and related
Eubacterium
genomes (
SI Appendix, Fig. S10
A
). All these structures lack the P3 stem, a signature of Mo-dependent Moco-responsive riboswitches, confirming that they are W-dependent (
43
). The
worABCSL
and
tupABC
regulatory regions contain conserved RNA structures that overlap the ribosome-binding site (RBS), termed RBS-sequestors, and complementary RNA regions that overlap both the RBS-sequestor and one of the conserved helices in the Tuco riboswitch, while the Tuco riboswitch at the
fetABC2
operon directly sequesters the translation initiation site. In the other two operons (
fetABC1
and
focA
), these additional hairpins are followed by poly-U tracts and are therefore candidate rho-independent terminators of transcription (
45
). In the WOR1 and
tupABC
operons, complementary RNA regions overlap both the RBS sequestor and one of the conserved helices in 5′-region of the Tuco riboswitch (
SI Appendix, Fig. S10
B
). Thus, the comparative genomics suggests that three out of five Tuco-regulated operons in
E. limosum
(
worABCSL, tupABC,
and
fetABC2
) are controlled by alternative RNA structures at the level of translation (
SI Appendix, Fig. S10
B
-i, ii, and v
), while two operons (
focA
and
fetABC1
) are regulated at the transcriptional level by the Tuco riboswitch followed by a terminator hairpin (
SI Appendix, Fig. S10
B
-iii and vi
). The proposed Tuco regulatory mechanisms are corroborated by RNA-sequencing on lactate with and without W limitation. Expression of genes encoding both FetABC1 and FocA were up-regulated when W was limited, while the transcription of those encoding WOR1 and TupABC was not affected.
In addition to the local LctR regulon described above, two global regulators were identified using a comparative genomics approach to predict candidate TF binding sites in upstream regions of lactate-responsive genes from
E. limosum,
one of which involves W but not WOR1 [
SI Appendix, Fig. S7
; (
33
,
34
)]. The DNA-binding motif of the global regulator Rex, which responds to intracellular redox potential (NADH/NAD
+
ratio), was identified [
SI Appendix, Fig. S11
; (
46
)] in over 50 lactate-responsive genes, including those encoding enzymes for tungstopyranopterin synthesis and the tungstate-binding storage protein Tub (
32
), as well as for LCT, POR, ACS/CODH, lactaldehyde reductase, and all butyrate production pathway enzymes (
Dataset S6
). However, the Rex and LctR regulons do not include WOR1, other lactate-induced operons associated with Tuco riboswitches, nor FDH. Surprisingly, our analyses revealed that these operons have a common upstream DNA motif that resembles the binding site of CcpA, a catabolite control protein that regulates carbohydrate degradation [
SI Appendix, Figs. S7 and S11
; (
47
,
48
)]. Similar sites were upstream of 46 lactate-induced genes in
E. limosum
(
Dataset S5
), including all Tuco-regulated operons (
SI Appendix, Fig. S9
; see promoter gene regions in
SI Appendix, Fig. S10
), the LctR-regulated LCT operon and the Rex-regulated LDR gene. However, the intergenic promoter regions of the FDH operon lack Rex and LctR sites so how this enzyme is regulated is unclear. In any event, the CcpA regulator, which is obviously unrelated to sugar metabolism in
E. limosum,
establishes a direct link between lactate and WOR1 regulation. We therefore conclude that the transcriptional response to lactate in
E. limosum
involves three TFs: i) the local lactate repressor LctR; ii) the global redox-sensing Rex repressor that also controls W storage and Tuco biosynthesis; and iii) the pleiotropic regulator CcpA that is involved in regulation by Tuco, which includes WOR1 expression, at both the transcriptional and translational levels (
Fig. 3
and
SI Appendix, Fig. S9
).
Discussion
Microbial butyrate production by dominant species such as
E. limosum
is important for a healthy gut microbiome. Butyrate is often found at high concentrations (~20 mM) in healthy individuals and the majority of it is utilized by the human epithelial cells (
49
,
50
) Indeed,
E. limosum
butyrate production decreases dysbiosis (
16
), inflammation, and colitis symptoms by increasing the integrity of the epithelium as was observed in colicky infants (
15
). During growth on lactate, we showed that
E. limosum
produces >25 mM butyrate in approximately a 1:1 ratio with acetate. This is a fivefold higher concentration of butyrate that was previously seen for
E. limosum
grown on other carbon and energy sources, such as methanol and H
2
/CO
2
where others have reported concentrations of only 5 mM butyrate (
51
), similar to what we observed during growth on glucose.
Previous work had shown that
E. limosum
has a high affinity for tungstate uptake even in the presence of the same concentration of molybdate (
29
) with TupA having a binding affinity in the sub-nM range (
52
) while in other organisms the homologous ModA binds molybdate with a similar affinity (
53
).
E. limosum
also contains a high affinity tungstate storage protein termed tungbindin or Tub, which also has a high affinity for tungstate [K
a
~ 1 pM; (
32
)] that is much higher than that of the homologous storage protein Mop found in Mo-utilizing microbes for molybdate (K
a
~ 1 nM) (
54
). Tub contains most of the W in glucose-grown
E. limosum
(
32
). Hence, the organism appears to be “poised” to metabolize any lactate encountered in the gut environment by incorporating the W into WOR1 and FDH, two enzymes that facilitate the conversion of lactate to SCFAs (
Fig. 3
). In some other anaerobes, both W- and Mo-dependent FDH enzymes are present and which enzyme is expressed depends on Mo and W availability (
55
,
56
). A similar situation exists with the closely related enzyme formylmethanofuran dehydrogenase (FMDH) of certain methanogens (
57
). In contrast,
E. limosum
encodes only one FDH and it appears to incorporate only W even when grown in the presence of equimolar Mo. The W (6 nM) present in lactate media without added W is less than the physiological concentrations of W found in feces, which is in the 8.7 nM to 31 nM range (
58
), indicating that
E. limosum
in the gut environment would likely have sufficient W available for lactate to SCFA conversion.
We propose that the function of electron bifurcating WOR1 is to detoxify acetaldehyde, generated during lactate metabolism by the remarkably high POR activity (41 units/mg), which is known to generate acetaldehyde as a side reaction (
38
). This correlates with the dramatic increase (sixfold) in acetaldehyde concentrations in lactate- versus glucose-grown cells (
Fig. 3
). The lactate to SCFA conversion pathway (
Fig. 3
) contains two other bifurcating enzymes, LCT and LDH. The bifurcating nature of WOR1 was proposed to allow very low concentrations of toxic aldehydes to be oxidized even in the presence of high concentrations of the acid (
29
). In lactate-grown cells, this energy-conserving property of electron bifurcating WOR1 may be even more relevant since, as the primary products of lactate utilization, high concentrations of acids, namely, butyrate and acetate, are also present in its growth medium. Electron bifurcation by LCT enables lactate oxidation to drive NAD
+
reduction, an endergonic reaction that is simultaneously coupled to the exergonic reduction of NAD
+
by low potential reduced ferredoxin. NADH is used as a reductant in both the butyrate and Wood–Ljungdahl pathways but, like LCT, the Wood–Ljungdahl pathway is heavily dependent upon a supply of reduced ferredoxin (see below) as it converts CO
2
to acetyl-CoA, (
Fig. 3
). A second function of WOR1 is potentially to generate reduced ferredoxin to drive both lactate oxidation (via LCT) and CO
2
reduction (via FDH). The importance of WOR1 for
E. limosum
may also extend beyond lactate catabolism as we were unable to obtain a deletion mutant even under glucose conditions. Accordingly, genes encoding subunits of WOR1, but not WOR2, were reported to be essential for growth on H
2
/CO
2
and also on glucose (
39
).
Anaerobic fermentative microbes that have ferredoxin-dependent metabolisms typically harbor a single type of ferredoxin produced at a high concentration which, under Fe limitation, can be substituted by a flavodoxin (Fld) (
59
). This was the case with
Acetomicrobium mobile
from which the first bifurcating WorABCSL was obtained (
29
). However, the situation is different with
E. limosum
. Its genome encodes two ferredoxins and three flavodoxins. Ferredoxin 1 (Fd1: B2M23_RS10700) has one [4Fe-4S] cluster and ferredoxin 2 (Fd2: B2M23_RS02975) has two. The three flavodoxins are Fld1 (B2M23_RS01705), Fld2 (B2M23_RS 10125), and Fld3 (B2M23_RS20265). Their genes are comparably expressed so no one redox protein dominates (
Dataset S3
) and an intense Fe-containing peak representing a single ferredoxin after QHP fractionation is not seen with the
E. limosum
extract (
SI Appendix, Fig. S3
). However, genes encoding Fld1 and Fld2 are up-regulated in lactate-grown cells (
SI Appendix, Table S5
), which is somewhat ironic given that iron uptake is dramatically increased under such conditions and flavodoxin is typically utilized under Fe limitation conditions (
SI Appendix, Fig.
S3
). Hence, it is not clear which ferredoxin serves as the electron carrier for the three electron bifurcation enzymes, WOR1, LCT, and BCD, and other “ferredoxin”-dependent steps shown in
Fig. 3
. Surprisingly, our data suggest that the flavodoxin(s), even under Fe-sufficient conditions, play a key role.
There are two other well-characterized organisms that metabolize lactate and also contain an electron bifurcating LCT and these are
Anaerobutyricum hallii
and
Acetobacterium woodii
.
A. hallii
is found in the infant gut (
16
,
60
) and
A. woodii
was isolated from sediment (
61
). Importantly, we show here that
E. limosum
metabolizes lactate differently from these two organisms.
A. woodii
also contains the Wood–Ljungdahl pathway but it converts lactate to just acetate and not to butyrate, while
A. hallii
does not have the Wood–Ljungdahl pathway and utilizes the butyrogenic pathway to produce butyrate but requires external acetate to do so. The pathways by which the three organisms convert lactate to acetate and/or butyrate, respectively, are compared in
SI Appendix, Fig. S12
and the relevant enzymes involved are summarized in
SI Appendix, Table S6
. In further contrast to
E. limosum,
neither
A. woodii
or
A. hallii
contains a bifurcating WOR or TupABC and do not appear to utilize W (
SI Appendix, Table S6
). Hence, as shown in
SI Appendix, Fig. S12
,
E. limosum
in essence uses the butyrogenic pathway of
A. hallii
in combination with the acetogenic Wood–Ljungdahl pathway of
A. woodii
to metabolize lactate. Such a combination of pathways enables
E. limosum
to uniquely generate the key gut metabolite butyrate from lactate, as well as acetate. While
A. hallii
and
A. woodii
have W-independent metabolisms,
E. limosum
has two W-containing enzymes and W-dependent regulatory mechanisms that are integral components of its lactate degradation pathway (
Fig. 3
).
Materials and Methods
Microbial Growth.
E. limosum
ATCC 8486 was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection in Manassas, VA, USA. The growth media for
E. limosum
contained 0.5 g/L yeast extract, 1 g/L cysteine, 10 mM 3-(N-morpholino)propanesulfonic acid (MOPS), 1 mM KH
2
PO
4
, 1X salts, vitamin mix, trace elements, 10 mM acetate, 100 nM (NH
4
)
2
MoO
4
.4H
2
O and 100 nM Na
2
WO
4
.2H
2
O (
29
). For the W-limited medium, 100 nM tungstate was not added. The pH of the medium was adjusted with 5 M NaOH to 7.0 and was filter sterilized using a 0.2 μM filter before use. For Mo and W limited medium, Milli-Q filtered water was used to make up the growth medium, and Na
2
WO
4
.2H
2
O was not added to the trace elements solution. For glucose-grown cells, 28 mM glucose was added to the media. For lactate-grown cells, 70 mM lactate was added to the media.
E. limosum
was grown anaerobically with a headspace of 20% CO
2
and 80% N
2
at 35 °C with shaking. Growth curves were carried out in either 50 mL serum bottles with 0.5 mL taken every time point for GC and HPLC analysis or in a Bioscreen C (Thermo Labsystems, Milford, MA) placed in an anaerobic chamber (Plas Labs, Lansing, MI) with low agitation.
Transcriptomics.
E. limosum
cells were grown in 50 mL serum bottles under glucose and lactate conditions, with and without 100 nM W added. At mid-log phase (OD
600
= 0.3), 50 mL of cells were spun down for 15 min at 15,000×
g
at 4 °C. The cells were resuspended in 0.5 mL of the supernatant and 2 mL RNAprotect Bacteria Reagent (Qiagen). The Qiagen RNeasy Mini Kit was used as suggested with an added lysozyme treatment and bead beater (model and parameters) was used to extract RNA. An additional on-column DNase I treatment was performed as suggested with the kit. The RNA quality was assessed using A260/A280 on a Nanodrop and a Bioanalyzer RIN>7 (as suggested by Novogene). RNA sequencing, data collection, and differential analysis were performed by Novogene Co, Ltd. (Sacramento, California). Differential expression analysis was done by Novogene and involved normalizing read counts using DESeq (
62
), a negative binomial distribution for p-value estimation, and the BH method for FDR estimation (
62
). Differentially expressed genes were filtered for significance using a threshold of adjusted
P
-value ≤ 0.05) and a fold change ≥threefold (
SI Appendix, Fig. S13
). For qRT-PCR, cDNA synthesis was performed using 1 µg purified RNA with the Affinity Script QPCR cDNA synthesis kit (Agilent). qRT-PCR was conducted using Brilliant II SYBR Green QPCR Master Mix (Agilent). Primers (
SI Appendix, Table S7
) were designed to amplify a 150 bp product within the target gene. The gene encoding DNA topoisomerase (ATP-hydrolyzing) subunit B (
gyrB
, B2M23_RS03195) was used as the reference gene.
Bioinformatic Approaches for Riboswitch and TF Regulon Reconstruction.
For prediction and reconstruction of new TF regulons, we utilized the established comparative genomics approach (
63
) based on de novo inference of TF binding site motifs and genome-wide identification of similar regulatory sites in a set of eight closely related genomes representing the
Eubacterium
genus. This approach included identification of palindromic DNA motifs in a training set of intergenic regions using the expectation–maximization method, construction of a positional weight matrix (PWM) and scan of
E. limosum
genome for additional candidate TF binding sites using the Genome Explorer software, as previously described (
64
). During PWM scan for each TF regulon, we analyzed the whole genome upstream regions (up to −350 bp relative to the translation start site) and used thresholds for site scores defined as the lowest score observed in the training set. DNA motifs of previously characterized TFs were extracted from RegPrecise database (
65
). TFBS consensus sequences were visualized using Weblogo 3 (
https://weblogo.threeplusone.com/
). The pyranopterin-based RNA motifs were extracted from the Rfam database (RF1055 family) and reconstructed Tuco/Moco regulons were analyzed in the RegPrecise database (
66
). Comparative genomic analysis of the Tuco regulon genes in eight
Eubacterium
genomes was performed with the SEED genomic database via multiple-sequence alignments of their intergenic DNA regions using ClustalW2 (
67
). The RNA secondary structures of Tuco riboswitches, as well as potential RNA secondary structures of antiterminators and antisequestors, were predicted using the RNA Mfold program utilizing free-energy minimization algorithm for RNA folding (
https://www.unafold.org/mfold/
).
Deletion Mutants.
E. limosum
with pJIR-Cas9 were used to generate deletion mutants as previously described (
39
). Competent cells were prepared by harvesting
E. limosum
mid-log and washing twice with cold 10% sucrose. After preparing competent cells, 2 µg of plasmid with CRISPR sgRNA and homologous arm fragment or just the linear homologous arm fragment (primers and constructs in
SI Appendix, Table S8
) were added to the cells. Linear fragments with homologous arm fragments and antibiotic resistant cassette were also used as described (
41
). Electroporation was done in 1 mm cuvettes with 100 µL of cells at a 2.0 kV (600, 25 μF). The cells were transferred to a serum bottle for recovery at 35 °C for 12 h. Cells were plated and incubated in an anaerobic box for 3 d. Colonies were selected in glucose and lactate media with 30 ng/mL anhydrotetracycline, 15 µg/mL thiamphenicol, or 1 µg/mL clarithromycin was used to select for deletion mutants. Colony PCR and sequencing was used to verify the deletion mutants.
Enzyme Activity Assays.
Aldehyde oxidation assays were performed in an Agilent Cary 100 UV/Vis spectrometer by following the reduction of benzyl viologen at 600 nm (ε = 7,400 cm
−1
⋅ M
−1
). The reaction mixture (2.0 mL) contained 50 mM HEPES, 2 mM MgCl
2
, pH 7.5, 1 mM benzyl viologen (BV), ∼4 μM sodium dithionite, and the sample. The reaction was initiated by the addition of various aldehyde substrates (250 μM) as indicated. Assays were conducted at 35 °C. One unit (U) catalyzes the reduction of 1 μmol benzyl viologen/min. Specific activity is calculated based on the concentration of protein measured using the Bradford reagent (Bio-Rad; Bradford, 1976). The activities of pyruvate ferredoxin oxidoreductase (POR) and formate dehydrogenase (FDH) were measured by following the reduction of 1 mM BV at 600 nm using 5 mM pyruvate or 5 mM formate as the substrate. POR and FDH activity were measured in the same manner except for POR, CoA (0.25 mM), and TPP (0.25 mM) were added to the assay mixture. Aldehyde stock solutions (50 mM) were made in 95% ethanol. The 96-well plate aldehyde oxidation assays were performed under anaerobic conditions (5% H
2
, 95% Ar) in a Coy anaerobic chamber at 25 ºC as previously described (
29
). Reaction mixtures (200 μL) contained 50 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 250 μM of the aldehyde or related substrate, 750 μM benzyl viologen, ~4 μM sodium dithionite and 1 to 100 μg cell extract or partially purified protein.
Metal Determination.
Metal concentrations were measured as previously described (
29
). Samples were diluted into 2% (v/v) trace-grade nitric acid (VWR) in acid-washed 15 mL polypropylene tubes. Samples were incubated at 37 °C temperature with shaking for at least 1 h and then centrifuged at 3,000×
g
for 20 min to pellet debris. Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) was done using an Agilent 7,900 ICP-MS fitted with MicroMist nebulizer, platinum (Pt) cones, x-lens, and an Octopole Reaction System collision cell with He mode (Agilent Technologies). Results are reported as the average of three analytical replicates.
Aldehyde Determination by HPLC.
To quantitate individual intracellular aldehydes,
E. limosum
cells were harvested from 500 mL cultures at 4 °C, resuspended in 0.5 mL PBS and 0.5 mL acetonitrile, and lysed by sonication. Known aldehydes were added where appropriate as standards. The aldehydes were derivatized by adding 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (2,4-DNPH; 4 mg in 2 mL) and after overnight incubation at 4 °C, the samples were clarified using a syringe filter (
68
). Acetaldehyde, propionaldehyde, butyraldehyde, lactaldehyde, and crotonaldehyde (0.025 to 5 mM) were used as standards, Derivatives were analyzed using an Agilent 1260 HPLC equipped with a Poroshell 120 EC-C18 column (50 mm × 4.6 mm, 2.7 µm; Agilent Technology) and UV-visible diode-array detector (
69
).
Anion-Exchange Chromatography.
In an anaerobic chamber (95% Ar, 5 % H
2
), ~10 g frozen
E. limosum
cells were thawed and resuspended in 40 mL of lysis buffer containing 50 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 5% trehalose (w/v), 1 mM cysteine, 1 µM FMN, and 1 µM FAD, 100 mg/L deoxyribonuclease (DNase) I, and Protease Inhibitor Cocktail Tablets (Roche, New York, MO). Cells were lysed on ice by sonication (30 s intervals, amplitude 80; model Q55; Qsonica, Newton, Connecticut). A cytoplasmic extract was prepared by ultracentrifugation at 100,000×
g
for 1 h at 10 °C. Anion exchange chromatography was carried out using a 5-mL Q Sepharose high-performance (QHP) column pre-equilibrated with 25 mM HEPES, pH 7.5 containing 1% (w/v) trehalose, 1 mM cysteine, 1 µM FMN, and 1 µM FAD. Bound proteins were eluted with a linear gradient from 0 to 500 mM NaCl in the same buffer at a flow rate of 5 mL/min. Fractions were collected in Ar-flushed serum vials sealed with rubber stoppers and were stored at 4 °C.
