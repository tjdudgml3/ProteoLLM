1
Introduction
During or following their synthesis, proteins can be modified by the covalent attachment of various chemical groups or molecules to amino acid residues within their polypeptide chains. These modifications often alter a protein's structural conformation, and hence modulate its activity, making them important regulators of protein function. Two examples of such modifications are phosphorylation and glycosylation which involve the addition of phosphate groups or carbohydrates to a protein's backbone to form phosphoproteins or glycoproteins, respectively (
Fig. 1
). The carbohydrate portions of glycoproteins can themselves be further modified by enzymatic reactions leading to greater diversity of proteoforms and thus adding yet another layer of complexity to the proteome. One way that this can occur is via phosphorylation and in this sense the appended phosphate moiety can be considered a ‘modification of a modification’. As part of their processing in the secretory pathway, some N-linked glycans (N-glycans) can be modified by the addition of phosphate groups to their monosaccharide moieties and here we refer to such phosphorylated N-glycans as phospho-N-glycans (PNGs). In vertebrates, phosphorylation of N-glycans occurs primarily on mannose residues giving rise to mannosylphosphorylated N-glycans (MPNGs), though in other branches of life it can also occur on other monosaccharides (
Fig. 1
). For example, it has been reported that sialic acids can be phosphorylated at the C-9 position [
1
,
2
] and that some brittle star N-glycans contain phosphorylated N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) residues [
3
]. N-glycans are not the only class of glycans that can be phosphorylated. For instance, some O-linked glycans (O-glycans), such as O-mannosylated glycans, can similarly be modified by the attachment of phosphate groups to their mannoses or other monosaccharide residues (
Fig. 1
), with the oligosaccharides of the mammalian Dystroglycan protein being a prime example [
4
,
5
].

Oligosaccharide phosphorylation represents a crucial element in the field of complex carbohydrate chemistry and glycobiology. By altering the three dimensional structure and physico-chemical properties of protein-bound glycans, this modification plays a vital role in regulating the functions of glycans and, hence, the underlying glycoproteins. Not only does the addition of a phosphate group modify the structural conformation of a glycan, it also imparts a negative charge, thus affecting the molecule's hydration and lubricity. The collective effect of these changes ultimately influences how the glycoprotein interacts with its environment and with other molecules, particularly glycan-binding proteins. For example, phosphorylation of a mannose residue on a glycan can render it resistant to mannosidases [
6
,
7
] or affect its binding to lectin receptors [
8
], thereby having direct consequences for the glycoprotein's stability or activity. Understanding how oligosaccharide phosphorylation impacts molecular interactions and signalling pathways is essential for unravelling the complexities of glycan behaviour in health and development of disease.
In this article, we explore N-glycan phosphorylation, a post-translational carbohydrate modification that significantly impacts glycoprotein function, stability, and localisation by altering the structure of N-glycans. One of the most extensively studied examples of this modification is mannose-6-phosphate (M6P), which serves as a critical signal for transporting lysosomal enzymes from the Golgi apparatus to the lysosomes. While M6P-tagged N-glycans are well-characterised for their role in lysosomal trafficking, the broader implications of N-glycan phosphorylation in other biological contexts remain poorly understood.
In the central nervous system (CNS), N-glycan phosphorylation is particularly intriguing, as the brain is enriched with phosphorylated N-glycans compared to other tissues [
9
]. Although lysosomal hydrolases dominate the known pool of phosphorylated N-glycoproteins in the brain, numerous studies have shown that some non-lysosomal, brain-specific glycoproteins are also modified in this way indicating that PNGs may have roles beyond lysosomal trafficking that include their involvement in various neurobiological processes. While PNGs have been implicated in several key cellular processes, including cell signalling and protein turnover, the precise functions of these modifications in the brain are presently unknown. Here, we explore the possibility that multiple oligosaccharide phosphorylation pathways operate in the brain to generate discrete pools of phosphoglycoproteins, some of which are modified with atypical PNGs that are functionally distinct from M6P and may be involved in aspects of cell biology specific to neural cells.
1.1
Aim and overview
The objective of this article is to provide an overview of evidence that suggests there may be an alternative mechanism of N-glycan phosphorylation operating in the brain, thus challenging the currently held canon and raising the possibility that phosphorylation of N-glycans, at least in the brain, is more complex than previously recognised. We begin with a synopsis of the canonical M6P N-glycan phosphorylation pathway in which we concisely summarise what is known about this paradigmatic sugar modification, focussing primarily on the synthesis, structure, and distribution of the M6P modification. Next, we synthesise what is known about phosphorylation of N-glycans in the mammalian brain including the distinguishing features of neural phosphoglycoproteins and their phosphorylated glycans. This section combines data from the literature as well as novel insights derived from our own independent analyses of publicly available datasets. We then introduce the concept that, in addition to the conventional pathway, the brain may employ an alternative, non-canonical oligosaccharide phosphorylation mechanism for the modification of a discrete set of neural glycoproteins and we systematically present the evidence that underpins this hypothesis. Finally, we conclude by discussing the implications of the hypothesis and proposing potential avenues of research that will address unresolved questions and advance our understanding of phosphorylated N-glycans in neurobiology.
1.2
A brief note on nomenclature
Glycosylation and phosphorylation are two types of PTM that sometimes co-occur on the same protein. Phosphate residues can be added to both amino acids and monosaccharides. Due to the combinatorial complexity and substantial structural diversity that exists when molecules are modified with both of these PTMs, it's not a trivial task to derive a coherent and universal system of nomenclature that covers all possible permutations. Yet, this is a prerequisite for clear and precise communication of scientific concepts. The currently used nomenclature in this field of biochemistry is patchy and, in our opinion, insufficient to unambiguously convey the nuance between various subclasses of similar types of molecules. Hence, for the sake of clarity, in
Fig. 1
- we define (with illustrated examples) the logically derived terms that will be used throughout this article to refer to various classes of phosphorylated molecules. We endorse the recommendation of Davis e
t al.
[
6
] to use the location of the phosphate modification to distinguish between glycoproteins modified by phosphorylation of the glycan moiety (phosphoglycoproteins) versus those modified by phosphorylation of the polypeptide backbone (glycophosphoproteins). We propose to expand this dictionary of terms to cover all possible permutations, including proteins that have phosphate residues attached to both their amino acid chains and their glycans. We propose that such proteins be called phosphoglycophosphoproteins (PGPPs). The term has already been coined by other authors [
10
], though it is unclear from that context whether it was used to refer to the same type of molecule. One reason that this term is not in widespread usage could be that it has not hitherto been properly defined - an issue that highlights the current level of ambiguity surrounding the nomenclature of these two PTMs, particularly when they co-occur on the same molecule.
Regarding the distinction between different types of phosphorylated glycans (phosphoglycans, PGs), we suggest the following terms. Phospho-N-glycans (PNGs): N-glycans carrying a phosphate residue on any of their monosaccharides (mannose or otherwise). Phospho-O-glycans (POGs): O-glycans carrying a phosphate residue on any of their monosaccharides (mannose or otherwise). Mannosylphosphorylated glycans (MPGs): glycans of any class (i.e. O-linked, N-linked, or other) that contain a phosphomannosyl residue. Mannosylphosphorylated N-glycans (MPNGs): a subtype of PNGs that refers specifically to N-glycans that contain a phosphomannosyl residue. Mannosylphosphorylated O-glycans (MPOGs): a subtype of POGs that refers specifically to O-glycans that contain a phosphomannosyl residue. In this article, N-glycan compositions are presented using a code in which units are designated by a single letter (H = hexose, N =
N
-acetylhexosamine, P = phosphate) followed by a number indicating the quantity of the unit (e.g. H6N2P1). With regard to the nomenclature of mannosidic N-glycans, the common oligomannose precursor composed of two GlcNAc residues and nine mannoses is designated Man9 with the remainder of the mannosidic N-glycans in the series (i.e. oligomannose and paucimannose subtypes) similarly designated. Phosphorylated variants are appended with P or P2 for mono- and diphosphorylated forms, respectively (e.g. Man6P2 denotes the diphosphorylated form of the Man6 N-glycan).
2
The canonical M6P N-glycan phosphorylation pathway
The best-known and most studied sugar phosphorylation is M6P in which a phosphate moiety is added to the carbon 6 (C-6) hydroxyl group of certain mannose residues present on mannosidic and hybrid N-glycans [
11
]. The M6P post-translational carbohydrate modification is added predominantly to nascent lysosomal hydrolases during their processing in the secretory pathway and acts as a signal that directs their transport to the lysosome via M6P receptors (M6PRs) [
11
], though this is not a rigid rule. Some non-lysosomal glycoproteins are also modified with M6P (see
Table 1
) while some M6P-tagged lysosomal enzymes do not reach the lysosome [
12
,
13
]. Modification of mannoses with M6P occurs in both the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and the
cis
Golgi network by a single phosphotransferase enzyme complex that is active in both compartments [
14
], namely N-acetylglucosamine-1-phosphate transferase (GlcNAc-1-phosphotransferase, also referred to as PTase) [
[15]
,
[16]
,
[17]
]. This heterohexameric α2β2γ2 enzyme complex is encoded by two genes;
GNPTAB
, which encodes the α- and β-subunits, and
GNPTG
, which encodes the γ-subunits [
15
]. In a two-step process, this enzyme complex first adds GlcNAc-1-phosphate to the target mannose residue to produce GlcNAc-1-phosphate-6-mannose. In the second step, the GlcNAc is removed by the ‘uncovering enzyme’ N-acetylglucosamine-1-phosphodiester α-N-acetylglucosaminidase (NAGPA or α-GlcNAcase) in the
trans
Golgi to expose the M6P tag that is recognised by the M6PRs [
11
]. There are approximately 60 lysosomal hydrolases whose oligosaccharides are modified by GlcNAc-1-phosphotransferase [
16
] with an efficiency that is several orders of magnitude greater than for non-lysosomal glycoproteins carrying equivalent N-glycans [
17
,
18
]. The specificity of the interaction between GlcNAc-1-phosphotransferase and its target hydrolases lies in the presence of a protein domain that is common to all lysosomal enzymes but absent in non-lysosomal glycoproteins [
15
,
[17]
,
[18]
,
[19]
].

The M6P N-glycan phosphorylation pathway is essential for normal cellular functioning and disruption of M6P-dependent lysosomal trafficking leads to pathological consequences. Deleterious mutations in the genes that encode GlcNAc-1-phosphotransferase manifest as lysosomal storage disorders which are termed mucolipidosis type II (previously known as inclusion-cell disease) or type III (ML II and ML III, respectively), depending on the type of mutation [
20
]. These autosomal recessive conditions are characterised by defects in lysosomal function and the ensuing accumulation of non-degraded large molecules in these organelles. The more severe ML II is caused by mutations in
GNPTAB
that completely, or almost completely, abolish that catalytic activity of the α/β subunits resulting in a fatal outcome during early childhood. ML III has a broader phenotypic range and is caused by pathogenic variants in either
GNPTAB
or
GNPTG
that impair enzyme function but preserve some residual phosphorylation activity. Interestingly, different hydrolases, cell types, and tissues of the body are affected to varying degrees by M6P deficiency indicating that some are more reliant on this pathway than others to maintain lysosomal function. For example, in ML II patients [
21
,
22
] and in mouse models of mucolipidosis [
23
], the activities of many lysosomal enzymes are close to normal in the brain which suggests that certain enzymes may be processed through alternative M6P-independent lysosomal trafficking pathways in this organ. Despite this, CNS pathology is a feature of mucolipidosis in both ML II patients [
24
] and mouse models of ML II [
23
,
25
] indicating that brain function is nevertheless affected in some measure.
2.1
Topology of M6P tags
There is considerable heterogeneity among MPNGs regarding the degree and site(s) of phosphorylation. This appears to depend on several variables, including the N-glycosylation site (N-glycosite), carrier glycoprotein, tissue, and organism in question. Across various model systems, it has been shown that any of the mannoses that are not part of the canonical N-glycan core structure can potentially be tagged with M6P [
26
], including those present on the α(1–6)-branch of hybrid N-glycans [
8
,
14
], though the six possible candidate mannoses are not phosphorylated with equal probability. Several sources of evidence indicate that terminal α(1–2)-linked mannoses are more likely to carry phosphate residues [
13
,
26
] or at least that the presence of α(1–2)-linked mannoses is necessary for the phosphorylation of other (subterminal) mannoses [
26
]. Due to the presence of such α(1–2)-linked mannose residues, the Man6 to Man8 oligomannose N-glycan (OMNG) subtypes are favoured as substrates for modification with M6P, with the Man6 and Man7 species being the major carriers of one and two M6P tags, respectively [
13
].

2.2
Post-phosphorylation trimming of OMNGs and binding to the M6PRs
Although M6P tags are most frequently found on the terminal mannose residues of mature glycoproteins, this is often the result of phosphorylation of subterminal mannoses followed by trimming of the outer mannoses by mannosidases to produce the fully processed MPNG that can interact with the M6PRs [
8
,
14
]. The phosphorylated mannoses themselves are resistant to cleavage by α-mannosidase [
6
,
7
] which effectively prevents the conversion of phosphorylated oligosaccharides to complex N-glycans. Nevertheless, processing of MPNGs by ER and Golgi mannosidases, which occurs concurrently to phosphorylation by GlcNAc-1-phosphotransferase [
14
], plays an important role in modulating their affinity for the M6PRs. Specifically, the number and position of exposed M6P moieties (i.e. those which have been uncovered by the removal of the masking GlcNAc) present on a MPNG determine its degree of binding to the M6PRs [
8
]. The presence of M6P on terminal mannose residues increases binding affinity, as does having multiple M6P moieties. For this reason, smaller (i.e. trimmed) and diphosphorylated MPNGs generally display higher affinity for the M6PRs than larger and monophosphorylated MPNGs [
8
]. Moreover, when the M6P tag is located on mannoses of the α(1–3)-arm, binding to the M6PRs is enhanced relative to molecules on which the M6P tag is located on α(1–6)-arm mannoses [
8
]. In accordance with this, the affinity of the M6PRs for neutral hybrid MPNGs is relatively low. This binding is abolished in sialylated hybrid MPNGs indicating that the sialic acid interferes with the interaction between the MPNG and the receptor.
2.3
M6P on O-glycans
As mentioned earlier, phosphorylation of mannose residues is not restricted to N-glycans. Indeed, some types of O-glycans, for example O-mannosyl glycans, also contain mannose residues that can likewise be phosphorylated, as demonstrated in both human [
28
,
29
] and glyco-engineered yeast [
30
] cells. In mammals, the initial mannose of core M3 type O-mannosyl glycans (i.e. the one attached to the serine or threonine residue of the amino acid chain) can be phosphorylated at the C-6 position giving rise to M6P. However, there are several important distinctions, both mechanistic and functional, between the M6P modifications found on N- and O-glycans. Firstly, although it ultimately results in a chemically identical modification, the phosphorylation of O-glycan initial mannoses is catalysed by a completely different enzyme, Protein O-Mannose Kinase (POMK) [
31
,
32
], and does not proceed via a GlcNAc-1-phosphate intermediate. POMK, also known as SGK196, requires a GalNAc-β3-GlcNAc-β4-Man trisaccharide acceptor sequence [
31
,
33
] and, unlike GlcNAc-1-phosphotransferase, it directly transfers a single phosphate group to mannose C-6 without the need for subsequent uncapping (
Fig. 2
C). Secondly, the M6P tag on O-glycans is not recognised by the M6P receptor and hence does not act as a signal for trafficking of the glycoprotein to the lysosome. Consequently, the types of glycoproteins modified by the two M6P tags also differ. In mammals, the most prominent carrier of the O-mannosyl M6P tag is the α-subunit of Dystroglycan (α-DG) [
4
], a ubiquitously expressed transmembrane glycoprotein that links the cytoskeleton to the extracellular matrix and is essential for proper functioning of muscles and the nervous system [
5
]. Although the modification found on O-glycans could technically also be described as M6P, as a matter of convention, the term “M6P” as presently used in the literature is generally used only for describing this modification in the context of N-glycans. Nevertheless, to avoid confusion of these biosynthetically and functionally disparate modifications, we suggest authors use a clarifier to specify whether they are dealing with M6P on N-glycans or O-glycans (e.g. N-M6P and O-M6P, respectively).
3
Phosphorylation of N-glycans in the brain
In this section, we summarise what is known about PNGs in the brain, the N-glycosites they occupy, and the glycoproteins they modify. We delve into the structural diversity and abundance of neural PNGs, their distribution across different types of glycoproteins, and the microheterogeneity present at N-glycosylation sites modified with PNGs (i.e. phospho-N-glycosites). Overall, data on the topic are scarce and are derived from only a handful of studies, most of which were published before the turn of the millennium and deal with N-M6P. However, a recent study using unbiased contemporary analytical techniques has contributed significantly to our understanding of neural PNGs by providing the most comprehensive insight into the phosphorylation of brain glycoproteins to date. Using dual-mode enrichment of phosphorylated glycopeptides combined with mass spectrometry (MS)-based site-specific glycoproteomics, Huang et al. generated a valuable resource that contains data from five mouse tissues regarding the glycoproteins modified by phosphorylated oligosaccharides, the N-glycosylation sites occupied by phosphorylated glycans, the composition of the phosphorylated glycans, and the level of microheterogeneity at specific N-glycosylation sites [
13
]. Since the focus of the original article was predominantly on the newly developed methodology and inter-organ differences in N-glycan phosphorylation rather than a detailed investigation of glycan phosphorylation in the brain, we made use of the rich dataset to do a deep dive and extract additional insights into the phosphorylation of brain oligosaccharides beyond what was presented in the original study. 3.1
N-M6P in the brain
In most tissues, the N-M6P tag is rapidly removed once the labelled hydrolases reach the acidic environment of the lysosome where they become activated. A study in adult rats in which M6PRs were used as a probe revealed that the brain is exceptionally rich in N-M6P-containing glycoproteins compared to other tissues, having almost twice the amount as the next highest tissue, the testes [
9
]. This does not appear to be due a higher concentration of hydrolases in brain lysosomes [
9
], nor to the trafficking and accumulation of N-M6P-tagged glycoproteins to other (non-lysosomal) organelles since it was demonstrated in neurons that most of them are indeed located in lysosomes [
34
]. It has therefore been proposed that their abundance in neurons is attributable to inefficient removal of the N-M6P tag in the lysosome resulting in an N-M6P modification with an unusually long half-life [
34
]. Affinity purification experiments using M6PRs [
35
] have identified 43 glycoproteins in the mouse brain and 60 proteins in the human brain that are verified carriers of the N-M6P tag (
Table 1
). While the majority of these are predictably known lysosomal proteins, it is noteworthy that a substantial fraction of these glycoproteins have no known association with lysosomes (
Table 1
,
Table 2

3.2
Neural phosphoglycoproteins
In the brain, PNGs have been detected on many different types of glycoproteins that occupy a variety of subcellular locations, including the secretory organelles, extracellular space, cell surface (including synapses and myelin), and, of course, the lysosomes [
13
,
[35]
,
[36]
,
[37]
,
[38]
]. Given the well-documented involvement of canonical N-M6P phosphorylation in the trafficking of lysosomal enzymes and the comparative paucity of information regarding other types of phosphoglycoproteins or the modifications they carry, it is pertinent at this juncture to make a distinction between lysosomal and non-lysosomal proteins. For the purposes of our supplemental analyses, we have assigned proteins a subcellular localisation based on their UniProt annotation as well as information gleaned from the literature. However, it must be acknowledged that, due to the complicated nature of biology, the situation is not always clear-cut. Other researchers have previously demonstrated that many proteins can be classified under more than one subcellular localisation category using gene ontology (GO) cellular compartment terms [
37
]. For such ambiguous cases in which proteins are reported to reside in both the lysosome and other cellular compartments (e.g. Creg1, Psap), we categorised them as variable with regard to their subcellular location (in other words, these proteins are neither lysosomal nor non-lysosomal). Importantly, the fraction of non-lysosomal phosphoglycoproteins identified in a given study greatly depends on the method used (
Table 1
,
Fig. 3
A). When N-M6P-tagged proteins are specifically targeted using M6PRs, lysosomal proteins unsurprisingly comprise the majority of hits (approximately 70 %). However, by using an unbiased approach that allows for the detection of any phosphoglycoprotein (i.e. not only those tagged with N-M6P), the proportion of non-lysosomal proteins increases to 44 %, or even 58 % when phosphoglycoprotein enrichment is used. This suggests that when M6PRs are used, only a subset of phosphoglycoproteins is captured, which in turn raises a number of thought-provoking questions.
Firstly, why are some neural phosphoglycoproteins not recovered using M6PR affinity purification? Their sum is not insignificant; of the phosphoglycoproteins collectively identified from three independent studies of the mouse brain (
Fig. 3
), 46 % were not picked up using M6PRs. Can this simply be attributed to technical reasons? Clearly, no enrichment method works perfectly and there are bound to be both false positives and false negatives no matter what the experimental setup. Sorting the identified phosphoglycoproteins according to their subcellular location and responsiveness to M6PRs (
Fig. 3
B) revealed that a few lysosomal phosphoglycoproteins (which presumably must be tagged with N-M6P since they are shuttled to the lysosome) were not identified using M6PRs but were detected using other methods (
Fig. 3
B; Group B). Differences in the limit of detection between the various methods provide a simple and plausible explanation for these apparent ‘false negatives’. Then there was also a small number of phosphoglycoproteins that were pulled out using M6PRs but are not currently known to reside in the lysosome or have a lysosomal function (
Fig. 3
B; Group C). The exact reason for these apparent ‘false positives’ is unknown in this particular case, though there are several possible explanations. One is that they bear the N-M6P tag despite not being lysosomal proteins since it's known that some non-lysosomal glycoproteins can be modified with N-M6P [
12
]. Another is that they are bound by M6PRs despite not possessing the M6P tag since these receptors contain multiple domains and are known to bind non-M6P-bearing proteins (e.g. Insulin-like growth factor 2) in addition to N-M6P-tagged ligands [
39
]. Finally, it cannot be discounted that these hits arise due to non-specific binding of the M6PR or some other quirk that occurs under experimental conditions but not in vivo. Nevertheless, of particular interest is a rather large group of 31 non-lysosomal phosphoglycoproteins that were only detected by methods not reliant on M6PRs (
Fig. 3
B; Group D). The interpretation of this is that they carry phosphorylated oligosaccharides but do not interact with M6PRs. The sheer quantity of such proteins (almost as numerous as the ‘true positives’ in Group A) makes it unlikely that they were detected merely as the result of an experimental artefact, especially those that were detected in multiple independent studies (
Fig. 3
A). Therefore, the question must be posed: do they represent a discrete class of phosphoglycoproteins with properties that differ substantially from those of M6P-tagged lysosomal proteins and could there be a genuine biological reason why they were not recovered using M6PR affinity purification? For instance, perhaps they carry a different, non-canonical phosphate modification (NCPM), one that is not recognised by M6PRs.
A second and related question is why are so many non-lysosomal glycoproteins modified with PNGs in the brain? What kind of proteins are they, what is the nature of their phosphorylated oligosaccharides, and what is the function of their phosphate modifications if not to direct trafficking to the lysosome? These non-lysosomal phosphoglycoproteins are of particular interest given that, rare exceptions notwithstanding [
12
], conventional N-M6P is generally considered to be attached primarily to lysosomal hydrolases [
11
] yet no alternate oligosaccharide phosphorylation pathway has been described for other types of glycoproteins.
Table 2
lists 72 non-lysosomal phosphoglycoproteins identified in the mouse or human brain, as well as the composition of the PNGs found on them (when known). The majority are known transmembrane glycoproteins, many of which are expressed exclusively or predominantly in neural cell types (e.g. Nrxn1, Serpine2) where they participate in cellular processes important for proper development and functioning of the nervous system, such as neural cell adhesion, neurite outgrowth, differentiation, and myelination. Saliently, of all the organs Huang and colleagues investigated, the brain had the greatest number of organ-specific phosphoglycoproteins (
Fig. 4
) [
13
].

Notable examples from the latter group include Neurexin-1, Contactin-4, VAT1, AMOG, and Thy-1. To determine whether glycoproteins involved in particular cellular functions or pathways are enriched within this group, we performed GO annotation analysis of all the non-lysosomal phosphoglycoproteins lacking a verified N-M6P tag. However, the analysis did not yield any statistically significant results indicating that the candidate phosphoglycoproteins are involved in diverse biological processes and molecular functions (data not shown). Of course, the pool of glycoproteins modified with NCPMs could potentially be even larger than the small group mentioned above since it is not known whether canonical phosphate modifications (i.e. N-M6P) and NCPMs can coexist on the same molecule. If so, then it's possible that some of the N-M6P-tagged glycoproteins from
Table 2
may also carry NCPMs.
In summary, neural phosphoglycoproteins are a diverse group of proteins that can be found in a variety of subcellular compartments. In addition to the ubiquitous lysosomal phosphoglycoproteins, the brain contains a profusion of non-lysosomal phosphoglycoproteins, many of which are expressed only in neural cell types and are involved in fundamental neurobiological processes. Such tissue-specific phosphoglycoproteins are overrepresented in the brain compared to other organs. A substantial proportion of non-lysosomal neural phosphoglycoproteins can't be pulled down using M6PRs which may indicate that they bear unconventional phosphate modifications that differ from the canonical N-M6P tag.
3.3
Phospho-N-glycosites of neural glycoproteins
Mining the dataset generated by Huang and colleagues [
13
], we sought to learn more about the frequency, location, conservation, and microheterogeneity of phospho-N-glycosites present on neural glycoproteins. Across the 40 phosphoglycoproteins they identified in mouse brain, Huang et al. discovered a total of 50 such sites (
Fig. 5
). They occurred at a frequency of 1–3 phospho-N-glycosites per protein, though the majority of glycoproteins (80 %) contained only one (
Fig. 6
A). Strikingly, non-lysosomal glycoproteins only ever contained a single phospho-N-glycosite while multiple phospho-N-glycosites were present on nearly half (47 %) of the lysosomal glycoproteins (
Fig. 6
A) indicating that the N-glycosites of lysosomal glycoproteins more frequently bear PNGs than their non-lysosomal counterparts.

The number of distinct PNG compositions per phospho-N-glycosite (i.e. the number of phosphoglycoforms) varied from one to a maximum of seven, though the majority of phospho-N-glycosites (56 %) harboured only a single PNG composition (
Fig. 6
B). Interestingly, the microheterogeneity of phosphoglycoforms was significantly greater within lysosomal glycoproteins than non-lysosomal ones with the mean values for the two groups being 2.7 and 1.3 distinct PNG compositions per phospho-N-glycosite, respectively (
Fig. 6
C). Indeed, the most apparent difference between the two groups of glycoproteins was that the phospho-N-glycosites of lysosomal glycoproteins exhibited up to seven phosphoglycoforms while those of non-lysosomal glycoproteins never exceeded two. Nevertheless, it's evident from the work of Riley et al. that phospho-N-glycosites are not occupied exclusively by PNGs [
37
]. In addition to PNGs, they harbour many different kinds of non-phosphorylated N-glycans depending on the particular N-glycosylation site. These include hybrid and complex N-glycans that may be fucosylated and/or sialylated. The phospho-N-glycosite located at N42 of the Thy-1 protein is an example of extreme microheterogeneity harbouring a total of 37 unique N-glycan compositions of which only one is phosphorylated. We reasoned that perhaps lysosomal glycoproteins generally have greater levels of microheterogeneity across all glycoforms compared to their non-lysosomal counterparts and that the difference at the level of phosphoglycoforms is merely a reflection of this. To test this, we performed a similar analysis of the dataset generated by Riley
et al
. which, unlike that of Huang and colleagues, contains information about all N-glycans present on phospho-N-glycosites, not only phosphorylated ones [
37
]. Strikingly, when all glycoforms were considered, we found no statistically significant difference in phospho-N-glycosite microheterogeneity between lysosomal and non-lysosomal phosphoglycoproteins (
Fig. 6
D). Since phosphate residues are added primarily to OMNGs, we reasoned that the increased microheterogeneity of phosphoglycoforms on lysosomal proteins may stem from increased microheterogeneity of the underlying OMNGs. However, we found no statistically significant difference in phospho-N-glycosite microheterogeneity between lysosomal and non-lysosomal phosphoglycoproteins when only OMNGs were considered (
Fig. 6
E). Overall, these data suggest that the level of microheterogeneity of lysosomal and non-lysosomal proteins at phospho-N-glycosites is generally similar but differs specifically when it comes to phosphoglycoforms. We hypothesise, therefore, that the increased microheterogeneity of phosphoglycoforms on lysosomal glycoproteins arises from differences occurring at the level of N-glycan phosphorylation, not at the level of protein N-glycosylation.
To investigate whether differences in the amino acid sequences surrounding the phospho-N-glycosites might explain the disparity in PNG microheterogeneity between lysosomal and non-lysosomal glycoproteins, we searched for conserved motifs unique to these two groups of glycoproteins within the 25 positions either side of the phospho-N-glycosites using WebLogo [
40
]. However, we did not find any pattern of amino acid conservation in either group (
Fig. 6
F) suggesting that factors other than adjacent glycoprotein primary structure influence PNG microheterogeneity.
Although the level of total N-glycan microheterogeneity at phospho-N-glycosites was quantitatively similar between lysosomal and non-lysosomal proteins (
Fig. 6
D), we next investigated whether different types of N-glycans occur more frequently in one group versus the other by comparing the distribution of glycoforms according to glycan type. We found that mannosidic glycoforms occurred more frequently on the phospho-N-glycosites of lysosomal glycoproteins while the opposite was true for fucosylated and sialylated glycoforms which were more prevalent on non-lysosomal phospho-N-glycosites (
Fig. 6
G). The most striking difference was observed with sialylated glycoforms which accounted for only 1 % of glycoforms on lysosomal proteins, but 8 % of glycoforms on non-lysosomal proteins. Phosphoglycoforms occurred with approximately equal frequency in both groups constituting roughly 30 % of all glycoforms present on phospho-N-glycosites. However, we contend that this is almost certainly not an accurate reflection of the extent of phosphoglycoform occurrence in each group but arose because only a single PNG composition (Man6P) was detected in the study by Riley and colleagues [
37
] which nullified the difference in PNG microheterogeneity between lysosomal and non-lysosomal glycoproteins described above. Overall, these data suggest that compositionally more complex N-glycan types occur more commonly on the phospho-N-glycosites of non-lysosomal glycoproteins than those of lysosomal ones.
In summary, detailed analysis of the phospho-N-glycosites present on neural glycoproteins showed that there are several notable differences between lysosomal and non-lysosomal glycoproteins. Generally, lysosomal glycoproteins tend to have more phospho-N-glycosites per protein as well as greater microheterogeneity of phosphoglycoforms at these sites compared to their non-lysosomal counterparts. The increased microheterogeneity at lysosomal phospho-N-glycosites was specific to PNGs and was not observed for other types of N-glycan types. Together, these observations suggest that there are fundamental mechanistic differences in the way that the N-glycans of non-lysosomal glycoproteins are phosphorylated compared to those of lysosomal glycoproteins.
3.4
The phospho-N-glycome of the rodent brain
The brain boasts a distinctive N-glycome characterised by an abundance of organ-specific N-glycan compositions [
41
] (
Fig. 7
) and a variety of unique features not seen in other organs [
42
]. PNGs are part of this unique N-glycomic landscape but, compared to other N-glycan types, relatively little is known about their structural configuration or their abundance within the N-glycome. Early studies provided a glimpse into the basic properties of brain-derived PNGs. Brunngraber and colleagues found that the phosphate groups of rat brain glycans are present only on glycopeptides that bind to Concanavalin A, that they are bound exclusively to mannose residues, and that the N-glycans they are attached to have, on average, a 2:6:2 phosphate:hexose:N-acetylhexosamine compositional ratio, which corresponds to a diphosphorylated Man6 OMNG [
6
,
38
]. Nearly 40 years later, this latter approximation was proven to be highly accurate by contemporary analytical methods which determined that the two most frequently occurring PNG compositions in the mouse brain are Man6P and Man7P2 [
13
]. Other work has shown that the majority of neural PNGs are sensitive to Endoglycosidase H (Endo H) and thus belong to the hybrid or oligomannose class [
35
,
43
]. While all of these observations align with the attributes of conventional N-M6P-modified N-glycans, there is also evidence that the brain harbours some unusual PNGs. For example, Wing
et al
. suggest that a small proportion of sulphated brain N-glycans also contain phosphate residues [
43
]. If such sugars exist, they are likely to be hybrid N-glycans in which phosphates are attached to the mannoses of the α(1–6)-arm while the GlcNAc or glucuronic acid residues on the antenna of the α(1–3)-arm are modified with sulphate. However, we are not aware that such a structure has been conclusively identified in the brain to date. Perhaps the most remarkable discovery regarding the structural arrangement of phosphorylated oligosaccharides in the brain comes from nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) experiments that examined how phosphate residues are linked to the underlying N-glycans. By not limiting themselves to the singular N-M6P motif recognised by the M6PRs and instead opting for a broader approach, Brunngraber and colleagues demonstrated that, in addition to the canonical C-6 position, mannose residues on rat brain OMNGs can also be phosphorylated at the C-2, C-3, and C-4 positions [
44
]. These atypical phosphate linkages are not compatible with canonical N-M6P processing and suggest that, like the remainder of the brain's N-glycome, PNGs of the brain exhibit some peculiar attributes.

While this raises the possibility that they may be hybrid type N-glycans, the large number of hexoses (>6) makes this unlikely as such compositions are generally not observed with hybrid N-glycans (or any completed N-glycans, for that matter). Rather, we agree with the interpretation of other authors [
45

cis

trans

46

For the sake of simplicity, for the remainder of the article we have made the assumption that these N-glycans would eventually be processed to completion in the cell and we have therefore converted their compositions to those of their fully processed counterparts (e.g. H7N4P2 becomes H7N2P2 after removal of two masking GlcNAcs). Thus, considering only completely processed PNGs, we conclude that 11 discrete compositions were identified in the mouse brain. Given that each of the underlying OMNGs can have up to eight structural isomers [
47
,
48
] and that topological variation in the location of the phosphate residues (
Fig. 2

While this illustrates the underappreciated structural diversity present among this class of N-glycans, deep structural glycomics analysis [
49
] of brain PNGs will reveal the actual repertoire of structures expressed in the brain and hence the true extent of the brain phospho-N-glycome which is currently unknown.

] and can be phosphorylated in other cell types [
8
]. At this stage, it is not known whether the brain really does not contain any phosphorylated hybrid N-glycans or whether the method used is simply not effective at enriching this class of N-glycans.
Separating the neural phosphoglycoproteins according to their subcellular localisation revealed some salient differences in the composition of the lysosomal vs non-lysosomal phospho-N-glycome (
Fig. 8
D). Most notably, although the relative distributions of the five most frequent PNG compositions were similar across the two groups, there were some PNG compositions that were unique to lysosomal glycoproteins (H4N2P, H8N2P2, and H9N2P2) while others were specific to non-lysosomal glycoproteins (H3N2P, H9N2P, and H6N2P2). These differences in the makeup of the phospho-N-glycome contributed to a divergence in the degree of phosphorylation between the two groups: in the lysosomal group, 35 % of occurrences could be attributed to diphosphorylated PNGs while this figure was only 24 % for non-lysosomal glycoproteins. Together, these observations suggest that, in the brain, N-glycans are differentially phosphorylated depending on the eventual subcellular localisation of their carrier glycoproteins.
In summary, the phospho-N-glycome of the rodent brain is composed primarily of mono- and diphosphorylated OMNGs, though monophosphorylated PMNGs constitute a minor fraction. There is evidence that PNGs of the brain exhibit some unconventional structural properties, such as atypically linked phosphate residues. The spectrum of PNGs differs between lysosomal and non-lysosomal glycoproteins with particular PNG compositions found exclusively on one type of protein or the other. Overall, these findings are compatible with the concept that distinct N-glycan phosphorylation mechanisms act on different classes of neural glycoproteins.
3.5
Abundance of phospho-N-glycans in the brain
As stated earlier, the brain is relatively rich in N-M6P compared to other tissues [
9
]. But what fraction of the brain N-glycome is modified by phosphate residues and what is the contribution of NCPMs, if any? In many N-glycomic studies of the brain, PNGs are not mentioned at all. Even if the analyses were limited to cell surface glycoproteins, some quantity of PNGs would be expected since they are known to occur at the plasma membrane of neural cell types [
38
]. Occasionally, PNGs are identified merely as minor structures but their abundance is not quantified, the implication being that it's so low as to be negligible. In some studies, various purification strategies are used to enrich for phosphorylated glycans which are then analysed in isolation [
13
]. While such methods are currently necessary if detailed characterisation of this subclass of glycans is desired, they don't provide any information regarding their abundance within the context of the total N-glycome.
Among the studies in which the abundance of PNGs in the brain was quantified, the estimates vary extensively, ranging from <1 % to >25 % of the total brain N-glycome (see
Table 3
). It's likely that this enormous variability can at least partly be explained by differences in the analytical methods used in different studies. For example, the upper limit of this range (25 %), which is an outlier in the context of the broader literature, comes from a study by Wing et al. in which sensitivity to hydrofluoric acid was used as a proxy for the presence of phosphate [
43
]. In more recently published studies using mass-based methods of N-glycan analysis, the figure is generally much lower at <3 % of total N-glycans (
Table 3
). Then again, one must keep in mind that the abundance of PNGs is likely to be underestimated by some methods, such as matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionisation (MALDI) MS (especially when used in positive mode), because acidic carbohydrates tend to undergo fragmentation during MALDI ionisation [
50
]. In summary, a clear consensus regarding the precise abundance of PNGs in the brain is still lacking due to a relatively small number of published studies presenting inconsistent results. Nevertheless, the weight of evidence suggests that they make up only a relatively minor fraction of the total brain N-glycome, probably somewhere in the order of 3 %. This puts the abundance of individual PNG species well below 1 % of the total brain N-glycome which, for most standard analyses that don't make use of specialised methods for enrichment of phosphorylated glycoproteins, likely places them near the limit of detection. Taking advantage of such methods [
13
,
45
,
51
], and the continual development and refinement of even more effective methods, will undoubtedly enable more accurate quantification of this elusive class of N-glycans and shed light on other structural and biochemical elements of their abstruseness.

To complicate matters further, it appears that PNG content in the brain is not static. 54

56
]. These findings are not necessarily incompatible. It's possible that the decline in PNG content occurs after the age of 44 or that PNG abundance is differentially regulated across brain regions. Overall, the limited data that are available indicate that phosphorylation of brain N-glycans probably changes as a function of age, though the precise details regarding the trajectory are lacking and more research is required.
In summary, current evidence indicates that PNGs make up a relatively small fraction of the total brain N-glycome, though the exact composition and abundance of the phospho-N-glycome varies across brain regions and life stages. The potential contribution of NCPMs to the overall phospho-N-glycome will need to be investigated using fine-resolution methods that can distinguish between alternative types of phosphate modifications that differ subtly at the nanostructural level.
3.6
Functional significance of the PNGs attached to non-lysosomal neural glycoproteins
What is the significance of the phosphate modifications found on non-lysosomal neural glycoproteins if their purpose is evidently not to guide these proteins to the lysosome? In short, their biological importance is largely unknown. Given the lack of data on this topic, we can only speculate as to their probable functions. On the molecular level, it's likely that these phosphate modifications serve to regulate the activity or half-life of their carrier proteins. For transmembrane or secreted phosphoglycoproteins tagged with N-M6P, this could occur via interactions with the membrane-bound cation-independent M6PR (CI-M6PR) which can be situated on the cell surface and can activate or internalise extracellular ligands [
39
,
57
]. However, the biological roles of possible NCPMs are completely unknown. The possibility that they serve a similar regulatory purpose and, if so, the identification of their cognate receptors and mechanism of action, are areas that should be explored.
4
Evidence for non-canonical phosphorylation of mannose residues in the brain
We have demonstrated in the previous section that neural phosphoglycoproteins, as well as the PNGs they bear, exhibit several distinctive features that do not completely mesh with what is currently known about phosphorylation of oligosaccharides via the canonical N-M6P pathway. Based on these observations, we hypothesise that, in addition to the conventional pathway, the brain employs an alternative oligosaccharide phosphorylation mechanism for the modification of a discrete set of glycoproteins and that the biochemical properties of the resulting phosphate modification differ from those of N-M6P. Specifically, we propose that certain non-lysosomal glycoproteins of the brain may be modified by NCPMs. The evidence underpinning this hypothesis is discussed in the following sections.
4.1
Phosphoglycoproteins are particularly prevalent in the brain
Due to an unusually stable N-M6P tag, the brain is relatively rich in N-M6P-containing glycoproteins [
9
] which are primarily hydrolases that are confined to the lysosomes [
34
] and are more or less ubiquitous throughout different tissue types. However, many other types of phosphoglycoproteins are expressed in the brain. In the study by Huang et al., considerably more phosphoglycoproteins were identified in the brain and lungs than any of the other organs that were analysed [
13
] (
Fig. 4
). What was particular about the brain, though, was that it had by far the greatest proportion of organ-specific phosphoglycoproteins (
Fig. 4
). In fact, the 19 brain-specific phosphoglycoproteins that were detected comprise 41 % of all the organ-specific phosphoglycoproteins identified in that study which signifies that the brain is overrepresented as a source of non-ubiquitous phosphoglycoproteins. To some extent, this is unquestionably a reflection of the brain's distinctive proteome. Various studies of the mouse [
58
,
59
] and human [
60
,
61
] proteomes have indicated that the brain expresses the greatest number of organ-specific proteins of any organ. However, when all organs are considered, there is not a clear correlation between the number of organ-specific proteins identified in those studies and the number of phosphoglycoproteins identified by Huang and colleagues. This suggests that the relationship between the two is complex and that there may be tissue-specific factors that modulate the phosphorylation of protein-linked oligosaccharides.
4.2
Non-lysosomal proteins in the brain are extensively modified with phosphorylated glycans
The large number of phosphoglycoproteins found in the brain is not the only salient feature - it is also the types of glycoproteins that are modified with PNGs that stands out. As discussed earlier, the canonical N-M6P modification serves to direct protein trafficking to the lysosomes and is hence found primarily on lysosomal proteins [
11
,
17
,
18
]. It is striking, therefore, that a prominent feature of the neural proteome is the extensive phosphorylation of N-glycans attached to non-lysosomal glycoproteins (
Table 2
) [
13
,
[36]
,
[37]
,
[38]
]. Indeed, when unbiased methods are used (i.e. those not reliant on M6PRs), non-lysosomal proteins constitute 44 %−58 % of all identified neural phosphoglycoproteins (
Table 1
) which argues against this being an anomaly and instead suggests that it is an intrinsic feature of the brain's proteome.
The observation that a considerable proportion of neural phosphoglycoproteins are not being delivered to the lysosome raises several possibilities. One possibility, as suggested by others [
13
], is that these non-lysosomal glycoproteins bear a conventional N-M6P tag but that this type of modification has diverse functions. What these alternative functions could be is currently unclear and, to the best of our knowledge, no other functions for N-M6P have been identified other than lysosomal targeting of tagged proteins. Alternatively, it is possible that non-lysosomal phosphoglycoproteins of the brain bear an unconventional type of phosphate modification that differs from N-M6P. In support of the latter explanation, a sizeable subset of neural phosphoglycoproteins cannot be pulled down using M6PRs (
Fig. 3
) suggesting that the phosphate modifications they bear are structurally dissimilar to the N-M6P tag – a point elaborated upon further in the following section. Overall, the large number of non-lysosomal neural phosphoglycoproteins that evade recognition by M6PRs suggests that they may carry NCPMs and hence aligns with the idea that an alternative oligosaccharide phosphorylation pathway may be active in the brain.
4.3
The manner in which lysosomal and non-lysosomal glycoproteins of the brain are modified by PNGs differs
If we were to accept the null hypothesis, namely that the oligosaccharides of lysosomal and non-lysosomal neural glycoproteins are phosphorylated via the same mechanism, then we would not expect to find any significant differences between the phospho-N-glycoprofiles of the two groups. However, this is not the case. Instead, our investigations have shown that when considering the frequency, microheterogeneity, and types of PNGs attached to phospho-N-glycosites, there are discernible differences between lysosomal and non-lysosomal glycoproteins of the brain. The former tend to have a greater number of phospho-N-glycosites and these sites typically display a greater diversity of PNGs compared to the latter (
Fig. 6
). This difference in PNG compositional diversity is not due to an underlying difference in the microheterogeneity of OMNGs, or even the total N-glycan microheterogeneity, at these sites (
Fig. 6
d-E) which suggests that the difference is specific to PNGs and therefore occurs at the level of glycan phosphorylation rather than protein glycosylation. In addition, the two sets of glycoproteins exhibit divergent PNG profiles with particular PNG compositions occurring exclusively on either lysosomal or non-lysosomal glycoproteins (
Fig. 8
D). Therefore, when taken together, these observations suggest that there are fundamental differences in the biochemical mechanisms leading to phosphorylation of N-glycans on neural lysosomal glycoproteins versus non-lysosomal ones.
4.4
Phosphate groups on neural N-glycans may be atypically linked
The discovery by Brunngraber et al. that phosphate residues can be linked to N-glycans of the brain via C-2, C-3, or C-4 linkages [
44
] is perhaps the most convincing evidence for the existence of an alternative form of oligosaccharide phosphorylation in the brain. The canonical N-M6P modification is defined by its C-6 linkage. Thus, any other type of linkage is indicative of a structurally distinct phosphate modification that may arise through the action of an independent pathway that uses alternative molecular machinery. Regrettably, this work has not been significantly elaborated on in the intervening five decades and the findings are yet to be independently verified. Consequently, we know almost nothing about these atypically phosphorylated brain oligosaccharides or the phosphoglycoproteins to which they are attached. Nevertheless, their unearthing is evidence of an alternative phosphorylation mechanism that generates NCPMs on neural glycoproteins.
4.5
Phosphorylated paucimannose N-glycans have been detected in the brain
Another indication of a possible non-canonical phosphorylation mechanism is the presence of phosphate residues on oligosaccharides that are not typically phosphorylated, such as PMNGs. This class of sugars is generally regarded as uncommon in vertebrates [
62
], though numerous studies have confirmed that these simple structures are present in the brain, albeit at relatively low abundance [
53
,
[63]
,
[64]
,
[65]
,
[66]
,
[67]
,
[68]
], and that they have functional roles in neurobiology [
69
]. Phosphorylated paucimannose structures, including Man3P, have been detected on rodent brain glycoproteins [
13
,
53
] or in neural cell types [
54
] in several studies that have employed unbiased MS-based methods. These methods provide a more complete view of the phospho-N-glycome because they do not specifically target the N-M6P modification through the use of M6PRs nor are they restricted to the analysis of certain glycan subtypes via the use of selective enzymes (such as hybrid and OMNGs in the case of Endo H). The detection of Man3P by such methods is noteworthy as it raises the possibility that one or more of the residues of the pentasaccharide N-glycan core can be phosphorylated, though it's unclear at this stage which ones. This evidence conflicts with what is generally known about canonical N-M6P phosphorylation which, as mentioned earlier, has so far not been reported to occur on any of the core mannoses and is thought to require the presence of α(1–2)-linked mannose residues [
26
] (which are not present on PMNGs).
Could it be that phosphorylated PMNGs have been detected in the brain simply because PMNGs are expressed in the brain but not in other tissues? Essentially all the studies characterising the fundamental biochemistry of N-glycan phosphorylation were carried out in non-neural tissues, such as the spleen [
27
], liver [
26
], or in vitro cultures of various blood cell types [
7
,
8
,
14
], which, unlike the brain, are not renowned for their expression of PMNGs. One could presume, therefore, that the lack of phosphorylated PMNGs in non-neural tissues is simply due to the absence of PMNGs in these tissues generally. But this is not the case. It transpires that PMNGs are expressed in all of the above tissue types [
62
]. Indeed, Howard and colleagues were able to isolate the lysosomal β-glucuronidase enzyme from human spleen and, by indiscriminately releasing all N-glycans from the glycoprotein, demonstrate that it is modified by both paucimannose and OMNGs ranging from Man2 to Man9 [
70
]. Interestingly, while the larger species (Man4 to Man8) were detected in both their phosphorylated and neutral forms, no phosphate was detected on the smaller paucimannose structures. At first glance, this evidence seems to reinforce the assertion that N-M6P cannot be added to the core mannoses of lysosomal enzyme N-glycans and thus adds weight to the hypothesis that an alternative, non-canonical phosphorylation pathway enables the phosphorylation of core mannoses on neural glycoproteins. However, it is important to consider that Howard et al. did not employ MS in their study [
71
] since this further underscores how studies in which Man3P either was or wasn't detected can be partitioned according to the analytical method used.
Given that the Man3P glycan has thus far only been described in MS-based studies [
13
,
53
,
54
], it's possible that its presence is merely a method-related artefact. For example, it may arise from in-source fragmentation of larger phosphorylated OMNGs. While this seems unlikely due to the relative instability of phosphate groups [
72
] compared to the relatively more stable inter-mannose glycosidic bonds of OMGS, the possibility cannot be discounted without structural proof. So far, the precise structural configuration of the Man3P glycan (or glycans, if there are multiple isoforms) has not been determined. Detection of Man3P using a non-MS-based method would strengthen the case that it is a genuine PNG. Alternatively, deep structural glycomics would reveal the configuration of the Man3P molecule and thus unequivocally resolve the dilemma. All things considered, it is not yet clear whether phosphorylated PMNGs genuinely represent an atypical phosphate modification arising from non-canonical N-glycan phosphorylation pathways or merely a technical artefact and thus further research is needed to clarify the situation.
In summary, in this section we have synthesised the various pieces of evidence that collectively point towards the existence of an alternative N-glycan phosphorylation mechanism that appears to be active in the brain, especially for non-lysosomal glycoproteins.

Discussion
Perhaps driven by the discovery and characterisation of the N-M6P pathway, many early studies of oligosaccharide phosphorylation used M6PRs or selective endoglycosidases (e.g. Endo H) as molecular tools in their experiments. This restricted approach may have inadvertently resulted in other classes of PNGs being overlooked or, in subsequent studies when they may have been detected, misattributed to N-M6P. The advent of sensitive, unbiased methods for the analysis of phosphoglycoproteins and their modifications has, in recent times, changed the way that these molecules are studied and has ushered in a new era of large datasets containing an abundance of information regarding the occurrence, position, and molecular composition of PNGs. The application of such contemporary methods to neural phosphoglycoproteins has revealed a number of fascinating insights regarding the way that N-glycans are phosphorylated in the brain, some of which are not compatible with our current understanding of the canonical N-M6P pathway and require us to re-evaluate our perspective on oligosaccharide phosphorylation in brain cells. In this paper we have asked the question: could an alternative N-glycan phosphorylation pathway explain the distinctive features of neural phosphoglycoproteins that are at odds with what is known about the conventional N-M6P pathway?
At this stage, the cellular and molecular origins of these differences are completely unknown and will require further investigation. Nevertheless, here we can speculate about the biochemical properties that distinguish the non-canonical N-glycan phosphorylation pathway from the classical one with the goal that this will inform future research efforts on the topic. One possibility is that there is an enzyme other than GlcNAc-1-phosphotransferase that catalyses the addition of NCPMs to acceptor glycoproteins. For example, perhaps a hitherto uncharacterised mannose kinase which generates NCPMs on brain N-glycans in a manner similar to the phosphorylation of O-mannosyl glycans by POMK. There are four hexokinases known to be expressed in the brain and the action of these enzymes results in efficient phosphorylation of cytosolic mannose in neuronal cells [
73
]. Although this typically gives rise to M6P, could it be possible that a brain-specific hexokinase variant generates a different type of phosphorylated mannose molecule?
An alternative mechanism for the generation of such atypically phosphorylated monosaccharides may come in the form of non-canonical isomerase activity. Members of the phosphohexomutase family of enzymes, which catalyse the intramolecular transfer of phosphate groups within sugar molecules, would logically be the most likely candidates for such activity. Phosphomannomutases (PMMs) and phosphoglucomutases (PGMs) participate in the reversible interconversion between the 1-phosphate and 6-phosphate forms of mannose and glucose, respectively (
Fig. 2
D). PMM1 and PMM2 are two non-redundant paralogs that share 66 % homology at the amino acid level and have partially overlapping expression patterns and enzymatic activities [
73
]. These enzymes each participate in multiple reactions, albeit with differing kinetics. While their favoured substrates for isomerisation are phosphorylated mannoses, both enzymes also possess PGM activity while PMM1 additionally exhibits both glucose- and mannose-1,6-bisphosphatase activity [
74
,
75
]. To complicate matters, the presence of particular cofactors can differentially alter the kinetics of these enzymes favouring one reaction over another. For example, inosine monophosphate simultaneously inhibits the mutase activity but enhances the phosphatase activity of PMM1 while having no effect on PPM2 [
75
]. Therefore, it is clear that the physiological context is important in determining which reaction will take place and with what efficiency. Are there some as yet undetermined conditions, e.g. in certain cell types in vivo, under which these enzymes could exhibit non-canonical phosphomutase activity?
Studies of transcript and protein expression in rodents have shown that although both genes are expressed in the developing brain, expression of PMM2 is substantially reduced in the adult brain [
73
,
[76]
,
[77]
,
[78]
]. Low expression of the
PMM2
transcript has also been reported in the adult human brain [
79
]. In this respect, the brain is unique in that PMM1 is the major PMM isozyme and accounts for around two-thirds of PMM activity while in all other organs PMM2 is the dominant isozyme [
77
]. Mutations in PMM1 have thus far not been associated with human disease while partially inactivating mutations in PMM2 lead to the most common congenital disorder of glycosylation (CDG), PMM2-CDG, which in almost all cases presents with neurological involvement [
80
,
81
]. This is paralleled in mouse models where deletion of PMM1 apparently has no deleterious effects [
73
] while hypomorphic mutations in PMM2 result in reduced N-glycosylation of proteins [
82
] and complete loss of PMM2 activity causes embryonic lethality [
83
]. It is still not known why PMM1 can't compensate for the loss of PMM2 even though their expression and enzymatic activity overlap. Despite a significant amount of research into the action of PMM1 and PMM2, it's clear that there are still crucial details regarding the specificity, kinetics, and interplay of these two paralogs under physiological conditions that elude us.
Could there be a brain-specific phosphomannomutase variant that generates a structurally distinct phosphorylated mannose? Interestingly, in a study by Cromphout and colleagues, immunoblot analysis showed that the adult mouse brain expresses one or possibly several lower molecular weight forms of PMM1 that are not found in the embryonic brain, nor in any of the other nine tissues that were investigated [
73
]. The significance of this smaller variant is unknown and it was not detected in another study by the same authors [
78
]. Nevertheless, more recent analysis using single-cell transcriptomics data has verified that mouse
Pmm1
gene does indeed undergo alternative splicing in the brain and that multiple isoforms can be present, sometimes simultaneously within the same cell type [
84
]. The enzymatic activity and biological function of these alternatively spliced isoforms (and those of other phosphohexomutases) are presently unknown but they represent a source of variation that could unveil novel and surprising biochemical activity which may have a bearing on NCPMs in the brain. These possibilities will need to be investigated experimentally and will go a long way towards either validating or refuting the proposed hypothesis.
5.1
Technical challenges associated with the analysis of phosphorylated oligosaccharides
One of the reasons for the relative scarcity of information concerning this class of glycans is that their analysis poses significant technical challenges, in large part due to their inherent structural complexity, chemical instability, and low abundance. To combat the latter issue, various techniques have been developed to enrich phosphorylated glycans from complex biological matrices where they typically make up a small fraction of the analytes. These include the use of titanium dioxide, immobilized metal affinity chromatography (IMAC), or serotonin-immobilised columns [
13
,
45
,
85
]. Although these methods are effective in boosting their detection, they can suffer from biases and inefficiencies, leading to partial or selective recovery of phosphorylated species. Another drawback is that the enriched glycans are then analysed in isolation devoid of native context. Researchers who wish to investigate phosphorylated carbohydrates in intact biological samples (e.g. in the context of a whole N-glycome) still face numerous obstacles. Below we discuss some of the major challenges associated with analysis of phosphoglycans as well as strategies to mitigate them, if applicable.
Firstly, phosphorylation at specific residues introduces additional heterogeneity to glycan structures, which are already inherently complex. Variation in the positioning of phosphate groups increases the number of structural isomers which complicates their separation and identification in complex mixtures. It also introduces a negative charge to mannosidic N-glycans that are generally neutral (with the exception of hybrid N-glycans which can carry negatively charged residues such as sulphate groups, sialic acids, or glucuronic acids). This can hinder detection in some experimental setups (e.g. positive mode MS). For this reason, negative mode MS is generally preferred when analysis of native phosphoglycans is desired, though the trade-off is that detection of neutral oligosaccharides may suffer due to poorer ionisation [
86
].
Secondly, phosphate groups can be labile under certain experimental conditions. This can result in their loss during MS analysis and is particularly problematic for certain kinds of high-energy ionisation techniques, for example MALDI, where the phosphate groups are susceptible to in-source fragmentation [
50
,
87
]. Strategies to stabilise the phosphate groups include permethylation or neutral exchange of protons with metal cations (e.g. Na
+
/H
+
) [
87
,
88
]. These approaches have the added benefit of neutralising the negative charge of the phosphate group, thus enabling better ionisation in positive mode MS. When it comes to tandem MS, the use of electron-aided fragmentation techniques, like electron-transfer dissociation (ETD) or electron-capture dissociation (ECD), is recommended to preserve phosphate groups during fragmentation analysis.
Thirdly, the phosphate moieties of phosphoglycans can interact with the stainless steel components present in liquid chromatography instruments which can adversely affect recovery and/or lead to inconsistent results [
89
,
90
,
91
,
92
]. This can be remedied by the addition of ion-pairing reagents (e.g. triethylamine) to the mobile phase, separating at high pH, or ensuring that the sample flow path is bio-inert (i.e. metal-free).
Finally, even if these elusive molecules are successfully detected in the laboratory, another challenge lies in the lack of comprehensive databases and standards for phosphorylated glycans, making structural elucidation difficult. The development of these resources is necessary if we are to fast-track progress in understanding these biologically significant, yet enigmatic, sugars. In short, the combination of all these factors means that analysis of phosphoglycans places additional demands on researchers above and beyond what is typically required when only non-phosphorylated forms are considered and underscores the need for careful planning and execution of experimental strategies.
5.2
Limitations of current methods for the analysis of phosphoglycoproteins
The datasets that formed the basis of our supplemental analyses were generated using contemporary analytical methods that are robust and effective, though they are not without their limitations. For example, while the study by Huang et al. provides an in-depth exploration of phosphorylated oligosaccharides, one of the major limitations of the resulting dataset is that it deals exclusively with PNGs [
13
]. Although some non-phosphorylated glycopeptides persist following dual-mode phosphoglycopeptide enrichment, particularly those carrying negatively charged (e.g. sialylated) glycans, these were excluded from the analysis and not considered further because the authors were focussed solely on phosphorylated oligosaccharides (personal communication, Junfeng Huang; April 2024). Consequently, the resulting dataset does not provide any information regarding the relationship between PNGs and other types of N-glycans on particular phospho-N-glycosites or within the broader context of the total N-glycome. Also, the dataset was restricted to only one particular subclass of phosphorylated glycans, namely PNGs. Even though glycoproteins carrying POGs are known to be expressed in the brain, with α-DG being the most prominent example [
5
,
31
], the authors did not report on the detection of glycoproteins bearing such glycans using their dual-mode affinity approach. This may indicate that further refinement of phosphoglycoproteins enrichment strategies is necessary to achieve total coverage of the brain phosphoglycome (i.e. both PNGs and POGs).
Similarly, the dataset generated by Riley and colleagues [
37
] also has its limitations. While their unbiased method captures all N-glycan types, coverage of the phosphoglycome was poor due to the lack of phosphoglycoprotein enrichment and was limited to a single PNG composition (Man6P). Thus, it seems that, at present, an ideal method for the comprehensive analysis of the total N-glycome, which includes in-depth characterisation of low-abundance glycans such as PNGs, is lacking. Nevertheless, the continual refinement of existing methods as well as the development of new and advanced platforms brings us ever closer to this goal. For example, Huang and colleagues have recently described an improved fractionation strategy that enables glycoproteomic analysis of neutral, sialylated, and phosphorylated glycopeptides from the same biological sample in parallel [
45
]. The application of such novel methods to the analysis of brain phosphoglycoproteins will undoubtedly provide new perspectives on the contribution of PNGs to the brain's N-glycome.
Ultimately, our understanding of oligosaccharide phosphorylation in the CNS is limited by the technology available to us. Since no single currently-available technique can answer all questions, thorough characterisation of the neural phospho-N-glycome, including any potential NCPMs, at a quantitative and structural level will likely require a combination of several methodological approaches. For example, fluorescence-based glycan detection for sensitive and stoichiometric quantification of relative abundance, site-specific phosphoglycoproteomics for information about glycan occupancy and microheterogeneity, as well as deep structural glycomics analysis, such as NMR or tandem MS [
74
], for information about molecular structure and intramolecular linkages. While such multi-pronged strategies are seldom used at the present time due to the significant technical challenge of implementing them, they will be necessary if we are to shed light on the unknowns of the phospho-N-glycome.
5.3
Directions for future research
The various findings summarised here indicate that our understanding of brain phosphoglycoproteins is far from complete and leave us with many unanswered questions. Here we contemplate these questions which steer us towards the most pressing knowledge gaps that need to be addressed to make headway. First and foremost, could an alternative N-glycan phosphorylation pathway explain the distinctive features of neural phosphoglycoproteins that are at odds with what is known about the conventional N-M6P pathway? If so, what is the cellular machinery involved in the generation of NCPMs and what distinguishes them, structurally and biochemically, from conventional phosphate modifications? How widespread are NCPMs and does their expression vary across different brain regions, developmental stages, or physiological conditions? What is the neurobiological significance of NCPMs? And, finally, is this a phenomenon that is specific to the brain or do other organs also exhibit non-canonical phosphorylation of oligosaccharides?
The most direct way to test the proposed hypothesis would be to ask whether the phosphorylation of oligosaccharides attached to non-lysosomal proteins of the brain is affected in the absence of N-M6P processing. Such experiments could be performed in mouse models of ML II in which the GlcNAc-1-phosphotransferase enzyme complex is inactive [
23
,
25
]. If it remains unaltered, this would be strong evidence in support of the hypothesis which would open the door to follow up structural and functional studies aimed at uncovering how these NCPMs differ from the conventional kind and how they regulate the function the glycoproteins to which they are attached. This would most likely require the development of new molecular tools or assays for the specific detection, quantification, and manipulation of NCPMs. Given that structural analyses with linkage-level resolution are the only way to unambiguously differentiate between N-M6P and other types of mannose phosphorylation, such approaches will need to be employed at the level of individual candidate glycoproteins to unequivocally confirm the presence of NCPMs. Defining the molecular structure and biochemical properties of N-glycans bearing NCPMs would require the use of advanced structural techniques, such as NMR, tandem MS, or possibly even X-ray crystallography if the molecules prove amenable for such a method. Functional assays may rely on the identification of cognate receptors that interact with NCPMs or the identification of inhibitors that block their production. It's possible that strides towards understanding the biological role of NCPMs may be achieved most easily at the level of individual phosphoglycoproteins by interfering with N-glycan phosphorylation at specific phospho-N-glycosites or by performing functional assays with recombinantly expressed phosphoglycoproteins that have been dephosphorylated to remove their NCPMs. In summary, while this field of research represents fertile ground for exploration, it's clear that there is much work to be done if we are to unravel the complexity of oligosaccharide phosphorylation in the brain.
6
Conclusions
In summary, here we have presented evidence suggesting the existence of an alternative, non-canonical phosphorylation mechanism for N-glycans in the brain that operates in addition to the well-characterized N-M6P pathway. Through a review of the literature and our independent analyses of existing datasets, we have demonstrated that neural glycoproteins, particularly non-lysosomal ones, frequently carry PNGs that exhibit features distinct from those seen in the conventional N-M6P modification on a number of levels. Key differences in the composition, frequency, and microheterogeneity of PNGs between lysosomal and non-lysosomal glycoproteins support the proposed hypothesis, along with evidence of atypically-linked phosphate residues found on mannose residues of neural glycoproteins.
The precise biochemical mechanisms driving this putative non-canonical phosphorylation remain unclear and efforts should be directed towards more detailed structural characterisation of the atypical PNGs, identification of their potential receptors, and exploration of their roles in neural physiology. Understanding the functional implications of these distinctive phosphate modifications, especially in relation to non-lysosomal glycoproteins, is an important area for future research that may provide valuable insights into brain health and disease. Achieving a clearer understanding of these unconventional modifications will likely require the application and continued development of advanced analytical techniques, such as deep structural glycomics and functional glycomics. Ultimately, further elucidation of the non-canonical N-glycan phosphorylation pathway may reveal novel regulatory mechanisms relevant to neurobiology and expand our understanding of oligosaccharide phosphorylation beyond lysosomal targeting.
Overall, our work suggests that phosphorylation of N-glycans in the brain may be more complex and more diverse than previously recognised which compels us to re-evaluate our perspective on oligosaccharide phosphorylation in the CNS. We envisage that this report will stimulate thought, discussion, and research in the direction illuminated by this hypothesis. To fully explore the complexity of the neural phospho-N-glycome, a broader perspective is necessary. We urge researchers to remain open-minded when studying phosphorylated oligosaccharides and to explore possibilities beyond typical N-M6P-containing glycoproteins. More broadly, our work highlights the need for researchers to critically assess existing dogma and to exercise caution in adhering too rigidly to established frameworks.
