Introduction
Primary Sjögren’s disease (pSjD) is a systemic chronic autoimmune disease characterized by by oral and ocular dryness, caused by lymphocytic infiltrates and autoimmune damage to the salivary gland and lacrimal glands [
1
]. Several criteria have been developed to classify pSjD, based symptoms of clinical dryness, serological autoantibodies, and histopathological tissue analysis [
2
–
4
]. In the most recent 2016 ACR/EULAR criteria, higher weights were given to anti-SSA/Ro and minor salivary gland (MSG) biopsy, and the presence of anti-SSA/Ro antibody or a positive result observed in salivary gland tissue biopsy is mandatory [
5
]. According to mainstream classifications, a positive MSG biopsy is determined by the presence of focal lymphocytic sialadenitis along with a focus score ≥ 1 per 4 mm [
2
] [
2
–
4
]. MSG biopsy is critical, especially in seronegative subjects [
4
]. Despite that MSG biopsy involves only a small incision in the lower lip and is easily performed under local anesthesia, it is an invasive approach that brings complications such as bleeding, pain, and temporary sensory defect, sometimes even more serious like tissue necrosis and constant impaired sensory defect. [
6
,
7
]. These possible complications may discourage patients from cooperating with rheumatologists to perform this procedure, which can undoubtedly impede the timely treatment [
8
]. Thus, there is a growing need for an easier and non-invasive prediction method for pSjD.
In recent years, saliva has been drawing attention as a biospecimen for diagnostic tests because its collection is simple, convenient, painless, and safe [
9
]. Moreover, oral fluid has been reported to reflect the local environment of salivary glands, the target organ of pSjD. [
10
,
11
]. As a result, many researchers have been exploring biomarkers for pSjD in the saliva, including enzymes, secretory proteins, cytokines, and immune-system-related molecules through mass spectrometry [
12
–
14
]. However, whether these salivary proteins have the potential as markers to help classify pSjD remains elusive.
In this study, we performed quantitative mass spectrometry-based protein profiling to identify differentially abundant proteins. We selected 5 proteins, C3, CFB, CLU, NE, and CALR, which were upregulated in the saliva of patients with pSjD as potential biomarkers. Saliva was collected from a derivation cohort of patients with suspected pSjD, and levels of C3, CFB, CLU, NE, and CALR were measured. We evaluated the diagnostic value of these salivary proteins and their combination with the non-invasive basal clinical indices, aiming to establish an ideal non-invasive model for the prediction of pSjD.
Materials and methods
Patients and study design
This study was designed to identify non-invasive markers that could help diagnose pSjD. A total of 258 inpatients from the Department of Rheumatology and Clinical Immunology, the First Affiliated Hospital of Xiamen University between January 2021 and August 2022 were selected in our study. All of them were suspected pSjD patients when first admitted into the hospital and have been performed MSG biopsies which is a critical inspection for the classification of pSjD. Patients who fulfilled the 2016 ACR/EULAR classification criteria for pSjD were classified as the pSjD group while the remaining patients who did not meet the criteria were identified as the non-pSjD group and were used as controls. Patients with other connective tissue diseases and malignancies; sarcoidosis and amyloidosis; history of bone marrow transplantation and graft-versus-host disease; history of head and neck radiotherapy; concurrent AIDS, hepatitis B virus, and hepatitis C virus infections; severe dryness who could not provide saliva samples were excluded. Prior to their involvement in this study, all patients were given written informed consent. The study was reviewed and approved by the Local Ethical Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xiamen University.
Samples and biological parameters
Saliva samples were obtained when patients underwent salivary flow rate measurement which was usually performed at 8:00 pm or a time when they had fasted for at least 2 h. Brushing teeth or smoking was also not permitted 2 h before the performance. In the first 15 min, unstimulated salivary flow rate (uSFR) was measured. Then stimulated salivary flow rate (sSFR) was measured with a Vitamin C tablet in the mouth for 15 min. To collect saliva, patients were instructed to push their heads forward and drop it into a sterile container without making any effort with their mouths. To avoid protein degradation and arguments of time difference between different samples, protease inhibitors (Roche, Germany) were added to the collecting container in advance. After centrifuging the saliva samples at 9500 g for 10 min at 4 °C, debris and cells were removed. The supernatants were gathered and stored at -80 °C for further testing. White blood cell (WBC) count, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), serum C3, C4, IgG, C-reactive protein (CRP), antinuclear antibody (ANA) titer, anti-SSA/Ro60, anti-SSA/Ro52, anti-SSB/La, and Schirmer’s test, were collected from the patient’s clinical examination and laboratory results.
Mass spectrometry
Unstimulated saliva samples were used in proteomics experiments. Saliva samples were first precipitated overnight by cold acetone and re-dissolved in 8 M urea. Protein concentration in each sample was detected by BCA assay and adjusted to be consistent. 100 µg protein from each sample was subjected to in-solution trypsin digestion and dried. Label-free quantification proteomics analyses were performed on an EASY-nLC 1200 (Thermo SCIENTIFIC) coupled to a Orbitrap Fusion Lumos (Thermo SCIENTIFIC) equipped with an EASY-IC ion source. The peptides were dissolved in 10 µl 0.1% formic acid and were auto-sampled directly onto a homemade C18 column (35 cm X 75 μm i.d., 2.5 μm 100Å). Flow rate of 300 nl/min was used to elute samples for 120 min using linear gradients of 3–35% acetonitrile in 0.1% formic acid. The raw data underwent analysis by Proteome Discoverer 2.2 software against UniProt database.
Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
The candidate biomarkers (C3, CFB, CLU, CALR, NE) levels in saliva were determined using commercially available human ELISA kits. C3 ELISA kit (ab108823) was purchased from Abcam (USA), CALR ELISA kit (CSB-E09787h) was obtained from Cusabio Biotech (Wuhan, China), and CFB ELISA kit (NBP2-60564), CLU ELISA kit (DCLU00), and NE ELISA kit (DY9167) were purchased from R&D systems (Minneapolils, MN).
Statistical analysis
The statistical analyses were conducted using MedCalc (version 20, MedCalc software), SPSS (version 25, IBM), and GraphPad Prism (version 9, GraphPad software). As appropriate, the non-normally distributed data were compared using Mann-Whitney U tests and presented as medians with the corresponding 25th and 75th percentiles (interquartile range). For the categorical variables, Chi-square tests were employed. Logistic regression analyses were performed to explore independent associations of biomarkers with the risk of pSjD. Receiver operating characteristics (ROC) analysis was performed to determine the discriminatory ability of the salivary biomarkers and non-invasive model. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) larger than 0.70 indicated a good predictive power and the P value < 0.05 was considered a significant difference.

3.3. Systemic disease activity and glandular function were compared between high- and low-level salivary protein groups
According to the ROC curve analysis and Youden index calculation, the optimal cut-off values to distinguish high and low levels of the salivary proteins were determined, at 2623.00 ng/ml for C3, 173.50 ng/ml for CFB, 221.40 ng/ml for CLU, 0.00 ng/ml for NE, and 3.78 ng/ml for CALR (Supplementary Table

To determine whether these salivary protein levels could reflect the systemic disease activity and exocrine glandular function, the 121 patients with pSjD were divided into the high-level group (> cut-off) and low-level group (≤ cut-off), and EULAR Sjögren’s Syndrome Disease Activity Index (ESSDAI) score, uSFR, sSFR, Schirmer’s test result, and focus score between them were compared. The C3-high, CFB-high, and CALR-high groups had significantly lower uSFR and sSFR than C3-low, CFB-low, and CALR-low groups, respectively, which suggested that these salivary proteins had the potential to reflect the secretory dysfunction of salivary gland (Fig.

The combination of a non-invasive 6-biomarker (CFB, CLU, NE, anti-SSA/Ro60, anti-SSA/Ro52, and Schirmer’s test) model achieved significant discrimination of pSjD
To construct a non-invasive model for the diagnosis of pSjD, we utilize both univariate and multivariate logistic analysis. In the training cohort, univariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to examine the relationship between pSjD as a dependent variable while each salivary biomarker and clinical parameter as an independent variable (Supplementary Table
3
). And there is no multicollinearity between the independent variables. Continuous variables such as C3, CFB, CLU, NE, and CALR levels were converted to categorical variables coded as 1 or 0 according to the cut-off level. uSFR and Schirmer’s test results were scored as either 1 (positive) or 0 (negative) according to the 2016 ACR/EULAR classification criteria. Categorical variables such as anti-SSA/Ro60, anti-SSA/Ro52, and anti-SSB/La were coded 0, 1, 2, and 3 according to the number of “+”. Both continuous and categorical values of C3, CFB, CLU, NE, CALR, uSFR, Schirmer’s test result, anti-SSA/Ro60, anti-SSA/Ro52, anti-SSB, ANA titer, ESR, and IgG were associated with a diagnosis of pSjD according to the univariate analysis (Supplementary Table
3
). Multivariate analysis using a stepwise logistic regression model was performed to identify the final non-invasive predictors of pSjD. Candidate parameters of which
P
< 0.1 in univariate analysis were entered into the multivariate model. CFB, CLU, NE, anti-SSA/Ro60, anti-SSA/Ro52, and Schirmer’s test were retained in the non-invasive model as final variables (Supplementary Table
4
). Hosmer-Lemeshow test revealed an adequate fit for the model studied (
P
= 0.402).

3.5. 6-marker Model validation in an independent set
To validate these findings further, we assessed the logistic regression model using a validation set consisting of 49 patients with pSjD and 23 patients with non-pSjD (Table
1
). The resulting ROC curve had an AUC of 0.840 (
P
< 0.001, 95% CI 0.729–0.951), with a sensitivity and specificity of 78.72% and 86.36%, respectively. The model containing anti-SSA/Ro60, anti-SSA/Ro52, and Schirmer’s test had an AUC of 0.786 (
P
< 0.001, 95% CI 0.672-0.900) (Fig.
5
B). Therefore, our study confirms the additional discriminatory value of salivary biomarkers in discriminating between patients with pSjD and non-pSjD.
To offer a non-invasive alternative to ease patients’ diagnosis, the diagnostic performance of the 6-marker model, minor salivary gland biopsy (MSGB), combined MSGB and Schirmer’s test was compared. Data from the discovery cohort and validation cohort were pooled for the analyses. The AUC of MSGB as a single variable to discriminate pSjD and non-pSjD was 0.793 (
P
< 0.001, 95% CI 0.753–0.833). The AUC for a model containing MSGB and Schirmer’s test was 0.870 (
P
< 0.001, 95% CI 0.828–0.911). The 6-marker model showed a better diagnostic performance than MSGB as a single variable, meanwhile achieving a comparable diagnostic value with the model containing MSGB and Schirmer’s test (Fig.
5
C-D, Supplementary Table
6
). These results suggested the 6-marker model could be a viable replacement for MGSB.
4. Discussion
The present study applied the LC-MS analysis and found 261 upregulated proteins in the pSjD group, which enriched in several immune-system-related functional categories. Considering the relevance with the pathological processes of autoimmune glandular damage, we selected 5 upregulated proteins (C3, CFB, CLU, CALR, and NE) represent in complement and coagulation cascades, phagosome, and neutrophil extracellular trap pathways for further study. We demonstrated that levels of C3, CFB, CLU, NE, and CALR in the saliva of patients with pSjD were significantly elevated compared to patients suspected of pSjD in whom the diagnosis had been excluded. Levels of salivary C3, CFB, and CLU tend to reflect the severity of salivary gland inflammatory injury. A novel model based on the combination of salivary biomarkers (CFB, CLU, and NE), serum autoantibodies (anti-SSA/Ro60 and anti-SSA/Ro52) and Schirmer’s test showed a favorable diagnostic value for pSjD, with an AUC value of 0.930. This study provides an innovative tool for assessing the probability of pSjD without invasive procedures (e.g., MSG biopsy).
Recent studies have suggested that saliva proteome profiling by mass spectrometry may help identify useful biomarkers for diagnosing pSjD. One study utilizing LC-MS/MS performed on whole-saliva samples found that 34 proteins were significantly upregulated in saliva samples from the SjD group, of those, 10 proteins overlapped with our candidate biomarkers [
16
]. Additionally, NE, CALR, and TRIM29 were identified in this study as a panel that distinguished pSjD patients from non-SjD patients [
16
]. However, TRIM29 wasn’t significantly different between the two groups in our study. Another study utilizing a similar approach noted an increase in the expression of lipocalin-2 (LCN2), granulins (GRN), calmodulin (CALM), epididymal secretory protein 1 (NPC2), and calmodulin-like 5 (CALML5) in stimulated saliva of pSjD patients compared to healthy controls [
17
]. In our study, there was no significant difference between the two groups in CALML5, GRN, and NPC2, while LCN2 and CALM were not found in the mass spectrum. Within stimulated whole-saliva samples of pSjD patients and non-SjD controls, Lara et al. found that the proteins cis-trans isomerase FKBP1A, b-2 microglobulin, and CD44 antigen were most upregulated [
18

In spite of the heterogeneity, these studies showed an increase in inflammatory and immune response-related proteins in the saliva of patients with pSjD [

In our study, the LC-MS analysis showed proteins participate in complement and coagulation cascades, neutrophil extracellular trap formation, tuberculosis, and phagosome pathways significantly enriched in saliva from patients with pSjD. Among these biomarkers, salivary CFB, CLU, and NE were found to be independent predictors of pSjD by multivariate logistics regression. Previous studies have also supported these findings. It has been reported that increased CFB in cerebrospinal fluid of patients with fatigued pSjD and CFB mRNA expression was upregulated in the conjunctiva of SjD patients, suggesting the alternative complement system participated in the pathophysiology of many target organs of pSjD. Interestingly, C3 and CFB were both involved in complement systems, suggesting complement activation might play pathogenetic roles in salivary gland injury.
There has been much progress made in the understanding of pSjD pathogenesis in recent decades, and a complex interaction between innate, adaptative immunity and exocrine gland epithelial cells play an essential role [
40
]. It is generally accepted that genetic and environmental factors disturb immune homeostasis and trigger autoimmune responses, which are characterized by excessive autoantibody secretion [
41
]. Then, autoimmune attacks on exocrine glandular tissues cause glandular dysfunction and inflammation factors release, resulting in dryness symptoms, the main clinical features of pSjD [
40
,
41
]. According to the 2016 ACR/EULAR classification criteria, patients with anti-SSA/Ro antibody positivity and Schirmer’s test positivity could be classified to pSjD, which indicated activation of autoimmune and involvement of lacrimal gland. In such cases, the presence of glandular inflammatory injury was not assessed. Anti-SSA/Ro may occur in 1.7–17.5% of normal individuals, while the prevalence of dry eye disease ranges from 8.7 to 30.1%
42 43
. It could be a result of coincidence that dry eye symptom and anti-SSA/Ro positivity appear at the same time, in this case, whether glandular hypofunction is induced by autoimmune attack was in doubt. In the present study, salivary CFB, CLU, and NE were involved in the prediction model, which could reflect salivary gland inflammation and complete the missing link. Our data from two independent cohorts showed the 6-marker (CFB, CLU, NE, anti-SSA/Ro60, anti-SSA/Ro52, and Schirmer’s test) panel achieved significant discrimination of pSjD, with high sensitivity and specificity, and provided experimental evidence for the reliability. Moreover, the comparison between the 6-marker model and the model containing MSGB and Schirmer’s test showed that the non-invasive model could be used as an alternative diagnostic tool in patients with pSjD.
The collection of saliva requires litter training and minimum cost, and commercial ELISA kits are available for the detection of salivary CFB, CLU, and NE. The cost and technical accessibility of the 6-marker diagnostic model show the possibility to cover a broad range of clinical applications. To improve the accuracy of the 6-marker model in practical application, it’s necessary to determine the best cut-off value of salivary CFB, CLU, and NE using large cohort studies, as well as standardized saliva collection protocols and a uniform saliva collection time. As biopsy is an invasive examination and the 6-marker prediction model showed potential to replace MSGB, it’s recommended to use the model before MSGB, to reduce the number of unnecessary biopsies while missing fewer pSjD patients. As the non-invasive model can be repeated to monitor the patient’s condition, it would be valuable to explore further the application of the model in disease assessment and treatment plans.
However, this study is not without limitations. The primary limitation is that the sample size of the validation cohort is small, which causes wide confidence intervals for AUC and drop in sensitivity and specificity, as well as limits the statistical power. Secondly, it is important to note that this study was not longitudinal, but rather cross-sectional, so we cannot determine the impact of these salivary on the pathogenesis of pSjD and whether this elevation is progressive or reversible. Third, our cohort comprised patients from one center, so validation requires further multicenter studies and large cohort studies. Fourth, C3 ELISA kit used in this study determined the level of total complement C3 including full-length and fragments. Detection of activated fragments of C3 (e.g. C3d) could better represent the complement system activation, but in most saliva sample C3d was under the detection limit in our preliminary experiments. It may be the reason why C3 was not incorporated in the final prediction model. Finally, as we observed only a non-significant trend for higher levels of salivary C3, CFB, CLU, CALR, and NE in SjD compared to SLE and RA, it is important to note the potential limitation of the specificity of these immune-related markers, particularly in the context of other systemic autoimmune diseases.
Conclusions
To conclude, we have constructed a diagnostic panel based on salivary proteins, serum autoantibodies and Schirmer’s test that can discriminate patients with pSjD from non-pSjD. The panel underwent consistent internal validation within the discovery set and was subsequently validated with an independent set of patients. The 6-biomarker panel has the potential to serve as a valuable and non-invasiveness tool for discrimination of pSjD.
