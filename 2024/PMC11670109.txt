Understanding and making use of a patient’s own immune system to strengthen their response against cancer has brought cancer therapy to the next level (
1
). However, variation in patient responsiveness to immunotherapy limits the broad applicability and results in higher treatment costs, which in turn leads to growing needs for personalized treatment (
2
). To this end, noninvasive imaging of immune cell dynamics inside the tumor microenvironment (TME) could aid in predicting and following up the patient’s response to immunotherapy (
3
).
An important immune cell population that limits the efficacy of anticancer immunotherapy is the immunosuppressive tumor-associated macrophage (TAM). TAMs are versatile immune cells that can adopt different phenotypes inside the tumor in response to environmental cues such as hypoxia, acidosis, and stimuli from other immune cells, parenchymal cells, and the extracellular matrix (
4
–
8
). The immunosuppressive or protumorigenic TAMs are typically characterized by the surface markers CD163, CD204, and CD206 and they secrete anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-10 (
9
,
10
). These TAMs are known to suppress T cell recruitment and function and, therefore, constitute a major barrier to cancer immunotherapy, correlating with a poor prognosis in different types of cancer (
11
–
15
). Depleting this population could lead to an increase of T cells and the reprogramming of monocytes to a more inflammatory role, which results in the reduction of the tumor size (
16
).
Consequently, the noninvasive imaging of TAM dynamics to monitor immunotherapy responses has gained a lot of interest. Single-domain antibodies or nanobodies (Nbs) are antibody fragments derived from camelids that have emerged as imaging tools over the last decade. Their characteristics, such as small size (12 to 16 kDa), high stability, fast clearance, good tissue and tumor penetration, high solubility, and low immunogenicity make them ideal for noninvasive imaging (
17
–
19
). Moreover, due to the rapid renal clearance, a high tumor-to-background imaging ratio can be obtained as early as 1 h after tracer injection (
20
). Previously, a Nb against the macrophage mannose receptor (MMR or CD206) was generated in our group and has been clinically tested in a phase I clinical trial (
NCT04168528
) (
21
). The Nb has demonstrated adequate biodistribution and dosimetry and is currently undergoing evaluation for its tumor-targeting potential in patients with various cancers (
21
,
22
). While this Nb targets the immunosuppressive subset of TAMs leading to its potential therapeutic use (
23
,
24
), CD206 expression is not exclusively expressed on TAMs, but can also be found on lymphatic or liver sinusoidal endothelial cells, resulting in high background uptake in the liver (
21
,
25
).
In contrast, the scavenger receptor CD163 is solely expressed on monocytes and macrophages (
26
). Furthermore, CD163 is overexpressed on TAMs and is up-regulated on macrophages in various inflammation models (
27
). Therefore, CD163 could prove to be a better marker to noninvasively image macrophages, including TAMs, as compared to CD206. Its main function is the uptake of hemoglobin–haptoglobin complexes after intravascular hemolysis (
28
). Further, the receptor is known to be shed in an ADAM17-dependent manner and soluble CD163 (sCD163) can serve as a biomarker for different diseases (
29
–
32
).
In this study, we developed and validated a human/mouse cross-reactive anti-CD163 Nb-based immunotracer. After labeling with Technetium-99m (
99m
Tc) or Gallium-68 (
68
Ga), the Nb-based tracer was able to specifically target CD163
+
macrophages and TAMs, as measured via micro-single-photon emission computerized tomography/computed tomography (μSPECT/CT) imaging and micro-positron emission tomography (μPET)/CT imaging, respectively. Importantly, immunotherapy response could be predicted by imaging TAM dynamics in a lung carcinoma-ovalbumin (LLC-OVA) model upon anti-programmed cell death protein-1 (anti-PD-1) and macrophage-depleting anti-CSF1R combination therapy.
In conclusion, the anti-CD163 Nb-based immunotracer allows for the noninvasive imaging of CD163
+
TAMs to predict or follow-up immunotherapy responses.
Results
The Anti-CD163 Nb Shows High Affinity Binding to the Human and Mouse CD163 Receptor without Interfering with Hemoglobin Binding.
To identify CD163-specific Nbs, two llamas were immunized with the extracellular part of the human and mouse CD163 receptor. After immunization and biopanning, 318 unique Nb hits, showing binding to human CD163 (hCD163) and/or mouse CD163 (mCD163), were selected. Initial screening resulted in 187 hCD163-specific and 122 mCD163-specific hits with only 8 clones showing cross-reactivity for both receptors. The Anti-CD163 Nb Does Not Cause Immune Activation and Is Not Immunogenic.
To exclude undesired macrophage activation upon anti-CD163 Nb binding, which may indirectly result in T cell activation, C57BL/6J splenocytes were ex vivo cultured overnight in the presence of 2 μg/mL anti-mouse CD3 antibody (Ab) (as positive control for T cell activation), and 300 nM of an Irr Nb (as negative control) or 300 nM of the anti-CD163 Nb, upon which CD8
+
T cell activation was assessed. 300 nM was chosen as a concentration to assess the immunogenicity of the nanobody, as this is a high concentration whereby the nanobody binding to HEK293/CD163 cells reaches a saturation level (
Fig. 1
C
and
D

+
T cells, no CD8
+
T cell activation was detected in the presence of the Irr Nb nor the anti-CD163 Nb (
SI Appendix
, Fig. S4
A
and
B
) as determined via flow cytometry. Moreover, using a multiplex cytokine secretion assay, no inflammatory cytokines were detected in the splenocyte cocultures with the Irr Nb nor the anti-CD163 Nb, while IL-2, IL-6, TNF-α, and IFN-γ were strongly secreted upon coculture with the anti-CD3 Ab (
SI Appendix

To determine the immunogenicity of the anti-CD163 Nb, a DC/T cell coculture assay using peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) of 32 independent healthy donors was performed in the presence of Nb or control immunogenic proteins (
36

SI Appendix

Whereas addition of the immunogenic Keyhole Limpet Hemocyanin (KLH) protein to the assay resulted in the secretion of Th1 (IFN-γ) and Th2 (IL-5) cytokines, no cytokine secretion was seen after incubation with the anti-CD163 Nb, Irr Nb, or bevacizumab (control nonimmunogenic protein), demonstrating the low immunogenicity of the anti-CD163 Nb.

Discussion
A growing need for biomarkers exists to predict and follow-up immunotherapy response as there is ample evidence that a false application of therapy could lead to disease progression or immune-related adverse effects (
43
). Most biomarkers nowadays are focused on expression of tumor markers such as PD-L1, neoantigens, circulating tumor DNA, mismatch repair, and tumor mutational burden (
44
,
45
). However, these do not provide information on immune cells (e.g., T cells, TAMs, DCs, NK cells, etc.) and stromal tissue which can have a major impact on immunotherapy response (
46
). In recent years, immune monitoring has emerged as a possible way of evaluating immunotherapy response. However, a shortage of immunotracers undergoing clinical investigation exists and none of these tracers have been integrated into daily clinical practice (
47
).
The presence of TAMs is known to correlate with a worse prognosis in cancer patients and holds predictive or prognostic value (
9
). Consequently, macrophages have gained interest in the field of immunotherapy and immune-imaging (
48
). Recently, several anti-macrophage therapies have emerged that are currently in clinical trial, such as chemokine inhibitors (
49
), CSF1R inhibitors (
40
), anti-CD47/signal regulatory protein α (SIRPα) antibodies (
50
), Toll-like receptor (TLR) agonists (
51
) and STING agonists, among others (
52
). These therapies are based on the inhibition of monocyte recruitment, and the depletion or reprogramming of TAMs (
52
). Noninvasive imaging of macrophages could be helpful in the development of future TAM-based immunotherapies, as it allows the follow-up of cancer patients for the fast detection of immunotherapy response and for the evaluation of therapeutic efficacy and prognosis (
53
).
To allow noninvasive SPECT or PET imaging of TAMs, different targets have been evaluated, including the CD206 receptor (
21
,
25
), the translocator protein (TSPO) (
54
), the folate receptor-β (FR-β) (
55
,
56
), the C-C motif chemokine receptor 2 (CCR2) (
57
), SIRPα (
58
,
59
) and F4/80 (
60
). To date, the tracers against CD206 and FR-β are being tested in clinical trials for the imaging of TAMs in cancer (
NCT04168528
and
NCT03242993
). However, both molecules are also expressed on other cell types besides TAMs, with FR-β being found on CD34
+
BM cells and neutrophils (
61
,
62
) and the CD206 receptor on the surface of DCs, lymphatic and liver endothelial cells, perivascular microglia, and mesangial cells (
41
,
63
,
64
). This off-target expression can lead to antigenic sinks that result in reduced effectivity and even toxic adverse events by targeting healthy tissue (
25
). In this study, we selected the CD163 receptor as target for macrophage imaging, as it is a scavenger receptor that is specifically expressed on monocytes and macrophages (
26
). This macrophage-specificity was confirmed in our study since in vivo macrophage depletion using the CSF1R inhibitor PLX3397 resulted in a strongly reduced uptake of the anti-CD163 immunotracer in all investigated organs.
One potential caveat for noninvasive imaging is the shedding of the target, which can result in a detrimentally high background signal in the blood (
65
). The CD163 receptor is known to be shed by an ADAM17-mediated cleavage of the extracellular part (
29
). According to previous studies, the soluble form of CD163 circulates in the blood of healthy individuals at 0.7 to 3.9 mg/L and is up-regulated in lymphoma patients (
30
,
66
,
67
). In our study, measured levels of shed hCD163 receptor in healthy individuals were lower compared to the values described in the literature, which could be due to a different donor population or different measurement methods. However, the shed CD163 levels measured in human sera were comparable to the levels seen in mice, using our measurement method. As the levels of shed CD163 in mice did not result in a high background signal in the blood, it is likely that this will also not be the case in the clinic. Furthermore, other clinically tested Nb-based imaging targets, such as HER2 and CD206, are also shed. In these studies, no clear correlation was seen between the levels of shed target and the standardized uptake value (
21
,
68
).
Until now, no SPECT or PET anti-CD163 tracers are being tested clinically. Three preclinical studies have described the development of a
68
Ga-labeled anti-CD163 Ab (
69
), an
111
In-labeled anti-CD163 peptide (
70
), and a
64
Cu-labeled anti-CD163 peptide (
71
) for the imaging of macrophages in arthritis and atherosclerosis (
72
). Besides these tracers, other small format tracers have been developed for the purpose of macrophage imaging. These include small molecules binding to CD206 (
73
) or FR-β and a CXCR4-binding peptide (
74
). While these tracers are able to image macrophage dynamics in different disease models, the use of monoclonal Abs, peptides, and small molecules as tracers is accompanied by certain disadvantages. Monoclonal Abs exhibit a prolonged circulation time, poor penetration ability, anti-drug antibodies, and the requirement for complex eukaryotic expression systems, while peptides show poor proteolytic stability and solubility. Small molecules show a lower specificity and affinity for their target (
75
–
79
). As such, we set out to use Nbs as imaging scaffolds (
78
). Nbs have a superior solubility, stability and penetration ability, are easier to produce and allow for imaging at an early timepoint after injection as their circulation half-life is significantly lower than the larger molecules used in imaging (
80
,
81
). We showed that the anti-CD163 Nb was able to penetrate the LLC-OVA tumor and could already visualize CD163
+
TAMs 1 h postinjection. LLC-OVA tumors contained a threshold level of macrophages, that express sufficiently high levels of CD163, which is not the case for every mouse tumor (e.g., MC38 and B16-F10). Protein levels of CD163 are typically higher in human tumors than in mouse (
82
), suggesting that such threshold levels may be more readily reached in patients.
To monitor immunotherapy response based on CD163
+
TAM dynamics, a combination of anti-CSF1R and anti-PD-1 therapy was employed. As indicated by recent findings, the combination of a CSF1R inhibitor, which often induces the repolarization of protumor TAMs to antitumor TAMs rather than depleting them, with a T cell–based therapy such as anti-PD-1, results in an additive effect on tumor reduction (
42
,
83
). Hence, this combination resulted in responders, PR, and NR which could be distinguished based on the distribution of intratumoral CD163
+
TAMs using the anti-CD163 Nb-based PET tracer. A similar outcome was observed when employing Nb-based PET immunotracers targeting CD8
+
T cells and CD11b
+
cells (myeloid cells) for monitoring the response to anti-PD1 immune therapy (
84
). Interestingly, TAM distribution in the TME gave more insight into the therapy response compared to the total amount of uptake of the PET tracer, with NR showing a more heterogeneous distribution skewed toward the edges. These findings were in line with a recent study where two CD163 expressing TAM subpopulations, showing intermediate and high CD163 expression levels, were described (
85
). This small population of immunosuppressive CD163
high
TAMs was located at the tumor margins and was responsible for resistance to T cell–based immunotherapies. In addition, a second TAM population, with intermediate CD163 expression, was also observed and suggested that CD163 is not exclusively expressed on immunosuppressive TAMs. Flow cytometric analysis of the LLC-OVA tumors in our study supported these findings as we observed CD163 expression on MHC-II
low
and MHC-II
high
TAM populations. In addition, while we could already distinguish immunotherapy responses based on the CD163
+
TAM distributions, the interplay between TAMs and other immune cell populations, including CD8
+
T cells, is key during immunotherapy responses (
42
). As such, it is not unlikely that simultaneous or subsequent imaging of multiple immune cell populations could provide even more insight into specific immunotherapy responses.
While our study focused on the imaging of TAM dynamics, diagnostic imaging of CD163
+
macrophages could be applied to different disease fields. The presence of CD163
+
macrophages is associated with many inflammatory diseases, such as atherosclerosis, sarcoidosis, rheumatoid and idiopathic arthritis, the macrophage activation syndrome, systemic sclerosis, and acute-on-chronic liver failure (
27
,
86
–
90
). The anti-CD206 Nb has been used in vivo for the visualization of macrophages in different diseases such as atherosclerosis, myocardial infarction, and arthritis (
91
–
93
), but we anticipate more macrophage-specific images when using the anti-CD163 Nb. As described above, specific detection of CD163
+
macrophages in inflammatory diseases was possible using the radiolabeled anti-CD163 Ab-based or peptide-based PET tracers (
69
–
71
).
Finally, the anti-CD163 Nb could also be employed to direct therapeutic payloads to TAMs, since Nbs avoid Fc-receptor-mediated mistargeting (
94
). The anti-CD163 Nb could be linked to repolarizing agents, such as the TLR7/8 agonist Imidazoquinoline, aiming to shift TAMs to a more proinflammatory phenotype or it could be linked to cytotoxic agents such as clodronate liposomes or high-energy radioisotopes, as was previously described for the anti-CD206 Nb (
23
,
24
,
94
). Again, the anti-CD163 Nb could be a better option compared to the anti-CD206 Nb for the development of next-generation anti-macrophage therapies as we showed a superior specificity for macrophages.
In conclusion, we have generated and characterized a highly specific anti-CD163 tracer that is able to track CD163
+
macrophage dynamics via SPECT and PET. This Nb-based tracer could potentially be applied in patients to predict or follow-up therapy response together with other imaging agents and result in more personalized anticancer therapy.
