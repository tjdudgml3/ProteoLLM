1
Introduction
Plant‐derived triterpenoids are a large group of natural products widely used in traditional medicine (Liu and Cheng
2012
; Ren and Kinghorn
2019
). Due to their broad applications, these compounds awakened huge interest in different sectors, such as the consumer care, nutritional, and pharmaceutical industries (Caputi and Aprea
2011
; Bergman, Davis, and Phillips
2019
; Jahangeer et al.
2021
). Microbial‐based heterologous triterpenoid production is a promising alternative to exploiting plants or animals (Guo, Wang, and Huo
2020
) or complex chemical synthesis (Wang and Hui
2021
).
Saccharomyces cerevisiae
is a suitable, robust host for the heterologous synthesis of triterpenoids, which can be partially attributed to the native synthesis of 2,3‐oxidosqualene in the sterol pathway, the common precursor of many triterpenoids (Guo, Jacobsen, et al.
2022
; Guo, Wang, et al.
2022
).
However, this promising microbial platform has one significant limitation: To date, no terpenoid transporters have been described for
S. cerevisiae
(Fang and Xiao
2021
). While some studies have reported triterpenoids to be secreted into the medium (Zhou et al.
2016
), in our work, triterpenoids were primarily detected intracellularly (Czarnotta et al.
2017
). Hence, it is unclear whether the extracellular detection of triterpenoids was due to secretion or cell lysis‐related product release into the medium.
Due to their hydrophobic structure, triterpenoids are generally assumed to accumulate in or on the ER membrane (Dianat et al.
2023
), the subcellular compartment where triterpenoid biosynthesis is localized. Accumulation in lipid droplets and vesicular transport from ER to the plasma membrane has also been suggested (Fang and Xiao
2021
). Intracellular triterpenoid accumulation constitutes a major constraint for microbial production. It prohibits prolonged production of triterpenoids by the same biocatalyst and necessitates cell disruption for product release in the downstream processing step, causing higher costs, more complexity, and the loss of the biocatalyst. Hence, designing microbial processes where the product is secreted is preferred to improve process economics.
Based on the properties of triterpenoids, the application of a lipophilic solvent in a two‐phase liquid–liquid extraction system could be a suitable method to facilitate the extraction of triterpenoids from the cell. For instance, Sun, Kwak, and Jin (
2019
) applied a two‐phase in situ extraction with olive oil and dodecane to extract vitamin A from
S. cerevisiae
. Similarly, isopropyl myristate (IPM) was used as the organic phase for the extraction of triterpenoids and carotenoids from
Yarrowia lipolytica
(Zhang et al.
2020
) and from
S. cerevisiae
, respectively (Yamada et al.
2022
). Furthermore, other studies showed the successful extraction of triterpenoids with cyclodextrins (CDs), cyclic oligosaccharides consisting of glucose subunits (Moses et al.
2014
; Lan and Xiao
2020
). The spatial arrangement of the glucose molecules creates a hydrophobic inner cavity that enables the encapsulation of hydrophobic compounds. The outer part of cyclodextrins is hydrophilic due to the presence of hydroxyl groups (see Figure
S1
), which allows the interaction of cyclodextrins with aqueous systems. Hence, cyclodextrins can be applied in drug‐delivery systems to increase aqueous solubility of poorly soluble compounds (Carneiro et al.
2019
). Although the detailed mechanism of CD‐mediated triterpenoid transport is not understood, studies imply CD interactions with the cell membrane.
Motivated by these findings, we explored the potential advantages of in situ product extraction on the example of production of the pentacyclic triterpenoid betulinic acid in
S. cerevisiae
. A
S. cerevisiae
CENPK strain expressing a recombinant betulinic acid biosynthesis pathway (Figure
1
) was used throughout this study. In addition to betulinic acid, the strain also accumulates the less oxidized intermediates betulin and betulin aldehyde of this triterpenoid because of suboptimal activity of the heterologous cytochrome P450 monooxygenase. The high intracellular accumulation of these triterpenoids at around 0.20 ± 0.02 g/g
CDW
(Dianat et al.
2023
) makes this strain an ideal test bed for in situ product extraction. We performed solvent screening and applied the best‐performing solvent for in situ extraction to (1) evaluate the possible increase of the cells' capacity for production, (2) ease the downstream process by separating the product from the biomass, and (3) investigate the possibility of continuously utilizing the same biocatalyst.

2.2
Shake Flask Cultivation
Batch cultivations in 500‐mL shake flasks were conducted with a filling volume of 20 mL. A pre‐culture was used to inoculate the main cultures to a starting optical density measured at 600 nm (OD
600
) of 0.1. The OD
600
of the cultures was determined using a spectrophotometer (Ultrospec 10, Biochrom, Germany) with 0.5% phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) used as blank. The cultivations took place in a rotary shaker (New Brunswick Innova 44R) at 30°C, 300 rpm, and 25 mm shaking diameter.
2.3
Online Monitoring of Exhaust
CO

Online monitoring of CO

2

2

S2

Oxygen limitation in the sealed shake flask was avoided by calculating the total amount of oxygen available in the shake flask's headspace and adjusting glucose concentration to the total available oxygen, ensuring the possibility of complete oxidation (cf to Data
S1
: Methods for details). The cultivation took place at 30°C, 150 rpm, and 2.5 cm shaking diameter (Eppendorf, Germany) for 72 h, and the initial OD
600

2.4
Solvent Screening
For solvent screening, a batch culture in stationary phase was distributed onto 15‐mL reaction tubes, in which the extraction was conducted. The total volume was kept constant at 5 mL. For the initial screening, 20 vol.% of each solvent were tested (equals a volume of 1 mL solvent on 4‐mL cell culture). The solvent amount of 20 vol.% was chosen as this was in the range of 10–50 vol.%, which was applied in previous studies (Sun, Kwak, and Jin
2019
; Zhang et al.
2020
; Yamada et al.
2022
). After incubation at 60 rpm for 2 h, reaction tubes were collected and centrifuged at 4000 rpm (3011 × g) for 5 min. The interphase between organic and aqueous phases was marked. The organic phase was discarded, and the cell pellet was washed with 0.5% PBS (v/v). After discarding the supernatant, the inner tube surface was cleaned with paper towels to remove solvent residues. The pellet was resuspended in 0.5% PBS to the original volume as marked on the tube to obtain the initial biomass concentration. Technical duplicates of 800 μL were taken from the cell suspension and stored at −20°C until further processing. Furthermore, technical triplicates of 1 mL were taken for cell viability measurements. For a schematic overview, see Figure
S2
.

2.6
Repeated Batch Cultivation With Sequential Product Extraction
Repeated batch experiments with sequential two‐phase product extraction were carried out by the addition of IPM at the end of each batch cultivation, followed by cell harvest, phase separation by centrifugation (10 min, 4000 rpm (3011 g)), washing the pellet in 0.5% PBS and resuspension of the pellet in fresh medium. A glucose concentration of 50 g/L was used for the initial batch of the control experiment and the in situ extraction approach (for obtaining high biomass concentrations). After 72 h of the first batch, 150 vol. % IPM was added for in situ extraction, while nothing was added to the control. After 24 h of incubation, the cultures were transferred into a 50‐mL reaction tube, centrifuged, and the pellet was resuspended in fresh WM8+ medium with 16 g/L ethanol. After 24 h of cultivation, 150 vol. % IPM was added for extraction, and the culture was again incubated for 24 h. This procedure was repeated five times (
n
= 5). For a schematic overview, see Figure

2.7
Application of β‐Cyclodextrins for Triterpenoid Extraction
Prior to addition to the microbial culture, a weighed amount of β‐cyclodextrins (≥ 97% purity, Sigma‐Aldrich) was sterilized by exposure to UV light for 30 min. After incubation at 300 rpm and 30°C, the culture was poured into a reaction tube and centrifuged, and the supernatant and pellet were separated. The pellet was resuspended in 0.5% PBS and adjusted to the initial volume. The supernatant was evaporated; the residues were dissolved in chloroform and analysed by high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). Samples from the resuspended pellet fraction were analysed by HPLC‐CAD as well.
2.8
Determination of Triterpenoid Solubilities in
IPM
To determine maximal solubilities of betulin and betulinic acid, supersaturated solutions of these molecules were prepared in IPM. Diluted samples of these supersaturated solutions were analysed by HPLC to determine the concentration in the saturated solution. Each compound in each solvent was measured twice (technical replicates).
2.9
Fed‐Batch Cultivation in Bioreactor
Bioreactor cultivations were conducted in 1.3‐L vessels (BioFlo 120, Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany) with a working volume of 500 mL, an aeration rate of 1 vvm, 30°C, and a pH of 6 monitored online with an autoclavable pH probe (Mettler Toledo, Switzerland) and regulated with 30% (v/v) H
2
SO
4
and 30% (v/v) NH
4
OH. The dissolved oxygen tension (DOT) was measured with a pO
2
probe (Mettler Toledo, Switzerland) and kept above 30% using a stirring cascade (350–1200 rpm). The temperature was measured using a Pt100 probe and was automatically regulated via the heating jacket (Eppendorf, Germany) and the water‐cooling system. The setup of the fermenter was completed by a stirrer with two Rushton turbines, a gas sparger, and an exhaust cooler. The medium was inoculated with an OD
600
set to 0.4 via a septum using a sterile syringe. Pulsed fed‐batch cultivations were performed using a DO‐triggered pump system. The carbon source was pulsed into the bioreactor using a syringe via the septum when the DO exceeded 70%, indicating carbon source depletion. 150 vol. % IPM was transferred into the bioreactor via a separate feed tube at the end of the batch phase for in situ extraction. With each new pulse of carbon source, trace elements and vitamins were replenished.
2.10
Determination of Cell Viability and Cell Number
Cell viability analysis and cell counts were performed using the electrochemical impedance spectroscopy system Ampha Z32 (Amphasys, Lucerne, Switzerland). The single cell's impedance provides information about the integrity of the cell membrane, which correlates with cell viability. For the measurements, a 50‐μm microchip was used. To prepare the samples, 100 μL of cell culture was centrifuged, and the pellet was resuspended and diluted in 0.5× PBS to obtain an OD
600
of approx. 0.05. By diluting all samples in 0.5% PBS, the same conductivity was assured for all samples, and the impedance was only dependent on the cell membrane integrity. Viable and dead cells were distinguished by gating using a sample with heat‐inactivated dead yeast cells as control. Measurements were run at two frequencies of 2 and 12 MHz and a pump speed of 80 rpm. The trigger level was set to 0.008 V, and the measurement‐specific settings modulation, amplification, and demodulation were set to 5, 4, and 1, respectively.

2.12
Nanopore Library Preparation and
GridION

Samples of
S. cerevisiae
BA6∆ grown in both ethanol‐pulsed and glucose‐pulsed fed‐batch cultures, taken at the start (0 h) and end (approx.150 h), were used for whole‐genome DNA isolation and sequencing. Whole‐genome DNA was isolated and purified as described before (Schafhauser et al.
2022
). In brief, the ‘NucleoBond HMW DNA’ kit from Macherey‐Nagel (Düren, Germany) was used for DNA isolation according to the manufacturer's instructions. The isolated DNA was used for sequencing when the required quality criteria were fulfilled and when a 0.5% agarose control gel confirmed the presence of large DNA fragments (>> 10 kb) and the absence of visible amounts of RNA. A sequencing library with genomic DNA from the different yeast strains was prepared using the Nanopore Rapid DNA Sequencing kit (SQK‐LSK106, Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxford, UK) according to the manufacturer's instructions as described recently (Ullmann et al.
2022
). Sequencing was performed on an Oxford Nanopore GridION Mk1 sequencer using an R9.4.1 flow cell, which was prepared according to the manufacturer's instructions.
2.13
Base Calling, Assembly, and Analysis
MinKNOW v21.05.25 (Oxford Nanopore Technologies) was used to control the run using the 48 h sequencing run protocol; base calling was performed offline using guppy v5.0.16. The assemblies were performed using canu v2.1.1 (Koren et al.
2017
) and flye v2.9 (Freire, Ladra, and Parama

). For more insight, genomic contigs resulting after the assembly were analysed for large local similarities using BLASTn (Altschul

2016

Then, the mix was transferred onto S‐Trap Micro Spin columns (Protifi), trapped in a filter by centrifugations at 4000 × g for 1 min, and washed thrice with 150 μL S‐Trap binding buffer. Proteins were digested into peptides by adding a digestion buffer containing 2 μg trypsin and 25 μL of 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate (pH = 8) on top of the S‐traps. Then, the S‐trap was incubated in an oven overnight at 37°C. The digested peptides were eluted with 40 μL of 5% acetonitrile (ACN) in 0.1% formic acid, followed by 50% ACN in 0.1% formic acid and 75% ACN in 0.1% formic acid. All elutions were dried and resuspended in 5% acetonitrile (ACN) + 0.1% formic acid (FA) prior to liquid chromatography. Peptides were analysed by liquid chromatography‐mass spectrometry (LCMS). The HPLC system was an Ultimate 3000 RSLCnano (ThermoFisher, Germany), and the mobile phase was H
2

An aliquot of 2 μL sample was injected onto a trap column used for online desalting and washed at 20 μL/min prior to a resolving C18 nanoEaseTM M/Z CSH C18 1.7 μm (150 μm × 100 mm) column (Waters) using a 60 min gradient of 8 to 95% acetonitrile in 0.1% formic acid at 0.5 μL/min flow rate. The eluted peptides were electro‐sprayed into a Thermo nano‐Exploris Mass Spectrometer (ThermoFisher, USA). For full MS, a resolution of 60,000 and a scan range of 400–1200 m/z were applied. For MS2, the isolation window was 1.6 m/z, and the orbitrap resolution was set to 15,000. The post‐analysis was performed against an in‐house
S. cerevisiae
protein database using ThermoFisher Spectronaut (version 15.0). Data are available via ProteomeXchange with identifier PXD057071.

3.1

We added 20 vol. % of each solvent to a culture in the stationary phase, and after 2 h of incubation at RT, the concentration of triterpenoids in the cell pellet and the cell viability of the individual extractions were determined. Untreated cells from a stationary phase culture were used as a control. Cells from extractions with ethyl acetate, butanol, and hexanol showed considerably low cell viability (Figure

3.2
IPM
Does Not Impact Cell Metabolism
To investigate yeast metabolism in the presence of methyl decanoate, ethyl decanoate, and IPM (20 vol. %), we conducted online measurement of the CO
2
produced by
S. cerevisiae
when exposed to these three solvents alongside a control experiment without any solvent (Figure
3
). The course of CO
2
volume concentration in the headspace was tracked as it reflects the metabolic activity and can be correlated with distinct fermentation stages. The control culture without solvent addition showed the expected CO
2
growth profile characterized by two phases of exponential CO
2
evolution during exponential growth on glucose and ethanol and termination of CO
2
formation upon carbon source depletion and cessation of cell growth. Cultivations containing ethyl decanoate showed a CO
2
evolution like that of the control. However, after ethanol depletion at approx. 40 h, a further increase in the CO
2
partial pressure was observed, indicating metabolic activity of
S. cerevisiae
on ethyl decanoate. Because of the yeast's native ability to produce this molecule, it can likely also degrade this substance (Saerens et al.
2010
). Experiments with methyl decanoate showed a slower CO
2
production than the control, possibly due to slower cell growth caused by metabolic stress. This observation was also underlined by the cell viability of samples treated with methyl decanoate, which was 20% lower than the control. Methyl decanoate was henceforth excluded as a solvent candidate. In contrast, IPM addition had no pronounced effect on cell metabolism. IPM is commonly used in cosmetic and skincare industry, and regarded as safe (Vadgama, Odaneth, and Lali
2015
; Zhao et al.
2016
; Zhang et al.
2017
; Dib et al.
2021
). The results obtained in this study highlight its biocompatibility on yeast cells, which is also in accordance with the previously reported application of IPM in yeast cultures (Sun, Kwak, and Jin
2019

3.3
IPM
Addition Post‐Batch Cultivation Allowed Triterpenoid Extraction of More Than 80%
Since our initial screening results showed that extraction with IPM was a suitable trade‐off between our two main criteria, biocompatibility, and product extraction capability, we evaluated in situ product extraction with this solvent. Guided by experimentally determined solubilities of the two main triterpenoids betulinic acid and betulin in IPM, which were 1.3 ± 0.1 and 2.1 ± 0.1 g/L, respectively, we estimated that a solvent‐to‐culture ratio of 1.5:1 (150 vol.% IPM) would provide sufficient capacity for complete extraction of all triterpenoids produced in standard batch cultivation with 50 g/L glucose. We determined the distribution of triterpenoids in the solvent and pellet after different time points to investigate the influence of extraction duration. The fraction of extracted triterpenoids in IPM increased steadily with increasing incubation time, reaching more than 80% after 24 h (Figure
4A
). Combining 8 mM β‐CD and 150 vol. % IPM significantly improved the extraction efficiency. While treatment with IPM alone released 60% of the product within 2 h (Figure
4A
), 80% was extracted in the same period when 8 mM β‐CD was added in addition to IPM (Figure
4B
). The positive effect of β‐CD was expected, as β‐CD was shown to extract cholesterols and phospholipids from the cell membrane (Zidovetzki and Levitan
2007
). Due to the structural similarity of sterols and triterpenoids, it is likely that triterpenoids accumulated in the plasma membrane can also be extracted by and form complexes with CDs. However, adding 8 mM β‐CD alone did not significantly enhance product secretion from the cells (Figure
S7
). Therefore, we propose that β‐CD facilitates extraction into the organic phase by functioning as a shuttle.

Two biological replicates per each time point were measured (shown by (I) and (II)). Error bars refer to two technical replicates.
Lan and Xiao reported that 2‐hydroxypropyl‐β‐cyclodextrin (HP‐β‐CD) or 2,6‐dimethyl‐β‐cyclodextrin (DM‐β‐CD) enabled fast extraction of the triterpenoid ganoderic acid 3‐hydroxy‐lanosta‐8,24‐dien‐26 oic acid (GA‐HLDOA) from
S. cerevisiae
(Lan and Xiao
2020
). Failure to achieve similar results with β‐CDs could be due to differences in CD structure and polarity or different properties of betulinic acid compared to GA‐HLDOA.
3.4
Sequential Batch Cultures With Intermittent Extraction by
IPM
Increased Triterpenoid Production
To explore the potential enhancement of triterpenoid production by extracting these intracellular molecules between sequential batch experiments, we subjected a stationary‐phase‐batch culture grown on glucose to a 24‐h treatment with IPM. After extraction, the entire biomass was aseptically transferred into fresh medium for a new batch run with ethanol as carbon source. We chose ethanol, because it was shown to promote triterpenoid synthesis in yeast (Czarnotta et al.
2017
). Moreover, cell growth on ethanol is slower than on glucose, leading to reduced oxygen demand and decreases the risk of oxygen limitation in shake flask. The second batch run was followed by a new extraction step at the end of the batch cultivation. Six rounds of this sequence were performed (for schematic overview, see Figure
S3
). Sequential batch cultivations with alternating incubations without product extraction served as a control.
We measured the total product amount produced at the end of each batch round. For IPM‐treated cultures, this value comprised the triterpenoid amount in the pellet and the organic phase; triterpenoid amounts in the aqueous phase were insignificant (data not shown) and were not included. In the control experiment, the triterpenoid quantity in the entire cultivation broth was measured. To compare the triterpenoid production in both approaches, we determined the sum of total triterpenoids produced at the end of each batch as well as the total amount over all batches (Table
S1
). The total triterpenoid amount produced over all batch experiments was significantly increased in the experiment with IPM extraction compared to the control (Figure
5A
), supporting our hypothesis that intracellular product accumulation limits productivity.

Shown are the sum of total triterpenoids (intracellular (pellet) and extracellular (OP)) which is higher in the in situ extraction approach (grey), than in the control approach (black) (A), cell viability, which was similar in both in situ extraction approach (grey) and control (black) (B), and product yield in both approaches, which was slightly higher in in situ extraction approach than in the control (C) over six sequential batch runs. Nevertheless, we also observed that the production rate decreased with increasing number of batches, apparent from the flattening of the plotted total triterpenoid amounts for both approaches (Figure
5A
). While triterpenoid production increased in the first two batch rounds, after the third batch, the triterpenoid amount produced per batch decreased independent of intracellular product accumulation (Table
S1
). This observation lets us argue that other factors may limit triterpenoid synthesis in addition to intracellular product accumulation.
Impedance flow cytometry found that the cell viability in both cultivation setups was comparable (Figure
5B
), highlighting that treatment with IPM does not affect cell viability, in line with the measurement of CO
2
evolution (see section 0). The drop in cell viability observed after the fifth batch could not be explained and might be a technical error. In approaches with product extraction, we further observed differences in intra‐ and extracellular product distribution (Figure
5D
), specifically, a decline in the extracted product fraction from 90% to 30%. Since fresh solvent (150 vol. %) was used for every extraction, saturation of the extractant can be excluded as a reason for the reduced extraction efficiency. Instead, we hypothesize that the repeated exposure to the organic solvent induces changes in the cell membrane rigidity, which could impede the extraction of the hydrophobic products.
Overall, this experiment showed that product extraction enables higher total production of triterpenoids as well as higher product yield on substrate in at least three sequential batch rounds (Figure
5C
) while also pointing to additional hurdles limiting the reusability and extended productivity of the whole‐cell biocatalyst.
3.5
In Situ Extraction Is Applicable for Pulsed Fed‐Batch Bioreactor Cultivation
Sequential batch cultivation with intermittent product extraction showed a positive influence of in situ extraction on triterpenoid production in
S. cerevisiae
. However, this alternating process is practically complex and involves a risk of contamination. To address these concerns, we tested an alternative process configuration in which in situ extraction was continuously performed during fed‐batch cultivation in bioreactors. The fed‐batch process involved an initial batch culture on glucose followed by pulses of approximately 25 g/L ethanol injected upon carbon source depletion, as indicated by a rapid rise in the dissolved oxygen (DO) tension. For in situ extraction, 150 vol. % IPM and 8 mM β‐CD were added to the culture at the beginning of the fed‐batch phase simultaneously with the first ethanol pulse, whereas the control was run without addition of the extractant. We compared the total amounts of triterpenoids, squalene, and ergosterol in both approaches (Figure
6A,B
). Interestingly, no significant differences were observed in the final product titres between the control and the in situ extraction approach (Table
2
). After about 80 h, product formation slowed down, similar to our observations in the sequential batch experiments, where the production plateaued independently of product extraction.

In addition to the triterpenoid synthesis proteins, also native enzymes implicated in the mevalonate and upper sterol synthesis pathway (Figure
1
) were investigated since a potential downregulation could limit the supply of the precursor 2,3‐oxidosqualene and triterpenoid production. Most enzymes involved in mevalonate and FPP biosynthesis showed no clear trend, except for mevalonate phosphate decarboxylase (Erg19p), which was upregulated in both approaches. Low coverage of pathway enzymes with statistically significantly differential values prevented further conclusions about the proteome level of the mevalonate pathway. For enzymes involved in upper ergosterol biosynthesis, squalene synthase (Erg9p), downregulation on ethanol, and squalene epoxidase (Erg1p), downregulation was observed on ethanol and glucose. However, this was only partially consistent in all measurements. A similar trend was observed for enzymes involved in converting 2,3‐oxidosqualene to ergosterol. This was surprising, as we observed an increased ergosterol content in the cultivations (Figures
6
and
7
). The mutated lanosterol synthase Erg7p, which replaced the native enzyme in the engineered
S. cerevisiae
, showed low abundances and no significant change as expected (Guo, Jacobsen, et al.
2022
; Guo, Wang, et al.
2022
).
The reduced abundance of heterologously expressed proteins, as indicated by the proteomic data, underlines a possible genomic instability of the strain. The multiple integrations of the heterologous genes encoding for cytochrome P450 monooxygenase (CYP) and lupeol synthase (OEW) into the genome of
S. cerevisiae
BA6∆ had been realized by insertion into retrotransposon sites TY4 (Figure
9A
), reported to lack stability in cultivation over several generations (Maury et al.
2016
). Hence, we performed whole‐genome sequencing on cells at the start and end of the cultivation to track a possible loss of heterologous genes. Although TY4 sites are distributed over nearly all chromosomes of
S. cerevisiae
(Maury et al.
2016
), our results showed the presence of the genes of interest only on chromosome X. In all samples, the genes of interest were found to be located between
NCA3
(193,860–194,873 bp) and
ASF1
(196,287–197,126 bp; Figure
9
). Since the sequencing data contained gaps, we cannot exclude the presence of additional gene copies in these uncharted regions. In the ethanol‐pulsed fed‐batch approach, one of the two copies of the expression cassette consisting of CYP and OEW together with the bidirectional pTEF1‐pPGK1 and the terminators tADH1 and tCYC1 (Figure
9B
) found at the beginning of the cultivation (Figure
9B
) was lost at the end of fermentation (Figure
9C
). In contrast, genome sequencing found one copy of the genes OEW and CYP each at the beginning and the end of the glucose‐pulsed fed‐batch cultivation (Figure
9D,E
). However, additional copies of the terminators tADH1 and/or tCYC1 indicated a loss of previously integrated expression cassettes.

This insight provides an opportunity for creating strains with stabilized gene integration and protein expression. Potential gene loss through homologous recombination between TY sites (Qi et al.
2023
) can be mitigated by employing more stable integration methods (Jessop‐Fabre et al.
2016
) and the avoidance of reusing identical promoters and terminators to minimize the occurrence of homologous regions, for example artificial, sequence‐diverse promoters (Kotopka and Smolke
2020
).
4
Conclusion
In this study, we addressed the challenge of intracellular triterpenoid accumulation in engineered
S. cerevisiae
by introducing in situ product extraction through the synergistic application of IPM and β‐CDs. Our proof‐of‐concept study demonstrated the potential for extracting intracellular triterpenoids from living cells into a biocompatible organic phase, simplifying downstream processing and allowing for extended use of the same biocatalyst.
Through two distinct cultivation methods, a sequential batch approach in shake flasks and a pulsed fed‐batch approach in a bioreactor, we achieved up to 90% product extraction into the organic solvent IPM. Notably, we observed variances in extraction efficiencies between these cultivation modes, emphasizing the need for continued research to optimize this method. However, a fundamental limitation of IPM lies in its low triterpenoid solubility, necessitating the use of substantial volumes of the organic phase. Indeed, this study employed a solvent‐to‐culture ratio of 1.5–1, presenting a significant challenge for translating this method to large‐scale applications. Thus, future research should be directed towards the exploration of more efficient solvents or the design of co‐solvent systems that can address the solubility issue. Moreover, innovative techniques for separating the product from the solvent and enabling continuous reuse should be pursued.
We also uncovered unexpected limitations related to unstable gene integration, resulting in decreased enzyme levels and production instabilities that metabolic engineering needs to address.
In summary, our research highlights the promise of in situ extraction for enhancing microbial production of hydrophobic, intracellular products. However, for this specific case, fully realizing the benefits of in situ product extraction depends on the sustained activity of the triterpenoid synthesis pathway through metabolic engineering. This work encourages further exploration of in situ extraction strategies and provides valuable insights for those seeking to utilize this innovative approach in bioprocess engineering.
