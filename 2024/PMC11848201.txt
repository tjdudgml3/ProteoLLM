BACKGROUND
Older adults with type 2 diabetes mellitus (DM) are at an elevated risk of developing cognitive impairments, including dementia attributed to Alzheimer's disease (AD). While the association between DM and vascular pathologies such as cerebral infarction, atherosclerosis, and arteriolosclerosis is well established,
1
,
2
,
3
these factors alone are insufficient to account for the heightened risk of AD in this population. This raises the possibility of alternative biological mechanisms at the molecular level that may contribute to the complex interplay between DM and AD. One such avenue is the role of proteins as pivotal mediators of disease, regulated by intricate post‐translational modifications like phosphorylation.
4
,
5
AD research has shown that alterations in protein phosphorylation, especially in proteins like tau, are key markers of disease progression.
4
,
5
In DM, the current understanding is that there is abnormal signaling downstream of the insulin receptor. There are numerous points of crosstalk between the insulin signaling pathway and AD, with kinases in common being involved in AD‐related tau phosphorylation.
6
In a seminal study investigating activation and inhibition profiles of insulin signaling pathway constituents in brain tissue from subjects with AD, we previously described aberrant phosphorylation of insulin receptor substrate 1, RAC‐alpha serine/threonine protein kinase 1, protein kinase C, c‐Jun N‐terminal kinase 1/2, IkappaB kinase, mammalian target of rapamycin, and glycogen synthase kinase 3 (GSK3).
7
These play direct or indirect roles in tau phosphorylation as well. In DM, phosphoproteomics analyses have provided valuable insights into complex changes in insulin signaling.
8
However, most existing research focuses on animal models of DM‐related dementia, leaving a gap in our knowledge about these molecular changes in human participants.
8
To date, no studies have been conducted on the phosphoproteomic changes in the human brains of older adults with and without DM who also have varying levels of AD pathology.
To provide a more comprehensive accounting of potential nodes of crosstalk between pathophysiological processes of DM and AD, we applied a discovery‐type shotgun liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC‐MS/MS) approach to brain tissues from individuals with and without AD, with and without DM. Our study aims to examine the differences in bulk brain protein phosphorylations by comparing their abundance and analyzing co‐abundance networks in deceased older adults, both with and without diabetes and AD. Samples of DM and no‐diabetes (NDM) were selected to be balanced in sex, age at death, years of formal education, and
post mortem
interval (PMI) with an algorithm using Mahalanobis distance that identified individuals with a similar profile on those variables.
9
The phosphoproteomics analysis was performed using tandem mass tag (TMT)‐18 isobaric tag labeling followed by LC‐MS, providing a granular view of altered signaling pathways in AD compared to non‐AD among participants with diabetes versus without diabetes.
10
,
11
,
12
By focusing on identifying phosphoproteomic profiles, this study hopes to shed light on the molecular mechanisms that might explain the increased risk of AD among diabetic individuals, thereby offering new avenues for underlying understanding mechanisms that can inform the development of future treatments.

METHODS
2.1
Participants
Deceased participants from the Rush Memory and Aging Project (MAP) were considered for this study. MAP is an ongoing community‐based clinical‐pathologic study of aging that started in 1997. This study received approval from the institutional review board of Rush University Medical Center. Participants signed informed consent to undergo yearly testing and an anatomical gift act for
post mortem
brain donation as well as a repository consent for resource sharing. A balanced sample of 96 deceased DM participants and 96 deceased NDM participants was obtained using a minimum multivariate Mahalanobis distance algorithm in age at death (matching within 5 years), years of formal education (matching within 4 years), and PMI (matching within 12 hours of death) within each sex. That is, participants with and without diabetes were similar by design on age at death, years of formal education, hours of PMI, and percent of each sex. The mean age at death was 89.9 years (standard error [SE] = 5.7, minimum = 71, maximum = 102 years), 89.5 for DM participants (SE = 6.0), and 90.30 for NDM participants (SE = 5.4). The mean education was 14.1 years (SE = 2.9), 14.0 for DM participants (SE = 3.0), and 14.2 for NDM participants (SE = 2.7). The mean PMI was 6.9 hours (SE = 1.8), 7.0 for DM participants (SE = 1.9), and 6.83 for NDM participants (SE = 1.7). A total of 65.4% of the participants were women.
2.2
Brain pathology
Participants’ brains underwent autopsies and uniform neuropathologic evaluation at the Rush University Medical Center, under the supervision of a board‐certified neuropathologist. More details can be found in the Methods
S1
in supporting information. The neuropathologic assessment of AD followed the National Institute on Aging–Alzheimer's Association (NIA‐AA) consensus guidelines. The severity level of AD neuropathologic change is ascertained based on the Thal phases of amyloid beta deposition (“A”), Braak staging for neurofibrillary degeneration (“B”), and Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer's Disease (CERAD) neuritic plaque score (“C”). The methods to obtain Braak and CERAD are described in detail elsewhere.
13
,
14
For dichotomized grading, high or intermediate likelihood of AD by NIA‐AA pathologic criteria is considered AD present, whereas low likelihood (ADH) or no AD is considered AD absent (ADL). The guidelines are described in detail elsewhere.

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT
Systematic review
: Using PubMed and other online databases, there were limited studies on phosphorylated proteins using human brain specimens and none in the brains of older adults with and without diabetes and with and without pathologic Alzheimer's disease (AD). The relevant citations are introduced and appropriately cited.
Interpretation
: In this tandem mass tag–based phosphoproteome profiling in the
post mortem
human brain from 191 deceased older adults with and without diabetes and with and without pathologic AD a total of 11,726 phosphosites were quantified. Microtubule‐associated protein tau (MAPT) phosphorylated at T529 and T534 (isoform 8 T212 and T217) showed consistent abundance in AD and differential associations with diabetes status. Network analysis of co‐abundance patterns helped us gain insights into the biological systems involved, revealing networks of synergistic interactions between AD and diabetes. In particular, one module exhibited higher MAPT phosphorylation and another lower MAP1B phosphorylation.
Future directions
: Future studies will focus on (a) the validations and replications on different datasets; (b) examining phosphoproteome profiling in the
post mortem
human brain with and without diabetes from different races; and (c) examining phosphoproteome profiling the periphery, namely blood and muscle.

2.3
Diabetes status and related markers
The diabetes status was obtained during baseline and annual visits based on self‐reported medical history (four standardized questions) and/or the use of anti‐diabetic medications (insulin or any other agents) based on visual inspection of all medical receptacles and recording of name, dosage, and frequency into electronic form for data capture and coding (MediSpan). Participants who had a positive diabetes status at any time point during the study were classified as having DM for these analyses. Blood levels of hemoglobin A1c and a basic metabolic panel including blood glucose (mg/dL) were obtained using a commercial laboratory (Quest Diagnostics, Inc.) with the National Glycohemoglobin Standardization Program (NGSP) certification
21
following the manufacturer's protocols. Serum insulin, leptin, adiponectin, advanced glycation‐end products (AGEs), and receptors for AGEs (RAGE) were obtained proximate to death using enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs), following the manufacturer's protocols (respectively catalog # 90095, 80968, 80571, MBS267540, and DRG00).
22
Homeostasis Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA‐IR) was calculated as a function of insulin and glucose (e.g., glucose in mg/dL × insulin in mU/L /405).
2.4
Cognition
The Global Cognitive Score was derived from a comprehensive assessment involving 19 cognitive tests that collectively evaluated various domains of cognitive function, including memory, executive function, attention, and language.
23
Each test contributed to the overall score, with individual performances standardized to create
z
scores. The raw scores of individual tests were converted to
z
scores, using the baseline mean and standard deviation from the entire cohort. A global cognition composite was obtained by averaging these
z
scores.
2.5
Peptide and protein abundance
Post mortem
brain tissue from the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) was used for peptide and protein abundance analysis by Proteome Sciences plc. The DLPFC brain region is both known to be affected by AD (e.g., amyloid deposition) and plays a role in cognition as well as in insulin signaling and neuroinflammation.
24
Tissue lysates underwent trypsin digestion, and peptides were labeled using TMTpro reagents to create 12 TMTpro 18‐plex samples.
25
More details are available in Methods
S1
.
TMTpro 18‐plex samples were split into portions for analyses of the total proteome (non‐enriched) and phosphoproteome (phosphopeptide‐enrichment using High SelectFe‐NTA spin columns, ThermoFisher Scientific). Basic pH reversed‐phase chromatography (bRP) using the High pH Reversed‐Phase Peptid Fractionation Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific) was used to fractionate these samples into six fractions each. Fractions were dried and stored at −80°C until MS analysis.
Analysis with LC‐MS/MS was conducted with an EASY‐nLCTM 1000 system and an Orbitrap Fusion Tribrid Mass Spectrometer (Thermo Scientific). bRP fractions were resuspended in 2% acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid. Peptide separation occurred on a C18 Acclaim PepMap 100 trapping column and EASY‐Spray analytical column. Mass spectra acquisition involved full scans at 120,000 resolution and MS2 fragment spectra at 50,000 resolution, with a 3 second duty cycle.
The SEQUEST HT algorithm processed MS/MS data, setting trypsin as the enzyme (up to two missed cleavages allowed). Precursor mass tolerance was fixed at 10 ppm, and fragment tolerance at 0.02 Da. Modifications included TMTpro modification of N‐termini and lysine residues; carbamidomethylation of cysteine; methionine oxidation; and phosphorylation at serine, threonine, and tyrosine for immobilized metal affinity chromatography–enriched fractions. TMTpro reporter intensities were extracted from MS2 spectra, with peptide‐to‐spectrum matches (PSMs) filtered at a 1% target false discovery rate (FDR). Peptide and protein level abundances were determined as log2 ratios relative to reference samples in each TMTplex experiment. Information on the data processing is available in Methods
S1
.
2.6
Statistical analysis
2.6.1
Preprocessing
The phosphorylated and unphosphorylated peptides were analyzed at the peptide level, with phosphosite calculated as log2 ratios relative to reference samples within each TMTpro experiment. Sample outliers were identified through visual inspection of the distribution patterns of log2 ratio abundances of phosphosites and unphosphorylated peptides, resulting in the exclusion of one sample outlier (Figure
S1
in supporting information) resulting in a sample set of 191. Phosphosites with localization confidence < 75% were excluded. For peptides localized to the same protein and phosphorylation site, and sharing identical phosphorylation sites, log2 ratio abundances were averaged, and this average value was assigned to the respective phosphosite. For unphosphorylated peptides, abundances of peptides with identical sequences were combined.
2.6.2
Differential abundance of phosphosites
To determine the differential abundance (DA) of each phosphosite, a linear modeling approach was implemented using the limma package.
26
Linear models were fitted for each phosphosite, with log‐ratio abundances as the dependent variable. The models included terms for age at death, sex, batch, PMI, and participant group (AD and DM, AD only, DM only, and none as the reference). The linear model is represented as:

DA of phosphosites was identified by applying moderated

This analysis included both stratified and unstratified groups based on DM status. To explore the combined impact of AD and DM status on phosphoproteome abundance, a two degree of freedom likelihood ratio test (2‐df LRT) compared a full model incorporating AD status and the interaction term of AD status × DM with a model excluding these terms. Both models included the same covariates as described above. The FDR was controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure.
27
Abundant phosphosites were identified using two criteria: an absolute log fold change (logFC) > 0.2 and a 2‐df LRT FDR of < 0.1. Phosphosites that were consistently abundant across all samples, including those with a DM status, were classified as “consistently abundant” (Group A). Phosphosites that were abundant specifically within participants with a DM status and had a significant interaction term were classified as “differentially abundant” (Group B).
2.6.3
Relative abundance and association analyses
To distinguish phosphorylation abundance independent of the proteins from which they are derived, referred to as parent proteins, the relative abundance of differentially abundant phosphosites was calculated. This was performed across participant groups by subtracting the average log2ratio abundance of unphosphorylated peptides of the parent protein from the log2ratio abundance of the phosphorylated peptide, effectively representing the log ratio due to the logarithmic nature of the measurements. Furthermore, an association analysis using Spearman correlation tests was conducted to explore the relationship of these phosphosites with cognitive function metrics, cerebrovascular pathology, and serum insulin–related markers. An FDR < 10%, as per the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure, was deemed statistically significant for these tests.
2.6.4
Differential analysis using relative abundance
DA analysis of phosphosites used relative abundance ratios, calculated by subtracting the average log2ratio abundance of unphosphorylated parent protein peptides from their phosphorylated counterparts. This approach used linear modeling and LRT, similar to previously described methods, with relative abundance replacing TMTRatio as the dependent variable.
2.6.5
Co‐abundant module analysis and cell‐type enrichment
Phosphoproteome abundances of 7874 phosphosites from 191 subjects, adjusted for covariates, were scaled and centered, followed by co‐abundance network analysis using the wgcna package in R.
28
,
29
A soft‐threshold power of 14 was selected based on model fit.
30
Network construction used the blockwiseModules function. To assess any cell‐type enrichment patterns in module genes, similar to Seyfried et al.,
4
we constructed human homolog cell type–specific gene lists from Sharma et al.,
31
defining proteins with a log2 fold change of at least two compared to the next most abundant cell type. We assessed the significance of cell‐type enrichment using the one‐tailed Fisher exact test and adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Module identification concerning clinical features used module eigen (ME) phosphosites and module membership (MM). The ME‐phosphosite refers to the first principal component of a module that summarizes the main variation in phosphorylation levels across all phosphosites within the module, reflecting the key trends in their abundances or phosphorylation states. Modules significantly associated with clinical features were identified via module significance (MS). We performed pathway enrichment analysis for the hub genes in each module. Genes with the most connections or high kME values within a module, referred to as highly connected genes or hub genes, are selected when the strong intramodular correlation of phosphosites' kME > 0.6. Functional enrichment analysis for these key modules was conducted using clusterProfiler in R,
32
applying Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway analysis with an FDR cutoff of < 0.1. The top five enriched pathways by lowest FDR are reported for hub genes of each module. This aids in understanding the biological functions and pathways enriched in modules with high intramodular connectivity and their potential roles in clinical traits. More details are available in Methods
S1
.

3.4
Phosphosite co‐abundance network analysis
To comprehensively understand the differences in the phosphoproteome associated with AD in the context of DM status and gain insights into the biological systems involved, we used WGCNA. We identified phosphosite clusters with correlated abundance patterns and studied their subsequent association with molecular pathways and various biological traits. After quality control (see Methods section), we constructed a protein co‐abundance network using WGCNA. This network comprised 35 distinct modules (PM‐1 to PM‐35), with the majority (24 out of 35) containing < 100 phosphosites each (Figure
S4
in supporting information).
We examined these modules in several ways. First, we investigated the distinct abundance patterns depending on the presence of pathological AD and DM status (Figure
3A
). We also investigated the module composition. Interestingly, many phosphosites in each module were exclusively mapped to a single protein. Next, we obtained the ME phosphosites using the first principal component of module phosphosite abundance. ME phosphosites were examined in their association with cognitive function and serum insulin‐related markers during life as well as autopsy‐based cerebrovascular pathology (Figure
3B
). Finally, we examined functional annotations of the top five hub proteins in each module (assessed by module eigengene‐based connectivity kME; Figure
3C
). The cell‐type enrichment analysis of each module showed that PM‐25 is enriched for astrocytes, but the other modules did not generally show much enrichment (Figure
3D
).

DISCUSSION
This study examines phosphoproteomic profiles in nearly 200 human brain specimens from older adults with and without type 2 diabetes and with and without pathologic AD. It identified DA and relative abundance of phosphoproteins related to AD overall, and conditional to the diabetes status. In particular, MAPT, which is phosphorylated at the sites T529 and T534 (isoform 8 T212 and T217) simultaneously, was not only consistently abundant and relatively abundant for AD versus non‐AD, but it was also more abundant among DM participants (with and without AD). In addition, in the WGCNA, we were able to unveil 35 distinct modules with diverse abundance patterns related to AD conditional to diabetes status. In particular, the module PM‐11 mapped exclusively to MAP1B and was highly correlated with not only cognitive level proximate to death but also with the change in cognition over the years preceding death, as well as with hemoglobin A1c levels during life. In addition, cell‐type enrichment identified a module, PM‐25, enriched for astrocytes. These novel findings suggest that the PM‐11 module of brain phosphoproteomics has clinical relevance in both cognitive decline and DM.
Differences in brain protein phosphorylation are a hallmark of AD and pivotal in understanding the molecular mechanisms of cellular dysfunction and death in this and other neurodegenerative processes.

Our data suggest a substantial overlap of AD and DM molecular footprints in human brain pathologies. The systems‐level analysis, by mapping out crucial signaling pathways and biomarkers involved in AD progression, highlights the potential of phosphoproteomics in unraveling the complexities of the link between common and impactful neurodegenerative diseases and the metabolic dysfunction of DM. Indeed, our findings offer novel insights into the molecular mechanisms driving AD and diabetes. In particular, we identified modules enriched in pathways essential for axonal guidance and focal adhesion (PM‐24 and PM‐32). The module PM‐32 is much larger and contains 3572 sites, as opposed to the much smaller PM‐24 with only 110 sites. The abundance of these clusters showed a synergistic effect of DM in the presence of AD pathology. Both clusters were significantly associated with the markers of DM, specifically with lower adiponectin and higher A1c, and both were associated with lower cognition and faster cognitive decline, albeit only PM‐32 was significant. It has been reported before that axonal guidance is impaired not only in AD but also within the context of metabolic disorders.

Further, our data uncovered the phosphorylation of semaphorins, originally discovered as axonal guidance molecules, as part of the module PM‐32 being at the intersection of the AD and DM (SEMA4B:S793, SEMA4C:S760, and SEMA4D:S857). However, the role of semaphorins has only recently been appreciated in metabolic disorders including DM.

Netrins, originally discovered as axon‐guiding molecules, have a newly assigned role in epithelial cell migration and specifically pancreas development.

It is plausible that other molecules out of > 3000 members of the PM‐24 and PM‐32 modules, play a joint role in DM‐related processed and axonal guidance.
Protein co‐abundance or gene co‐expression modules are often derived from proteins or genes expressed in specific cell types, enabling us to investigate relevant cell populations.
4
,

Our cell type enrichment analysis of protein phosphorylation modules using cell‐specific protein signatures showed limited enrichment. This observation may be due to incomplete representation of cell‐specific phosphorylation signatures within the reference dataset or to the site‐specific nature of protein phosphorylation, resulting in a co‐abundance structure that is less influenced by global differences in cell abundance. We identified, however, that module PM‐25, associated with glucose and hemoglobin A1c, was enriched in astrocytes. This finding is significant because astrocytes, as glial cells, play a critical role in the pathophysiology of dementia. They are involved in several key brain functions, including maintaining neurotransmitter balance, providing metabolic support to neurons, and modulating inflammation. Additionally, astrocytes are vital for supporting neurons, maintaining the blood–brain barrier, regulating neurotransmitter levels, and overall brain homeostasis.
In summary, this brain phosphoproteomic comprehensive approach not only contributes to our understanding of the interconnected conditions of DM and AD but also opens new avenues for developing targeted therapeutic strategies. Given the commonality in the phosphorylation patterns, our data support the idea of leveraging DM drugs for AD treatment. In addition, it also provides evidence for the emerging hypothesis on the key role of the axonal, guiding molecules at the interplay of AD and DM and offers new directions for research and drug development.
